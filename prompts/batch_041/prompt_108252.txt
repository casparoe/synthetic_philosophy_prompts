I hold the following position about attributing beliefs and desires to AI systems. The intentional stance is not merely a useful predictive heuristic when applied to large language models; at least some belief and desire attributions to such systems can be literally correct. On this view, to say that a model believes that p is to say that its behavior is reliably and systematically best predicted and explained by treating it as representing p and acting on that representation, where the pattern is robust across contexts, integrated with its other attributions, and causally sustained by internal states rather than by shallow input-output regularities. I am not claiming that the model is conscious, nor that it has original intentionality in the sense associated with Searle; I am claiming that the vocabulary of propositional attitudes can be apt for it in roughly the way Dennett thought it could be apt for a thermostat, but with the further claim that for contemporary LLMs the aptness can rise to literal truth rather than mere as-if convenience.

What would count against this? I want the defeaters mapped, not just listed. In particular:

1. Which empirical findings about LLMs would be decisive against literal attribution, which would be serious but survivable, and which would be merely awkward? Consider prompt sensitivity, sycophancy, hallucination, chain-of-thought unfaithfulness, in-context learning, activation steering, probing for truth directions, sparse autoencoders, and the instability of apparent beliefs across semantically irrelevant changes. For each, say what it would show if it held up, and what a defender should concede.

2. Which philosophical objections have real force? Distinguish the indeterminacy-of-interpretation objection, the disjunction problem, the rule-following considerations, the frame problem, the grain problem for ascribing beliefs at the right level of abstraction, the derived-versus-original intentionality objection, and the objection that model outputs are more like role-play or quotation than assertion. Which of these are independent, and which collapse into one another? Also address the distinction between occurrent and standing beliefs for a system whose context window is finite, and the possibility that what looks like a belief is only a policy for generating text.

3. What would it take for the position to be false rather than merely qualified? If the defender retreats to the claim that the stance is useful but not truth-apt, is that a concession or a clarification? Is there a version of the position that risks nothing and therefore explains nothing?

4. What should a defender of the literal-attribution view concede up front? I am most persuaded by mechanistic interpretability results that seem to locate stable, causally efficacious representations of truth, falsity, and other features, and by the fact that LLMs can revise apparent beliefs in light of evidence in ways that look more than merely associative. I am least persuaded by behavior-only arguments from conversational fluency. Tell me whether those priors are defensible, and what would have to happen for me to give them up.

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which ones the position can accommodate without loss and which ones would force a genuine revision.
