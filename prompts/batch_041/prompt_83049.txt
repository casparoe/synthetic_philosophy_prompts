Having spent my career in experimental physics and my retirement reading philosophy and decision theory, I find myself stuck on a puzzle about a result rather than about an argument. Aumann's agreement theorem, as I understand it, says that two agents who share a common prior, whose posterior probabilities are common knowledge between them, and who update by conditionalization, cannot agree to disagree: if their posteriors for some event are common knowledge, those posteriors must be equal. The mathematics I accept. What I cannot make sense of is the decades of commentary around it, which seems to have settled into two camps that cannot hear each other. One camp treats the theorem as a profound constraint on rational disagreement, as showing that persistent disagreement between people who respect each other's evidence must betray some failure of rationality or of common knowledge. The other treats it as close to vacuous: the common-prior assumption is doing all the work, real agents never have common knowledge of one another's rationality and posteriors, and the theorem forbids at most a bookkeeping inconsistency rather than telling us anything about whether people ought to converge. Reading across both, I have the impression that neither side has ever been forced to concede anything, and I doubt this is simply because one side is wrong.

Here is my hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis. I suspect the two camps are answering different questions under one name. The theorem is a claim about agreement in probability, that is, identity of posterior probabilities within a fixed state space and a fixed partition of that space into possibilities. But the disagreements I spent my career inside — whether an anomaly was real, whether a signal was an artifact of the apparatus, whether a model had hold of the right variables at all — are almost never of that form. They are disagreements about the state space itself: about which possibilities exist, which variables matter, how the world is to be carved up before any probability is assigned. If that is right, then the theorem's defenders and its deflators may both be correct, the first about a narrow class of disputes and the second about everything else, and the apparent stall is the sound of two true claims about non-overlapping subject matters. I also wonder whether the distinction between common knowledge and mere mutual knowledge, which I only encountered recently, is quietly doing more of the work than either side acknowledges.

What I want, then, is a diagnosis and not a verdict. Which of these is it: a verbal dispute about what the word agreement is being made to mean; a genuine disagreement about whether the theorem is a normative or a descriptive claim; a disagreement about which real cases fall inside the theorem's scope, that is, about whether its idealizations are harmless or load-bearing; or two groups answering different questions under one heading, as my hypothesis has it? Tell me what each side actually commits to, whether my state-space suggestion survives contact with the real arguments, and what — if anything — would count as progress here: a sharper distinction, a formal extension of the theorem to agents who disagree about the space of possibilities, a change of question, or nothing at all, because this impasse is exactly what one should expect when a mathematical result is asked to arbitrate a philosophical dispute. I would rather have an honest no-progress-is-possible-and-here-is-why than another survey.
