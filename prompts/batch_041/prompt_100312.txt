I'm a second-year PhD student in criminology and I keep circling something that came up in our sentencing seminar and I can't get it out of my head. A bunch of countries — Germany, Finland, Sweden, roughly half of Europe, plus a few experiments here in the US — sentence people to "day fines" instead of a flat sum. The mechanics: the court first decides how many day-fines you get, and that number is supposed to track how serious the offense was / how blameworthy the offender is, so worse crime = more days. Then a seperate number — the value of a single day — gets set from your income, basically a slice of your daily disposable income (net of taxes and a living allowance). The total is days × daily rate. So two people convicted of the identical offense get the identical number of days, but the richer one hands over vastly more money. The stated point of the whole thing is to make the burden of the fine land equally hard on everyone regardless of income. Meanwhile in the US we do close to the opposite with flat fines and fees, which is why a $200 ticket can wreck someone living paycheck to paycheck and mean nothing to a banker.

What I want from you: what does this practice actually presuppose? It looks like it quietly splits punishment into two variables — a "how much punishment" variable that's meant to be desert-based and welfare-blind, and a "how much it hurts" variable that's welfare-sensitive and looks almost purely consequentialist (equalizing marginal disutility, or maybe just optimizing deterrence). Is that split coherent, or does it secretly assume that the severity of a punishment can be measured in some unit — days, units — that's independent of how much anyone actually suffers from it? And here's the part that really bugs me: we equalize felt burden for fines but we mostly don't for prison — a person with three kids and a chronic illness serves the same five years as a healthy rich guy, and nobody thinks the sentence should shrink to equalize the pain. So does the practice presuppose a view on which money-punishments and liberty-punishments answer to different theories, and if so which ones? Reconstruct the implicit theory as best you can, be explicit about what the practice strictly requires versus what it merely happens to fit (I suspect a pure retributivist and a pure deterrence theorist could both sign off on it for different reasons, and I want to know if that's right), and tell me what would have to change if we took seriously the idea that the severity of a punishment just is its felt impact. Also flag whether the two halves of the day-fine presuppose anything inconsistent about what makes a punishment just.

End with a last line starting "Verdict:" that names, in a short phrase, the single theory the practice most clearly presupposes.
