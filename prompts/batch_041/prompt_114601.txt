I work as a software engineer, and for the past year I have been building the scoring engine for an environmental consultancy that runs biodiversity impact assessments for developers. When a project will destroy a stretch of habitat, the tool calculates how many biodiversity units are lost and how many the developer must buy back elsewhere, where a unit is essentially habitat area multiplied by a condition score and a distinctiveness weight. The policy the engine implements is called no net loss, and offsetting is the last resort in a sequence that runs avoid, then minimize, then restore, then offset. What puzzles me is how much this whole apparatus appears to assume without ever saying it.

On the surface it treats habitat as a commodity: a hectare of degraded grassland here can be traded for a hectare of restored grassland there, so long as the numbers match. But the same tool contains a category of irreplaceable habitat where no amount of offsetting is permitted, as though some places carried a value that could not be substituted. And nowhere does the metric count individual animals, or whether the animals in question are living well or badly; it counts hectares, species, and habitat condition. The stated purpose is to protect nature, yet what is being protected seems to be an abstraction into which no particular creature's experience enters.

I would like you to reconstruct the theory of value this practice presupposes. Is it committed to biodiversity or ecosystem integrity mattering in itself, to individual animal welfare not counting at all in the wild, or to something more deflationary, such as habitat being a tractable proxy for whatever we really care about? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out, and which does it merely sit comfortably with without requiring? I am especially interested in a tension I cannot resolve on my own. The practice treats habitat destruction as a loss to be compensated, which seems to assume that the animals who would have lived in the destroyed habitat are worse off for its absence. But I have read that some argue wild animals' lives are on balance bad, full of hunger, disease, and being eaten alive, and that more habitat simply means more of that. If that were right, would the no net loss machinery be pointed in the wrong direction entirely, so that the welfare-maximizing move might be to destroy habitat rather than create it? Tell me whether the practice strictly requires the assumption that more habitat is good for animals or whether it can stay neutral on that point, and what would have to change if the metric were rebuilt around the summed welfare of sentient creatures, weighting a hectare by how many animals it holds and how well or badly those lives go, rather than by condition and distinctiveness. Finally, say where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories of value would each license exactly the same scoring formula.
