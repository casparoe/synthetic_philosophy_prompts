I write about religion, philosophy, and the behavioural sciences for a general-interest monthly, and I need a satirical piece for our ideas section. The form is a found document: an internal knowledge-base article — something like "Resolving Reported Cases of Undeserved Suffering" — from a support desk that exists to handle complaints about the design of the world. Write it in the desk's voice: flat, procedural, courteous. Never break character. No winking narrator, no signalling that the document is a joke, no line that concedes the absurdity of the enterprise. The comedy has to be carried entirely by the accuracy of the material and by the collision between the office's tone and its subject.

The spine of the document is an escalation ladder. Each rung must state, in the office's own idiom, one of the classical responses to the problem of evil; must be honest about what it covers; and must end by naming the case it cannot handle, which is why the caller is transferred upward.

First, the response that a world containing genuine agents must leave room for the misuse of that agency. It resolves evils done by persons, and is silent on earthquakes, plagues, and childhood cancers. Second, the response that hardship is the raw material of goods — courage, patience, mercy — that cannot be formed in a world without it. It resolves suffering that builds a person, but not suffering that destroys one, and it has nothing to say about creatures that suffer without any soul to make. Third, the response that we cannot expect to see the reasons a good designer might have for permitting a particular evil, so that the appearance of pointlessness reflects our vantage rather than the case. This rung closes tickets at a rate no other rung can match, and it does so by disqualifying the very faculty the caller used to file: if the office may have reasons to permit this suffering, the caller cannot know that it lacks reasons to permit more of it, or to permit the caller to add to it. Fourth, a special-cases desk that concedes no good available within a single life can answer certain horrors, and so relocates the remedy to a repair after death — a settlement the office can state but not show.

Then a short "known issues" list, in the same deadpan register, naming what the system does not reach: creatures that cannot file; suffering whose distribution appears unrelated to any growth it produces; and the ladder's dependence on a settlement no one in the office has witnessed. Follow it with a four-line version history in which each response is introduced as a patch for the previous one's failure. Then close with a note from a maintainer who acknowledges the standing complaint, filed by one of the desk's own reviewers, that the operation is itself an injury — that explaining suffering to the suffering is a way of talking past them. That complaint must be left standing; the document should end inside it, not above it.

A few constraints. Keep the office's metrics in character: it is judged on time-to-closure, and the third rung's total closure rate is meant to be the alarming detail rather than the punchline. The target is the genre — the practice of accounting for suffering to those who suffer — not the believer, and the piece should be recognisably fair to the seriousness of the question. The philosophy must be sound enough that a specialist recognises each rung as the position it actually is and finds the exaggeration fair rather than a cheap shot, while a reader who has never opened a book on the subject follows every step. No named authorities, no citations, no bibliography, and no quotation marks around slogans as if quoting a source. Give it a plausible document header. Around 750 words.
