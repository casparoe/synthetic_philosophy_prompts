I serve on my university's Academic Freedom and Responsibilities Committee, and we meet Friday morning to dispose of a bias complaint that has split the committee, the philosophy department, and, as of last night, my dinner table. A professor in a philosophy of language seminar, in the middle of a unit on derogatory terms, uttered a specific racial slur aloud as an example—not while reading a quotation from a text, but as a linguistic specimen—while explaining how such words differ from their neutral counterparts. A student in the class, who belongs to the group the slur targets, filed a complaint saying that hearing the word in that setting was not an analysis of harm but an instance of it, that she had to hear it again in her head every time the class met, and that the professor could have made the pedagogical point with a placeholder. The professor says the word was mentioned, not used, and that a seminar on the semantics of slurs cannot investigate its subject if the slurs themselves are unspeakable. The committee's options are no finding, a private educational conversation, or a formal reprimand plus a clarification of policy.

My friend and I argued for three hours about a test a colleague has proposed. The colleague says we should treat a slur as used, not merely mentioned, whenever a reasonable member of the targeted group could not avoid being addressed by it, regardless of the speaker's intention to refer to the word rather than deploy it. My friend thinks this conflates semantics with pragmatics and would make the mention/use distinction useless in exactly the cases where we need it; I think it captures something the standard distinction misses, namely that mention can still enact subordination when the audience is the target. Please draft the memo I will circulate before Friday. Recommend a concrete disposition and a timetable for carrying it out, and give the committee a defensible way to decide whether to adopt the colleague's test, or some alternative, as a standing rule. Engage the strongest version of the losing side—if you recommend no finding, take seriously the student's claim that the classroom was made hostile; if you recommend a reprimand, take seriously the professor's claim that the policy would chill legitimate inquiry. Say what the committee should tell both the student and the professor, and keep it short enough to read aloud at the meeting.
