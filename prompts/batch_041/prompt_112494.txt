I'm a junior in high school, and I'm in a small philosophy club that meets on Thursdays after school. This term we've been working through decision theory, and the session I can't stop thinking about was on the Death in Damascus problem — the one where a man learns that Death has an appointment with him in Damascus, so he flees to Aleppo, and it turns out the appointment was in Aleppo all along. What stuck with me wasn't the twist but the way the two main theories treat it. The evidential view says the man is stuck either way, because his choosing to stay is evidence that Death is in Damascus and his choosing to flee is evidence that Death is in Aleppo, so both options carry the same bad news; the causal view says Death's location is fixed regardless of what he does, so the choice is causally inert and he might as well pick on some other ground. I want to close our last meeting by reading a poem that takes this seriously rather than just retelling the story, and I'd like you to write it.

Could you write it as a sestina, with the six end-words doing the philosophical work? I'd like two of the end-words to be the two cities, so that as the permutation moves them through the stanzas they keep trading places — the poem's form should make the reader feel that staying and fleeing are the same shape, and that the evidence keeps following the choice instead of the other way around. The other end-words should carry the rest of the problem: I want the appointment and the attempt to escape among them, and the sixth is your call — whatever single word best names the thing the man can't get out from under. The envoi is where I want the two cities to finally land in the same line, not as a resolution but as the moment the reader sees that the escape was the appointment. Someone who knows the problem should recognize the evidential and causal verdicts as accurately rendered — the poem shouldn't take sides or turn fatalist, and it shouldn't end on a shrug; the vertigo should stay open. A reader who doesn't know the problem should still get a good poem out of it.

Please give it to me as a letter — continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold or tables, the way you'd write an email — with the poem itself set out in its stanzas, since the line breaks are the form. And please don't explain the philosophy outside the poem; if the poem needs a note, I'd rather it fail as a poem than succeed as a footnote.
