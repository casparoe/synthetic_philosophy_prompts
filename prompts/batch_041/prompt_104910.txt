I spent thirty-five years as a research scientist in infectious disease, most of it designing and running field trials and cohort studies in places where the study budget dwarfed the local health budget, and I sat on our research ethics committee for eleven of those years. I have recently retired and begun reading moral philosophy, largely on my own, and I have found something mildly embarrassing: I hold firm verdicts about which consents are real and which are not, and I cannot state the principle behind them. I would like you to find that principle, or to persuade me there is none. I would rather be told plainly that one of my verdicts has to go than be offered an account that saves all of them at the cost of being unusable in an actual consent conversation.

Here are my verdicts.

1. A trial consent form in a malaria-endemic region details the experimental vaccine's side effects at length but never mentions that participants will not receive the insecticide-treated bed nets the local clinic normally distributes, nets that many participants assume they will keep. I think the consent was invalid — the omission was material, though nobody lied.

2. A woman enrolls in a trial chiefly for the per-visit payment, which comes to about a month's wages; she would rather not take an experimental drug, but she needs the money. I think she consented. The offer was not a threat, and she weighed it and chose.

3. A man in the early stages of dementia agrees to donate a kidney to his son. He understands the operation, the pain, and the recovery, and repeats his reasons without prompting, but his neurologist says he cannot keep the long-term risk to his remaining kidney in view. I do not think he can consent.

4. A prisoner volunteers for a low-risk pharmacokinetic study in exchange for canteen credit. However freely he appears to volunteer, I do not think his consent can be valid.

5. A man lies to a woman about having had a vasectomy; she would not have slept with him otherwise. I think what he did was a serious wrong, but I am not persuaded that she failed to consent — she agreed to the act itself, and the lie concerned a consequence of it.

6. A surgeon, describing a rare complication, says it almost never happens; the true figure is one in two hundred. The patient agrees to the operation. Invalid.

7. A patient signs a form permitting any future research use of tissue removed during a routine biopsy. I think this is valid consent, even though she cannot possibly know what the future research will be.

8. A man in severe pain, told he must decide within minutes whether to have an emergency procedure, says yes. He understands what is proposed. I think this counts, though he had no time to think.

9. An unconscious accident victim is enrolled in a trial of a new resuscitation fluid; he survives, recovers, and says he would have refused had anyone asked him. I think the enrollment was permissible, but I would not call it consent.

The case I keep circling back to: a participant consents to a trial of a drug whose long-term effects are genuinely unknown to everyone, investigators included; five years later the drug is found to cause a condition she would have refused to risk. Nobody deceived her — the fact did not exist as knowledge when she agreed. Did she consent? Some days I say yes, because she accepted the epistemic situation she was in; other days I say that consent to a risk nobody has specified is not consent to anything in particular.

What I want from you: state the principle, or the two or three competing principles, that best fit these verdicts; for each clause of it, tell me which case forces that clause; tell me what the principle implies for cases I have not listed (I would particularly like its verdict on consent given on another person's behalf, and on consent that is withdrawn only after the fact); and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. If the price of fitting cases 2 and 4 together is a distinction no one could apply in a real consent conversation, say so, and tell me which of the two I should give up.
