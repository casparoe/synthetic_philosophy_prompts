I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of a decade now, and there's a family of worries I keep circling back to that I think gets run together under a single label. The consequentialist is supposed to choose the act with the best consequences, and the standard objection is that we are simply clueless about the remote consequences of almost anything we do: the causal ripples fan out indefinitely and any honest attempt to sum them up is guesswork. I got into this arguing with a friend who treats the objection as a clean reductio of the whole view, and my stubborn reaction was that the objection seems to cover at least three quite different situations, and that lumping them together is a large part of why the debate goes in circles. In the first situation the uncertainty is ordinary and symmetric: we don't know which way the effects cut, but they are bounded and roughly cancel in expectation, so the choice is no harder than any other decision under uncertainty. In the second, the distribution of consequences is heavy-tailed in the relevant sense, so that the expected value of an act is dominated by outcomes so far out in the tail that no sample or model we could build would pin them down; here the calculation is not merely uncertain but unstable, since small changes in our priors flip the verdict, and the same feature shows up in the little cases where a minute probability of an enormous payoff swamps everything else in the sum. In the third, the sum itself misbehaves: the relevant consequences may be unbounded, or the values may not be cardinally comparable across the options, so there may be no fact of the matter about which act is better, which is a different problem from not knowing it.

I want to write a term paper (roughly six thousand words, one semester) that takes the form of a research proposal rather than a finished argument, so I'd like the proposal to state the question so that it has a determinate answer. Something along the lines of: which of these three phenomena actually deserves the name cluelessness, and for which of them does the consequentialist's standard move of maximizing expected value still yield a determinate and action-guiding verdict? Then I'd want it to explain why the existing positions leave this open, since as far as I can tell everyone agrees there is an epistemic problem and then either bites the bullet or quietly narrows the scope of what counts as a consequence, without asking which kind of unpredictability is doing the work. The method would be taxonomic and analytic: separate the three cases with clean toy models, test whether the familiar replies to the cluelessness objection — restricting the consequentialist's attention to foreseeable consequences, evaluating decision procedures rather than acts one at a time, accepting some threshold of evidential relevance — handle each case, and see whether any of them survives the heavy-tailed and the unbounded cases or whether those demand a genuinely different treatment. I'd like the proposal to say what a result would look like (a mapping from kind of unpredictability to which decision-theoretic resources are still available), what would count as the project failing (for instance, if the three cases collapse into one under pressure, or if the taxonomy turns out to be doing no work in any of the standard replies), and to give an honest assessment of scope, being clear about which part of this is realistically doable in a semester and which part is a dissertation. I don't need citations or a literature review, I'll fill those in myself, and I'd rather the proposal stand on the distinctions than on who said what, so please keep it free of named theories and specific papers.
