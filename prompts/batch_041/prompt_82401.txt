I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of biology, and I need to turn a half-formed idea into a proposal for a summer research grant — roughly three months of full-time work, with a short paper as the deliverable. The idea came out of an argument with a friend in a seminar on information in biology. The long-running dispute over whether DNA genuinely codes for proteins — whether the genetic system carries information in a robust, content-bearing sense or only in a thin, causal-covariational sense — seems to me to have stalled because the word code is being asked to do two jobs at once, and because the two available vocabularies are each too coarse to settle it. What I want to try is importing a third vocabulary, the engineering one: a code as a mapping from messages to signals that is (i) arbitrary, in the sense that it could have been otherwise and is fixed by convention or history rather than by physical necessity, (ii) systematic enough that the mapping composes, so that structured messages yield structured signals, and (iii) redundant in a way that supports error detection or correction. My hunch is that the genetic system satisfies some of these hallmarks robustly and others only by loose analogy, and that the real philosophical work lies in saying which, and in deciding whether the resulting profile is graded or binary — that is, whether being a code admits of degrees at all. Please turn this into a proposal: state the question so that it has a determinate answer, explain why the existing positions in the debate leave it open rather than settling it, set out the method and a month-by-month plan for the three months, and say what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing. I'd also like a paragraph justifying this approach over the obvious alternatives — a straight synthesis of the existing literature, or an empirical survey of how working biologists actually use the term — since a reviewer will ask.
