I'm putting together a little teaching unit on Moore's paradox and I want a dialogue rather than an essay. Two characters, both competent, neither one a strawman, and they should actually answer each others points instead of taking turns giving speeches.

Here's the case I want them arguing about, and I'm making it up, so don't worry about whether it's in the literature. Call her the Aldergrove patient. After a head injury she has a stable, repeatable condition. Asked 'is it raining outside' she says yes, confidently, and acts on it -- grabs her coat, cancels the picnic -- and this never wavers over months of testing. But asked 'do you believe its raining', she says no, flatly. If you point out that she just said it was raining, she agrees she said it, doesn't take it back, and doesn't seem confused or joking. She'll say things like 'it's raining and I don't think it is', over and over, with no distress and no sense of having contradicted herself.

So: is she in a Moore-paradoxical state of mind or not? One of the two should hold that the absurdity is only about assertion -- the sentence is unassertable because of the norms governing assertion, but the underlying state of believing both halves is either possible or at least not obviously impossible, and Aldergrove is the test case. The other should hold that the absurdity is a fact about thought rather than talk, so the case has to be diagnosed some other way: one of her reports isn't a genuine belief report, or she doesn't really believe one of the conjuncts, or something along those lines. Let them fight over which escape is the best one, and let one of them float a criterion for when a verbal report counts as a belief report at all and the other try to break it with a case.

I'd also like them to get into the messy parts. The difference between the omissive form ('p, but I don't believe p') and the commissive form ('p, but I believe not-p') -- does the case pull them apart? Why the past tense version ('it was raining but I didn't believe it') and the third person version ('it's raining but he doesn't believe it') are completely fine, since thats the main clue about where the absurdity lives. Whether she's self-deceived rather than paradoxically believing. Whether the second report is a belief report at all or a report of a feeling -- an absence of conviction, the missing 'it seems to me' -- and whether that distinction does any real work or is just a dodge. And whether it matters that we're describing one person rather than two systems in one head doing the talking.

End with them not fully agreeing but having narrowed things down to a real disagreement, and be honest about wich parts the case doesn't settle.
