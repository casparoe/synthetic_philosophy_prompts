I have been reading rationalist and agent-foundations blogs for the better part of fifteen years, and I keep returning to a cluster of problems that I think is underrated even by the people who care about it. I would like your help working out which parts of it actually deserve the effort.

The cluster has two halves. The first is ontology identification. An agent can be uncertain not only about which world it is in but about what the world is made of: whether the thing it has learned to call a particle is one object or two, whether the boundary it draws between itself and its environment tracks anything real, whether the category it uses for a person is a joint of nature or an artifact of its training data. Its sensors can be perfectly reliable, its predictions accurate, and its ontology still wrong in the sense that matters, because its actions and its values are defined over the categories it has, not over whatever the world actually contains. A sharp version of this: two ontologies can each be expressible only at the cost of the other, so an agent cannot assign a prior over them jointly and the choice between them is not a bet it can hedge. The second half is inaccessible information: cases where the truth of a matter is settled but permanently out of reach — facts outside the agent's future light cone, facts about the substrate it runs on, facts about which of two empirically identical worlds it inhabits. The interesting question is not whether such facts exist but what a rational agent should do with the fact that they are unavailable, and whether uncertainty about them should be a reason for caution, something to bracket, or a defect in the agent's model of its own situation. A sharp version here: an agent that suspects the world may be richer than its vocabulary permits, that there may be morally considerable things it cannot represent as such, faces the question whether to adopt a precautionary policy toward ontology itself.

Both halves share a self-referential sting that I suspect is where the real work is: the agent's ontology and its information-access situation are things it is using to think with while being uncertain about them. That makes the standard machinery — hypothesis spaces, priors over worlds, expected utility — look as though it has assumed the problem away.

Here is what I want from you. Suppose you are advising a small theory group: three years, two or three people, one with a background in mathematical logic and one in philosophy, no lab and no empirical data of their own. Which open questions in this cluster should they put first, and which should they deliberately leave alone? I care much more about the reasoning than the list. For each question you nominate, tell me why it is upstream of the others, whether it is tractable with the tools those two people plausibly have, and what argument or result over the next couple of years would move it up or down your ranking. I would also like to know which questions here look deep but would not change much downstream if answered, and why the ones you deprioritize are safe to deprioritize.

Please write this as continuous prose, the way you would write a long email to a colleague — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
