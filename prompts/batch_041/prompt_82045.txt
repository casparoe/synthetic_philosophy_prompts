Bit of an odd one, this, and a long one — but if I trim it down I'll lose the parts that matter.

I took early retirement from a chemistry lectureship a couple of years back and, for reasons I'm still working out, I've been sitting in on a part-time philosophy course. Two people from the seminar, Alan and Ruth, invited me along to their fortnightly games night. The game is homemade: no board, just a rulebook, and the players are allowed to rewrite the rulebook as they go. Five of us round the table.

Starting rules:
1. Turns pass clockwise.
2. On your turn you may propose one change to the rulebook. It's adopted if more players vote for it than against.
3. An adopted change takes effect the moment the vote ends, and applies to everything from that point on.

Three arguements followed. I've set them out as fairly as I can; I definately couldn't tell who had the better of it.

Round one. Alan moved to change rule 2 so that a change needs three-quarters of the players rather than a bare majority. The vote was three for, two against. Alan said that was that. Ruth said it couldn't have been adopted, because the rule he was introducing is the rule that says three-quarters, and his own move only got three votes out of five, nowhere near three-quarters. Alan said it wasn't in force yet — rule 3, it applies from that point on — and that her way of looking at it would mean a rulebook could never make its own amendment harder, which can't be right, or nobody could ever entrench anything.

Round two. Alan proposed rule 4: rules 2 and 4 may only be changed by a unanimous vote of all players present. That went through four to one, which clears three-quarters, so adopted. Ruth then moved to repeal rule 4. Vote: four for, one against. Alan said invalid — rule 4 needs unanimity and there was one against. Ruth said he had it backwards: if the repeal goes through then rule 4 isn't there any more, so it can't have blocked the vote that got rid of it. Letting a rule veto its own abolition is circular; you can't use the rule's say-so to decide a vote about whether the rule survives. Alan said she was doing exactly what she'd done in round one, only in reverse: reading the validity of the vote off the rulebook as it stood afterwards rather than during.

Ruth then conceded that, on the rules, he might be right, and put her real point: the rulebook's authority isn't made of anything except the five of us continuing to go along with it. If all five of us had voted to scrap rule 4 and then carried on as though it were scrapped, it would be scrapped — not because the vote was legal, but because there's nothing underneath the rulebook saying we have to obey it. Alan said that was changing the subject. Inside the game, the repeal failed.

Round three. Ruth proposed rule 5: any change adopted with fewer than five votes is void. Vote: four for, one against. Ruth said rule 5 is void, obviously — it didn't get five votes. Alan said no, if it's void then it never applied to itself, so four votes was plenty, so it was adopted, so it's void. She said that just proves her point. He said it proves the opposite. Neither would move.

Could you go through it round by round and say who's right about what? I don't need a scoreline — I need to know where each of them went wrong at each step, and what both of them are missing. And then, if it isn't too much of a leap: thirty years of spectroscopy left me with a firm belief that there's a fact of the matter about whether a peak's been assigned correctly, and I'd like to know whether Alan is right that there are facts about what the rulebook implies, or Ruth is right that it's only ever words plus whatever we'll put up with.
