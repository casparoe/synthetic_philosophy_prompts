Me and my mate Dana spent about four hours last night arguing about whether you can believe every claim in your own book and also believe the book contains at least one error, and whether that's really the same thing as the lottery ticket case, and I came out of it realising we weren't disagreeing about the puzzle so much as quietly disagreeing about about five background things I'd never bothered to identify. So now I want to turn it into an instrument for the reading group.

Here's the setup. It's an epistemology reading group, ten-ish people. About half are Bayesians who think flat-out belief is a fiction we tell ourselves and everything should be credences plus a decision rule. The other half are more old-school and think credences are a modelling convenience and belief is the real thing. Two of them work on legal epistemology and care about statistical evidence in trials. I want a decision tree they can work through individually and then compare results, because I suspect half our arguments are actually people sitting at different nodes without knowing it.

So: build me a decision tree that starts from someone who has felt the pull of both the lottery case (a ticket in a huge fair draw, you're about 0.999999 sure it lost, can you just believe it lost?) and the preface case (a book with fifty claims, each of which you're about 0.99 sure of, so you're sure the book has at least one error, but you believe each claim), and leads to a named position. Each branch node should be one commitment that separates the positions, phrased as a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. I don't want a question like 'do you accept conjunctive closure?' — I want the node to be a case or a claim, so the answer falls out of what they say, not what they'd call themselves.

The commitments I think belong on the tree, in whatever order the logic wants: (a) is flat-out belief a real attitude at all, or just a credence above some line; (b) if it's real, can a rational belief be less than certain, or does belief need probability 1; (c) when you notice the tension, is the repair to drop a belief, or to give up the idea that rational beliefs have to fit together, or to give up the idea that they have to be jointly consistent (that last one is a different move from the second and I care a lot about the tree not smearing them together — probabilistically coherent but logically inconsistent is a live option and I want a leaf for it); (d) is the lottery case special — is there something distinctively bad about purely statistical grounds for belief, as opposed to the general fallibility of all our beliefs — or is the ticket just fallibilism doing its thing; (e) does the interesting action happen at belief, at knowledge, or at assertion.

Cases I want used, because I think they split people differently and I'm tired of everyone reaching for the same two examples: the ticket; the book; a long computation you did yourself where you know your own per-step error rate, so you're confident of the conclusion and also confident you made a slip somewhere; a book whose preface claim is itself one of the numbered claims in the book; a book with fifty authors each certain of their own chapter; and a legal case where the only evidence against someone is a bare statistic — say a town where 90% of the buses are blue and the only witness evidence is that the bus was blue. Include that last one, and phrase the node so it's about whether that statistic could ever license flat-out belief, not about courtroom procedure.

Leaves I want to see: credence-only eliminativism about belief; an infallibilism where belief or knowledge needs probability 1 and so the ticket is simply not believed; a fallibilism that keeps the claims of the book together by accepting that a rational person can hold jointly inconsistent beliefs; a fallibilism that drops the requirement that rational beliefs be closed under conjunction, so the ticket and the book are one phenomenon seen twice; a lottery-specific view where merely statistical evidence is a defective ground for belief; an anti-luck or safety view where the ticket belief fails a condition that the book claims pass; a threshold-with-stability view that tries to buy conjunction back by making belief context-relative; and a view that says the paradox is about assertion or the norms of writing a preface rather than about belief at all. Rename any of these if my labels are bad, but keep the distinctions.

Size: six to nine branch nodes, eight to twelve leaves. Where two leaves can't be separated by any question you can put to a reader, say so explicitly instead of pretending the tree distinguishes them — I'd rather know which of our disagreements are undetectable from the inside. And for any combination of answers that no actual position occupies, tell me whether that's because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's bothered — that's half the reason I want the tree.
