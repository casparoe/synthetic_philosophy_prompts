So — retired field ecologist here, thirty-eight years counting birds and beetles, and since I hung up the binoculars I've been trying to teach myself philosophy, which is going about as well as you'd expect. I've ended up in a discussion group with a few other ex-scientists, I've been volunteered to lead the session on causation, and the thing I keep tripping over is Simpson's paradox. I could use your help building something.

Here's why it bugs me. Back in the 90s I had a long survey where one of my warblers (one of the ones I could actually identify by ear) was clearly down overall, call it a fifteen percent drop, but when I broke the numbers out by habitat type it was up in every single one. Wet meadow, scrub, the lot. What had happened was that the mix of sites I was sampling shifted over the years — I lost access to a couple of wet sites, picked up a lot of scrub. So the aggregate number was real, the within-habitat numbers were real, and they pointed in opposite directions. A young colleague waved the whole thing off as just Simpson's paradox and I realised I couldn't have said what he'd actually comitted himself to by saying that.

What I want is a decision tree — a flowchart I can hand round the group — that takes someone from "I've got two tables, pooled and stratified, and they disagree" to a nameable position on what to do about it. Build it out of the questions, not out of a list of famous people. Every node should be something a person can answer without already knowing which camp their answer drops them in.

Some things I'd definately like the tree to be able to separate, because I think these are the real fault lines:

- the person who says there's no paradox at all, it's just weighted averages doing what weighted averages do, versus the person who thinks the reversal is telling you something about causation;
- the person who says there's no fact of the matter and it just depends which question you're asking, versus the person who thinks exactly one of the two tables is the correct one;
- if there is a fact of the matter, what fixes it. Here I want at least one node on the third variable and what role it's playing: is it a common cause of both the others, a downstream effect of one that then feeds into the other, or a common effect of both? I'm told these three cases come apart badly and I'd like the tree to make me see how;
- the prediction-versus-explanation split: if all I want is to guess next spring's count, do I even have a problem?
- the person who says the whole fuss is an artifact of which summary measure you use — I've been told odds ratios and risk differences behave differently when you pool strata, which I don't fully understand, so keep that node self-contained;
- and the decision angle: if one option looks better in every stratum but worse overall, and I have to pick one, does expected utility settle it, or is that still a causal question in disguise?

Aim for eight to twelve branching questions and six to eight leaves. Each leaf gets the name of the position (or "unnamed" if nobody actually holds it) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any two leaves your tree can't really separate, and if there's a combination of answers that looks logically available but nobody seems to be standing there, say so and tell me whether that's because the view is incoherent or just unloved. And keep it readable for people who, like me, have to look up "collider" every single time.
