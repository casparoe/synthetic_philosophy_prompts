I write about science and ideas for a general readership, and I am preparing a long-form essay on the ethics of reproductive selection. Before I commit to a position in print, I want a rigorous audit of whether my commitments can be held together. Here is where I have landed. (1) Procreative beneficence: when prospective parents can choose among possible children, they have a pro tanto moral reason to choose the child whose life is expected to go best, where best is measured by the child's own expected well-being rather than by the parents' preferences or social convenience. (2) That reason is non-overriding and does not justify state coercion; it operates inside a robust sphere of reproductive autonomy. (3) Disability is substantially a social construction: the principal burdens of disability are produced by inaccessible environments, stigma, and institutional exclusion, not by impairment as such. (4) It can be morally permissible, and sometimes admirable, for parents to choose a child with a disability, for example deafness, when that choice is embedded in a valued cultural community and a positive identity; and a deaf couple who use preimplantation genetic diagnosis to select an embryo carrying a deafness-associated variant should be permitted, and their choice should not be described as a harm to the child, even if a hearing child would score higher on conventional social metrics of expected well-being. (5) There is no principled therapy-enhancement distinction; the distinction is a political artifact, and attempts to police it are incoherent. (6) The historical wrong of eugenics lay in coercion, state direction, and the ranking of lives, not in selection as such; a liberal, voluntary, pluralistic eugenics is therefore not the same moral object. (7) The non-identity problem shows that a child cannot be harmed by being brought into existence with a condition, provided her life is worth living, so the complaint that selection wrongs the selected child is incoherent.

Which of these commitments are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and how severe is each tension? Rank the genuine tensions by severity. For each, say which commitment would have to give way or be revised, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision costs. Do not assume that I would surrender any particular item; I need the trade-offs. I am not asking for a literature review or citations; I want the audit.
