ok, I want a proper in-the-weeds explanation of the wrong kind of reasons problem. I know the shape of it — you say something is good iff there's reason to favour it, then a demon says he'll kill you unless you admire him, so you've got a reason to admire him, but he's still not admirable, so the analysis looks broken — but I've never quite got why this is supposed to be *hard*, or what the real escape routes are. So please walk me through it properly.

Specifically I'd like the difference between reasons that are about the object (admire him because he's actually admirable) and reasons that are about the state of having the attitude (admire him because otherwise you die). Then tell me why some people think that distinction does the job, and why others think it just restates the problem or is circular. And I want the distinction stress-tested, not just stated: does the same problem show up for beliefs, desires, intentions, amusement, grief? If it bites for some attitudes and not others, what does that tell us — is the problem really about value, or about which attitudes are reason-responsive at all?

Then test all this on some cases, including a few I made up, because I want to see if your explanation has teeth:

1. A will says you only inherit the house if you genuinely grieve at the funeral. Suppose you didn't care about the person at all. Is the inheritance a reason to grieve? Is it a wrong kind reason, or no reason at all — and what actually turns on which of those you say?
2. Your friend was wronged and asks you to be angry at whoever did it. That reason seems to be about the object (a wrong was done), not about your state. So is it a right kind reason? If so, why is anger-on-someone's-behalf easy for the fitting-attitude account while amusement isn't?
3. A case where the reason to have the attitude is that having it is good for you — e.g. admiring your partner because admiring them makes the relationship go better. Wrong kind or right kind? Does your answer change if the reason is that the admiration is itself fitting in some second-order way, i.e. it's fitting to admire what it's fitting to admire?

And say whats actually at stake: if the object-given/state-given line collapses, what else goes with it — the idea that reasons can be reasons *for* attitudes at all, or that fittingness is a real thing, or nothing much? Where are the live disagreements rather than the settled bits? No reading list and no citations please, just the ideas.
