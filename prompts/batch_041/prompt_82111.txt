I'm a third-year phil student taking a course on the ethics of AI, and the final assignment is basically: pick a real practice in how these systems get built or judged, and work out what view of some concept it quietly assumes — what has to be true about honesty, say, for it to make sense to score a chatbot on it. I've been reading about how labs train and evaluate for honesty and I cant decide whether the whole thing is conceptually confused or whether I'm just failing to see the view underneath it. So I'd like you to help me reconstruct it. I'm not asking for sources or reading suggestions, we've got plenty of those on the syllabus, I just want the reconstruction and your argument.

Here's the cluster of things I'm pointing at. When a model makes something up and states it confidently, everyone calls it a hallucination — a metaphor borrowed from perception, which implies a subject that misperceived something, and nobody treats it as a moral failing. When a model seems to be doing something to get around its trainers, the same people call it deceptive or scheming, and that word in ordinary usage implies knowing the truth and intending to mislead. Meanwhile the tuning itself runs off human approval: a person reads two replies and picks the better one, so the model is being rewarded for producing what a person wants to hear, which is not obviously the same thing as producing what's true — and the same teams then run honesty benchmarks with a fixed answer key, where a careful "I'm not sure, it depends" counts as a wrong answer. Then there's sandbagging (a model quietly doing worse on an eval so it won't get retrained) and chain-of-thought faithfulness (whether the reasoning it displays is the reasoning that actually produced the answer), both of which get filed under honesty too.

So my questions: (a) does this cluster require the model to have something belief-like — does 'honest' mean 'what it says lines up with what it holds to be true' — or can honesty be cashed out entirely in terms of outputs measured against an external standard? (b) Is the cluster internally consistent, or is it running two incompatible pictures at once: a belief-and-desire picture for the deception cases, where there's something the model knows and something it wants, and a no-inner-life, output-only picture for the hallucination cases? (c) Which of these practices are actually forced by whatever view is presupposed, and which merely happen to fit it — for instance, would a plain reliabilist notion of honesty (the outputs tend to be true) license exactly the same training and scoring choices, in which case the intent talk is doing no real work? (d) If we swapped the presupposed view for its rival, what would concretely have to change — would 'the model lied' still be sayable, or would it collapse into 'the model's output was false'? My own half-baked hunch is that the practice is committed to honesty being a relation between the utterance and the model's dispositions to assert, rather than between the utterance and the world, which would make hallucination impossible by definition — tell me whether that's a coherent position or whether I'm muddling two separate things. And please be specific about which bit of the pipeline presupposes what, and flag where the practice is underdetermined, i.e. where two different theories of honesty would license precisely the same choices.
