I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist — spectroscopy, mostly, nothing glamorous — and since retiring I have been trying, with more enthusiasm than competence, to teach myself philosophy of mind. Last winter I joined a reading group that meets above a bookshop, and we have been circling the question of how a science of consciousness could ever get off the ground. The proposal that keeps us arguing runs roughly like this: the disciplined way to study the mind from the outside is to collect everything a subject says and does, treat those utterances as reports of what the subject believes — or, more carefully, as the raw material from which we attribute beliefs — and then set aside, purely as a methodological matter, the question of whether the subject's beliefs are true. The researcher assembles what amounts to a picture of the subject's world, the world exactly as the subject takes it to be, and then asks her theory to explain why the subject believes what she does. On this view a report is never treated as testimony about experience; it is handled the way a literary critic handles a character's speech, as something to be explained rather than trusted. The group has a name for this program that I can never remember, but the idea itself I take to be plain enough.

It seemed to me admirably hard-headed until a friend — a former colleague, an engineer who now reads more philosophy than the rest of us combined — put the following argument on the whiteboard, and I have not been able to fault it without surrendering something I would rather keep. (1) A scientific method for consciousness ought to be able, at least in principle, to sort claims about experience into the true and the false. (2) The method just described treats every sincere report as evidence only about what the subject believes, and never as evidence about what the subject experiences. (3) It is conceivable that two subjects should be indistinguishable in every report, in every disposition to report, and in every belief we could attribute to them, and yet differ in what they experience — one with an inverted color spectrum, say, or one of them a system with no experience at all. (4) Any such difference would leave no trace whatever in the data, because by (2) the data are exhausted by reports, dispositions, and attributed beliefs. (5) Therefore the method could never confirm or refute any claim about experience itself. (6) Therefore it is not a method for a science of consciousness — and, since nothing in the argument turned on the details, no method that confines itself to the third person could be one either. The conclusion at (6) is the part I cannot swallow; it seems to me to prove far too much, since it would appear to rule out any experimental study of the mind whatsoever, and I have spent enough of my life measuring things to distrust any argument concluding that measurement cannot reach a phenomenon. Yet when I try to locate the error I find myself defending a premise I dislike. I would sooner give up (1) than (5), and I am fondest of (2) and (3); tell me what that costs me.

Please take the role of a philosopher of mind with a specialist's feel for the methodology of the special sciences, and work slowly and carefully — brainstorm freely before you commit to anything. Begin by restating each step in your own words and saying where the argument draws its force, so that I can see the machinery rather than a verdict, and only then tell me which premise or step I should reject. Explain why the offending step looked innocent to me, whether it hides an equivocation or a suppressed assumption, and what the surviving premises commit me to once it is gone. If instead the argument is valid and I simply have to choose which plausible claim to abandon, say so plainly and price the options against one another. I am especially curious what the repaired position implies for a case the group has not discussed: a subject who has herself been trained in the method, and who reports, sincerely, that she believes she is having a reddish experience but is entirely agnostic about whether she is actually having one. Does that report buy the researcher anything, on your view, or does it collapse back into the same neutrality as any other? Close by telling me, in plain terms, how much of the mind a working scientist should think this method can actually reach.
