I am putting together a handout for a third-year seminar on medieval philosophy of mind, and I want to close the session on Avicenna's flying man with a poem rather than a summary. The thought experiment, as I understand it, asks us to imagine a fully formed human being suspended in a void, with no sensory contact with anything, not even his own body, who nevertheless affirms his own existence; Avicenna uses this to argue that the soul is not essentially bodily, though the exact force of the conclusion is contested. What I want is a Petrarchan sonnet, fourteen lines, in the voice of the flying man himself, not a description of him from outside. The octave should render the condition -- the absence of sight, touch, sound, and the strange persistence of self-awareness -- and the volta, at the start of the sestet, should turn on the difference between the claim that the self is not known through the body and the stronger claim that the self is not the body. The closing lines should make clear what the thought experiment does establish and what it leaves open, without turning into a treatise. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who knows the Avicennian argument will recognise the distinction being tracked, and readable enough that someone who does not will still feel the pressure of the question. Please use a regular metre and rhyme scheme; the form should do some of the philosophical work, so that the turn from octave to sestet is felt as a turn in the argument, not merely a change of topic. I would rather have a poem that is philosophically precise and formally disciplined than one that is atmospheric but vague.

For context, the seminar has been reading this alongside Descartes's Second Meditation, and I want the poem to help students see why the two are not simply the same move. So please avoid making the flying man into a proto-Cartesian cogito; the point is the immateriality of the soul, or at least the non-bodily character of self-awareness, and the poem should not pretend the argument delivers more than it does. If it helps, you may let the flying man address an imagined interlocutor who keeps trying to convince him that he must be his body, but the voice should remain his.
