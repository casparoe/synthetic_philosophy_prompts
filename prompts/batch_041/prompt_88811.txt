I work as an interpretive naturalist at a state park. Most of my day is spent answering some version of 'why does it do that?' or 'what is that for?' — on walks, at the visitor center, and in the short labels and program descriptions that go out under the park's name. I have to answer in a sentence or two, without jargon, to people who reasonably take what I say as the park's considered word.

The longer I do this, the less certain I am that I know what I am claiming when I say a trait is 'for' something. Sometimes I seem to mean that the trait was shaped over many generations because it helped ancestors survive or reproduce; other times I only mean that it does something useful for the organism right now. I have learned the hard way that these can come apart. A biologist friend caught me giving a tidy selection story for a structure that, as far as anyone can tell, never had any selection pressure behind it — it simply comes along for the ride with something else. Another time I explained a behavior as being 'for' mating when it was at least as likely to be a side effect of something unrelated. I have also been asked, reasonably, what the point is of a structure that clearly mattered to ancestors but appears to do nothing for the animals in front of us now. I would rather have a method than keep improvising.

Please write me a decision procedure I can actually run in the minute or two before I answer, whenever I am about to say that some trait has a function. I want the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a good answer to each; the point at which the procedure tells me to say something weaker or vaguer; and the point at which it tells me that the question is a judgment call rather than a fact, so that I should either flag my uncertainty aloud or hand it to someone with more training. I do not have the technical vocabulary, but I understand that there is a genuine, unresolved disagreement among people who study this about what it even means to say that a trait has a 'function,' and I do not want the procedure to paper that over. If the honest output is sometimes 'this has a function in one sense and not in another,' I want the procedure to say so, and to tell me how to phrase that for a visitor without sounding evasive.

Then run the procedure on three cases and tell me where each one lands: (a) a showy trait that everyone assumes was selected for attracting mates, where I want to know exactly what that claim commits me to; (b) a feature that is clearly doing something useful for the organism right now but that no one thinks was shaped by selection for that particular use; and (c) a feature that was plainly shaped by selection in the ancestors but that, in the population I am actually talking about, no longer performs that job. At least one of the three should come out with an answer I will not like — something less confident than what visitors expect, or than what I would instinctively say.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do. I would like an honest list of the function questions I should refuse to answer on my own, and of the cases where the best I can do is tell a visitor that the park does not know.
