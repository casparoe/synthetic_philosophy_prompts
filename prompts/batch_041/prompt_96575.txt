I run a small analytics team — four analysts, none of them statisticians — at a state public-health agency. Every Monday we publish county-level probabilistic forecasts: for each of roughly thirty counties, the probability that intensive-care occupancy will exceed ninety percent at some point in the next fourteen days. Those numbers feed an alert system, and above a threshold the state pre-positions staff and equipment. A false alarm costs money and public credibility; a missed surge costs lives. Our internal scorecard is a running embarrassment. We mark a forecast as a success when the event happened and we said it would, and as a failure otherwise, which means an analyst who said ten percent and was unlucky goes down as wrong while an analyst who said ninety percent and got lucky goes down as right. Nobody trusts the scorecard, so nobody argues with it, and the forecasts are drifting.

I have been reading around in a literature I only half understand — accuracy-first epistemology, the idea that what makes a credence good or bad as a credence is how close it comes to the truth, made precise with scoring rules. I want you to convert whatever is genuinely usable in that material into a procedure my team can run. Concretely, I need a sequence of steps with an order to them, a statement of what would count as settling each step, the point at which a step can come out no and we stop, and the points where no rule will do the work and a named person has to own the call. Along the way the procedure has to take positions on the following, and be explicit about which of its positions are the theory talking and which are just us making a decision.

First, how we should score ourselves. I understand that certain scoring rules are the only ones under which an analyst's best move is always to report what she actually believes, and I want to know what follows from that and what does not: in particular, what to do about the fact that we must choose one rule, and that different defensible rules can rank two sets of forecasts differently. Second, whether the coherence result — that credences violating the probability axioms are guaranteed to be beaten by some alternative under every reasonable rule — buys us anything operationally, and where its reach ends. I gather the parallel argument for updating by conditionalization needs an extra assumption about what our future credences will be like, and I would rather see that assumption stated than buried. Third, how to distinguish a disagreement about a number from a disagreement about what the number is about, since our analysts have been known to mean different things by the same threshold. Fourth, how to combine two analysts' numbers into the one we publish, and whether that published number is itself a credence that can be scored or a decision that merely wears the costume of a number. Fifth, whether and how far my own number should move when a colleague disagrees with me, and whether that movement is something an accuracy-based story can justify or something we do for reasons it does not cover. Sixth, how to decide whether to spend the last twenty minutes of the meeting gathering more evidence instead of sharpening the numbers we have; I suspect the framework says nothing about which questions we should be asking in the first place, and I want that gap marked rather than papered over. And seventh, where the procedure must hand off to a human because the remaining question is about values — how much alert cost to trade against surge risk, whether to publish a range rather than a point, how much weight to give a county officer's local knowledge against our model.

Then run the procedure on three cases. Two analysts give 0.15 and 0.55 for the same county, and the one at 0.15 is the senior analyst who has called the last three weeks correctly. A county health officer tells us the situation is unprecedented and refuses to give a number at all. And our most calibrated analyst — the one whose seventy-percent forecasts come true about seventy percent of the time — turns out to be the least informative member of the team, while the one everyone calls overconfident has the sharpest calls. At least one of these should come out in a way we will not enjoy. Close by telling me what this procedure cannot do, and where it will quietly misfire. No citations, no reading list, no pointers to papers; assume I cannot look anything up.
