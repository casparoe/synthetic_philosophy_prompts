My aunt Ruthie died in January, 87 years old, forty-one of them as an actuary at a mid-size life insurer in Ohio. If you knew her at all you knew one thing about her: she would not let you say "the odds were against her," or "what were the chances," about anything that only ever happened once. The idea she took from Keynes and repeated at every single Thanksgiving was that how likely a conclusion is and how much evidence is actually sitting behind it are two different things — two claims can be equally probable, she'd say, but one of them can have a whole career of careful observation underneath it and the other can be a hunch, and it's the second thing, the weight, that ought to decide what you do. She also thought alot of real choices just can't be put on the same scale at all, so asking which one was more likely is a question with no answer, not a hard question.

She's getting a memorial service, about 200 people. Maybe half are her old colleagues from the actuarial world who have heard the spiel; the other half are family and neighbors who will glaze over if I say the word epistemology. I'm giveing the eulogy. Five-ish minutes. I need it to actually land as a tribute — a couple of laughs early, quiet at the end — and I don't want a single sentence in it that she would have called false, including the soft stuff people say at memorials. There should be one real story about her that does the work of the idea without ever labeling it. And please don't let it come out sounding like she thought nothing could ever be known; that misreading drove her nuts her whole career.
