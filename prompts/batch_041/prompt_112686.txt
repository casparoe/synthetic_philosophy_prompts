I took my doctorate in physical chemistry in the late 1960s and retired five years ago. Since then I have been reading economics and philosophy of science more systematically than my amateur dabbling ever allowed, and the short essay below is my first attempt to bring the two together. I would like your comments on it, and I am chiefly interested in whether it is *accurate* — whether I have the models right, whether the distinction I draw is a real one or something I have reinvented in ignorance — and only secondarily in whether the argument, once corrected, is worth anything. I do not want a reading list or citations; I want to know where I am wrong.

**Bandwagons in physics are not information cascades**

In 1989 my field briefly lost its head. Within weeks of the first announcement, groups that had never touched the problem dropped their own programmes and attempted replications; a minority reported positive results, the majority found nothing, and the episode is now a byword for premature announcement. The usual explanation invokes an information cascade: each laboratory, seeing what its predecessors had done, rationally inferred that there must be something there and discounted its own doubts accordingly. I want to argue that this explanation is not merely imprecise but backwards in an interesting way.

The engine of the cascade model is the privacy of private signals. Agents cannot inspect one another's evidence; they see only actions, and so must infer the evidence from the action. Science violates this premise almost completely. The private signal in a laboratory is the data and the method, and both are published. A follower can, in principle, evaluate the original evidence rather than infer it from the fact that someone acted on it. The channel that drives cascades is therefore largely closed in science — and it is closed precisely because scientists are obliged to make their signals public.

What actually drives the bandwagon is the payoff structure. The cost of being absent from a hot area exceeds the cost of being wrong within it: a failed replication in a fashionable field is a normal result, whereas silence is a career decision. Add the incentives of editors, referees and funding panels, which favour the exciting report, and you have conformity driven by reputation and incentives rather than by inference from others' actions.

There is a further inversion. A genuine cascade is fragile: it breaks as soon as sufficiently informative evidence becomes public. In the episode I am describing, the evidence that should have broken it — the null replications — was available almost immediately and was simply not credited. What sustained the bandwagon was the absence of the very thing that would have dissolved a cascade, which suggests that the phenomenon is close to the inverse of the model rather than an instance of it.

Two consequences follow. First, the rationality at work here is decision-theoretic, not epistemic: the agents may be maximising expected payoff perfectly well while the community's belief-forming process is not tracking the truth at all. Second, the cascade models assume a common prior and a known signal precision, and so cannot describe a community whose members disagree about the reliability of the measurements in dispute — which is exactly the situation in a replication controversy.

I suspect I am using "information cascade" more loosely than the economists do, to mean any self-reinforcing convergence of belief; tell me whether that is a muddle, and whether anything survives once the term is tightened to its technical sense. I also suspect I may have overstated one or two things in order to make the contrast sharp.
