I am a retired population ecologist — thirty-odd years spent modelling fish stocks and the ways they collapse — and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science, largely in order to make sense of a puzzle from my own field that I never managed to resolve while I was still working in it. I would be grateful for your help with it.

The pattern is this. Every so often someone advances a prediction about a long-run trend: that a population will outrun the resource that feeds it, that a fishery will be fished down to collapse, that a system under sustained pressure must eventually give way. The oldest instance is the one my field inherits from Malthus, and it is still the one I return to. The prediction is not empty. It names a mechanism, and it forbids something — it says the trend cannot continue indefinitely, that at some point the curve must turn. What it leaves open, and in a sense has to leave open, is the timescale. Then the strange thing happens, the thing I want a name for. For decades the prediction appears to fail: catches hold up, substitutes appear, the reckoning is postponed. Those who made the prediction say the failure is temporary, that the mechanism has not been repealed but only deferred; those who doubt it say the theory has been refuted and is being kept alive on hope. Then, sooner or later, a crisis of the predicted kind arrives, and the first group calls it vindication. What I cannot get past is that the very same history supports both readings. The crisis counts as confirmation only if we grant that it was due by now; the long delay counts as refutation only if we treat the original timescale as binding. And the theory can never be pinned to a timescale, because the moment it commits to one — that collapse will arrive by some particular date — it becomes a testable prediction, and testable long-run predictions of this kind have a habit of turning out wrong. So the theory survives precisely by leaving unspecified the one parameter that would make it vulnerable: to specify it is to risk killing it, to withhold it is to keep it safe. And yet it is plainly not vacuous, and I do not think it is merely a case of a theory being patched whenever it fails, because no patch is ever added — the vagueness was there from the start.

I have been calling this being right but early, but I suspect that is a slogan doing the work of a concept. What I want to know is whether the epistemic situation I have described has a name, or a cluster of nearby names, and how it ought to be carved. I can see that it sits somewhere in the neighbourhood of the familiar complaint that certain theories can never be falsified, and also near the complaint that a theory is being rescued by its defenders; but I do not think either quite captures it, since this theory does make risky claims and is never actually amended. Nor do I think it is the same as the idea that a prediction can bring about its own failure by being believed and acted upon — though in the population case the two are surely tangled together, and I should like to know how to separate them. So: which established concepts come closest, where exactly does each fall short, and if none of them fits, does the situation warrant a term of its own — and if so, what would its edges be? What would be a clear case of it, what would be merely adjacent, and what would look almost identical but turn out to be a different thing altogether?
