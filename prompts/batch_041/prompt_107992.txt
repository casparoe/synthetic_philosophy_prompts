I run the patient-reported-outcomes group at a mid-sized contract research organization. We write and adapt questionnaires about subjective symptoms — chronic pain, tinnitus, fatigue, depersonalization, the strange sense of presence patients report during virtual-reality exposure therapy — and our scores end up as endpoints in trials that regulators and payers later treat as evidence that a treatment works. The failure mode that keeps me up is not a clumsily worded item. It is an item that measures nothing except the respondent's willingness to endorse a sentence, and a scale that is reliable, sensitive to change, and empty. We admit or reject several items a week, and every admitted item is signed off by our clinical lead and by the patient advisory board.

I would like a decision procedure for admitting an item, and then a whole scale, grounded in Wittgenstein's private language argument — the actual moves in it, not the slogan that a private language is impossible. Write it as an ordered sequence of questions my item writers can run in fifteen minutes per item with no philosophy in their backgrounds: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the sequence terminates in a refusal, and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is a value judgment or a matter for the advisory board rather than something the argument settles. Keep the distinctions the argument genuinely turns on, and say which ones I can afford to drop for usability's sake. Mark explicitly where the argument, taken seriously, cuts against standard psychometric practice — in particular against the assumption that the respondent's own report of their inner state is the benchmark the item is validated against — and where you are adding a house rule of your own rather than drawing one from the argument.

Then run it on three cases: (a) a single-item zero-to-ten loudness rating for tinnitus, where no one but the respondent can check the number; (b) a caregiver-completed observational scale for pain in advanced dementia, where the sufferer cannot report at all; (c) an item we have used for years and are attached to, phrased roughly as an invitation to describe a quality of experience that only the respondent could identify, which I expect your procedure to reject. For each, give the verdict, the step that decides it, and the objections a good psychometrician would raise against that verdict.

Close by saying what the procedure cannot do: the questions it is silent on, the verdicts it will get wrong, and the cases where it will merely reproduce whatever the surrounding clinical culture already believes. No citations or reading list — I won't check them, and the argument is the part I care about.
