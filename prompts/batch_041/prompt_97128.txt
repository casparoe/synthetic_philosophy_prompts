I've been chewing on Hobbes's philosophy of math and I think I've talked myself into his line: geometry is the paradigm of certain knowledge precisely because we make its objects ourselves — we draw the lines and figures, so we grasp their causes — whereas natural philosophy is stuck guessing at causes we didn't produce. He runs the same move for politics (we make the commonwealth, so civil philosophy is demonstrable too). I'm tempted to treat this as a genuinly good account of what's special about mathematical knowledge, not just a historical curiosity. So what would count against it? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including the ones Hobbes's own defenders would have to concede, and I want to know what the view predicts that its rivals don't. In particular: does it survive the fact that we don't get to choose which mathematical truths hold, and the fact that math applies so uncannily well to a nature we supposedly didn't construct? Is it really distinct from just collapsing into conventionalism, and does anything ride on that? And yes, I know his own attempts at squaring the circle were a disaster and that Wallis demolished him — I'm asking whether the failure of the practice tells against the account of the method. Be upfront about what's actually settled here versus genuinely contested among people who study Hobbes, instead of selling me a contested reading as the consensus.
