I am building a diagnostic handout for a graduate seminar on knowledge and practical stakes, and I want it to sort readers by what they say about cases rather than by which label they already wear. My students can recite the standard taxonomy; what they cannot do is tell me which of the familiar positions their own answers commit them to. Please write the instrument as a decision tree.

Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of positions: first the questions, then the combinations of answers they permit, then the positions located in that space. The space is fixed by four axes, and the tree should be recognizably a traversal of them, though it need not use every axis. (i) Where the variation is located: in the truth-conditions of the ascription, in the truth-conditions of the state ascribed, in the pragmatics of asserting the ascription but not in what is true, or not in knowledge at all but in the norms governing action, assertion, or practical reasoning. (ii) Whose position does the work: the speaker's, the subject's, a later assessor's, or the conversational score. (iii) What it is that shifts: the evidential threshold, the relevance of error possibilities, the subject's evidence itself, or nothing epistemic at all. (iv) What to do with the intuitions that drive the debate: build the semantics around them, treat them as artifacts of the vignettes, or accept them as real but as showing only something psychological or pragmatic.

Every branch node must be phrased as a question a reader can answer without knowing which position it leads to: put a case or a structural commitment to them, not a label. At a minimum the tree must contain these nodes, and I want each phrased as a case rather than as a topic: a low-stakes bystander who ascribes knowledge to a high-stakes subject whose own verdict is that she does not know; the mirror image, a high-stakes speaker assessing a low-stakes subject; a later assessor whose stakes differ from the original speaker's, where we ask whether both verdicts can be correct; a pair of cases identical in the subject's total evidence with only what is at stake varying, where we ask whether anything epistemic has changed; a case in which the subject's belief is fixed and only what she may rely on in acting changes; and a case in which it would be improper or misleading to say that she knows even if she does.

The leaves must include, at least, each of the following, named by content rather than by author: the word is sensitive to the speaker's context; the word is sensitive to the assessor's context rather than the speaker's; the state itself is sensitive to the subject's practical interests; the ascription is sensitive to the question under discussion; knowledge is invariant and only assertability is not; knowledge ascriptions are not truth-apt at all; knowledge is invariant and the intuitions are an error to be explained away; the cases concern the norms of action or assertion and leave knowledge untouched; the subject's evidence itself includes practical facts; and the view on which the word has no single sense and the debate's central question has no unique answer.

Give each leaf the name of the position, one sentence stating its most awkward commitment, and the answer-path that reaches it. Then, in a short closing section: mark which cells of the space your tree leaves empty and say briefly whether each is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely undefended; and flag every pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, with the case that would be needed to separate them if one exists. For each case I supplied above, state in one line which branch it decides and which leaves remain tied on it.

Keep it to no more than fourteen branch nodes and twelve leaves. Do not cite anything or tell me what the literature says; I know the literature, and I want the instrument to stand on its own for readers who cannot look anything up.
