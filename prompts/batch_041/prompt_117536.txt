I have recently become the convenor of a reading group that has met, in one form or another, for over a decade, and the experience has been quietly disorienting. A great deal of what governs the group — which chair is whose, which of two equally good rooms we use, how long an objection may run before someone calls time — is a regularity that nobody chose and nobody can justify, and that would have worked just as well had we settled the other way; yet some of these regularities are load-bearing and survive every disruption, while others dissolve the moment a single person stops observing them. I want a room full of students to feel that asymmetry in their hands rather than hear me assert it, and I have been rereading Lewis's account of convention (Convention, 1969) as the frame for it.

Please design a single 90-minute session for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in social ontology: 18 students, movable chairs, a whiteboard, no budget, and no real money at stake. The activity has to carry the idea on its own. The students should not hear Lewis's name, or any statement of his conditions, until the debrief, and my opening should be a straight-faced cover story that gives nothing away.

Build the session as a sequence of simultaneous, silent choices scored in points rather than cash, so that each round is a live coordination problem with several equally good solutions. Roughly: (a) a round with no cue, where the group must converge on one of several symmetric options and predictably scatters; (b) the same game with an arbitrary public cue that carries no information about payoffs — and here I want you to run two sub-groups simultaneously under two different arbitrary cues, so that the group discovers for itself that the regularity it lands on is the one that was salient, not the one that was better; (c) a round with precedent and latecomers, in which people who never saw the original cue have to be brought in, so we can watch how much of the regularity is transmitted by expectation and how much by instruction; (d) a round in which I quietly alter the payoffs so that members now prefer different options while still preferring any coordination to none, and we see whether the regularity holds, what reasons people offer for it, and whether anyone begins to police it.

Then the debrief: map each round onto the parts of Lewis's analysis — the regularity itself, its arbitrariness, the expectation of others, and the way conformity is rational only conditionally on others conforming — and be explicit about the difference between everyone knowing and everyone knowing that everyone knows. Then push past him: the moment interests diverge we have left the setting his analysis was built for, and whatever keeps the regularity alive has taken on a different character, closer to a norm upheld by sanction than to a convention sustained by expectation alone. Say honestly which of the two the fourth round actually produced in my room.

Give me the materials, the exact wording of the payoff announcements, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a script for the first sixty seconds. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — including the ways my own room will smuggle in a cue I did not intend — and what I should have ready when it does. Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: in particular, whether a group of co-present people can be made to feel the absence of common knowledge at all, and whether the long-run selection between rival conventions is something I can stage, or something I will simply have to assert in the debrief.
