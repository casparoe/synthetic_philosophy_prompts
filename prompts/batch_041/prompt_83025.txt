Write a short story of roughly 1,800 words that dramatizes — without ever stating — the following distinction.

There are two ways of being insulated from a serious opposing view, and they are routinely mistaken for each other. In the first, the opposing view is simply absent: nothing in the character's circumstances brings it into contact with the disagreement, and precisely because the absence is environmental rather than enforced, the character can sincerely believe that they have considered the other side — that they read something once, that a colleague holds it, that they would certainly know if there were a real case for it. In the second, the view is not absent at all. The character meets it constantly, may even seek it out, and yet it never lands, because they have been taught in advance how to receive it: that its advocates are dishonest or unserious or paid, or that the very insistence with which it is pressed is itself a tell. The two conditions have opposite remedies. The first is punctured by exposure — put the person in a room with the argument and something gives. The second tends to be strengthened by exactly that exposure, since meeting the argument is the ordinary condition of its life and the meeting has already been assigned a meaning.

Now the story: a character who begins inside the first condition and, over the course of the story, comes to live inside the second, without ever registering the change as a change — who believes, if anything, that they have just escaped a bubble rather than entered one.

Requirements:

- The reader must not be able to identify either group by its politics, religion, or any other doctrine. Keep the setting ordinary — a workplace, a trade, a family, a club — and make the difference between the two groups one of mechanism rather than belief. Ideally the two groups hold many of the same views.
- Invent one concrete, recurring practice that the second group performs: something done on a schedule, with a procedure, that does the work of pre-assigning an interpretation to any counter-evidence before it arrives. Make it specific and mundane enough that a reader would recognize its shape from ordinary life. It must involve no prohibition on reading the other side; if anything, the group encourages it.
- The character's own epistemic virtue must be the mechanism of their capture. Their openness, their distaste for smugness, their allergy to being the sort of person who only talks to people who already agree — whatever it is, it has to be the thing that carries them across, so that the trap closes on a strength rather than a weakness.
- Neither group may be stupid or villainous. The first should be decent and a little complacent. The second should be more articulate, more self-critical in appearance, and in some respects more honest than the first; the people inside it should mean what they say and should not be depicted as cynical manipulators.
- Include one scene in which the character does exactly what the first group never did — goes looking for the strongest version of the opposing case — and it makes no difference.
- Do not use the phrases echo chamber or epistemic bubble, and do not let any character explain the distinction or the trap. No lecture, no closing realization speech, no narrator's aside telling the reader what to conclude. If a diagnosis is offered, let a character get it partly wrong.
- Tell it in close third person, and end on an action or an image rather than a summary.
