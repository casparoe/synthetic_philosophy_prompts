Ok so here's a thing I keep going back and forth on. Suppose someone builds what I'll call a two-ledger machine ethicist: one ledger scores candidate actions against a fixed, hand-specified welfare function (expected wellbeing summed over affected parties, say), a second ledger asks of each candidate "could I justify this to every party it touches, in terms they could accept?" — and where the two ledgers disagree past some threshold, the system halts and kicks the call to a human. The claim I want a number on: over the next few decades, an architecture like this (two or more partial moral procedures running side by side, with explicit disagreement-escalation) will turn out to be more realiable in real deployment than any system that implements a single moral theory, whether that's a rule engine, a trained value model, or a constitution-style list of principles.

Give me your actual credence, and decompose it. Terms I'd want at least: how likely it is that any single moral theory is even implementable as a scoring procedure without the implementation smuggling in the very judgments it was supposed to generate; whether combining theories actually beats the best single one or just averages their errors; whether "reliable in real deployment" is even measurable, given we don't agree on the ground truth; and how much the escalate-to-human move buys you versus being a way of hiding the hard part. Number and one line of justification for each, then how they combine, then the term you're least sure about and what would move it. 300 words max, and lead with the bottom-line number.
