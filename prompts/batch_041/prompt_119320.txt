ok so I'm doing a piece on the statue fight in my city (and the street-renaming mess that got rolled into it) and I keep hitting the same move. At the hearing a councilman said taking the statue down is like tearing a page out of the city's history book — you might not like the guy on the pedestal, he says, but you don't get to delete the past. Now the letters page is full of it: you can't erase history, you don't rip out a chapter just because you dislike it. I called a historian friend, who snorted and said a statue is nothing like a page in a book, it's more like a trophy in a display case, and nobody thinks clearing out the trophy case is book-burning. I write about ideas for a living so I should be able to do better than snorting back at her.

So audit it for me. Lay out the correspondence table the way the councilman is actually using the thing — what maps onto the page, the book, the author, the reader, whoever owns the book — then where the mapping breaks, and which of those breaks definately matter for the job the analogy is doing, which is blocking removal. The book image seems to be carrying a lot: that the statue is a record rather than a claim, that the record lives in only one place, that the past is the kind of thing you can delete, that the "we" who'd be deleting it is the same "we" who wrote it in the first place. Are those imports, or are they things the target genuinely has? Then the payoff: once the disanalogies are on the table, does "you can't erase history" survive as a reason to leave it up, or was the analogy carrying the whole argument?

And if theres a better comparison out there — for statues specifically, and seperately for street names, if those two come apart — I'd rather have that than a verdict. No reading list please, I've got to many tabs open already.
