Write a pantoum of no more than twenty-four lines, spoken by the sober symposiarch whom the Athenian Stranger appoints in Plato's Laws to preside over a drinking party. The Stranger argues that such a party, properly run, is not licensed indulgence but a gymnastic of the soul: wine is a drug that makes the old young again and strips a man down to whatever his character really holds, so that the sober ruler can study the drinkers as one studies a mirror and see which kind of cord pulling the human puppet is doing the pulling — the golden cord of reason and law, or the hard iron cords of fear and pleasure. Let the form do that work. The two refrains should be the two cords, so that a line, when it returns in a later stanza, is heard as pulled by the other one; the last quatrain should close the loop without letting either cord win, since the Stranger's point is the test, not the verdict. The speaker should be neither a scold nor a tempter — he is running an experiment on his guests and on himself — and the poem should leave it open whether the golden cord can hold a man who has been made young and shameless again. It should be accurate enough that a reader of the Laws recognizes the doctrine of the puppet, the drug, and the sober judge of the drinking party, and legible enough that someone who has never opened the dialogue feels the pull of both cords. Begin with the two refrain lines set apart, then the poem, and close with a single sentence identifying which cord each refrain stands for and where each belongs in the Stranger's argument.
