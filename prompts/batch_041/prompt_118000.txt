Question (20 marks). Bernard Williams argues that all reasons for action are internal reasons.

(a) Set out the distinction between internal and external reason statements, and say what Williams thinks is wrong with external reason statements. [8]

(b) Explain the role of the sound deliberative route in his account of what it is for an agent to have a reason. What work is that clause doing that a plain desire-satisfaction story would not do? [7]

(c) Critics say Williams's view leaves no room for moral reasons to bear on someone who does not already care. State the objection as strongly as you can, and explain how Williams would answer it. [5]

Her answer:

Williams is attacking the idea that there are reasons which apply to an agent no matter what she happens to care about. Those are external reason statements, and he argues that they are all false. His own view, internalism, is that the claim that A has a reason to do something is true only if there is a sound deliberative route from A's subjective motivational set (S) to A's doing it. S is what the agent actually wants, cares about and is committed to.

The argument is that a reason statement has to be able to explain the agent's behaviour. If I say that a man has a reason to be kinder to his wife, and he has no interest at all in being kinder, then my statement explains nothing that he does or feels; it floats free of his psychology. A statement that cannot do that explanatory work is not a reason statement at all. So external reason statements are false, and every genuine reason has to hook up with what the agent already cares about.

This is really the doctrine of motivational internalism, that to judge that you ought to do something is already to be moved to do it, since otherwise the judgement is not really a judgement about your reasons. Williams is therefore a Humean: practical reason is instrumental, and deliberation is just working out the most efficient way of getting what you already want. The sound deliberative route is not a route to new ends, only to better means.

It is worth adding that Williams does not think an agent always acts on the reasons she has. She may be ignorant of the relevant facts, or she may simply not bother to think things through properly, and then she fails to act on a reason that she has all the same. That is not a counterexample to internalism, because internalism is about the truth of the reason statement, not about what the agent actually does.

The consequence is that a man who genuinely does not care about anyone else has no reason to be decent, and I suppose no reason to reproach himself for it either. The standard objection is that this makes moral criticism of such a man impossible. Williams's answer is that the objection begs the question, since it assumes that there must be a reason for him to be decent, which is exactly what is at issue.
