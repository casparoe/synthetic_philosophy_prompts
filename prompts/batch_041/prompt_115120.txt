I teach a 90-minute session on the Anthropocene to a mixed group of 18–24 upper-level undergraduates and graduate students, most of whom arrive already convinced the Anthropocene is obviously real and that the only live question is the date. Design a structured deliberation that makes them feel that fixing the start is a decision taken through a procedure rather than a discovery, and that the procedure — not the evidence — can move the answer.

Split the room into three committees and give all three the same evidence packet (I'll supply it: a 1610 dip in atmospheric CO2 tied to the collapse of indigenous populations in the Americas, the 1780s inflection of the Industrial Revolution, the 1945 Trinity fallout horizon, the working group's mid-century base keyed to plutonium from bomb tests, and a mid-1960s peak in fallout radionuclides). Vary only the ratification rule: unanimity; simple majority; supermajority with an outside body that can veto. Make "no new unit" a live option in every committee. A committee's date counts only once it plants a physical golden spike in a single core section, and you supply a second core from another continent that does not show the same horizon, so a marker that cannot be correlated globally fails. Add whatever roles you need, but the ratification rule is the variable I want doing the work.

The debrief should map what happened onto how stratigraphic boundaries are actually ratified, the requirement that a marker be a measurable proxy rather than a meaningful human artifact, and the fact that the real proposal was voted down at the ratification stage while the term kept circulating anyway. Write the answer under these headings, in this order: Goal and setup (~150 words); Materials and evidence packet (~150); Roles and rules (~300); Run of play (~250); Debrief (~300); What will predictably go wrong the first time, and how to handle it (~200); What this activity cannot reproduce and I must supply myself (~150).
