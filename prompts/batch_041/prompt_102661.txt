I run a graduate seminar on formal epistemology and rational choice — sixteen students, one ninety-minute session — and I want to spend it on Aumann's agreement theorem. My aim is for the theorem to arrive as something the students have already felt in their hands rather than as a result I state and they politely accept. Design me a runnable activity, built around the mechanism sketched below; you should feel free to repair, tighten, or replace any part of it if something else serves the goal better.

The mechanism. Participants work in pairs against a hidden binary state — for instance, which of two urns a ball was drawn from. Each partner receives a private signal about the state, the signals are conditionally independent given the state, and the prior over states together with the signal distributions is shared by both partners and known by each to be shared. Each partner privately forms a posterior. They then announce their posteriors simultaneously as numbers, each treats the other's number as evidence, updates, announces again, and repeats until the numbers stop moving. My expectation is that the numbers will settle on a common value and that this will surprise them, since going in they will insist that two people with different private evidence can rationally and permanently disagree.

I would like the design to include the following.

- Rules and materials that make the shared prior and the shared signal structure visible rather than stipulated on a slide. The students should be able to see that they are inside one model, not merely be told so. Keep the arithmetic simple enough to do by hand, and supply whatever tables or props that requires.
- A second stage, ideally an extension of the same setup rather than a fresh game, in which disagreement is allowed to persist, so that they can feel the two different ways a disagreement can survive the theorem: (a) the partners are not in the same model — different priors, or different partitions of the same state space — and (b) the disagreement is not common knowledge, for instance because a third participant holds a signal that neither partner knows the other has seen. I want both routes available within the single session.
- An optional betting stage in which the pair wagers points against each other at odds reflecting their announced posteriors, so that they notice neither will take either side once the posteriors are public, though they will before. Points, not money.
- A debrief that maps particular moments in the activity onto the theorem's assumptions — a common prior, common knowledge of the posteriors at the actual state, Bayesian updating, and the fact that the conclusion concerns a single state rather than a process of convergence — and that leaves them able to say where real-world disagreement must therefore be living.

Please also tell me the following.

- The predictable first-run failures, including what goes wrong with the hand arithmetic and with the announce-and-update loop, and what to do about each.
- Which feature of the theorem the activity cannot reproduce and that I must supply myself in the debrief. I have a suspicion I want you to check. It seems to me that the activity will make them feel disagreement is impossible, but that it does not literally reproduce the theorem's setup, because the theorem requires common knowledge of the posterior functions — what each partner would believe at every state, not just the single number each one announces — and because it is a static result about one state rather than a claim that conversation converges. Tell me whether that is the right way to state the gap, what else the activity leaves out, and how to supply the missing piece without undoing what they have already felt.
