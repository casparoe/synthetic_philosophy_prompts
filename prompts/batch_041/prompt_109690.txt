I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, working on evidence and self-locating belief, and for the past term I've been sitting in on a seminar that has turned into a standing argument among four of us — a metaphysician, a cosmologist, a Bayesian statistician, and me — about anthropic reasoning. The odd upshot is that the more cases we discuss, the more confident I become about my verdicts on them and the less I can say about what principle, if any, they express. I'd like you to help me find out. I should say up front that I'm not asking for references, attributions, or a reading list; I know the terrain well enough to place things myself, and what I want is the principle, not the bibliography.

Here is what I think, case by case. (1) Sleeping Beauty: a fair coin is tossed on Sunday; heads means I'm woken once, on Monday, and the experiment ends; tails means I'm woken on Monday, put back to sleep with the memory of that waking erased, and woken again on Tuesday. When I wake and can't tell which day it is, I think the coin is one-third likely to have been heads. (2) The separated twin: a fair coin is tossed; heads means my mother has one child, me; tails means she has two, me and an identical twin, and we're separated at birth and never learn of each other. I don't think the possibility of that hidden twin makes tails any more likely than heads. (3) The duplicate rooms: a fair coin is tossed; heads means a machine builds one room with one copy of me in it, awake; tails means it builds a thousand rooms, each with an awake copy of me, none aware of the others. I wake in a room, and I still put the coin at 50/50 — being one of the thousand doesn't seem to me to make tails a thousand times more likely. (4) The presumptuous philosopher: a fair coin decides between two cosmological hypotheses, one on which the universe holds exactly 10^10 people ever, and one on which it holds 10^100 times as many, spread across causally disconnected regions. Before I consider the fact that I exist at all I'm at 50/50, and after I consider it I'm still at 50/50; the suggestion that I should now be all but certain of the vast universe strikes me as absurd. (5) Doomsday: I learn that I'm roughly the hundred-billionth human to have been born, and I think this gives me no reason whatever to expect the human story to end soon, even though a long future would make my birth rank an improbable draw. (6) Fine-tuning: I think the bare fact that I observe a life-permitting universe is no evidence for a designer or a multiverse, since that observation was guaranteed under every hypothesis in play. (7) The insects: a fair coin decides whether the world contains 10^10 humans and 10^20 insects or 10^10 humans and 10^30 insects; I'm a human, and I think the coin is 50/50, with the insects simply not entering into it. (8) Boltzmann brains: I think the standard worry is a real one — the coherence and order of my present experience genuinely tells against cosmological theories on which disordered disembodied minds vastly outnumber ordinary ones, precisely because an observer picked at random in such a world would probably not be having experiences like mine.

I'm aware that (1) and (8) are the odd ones out, and that they are odd in different ways: (1) treats additional moments of my own experience as evidence, and (8) treats facts about what a typical observer is like as evidence, and both are exactly what (2) through (7) seem to deny. (1) and (3) in particular look to me like the same case told twice, once with the extra copies of me spread across time and once across space, so I suspect one of those two verdicts has to go. What I want from you is this. State the strongest single principle, or small family of principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, precisely enough that I could apply it mechanically to a case I haven't thought of; for each clause, name the case that forces it. Then tell me what the principle predicts for at least two cases I haven't listed — one where you expect the prediction to be uncontroversial and one where you expect it to be counterintuitive — and give me an honest accounting of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, with a recommendation about which to give up and why. If the honest conclusion is that nothing does better than a patchwork and I'm simply inconsistent, I'd rather hear that, along with which verdict is the outlier and what would have to change for the set to hang together. And if the real difference between the cases where I update and the cases where I don't is just the difference between self-indication and self-sampling under another name, say so, and tell me which of the two my verdicts actually commit me to.
