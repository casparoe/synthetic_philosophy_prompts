I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been asked to run a session for our office ideas club: about twenty people, mostly engineers, one ninety-minute slot. My topic is futarchy — the proposal that a group should first settle on the outcome it wants, expressed as a single measurable welfare metric, and then let prediction markets rather than a second vote decide which policy to adopt, on the grounds that votes are the right way to fix ends while markets are better at aggregating information about means. I find the idea genuinely seductive and I suspect I'm being seduced for the wrong reasons, so I want a session in which the group feels both the attraction and the snag for themselves rather than hearing me explain either.

Please design the activity: the rules, the materials, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, a payoff scheme that runs on play money or points rather than real cash, and a debrief. Build it so the group actually operates a miniature futarchy rather than discussing one — they should have to settle the metric by some non-market procedure, then price two conditional markets (what the metric will look like if policy A is adopted, and what it will look like if policy B is adopted), with the decision rule being simply to adopt whichever conditional price comes out higher. Two wrinkles I want designed in, because they are where I think the proposal gets interesting rather than merely clever. First, include one candidate policy that would itself change how the metric is defined or measured, so that the clean division between ends and means is strained by something concrete the group has to argue about, not by a lecture from me. Second, give one participant a conspicuously larger budget and a private stake in the outcome, so the group has to work out afterwards whether what moved the price was information or manipulation — and make sure the rules leave that genuinely ambiguous.

The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto parts of the idea: what the two conditional markets were each supposed to capture, where the metric-setting step smuggled in a value judgement that no market could have priced, and what the group's own trading behaviour does and does not show about the mechanism's real-world prospects. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure, and be honest about which feature of futarchy the activity cannot reproduce at this scale and which I will therefore have to supply myself in the closing discussion.

Finally, wherever the debrief touches on whether the mechanism actually works, I want the claims separated into those that are settled and those that are genuinely contested among people who study prediction markets and social choice, with nothing contested presented to my group as the consensus. I'd rather they leave with an accurate sense of the open questions than with a tidy verdict.
