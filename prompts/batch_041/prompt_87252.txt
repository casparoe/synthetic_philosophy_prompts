I am a retired field ecologist—thirty years spent mostly on long-term soil-monitoring plots—and since retiring I have been working through the political and literary essays I missed in graduate school. Orwell has become a recurring preoccupation, particularly his insistence that political language is not merely decorative but constitutive of political thought: that euphemism, vagueness, and prefabricated phrases can make certain thoughts harder to have, and that a writer's first duty is to make the meaning of a sentence clear. I find this diagnosis powerful, but it is a diagnosis of a world of typewriters, printing presses, and centralized propaganda ministries. I am not a specialist in literary criticism or political theory, and I am not asking for a bibliography or a reading list; I am trying to think through a problem that seems to me genuinely new.

What I cannot work out is what Orwell would have made of a phenomenon that did not exist in his lifetime: the mass production of political language by large language models. Consider a concrete case. A political campaign or a state agency now uses a model fine-tuned for helpfulness, harmlessness, and honesty to generate tens of thousands of individually tailored letters, social-media replies, and talking points. The text is grammatical, emotionally attuned to each recipient, and almost never says anything that could be fact-checked as false; it is optimized instead for engagement, retention, and avoiding brand risk. The model has no beliefs, no intentions, and no fear of the Ministry; it produces the statistical average of what sincere political speech looks like. Meanwhile, citizens use the same tools to draft their own comments, petitions, and testimony, so that the boundary between authentic and synthetic political voice becomes difficult to locate. I have been calling this, in my own notes, the problem of synthetic sincerity, though I am not wedded to the label.

I would like you to predict, as carefully as you can, what Orwell's position would have been on this. Do not give me a list of his quotations—I am not asking for cited passages—and do not invent quotations and attribute them to him. Instead, construct the argument he would have made. Would he regard synthetic sincerity as a continuation of the linguistic corruption he diagnosed, or as a qualitatively new threat? Would he see the machine's apparent inability to mean anything as making it less dangerous than a human liar, or more? What would he say about the fact that the new propaganda is decentralized, personalized, and optimized by reward models rather than dictated by a Ministry? Would he defend clarity as a political virtue when clarity can be generated on demand without any corresponding clarity of thought? And what would he say about the ordinary citizen who uses the same tools to speak back? Please write this as a sustained essay, not a set of bullet points. Attribute to Orwell a coherent position, distinguish what follows from his known commitments from what is speculative, consider the strongest objection to your prediction, and reach a definite verdict about what he would have thought. You may assume he has been given a lucid explanation of the technology and has read a representative sample of its output; you may not assume he has any knowledge of the specific companies, models, or controversies of our time.
