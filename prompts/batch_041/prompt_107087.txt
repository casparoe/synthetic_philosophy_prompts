My old officemate from grad school is a constructive analyst — the kind of mathematician who won't touch the law of excluded middle, who insists that a proof of "there exists" actually produce the thing, and so on. I've been keeping a running list of her verdicts on statements people throw at her, and I want you to guess what she'll do with a new one.

Her track record so far (accept / reject):

- "There's no largest prime." — accept.
- "The square root of 2 isn't a ratio of two whole numbers." — accept.
- "For every real number x, either x = 0 or x ≠ 0." — reject.
- "Every continuous function on [0,1] reaches a maximum." — reject.
- "Every continuous function on [0,1] that's negative at 0 and positive at 1 has a root somewhere in between." — reject.
- "For any statement P, it's contradictory to suppose that P fails and that not-P fails too." — accept.
- "For any whole number n, if it's not the case that n isn't even, then n is even." — accept.
- "For any whole-number equation, if it has a solution, then it isn't contradictory to say it has one." — accept.

The new one, which I haven't sprung on her yet: "For any real number x, if it's contradictory to suppose that x = 0, then x is really away from 0 — there's a positive rational y with y < |x|."

Give me your best guess at her verdict, but what I actually care about is the rule she's running. My hunch is that more than one rule fits everything above. So: lay out the candidate rules, say what each one predicts about the new case, tell me which you'd bet on and why, and name the single further statement I could put to her that would best separate the candidates. If her past verdicts honestly don't pin it down, say that instead of forcing a call.

And definately tell me how confident you are in the whole thing, and single out the one point you're least sure about. American English, please.
