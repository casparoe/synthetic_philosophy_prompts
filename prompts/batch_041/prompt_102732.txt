I sit in on a small ethics reading group that has spent the last few meetings on the case in which you must choose between one person being tortured for fifty years and an enormous number of people — pick ten to the power of thirty, or whatever absurd figure you prefer — each receiving a single momentary speck of dust in the eye. At the last session someone tried to close the discussion with a maxim: he said the law already has a name for this, "the law does not concern itself with trifles," in Latin de minimis non curat lex, and that this shows the dust specks simply do not count, so the torture is plainly the worse option. I have two worries I cannot prise apart. The first is that a rule about what courts will bother to hear cannot by itself settle a question about what is good or bad for people. The second is that I cannot even state what the maxim would have to mean for it to do the work he wants, because "trifle" seems to shift meaning with every reading I try.

Please take the maxim apart for me. I want the distinct precise claims it could be making, set out one after another from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical — for instance, that a tiny harm is no harm at all; that a tiny harm is a real harm whose weight is merely so small it can be neglected in practice; that there is a threshold of magnitude below which harms carry literally zero weight in the moral calculus; that tiny harms have real individual weight but do not aggregate, so no number of them sums to anything; that they do aggregate but are lexically outranked, so no quantity of them could ever outweigh a torture; and that the maxim is not a claim about value at all but about the law's institutional competence, its limited time, or its inability to measure small things reliably. For each reading, tell me whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false, and then — the part I actually care about — which of them would genuinely support my friend's move of dismissing the specks outright, and which would leave him with nothing but a pun on the word "trifle."

One further wrinkle, because I suspect it changes which reading is in play. In the version we were arguing over, the person applying the maxim is not a court but one of the people who would receive a speck, and the specks are individually below any threshold of noticeability even though collectively they amount to fifty years of mild but constant discomfort for each of the astronomically many people involved. Does it matter whether the maxim is being wielded by an institution deciding what it will hear or by a moral agent deciding what to weigh? Is there a reading under which the maxim, far from settling the case, is exactly the thing the case was built to test? And do any of the readings force a choice between keeping the maxim as a sensible rule of practice and keeping it as a claim about value — because it seems to me the friend needs the strong claim about value and can really only defend the weak one about practice. Please write this as continuous prose, the way you would in a long email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold or tables; go through the readings one by one, but in sentences rather than a list.
