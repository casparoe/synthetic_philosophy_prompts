I'm a graduate student in mathematics who also sits in on a philosophy of mathematics reading group, and we're closing the term with a session on the axiom of choice and its equivalents. I've been asked to open the session, and instead of giving a talk I want to read a short poem about Zorn's lemma -- partly because it's the equivalent people find most slippery, and partly because I think the right form can do some of the explaining for me. Most of the room will know the statement cold (a nonempty partially ordered set in which every chain has an upper bound has a maximal element), but I want the poem to make them feel the two things that usually get lost in the statement: that the maximal element is not a greatest element, and that the proof delivers existence without ever handing you the object.

So here is what I'd like you to write. Let the poem's form track the lemma's structure rather than merely name it. Have it proceed as a chain -- each line comparable to the one before, in whatever sense you can sustain (rhyme, or one line's image contained in the next, or a term recurring and being subsumed) -- so that the reader watches a chain being extended, with the limit steps where the chain closes off and a fresh element is thrown above it. Bring the poem to a final line that is a maximal element: nothing in the poem could be placed above it without breaking the poem's own order. But leave at least one line standing off to the side that is incomparable with that final line, so that a reader who knows the lemma can see the gap between maximal and greatest, and a reader who doesn't can still feel why the distinction matters. And I want the non-constructive character to come through -- the poem should be able to say that the top exists while being unable or unwilling to name or exhibit it; no recipe, no construction, just the guarantee. Something in the range of fourteen to twenty lines. I don't need a fixed form, but if a received form (a sonnet, a sestina, a pantoum) lets you do the chain-and-limit work more honestly, use it and tell me which one you chose and why. Accuracy matters more than beauty here -- a mathematician in the room should not wince -- but it should still read as a poem and not as a proof set in line breaks.

After the poem, on its own line beginning "Coda:", give me a single sentence naming which feature of the poem corresponds to the hypothesis of the lemma, which to the maximal element, and which to its non-constructive character.
