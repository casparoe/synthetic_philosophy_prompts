I'm a third-year PhD student in epistemology, working on the norm of assertion, and I've reached the stage where I have to decide what I will actually spend the next two years arguing for. My working hunch is that the field keeps circling a distinction it has never properly separated: between what makes an assertion permissible to make — call this the licence question — and what makes a completed assertion criticisable, or liable to sanction, once made — call this the liability question. Nearly every candidate norm on offer (knowledge, truth, belief, justification, rational credibility, a no-false-implicature condition) is presented as though it answered both questions at once, and I doubt that any single condition can. Two cases push me towards that doubt. The first is a speaker who asserts something true and well-evidenced that she nevertheless does not know, where the hearer is entirely reasonable to rely on her. The second is a speaker who does know, but only by a route that would not survive any challenge, so that hearers who press her are also being reasonable. In both, the licence verdict and the liability verdict appear to come apart.

What I want from you is an agenda, not a survey. Given that distinction, which open questions about the architecture of assertion norms are genuinely upstream of the others, and which should I actually work on? The candidates I have in mind include: whether there is a single norm or a division of labour between a permissive norm and a critical norm; whether the norm is to be derived from the function of assertion (transmitting knowledge, adding to the common ground, or something else) or defended directly; whether the norms governing assertion are constitutive of the speech act or merely social and conventional; how assertion norms interact with the norms of belief and of practical reliance on testimony; and whether the whole debate is about what speakers implicitly follow or about an ideal standard of correctness. I do not want to be confined to that list, and I would rather you told me if one of my framing assumptions is the real problem.

For each question you would prioritise, explain why it is upstream — how many other disputes resolve once it is settled — how tractable it is for one person with two years, a desk, and no laboratory, and what evidence or argument over that period would move it up or down the ranking. Then tell me which questions I should deprioritise because their resolution would not change much downstream. Be explicit about the trade-offs between importance, tractability, and how much else depends on the answer, and about where those criteria pull against each other. State how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
