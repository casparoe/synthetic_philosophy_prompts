I'm a phd student in philosophy and I run a reading group that's been going through the Victorians and the whole psychical-research thing, and next week is Sidgwick, which honestly I've been dreading because everyone in the group wants to do the "was he a credulous Victorian or not" debate and I think thats the boring question. What I want to open with is a poem that's actually about the specific bind he was in.

So: write me a pantoum — the form where lines 2 and 4 of each stanza come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next one, and the opening lines circle back at the very end — 4 or 5 quatrains, in Sidgwick's own voice, late in life, at his desk after a sitting or after working through another batch of reports. The whole reason I want the pantoum is the repetition. I want two lines that keep returning, one of them the methodologist's line (the evidence isn't enough, I have no right to conclude, one more case and still no rule) and the other the line of the man who badly wants to be told he doesn't end at death. And here's the part I actually care about: by the time the poem loops back around, those two lines should have swapped jobs. The sentence that reads as hard-won caution early on should sound by the end like the ache underneath it, and the line that starts out as the wish should come out sounding like the only honest thing he can still say. I want a reader to feel how the same words keep meaning opposite things depending on which horn of his problem he's sitting on — the horn where reason tells you to look after yourself, the horn where it tells you to look after everyone, and the half-suppressed hope that a future life would make those two agree so he could stop having to choose.

Don't name the theory anywhere in the poem and don't let him announce a conclusion, because he never reached one. Keep the SPR scaffolding light — sittings, reports, maybe the census of hallucinations if you want it — since thats backdrop, not the subject. Someone who actually knows Sidgwick should be able to tell which recurring line is which and why they trade places; someone who doesn't should just get a good poem about a careful man hoping against his own method.

Then, after the poem, a short section titled "what the form is doing" — 200 words or so — on why you repeated the lines you did, where you put the swap, and what the poem would lose in free verse.
