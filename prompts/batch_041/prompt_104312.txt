So I've been messing around with Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot — the one where he spends a whole book translating the same little French poem dozens of different ways — and it got me thinking about something I want you to argue about.

Here's the setup. You're translating a poem that's locked down hard by formal constraints: rhyme, meter, maybe an acrostic, maybe a fixed syllable count per line. Now imagine you're going into a target language where the constraints can't all be kept at once. Not "really hard to keep" — genuinely impossible, jointly unsatisfiable in that language, even though each constraint on its own is perfectly doable. So you have to break at least one. I've been calling this a constraint deficit, for lack of a better term.

I want you to argue for this position and actually defend it: when the constraint you drop is a load-bearing one — the kind thats doing real work in the poem, not just decoration — what you've made isn't a translation of that poem anymore. It's a new poem in conversation with the original, and the honest thing is to label it as such instead of passing it off as the same work in another tongue.

Don't just say "it depends" and walk off. Take on the obvious objection head-on: every translation loses something, so where exactly is the line, and why is your line the right one rather than just a convenient one? Give me an actual criterion and stick to it, even if it means some well-loved translations come out the other side as adaptations.

One more thing. Keep straight which parts of this are settled in translation studies and which are genuinely up for grabs among people who know the field, and don't dress up a contested view as if it were the consensus.
