I'm a junior at a science-and-humanities magnet school, and I'm in a seminar called Minds and Machines that meets at 7:40 in the morning, which is the only reason I've had the patience to sit with Hofstadter's essay on variations on a theme, the one collected in Metamagical Themas. Our teacher glosses his picture as knob-twiddling: you imagine a theme as a point in a conceptual space with a set of knobs sticking out of it, and creativity is turning one of those knobs a little and noticing what happens. Two claims from the unit are the ones I keep arguing about with my friend Priya. First, the turns that feel like imaginative leaps are often no larger, measured in the space, than the turns that feel trivial, so the size of a creative move isn't the size of the knob-turn but the size of its effect. Second, and this is the part I actually want a poem about, none of it works unless you can already tell which features of a theme are load-bearing and which are merely along for the ride — a variation only counts as a variation if the theme survives it, and knowing where that boundary lies is most of the skill. For my final project I want to write a poem that enacts this rather than explains it, and I'd like you to write it for me.

The poem should be a theme and variations, and I want the mechanics visible enough that our teacher can check my work. The seed is a line I wrote myself, so there's no attribution problem, and I'd like it printed alone at the top, before anything else: "My grandmother's chair stands empty at the head of the table." Then six variations of four lines each, unrhymed, with lines of roughly equal length, so that nothing varies but what I've asked to vary; a variation may reword the seed inside itself, but please don't use the seed line as a refrain repeated verbatim. Each variation turns exactly one knob of the seed, and I want the six knobs to be these: whose chair it is; what the object is; what state the object is in; where it stands; when the scene is taking place, including tense; and who is speaking about whom. After each variation, add one italicized sentence in brackets naming the knob that was turned and saying, honestly, how far the theme was pushed before it stopped being a variation on that seed — cheap, expensive, or over the line. One of the six must be a false variation: it should read like an ordinary small turn, but it should have left the space, and its note should say so and say what got lost when it did. The last variation should feel like the biggest imaginative leap in the whole poem, and its note should report, without apology, that it is no larger a turn than any of the others.

Two things about how it should read. Someone who knows the model should be able to hold the notes up against the variations and find that they're true, which means the notes can't be flattering — if a variation turns out to be a bigger move than I claimed, say so. But someone who reads only the verse and skips every bracketed sentence should still get a real poem, one that earns a second reading, and it should be about the chair, my grandmother, and the table rather than about creativity; the meta-commentary belongs in the notes, not in the lines. Please don't let it rhyme, and keep the diction plain enough that a reader can see exactly which word moved.
