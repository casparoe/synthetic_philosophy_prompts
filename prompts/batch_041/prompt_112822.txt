I am a doctoral candidate in the philosophy of biology, and I have been drafted into chairing the Q&A at our department's colloquium next term. Our visitor is a philosopher who has spent her career defending a sophisticated version of the selected-effects (etiological) account of biological function — the view that ascribing a function to a trait is roughly a matter of saying what that trait was selected for in its lineage — and who is known for insisting, against a decade of criticism, that the account survives essentially intact. I should like you to take on the role of a philosopher of biology with a specialist's grasp of the function debate and draft ten to twelve interview questions, each with a follow-up, that press her where the literature genuinely presses rather than inviting her to restate the position. Please begin by thinking your way through, for your own benefit, which objections to the selected-effects account have plausible replies and which do not, and then order the questions so that the exchange moves from the objections she can dispatch in a sentence to the ones that cost her something; every follow-up should be keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, so that the sequence has a cumulative pressure rather than feeling like a list.

I want at least one question each on the following: (i) traits with no selection history — a gene that has just arisen de novo, an organ in a mutant that never had a selective past, a lineage that has drifted rather than been selected — and what happens to the account if it retreats to trait types rather than trait tokens, since that retreat may make it uncomfortably hospitable to the causal-role alternative; (ii) the indeterminacy of "what the trait was selected for", given that selection acts on whole phenotypes, and how the account fixes the correct description of a trait's function when several descriptions are equally consistent with the lineage's history; (iii) the source of the normativity in function claims, including the awkward case of a trait whose selected-for effect has become impossible (the organ retained, the behaviour it served now unavailable), and whether past selection can license present-day malfunction talk at all; (iv) exaptation and co-optation, where a trait is stably maintained for a new effect while the old one still obtains — does it now have two functions, and what does the answer do to function-based explanations of malfunction; (v) artefacts, synthetic-biology constructs and deliberately designed mechanisms, where there may be no selection history in the relevant sense, and whether counting the designer's intentions as a selection-like process collapses the account into an intentional one; (vi) the long-running dispute over how much of the genome is functional, where one camp treats reproducible biochemical activity as sufficient and the other demands evidence of selection; (vii) function attributions to entities that are not lineages at all — ecosystems, keystone species, engineered devices; and (viii) whether the account earns its explanatory keep against the causal-role or goal-contribution alternative, or merely redescribes what we already knew. Please invent the concrete cases you need where the stock examples are too well worn — a bacterium on a space station, a trait maintained in a population under a designer's hand, a gene whose product does nothing measurable — and where you use a named position, describe it rather than attributing it to an author. Do not give me citations, reading lists or quotations, and do not put any passage in quotation marks: I can chase the sources myself, and I would rather the questions stand on their arguments. Our guest is not a public figure, so write for an unnamed defender rather than putting words in a living person's mouth.

Finally, I should like the answer to be honest about its own footing. After the questions, add a short closing note saying which one you think is most likely to misfire — that is, where the objection rests on a premise the guest can reasonably reject — and what answer from her would make you think the account survives that objection, as against what answer would make you think it fails. Say plainly where your own confidence in these objections is lowest, and what evidence or argument would move you. Please write in British English, number the questions, and label each follow-up clearly beneath its question.
