For several years now a recurring argument among friends — one of them a Swedish economist, one a Norwegian political scientist, the rest of us bystanders — has been whether Sweden or Norway is the more liberal country, and in all that time it has never once been settled; we simply re-run the same exchange with fresh examples. The case for Sweden, as I hear it made, is that Sweden has gone much further in marketising its welfare state: tax-funded private schools, for-profit firms running preschools, clinics and elder care, and a wealth tax abolished in the 2000s, whereas Norway still taxes wealth, keeps a sovereign wealth fund and a controlling stake in Equinor, and has been warier of letting private firms into welfare provision. The case for Norway is that this is exactly backwards: what Sweden calls freedom is exposure to the market, and Norwegians are freer precisely because they are more secure, less dependent on an employer or a landlord, and cushioned by a fund that is theirs collectively; and, further, that because Sweden is in the European Union and Norway is not, Norwegians are not subject to laws made elsewhere. (Someone always observes that Norway, through the EEA, adopts a great deal of EU law without voting on it, and the conversation moves on unresolved.) My own suspicion, offered as a hypothesis rather than a conclusion, is that we are not really disagreeing about the two countries at all, but about the word 'liberal', under which at least three different ideas are being run together: freedom as non-interference by the state; the freedom that consists in having the resources and security to live as one chooses, which may require a strong state rather than a weak one; and 'liberal' in the older sense of a liberal-democratic order of rights and the rule of law. If that is right, what remains of the dispute once those senses are separated — a substantive disagreement about the countries, a disagreement about which of the three things matters most, or nothing at all? Are there hidden empirical questions doing the work, about whether marketised arrangements deliver what their defenders claim, or about whether the security of the Norwegian model really leaves people more able to act as they wish? And is there a fact of the matter here in the first place: what, if anything, would settle which country is more liberal — an index, a set of agreed measures, a stipulated definition of the word — and if settling it required weighting the dimensions against one another, would that not be an evaluative choice that no amount of evidence could make for us? I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict on the countries, though if the diagnosis leaves a verdict standing, I will take it. Please write it as continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables — the way one would write a long letter.
