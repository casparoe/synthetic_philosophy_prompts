My old lab partner has fallen for a line of thinking I half agree with. He says that when you call fabricating data "wrong" you're not stating a fact that could be true or false — you're just venting a feeling, the way you'd shout at a bad driver. As a scientist I've got alot of sympathy for that; it's the only picture that sits comfortably with everything being ultimately physical. The trouble is he won't stop there. He'll happily argue: if fabricating data is wrong, then publishing a paper you know is built on fabricated data is wrong; fabricating data is wrong; so publishing that paper is wrong. And that's a real argument — the conclusion does follow. But look at the middle bit. The first time "fabricating data is wrong" shows up it's tucked inside an if-clause, and he isn't venting anything there, he's just supposing it; the whole if-sentence is the sort of thing that can be true or false. So the same words seem to be doing two completely different jobs, and I can't see how his "it's just a feeling" story covers the second one.

He reckons he's got an answer: that when a moral claim turns up inside an if-clause, or under a "maybe", or in a question, it's still expressing an attitude, just a more complicated one, and that's why we're allowed to call moral claims true and reason with them. To me that sounds like he's relabelled the problem rather than solved it, but I'm new to all this and might be missing something.

What should I say to him? Start with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, and please don't restate my question or warm up first. Keep it in plain terms — I'm a retired chemist, not a philosphy professor.
