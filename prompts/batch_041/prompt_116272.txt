Harsanyi's aggregation theorem is frequently presented as a neutral derivation of utilitarianism. It is usually stated in two parts. The first is a representation result: suppose that every individual's preferences over lotteries satisfy the expected-utility (von Neumann–Morgenstern) axioms, so that each individual can be assigned a cardinal utility function unique up to positive affine transformation; suppose that the social ordering of lotteries satisfies those axioms as well; and suppose that the social ordering satisfies a Pareto condition, preferring one lottery to another whenever every individual prefers it. Then the social ordering can be represented as a weighted sum of the individuals' utility functions, with non-negative weights. The second part delivers the utilitarian conclusion: the weights must be equal, since a person choosing from behind a veil of ignorance, who regards every position in society as equally likely to be their own, would attach the same weight to everyone's interests.

Please write an argument for the following position: the theorem does not establish utilitarianism, and its reputation for neutrality is doubly misleading. First, the equal weighting is an independent normative premise rather than a consequence of the axioms; the representation result on its own leaves the weights free, so anyone who wishes to move from it to utilitarianism must supply and defend a further premise about fairness. Second, the axioms are not distributionally innocent in the way that the phrase 'neutral derivation' suggests: the demand that the social ordering satisfy the expected-utility axioms together with the Pareto condition already imposes a particular and contestable structure on aggregation, namely that the social ranking be linear in the individuals' utilities, which appears to exclude any view on which the weight carried by a person's utility depends on how low that utility is.

In the course of the argument, address the strongest reply available to a defender of the theorem: that a Pareto-respecting social ordering which satisfies the expected-utility axioms simply is the utilitarian structure, so that there is nothing left for a separate premise to accomplish, and that the veil of ignorance is a plausible principle of fairness rather than an ad hoc assumption. Consider also whether the theorem's Pareto condition, applied as it is to individuals' preferences over lotteries rather than to the distribution of utility that results, is itself a substantive commitment rather than a minimal requirement of rationality.

End by saying how confident you are in the argument you have given, and identify the single step of it in which you have least confidence.

Write in British English.
