I'm the mayor's chief of staff in a mid-size city, and our new charter created a citizens' assembly: 58 residents picked by stratified lottery, meeting four weekends to decide how to spend a $40M surplus, with a referendum later to ratify whatever they recommend. I give the opening remarks Saturday morning. In the room: the 58 members (nervous, a few openly annoyed, one guy in the back telling anyone who'll listen that he never volunteered for this), most of the city council, half of whom think the whole thing is a stunt, two reporters, and a visiting political theorist who's made a career of argueing that deliberative minipublics mostly launder elite opinion. Write me about five minutes that gives these people a real reason to take their own authority seriously. What I can't do: call them wise or qualified; call them representatives or tell them to speak for their neighborhoods; say they were chosen because they're special; or lean on the jury analogy, since the retired bus driver in seat 12 just did jury duty and will visibly roll her eyes. I can be blunt that they're a random sample. So what actually grounds the authority of a body like this, said out loud to non-philosophers? How do I answer the guy who says "I'm nobody, why would anyone listen to me," and the theorist's jab that four weekends of briefings just installs whoever did the briefing? End by handing over the gavel without promising the council will accept the result. American English, and don't let it turn into a civics lecture.
