I am a professional mediator in the public sector — pay and grading disputes between a trust and a union, budget splits between two departments, service-level disagreements between a regulator and the body it oversees. I run two or three of these a month, the sums at stake are large, my only power is to recommend, and the parties have to sign the settlement themselves. I have no economics training. I want a decision procedure grounded in the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution — the idea that the fair settlement is where the straight line from the disagreement point to the ideal point (each side's best case if the other conceded everything) meets the set of feasible settlements — that I can actually run in a two-hour session after a day of preparation.

Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a recommendation, and where it must be handed back to the parties because the question is one of values or of who should bear risk rather than of arithmetic. It has to say what to do when the feasible set is not a smooth trade-off but a bundle of all-or-nothing packages; when I cannot get honest numbers for anyone's utility and must work from stated claims instead; when a claim is an opening bid rather than a genuine ideal point; and what to tell the parties when the outcome shifts because an option nobody wanted was added or withdrawn. Mark the steps that are judgment calls wearing the costume of a formula, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do.

Then run it on three disputes: a pay claim; a budget split between two departments where one carries a statutory duty the other lacks; and one where it hands most of the surplus to the party I privately think has the weaker case, and say whether I should follow it anyway.

Please write in British English.
