I have been turning over the phrase the will of the people for a long time, and I have agreed to lead a session on it at our library's monthly discussion evening. I have no academic background in any of this — I am a hospital administrator who reads widely — and the others in the room will be the same: neighbours, a retired teacher, two local councillors, a handful of people active in local campaigns. None of them has read any political theory, and I want you to plan how I should explain this, not explain it to me.

What put me onto the topic was an experience of our own. Our residents' association spent eighteen months consulting the town about a proposed development on the old playing fields, and we ended up doing it three times. The first was a single yes-or-no question, and the answer came back against the development, but only narrowly. The second asked three separate questions — whether new housing was needed, whether the traffic on the access road was a serious problem, and whether the green space should be protected — and large majorities answered yes to all three; the council read that as broad support for the development, which nobody in the room felt they had given. The third offered three options — the full development, a smaller one, and none at all — and none won on first choices, but once the smaller option was eliminated and the second choices of its supporters were added, the full development came out ahead. The same streets, largely the same people, three good-faith consultations, three different answers about what the town wanted. I keep hearing the phrase used elsewhere as a weapon — the real people against the elites — and I have no interest in that fight; I want the evening to be about what the phrase could even mean.

Please plan the session for me. I want the order the ideas have to come in and why each depends on the one before it, so that the phrase stops sounding obvious to them by the middle and stops sounding like empty noise by the end — I want them to leave thinking it does real work. I would like our three consultations to be the single example that carries the whole evening, but tell me honestly whether that case can bear the weight, and what it would have to include or leave out if it can, or what a second, smaller case would have to supply if it cannot. Tell me the misconceptions they will bring or form — my own guesses are that they will assume the will of the people is simply whatever the majority wants, and that it exists beforehand and a vote merely discovers it — where each of those takes hold, and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off. Give me one question to put to them near the end that would show whether they have grasped the difficulty or only been talked into it. And tell me plainly what I should say I am not going to settle at this level.
