I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training, and I've somehow ended up having to write a proposal for a small research grant — six months, part time, one of those public-interest tech funders, mixed room of economists and engineers on the review panel. Topic is futarchy: the idea that we vote on what we want (some welfare metric) and then let prediction markets decide which policy to actually run, because the market prices tell you which option scores higher on the metric.

The usual objections I keep running into are manipulation, thin liquidity, and who gets to pick the metric, and honestly those all feel like engineering problems that some design tweek would fix. My half-formed worry is different and I can't tell if it's the whole game or nothing: the market is supposed to price two things at once — how the metric turns out if we do X, and how it turns out if we don't — but we only ever get to live in one of those worlds. One side of the bet never gets checked against anything real. So the market is being asked to put a number on a claim that nothing will ever settle, and if that's right then I want to know exactly when that's fatal and when it's harmless. My hunch is that it's fine whenever the decision gets repeated and randomly assigned — feature flags, staggered rollouts, A/B tests, the stuff I do at work — and that this quietly shrinks futarchy's real territory to a much smaller patch than its fans think, possibly even to cases where you could just run the experiment and skip the market entirely. But I might be wrong in an interesting way.

Turn this into a two-page proposal. I need: the question stated so precisely that it has a definate answer and isn't just "is futarchy good"; why the standard objections and the standard replies to them don't already settle it; the method — what I'd actually do, phase by phase across six months, including the small thing I want to build (a two-sided conditional market over something cheap and fast, run with colleagues at work, to see whether the side that never gets settled goes dead, drifts somewhere silly, or gets gamed); what the answer looks like in each direction and what would count as the project failing; and an honest read on whether this fits in six part-time months or is really a three-year thing. Don't give me a reading list or citations, I'll fill those in myself — just argue the case.
