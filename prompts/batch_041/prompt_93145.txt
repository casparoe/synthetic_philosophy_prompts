I spent most of last night arguing with a close friend about whether generations are real, and we kept talking past each other: he is convinced that Gen Z names a genuine kind of person with a shared outlook, while I suspect it is a marketing label wearing the costume of a demographic fact, and only afterwards did it occur to me that we were answering different questions. I would like to convert that argument into a decision tree for a reading group I host, so that participants can discover which position on the reality of generations they actually hold rather than which label they find congenial. Please build the tree from the logical space first and only then locate the positions in it. Each internal node should be a question a person can answer without knowing where it leads — for example, whether a birth cohort's distinctive outlook survives once you separate cohort effects from age and period effects; whether belonging to a generation requires a shared formative experience, a shared self-understanding, both, or neither; whether the boundary years are discovered or stipulated; and whether the label itself exerts a causal or self-fulfilling influence on the people it names. Each leaf should carry the name of a position — realism that treats a generation as a genuine collective constituted by shared formative experience; a thin cohortism that grants real cohort effects but denies that any collective subject exists; a performative or looping-kind constructivism on which the label helps make the kind real; a flat nominalism that treats generations as statistical artifacts and convenient fictions; and the critique that generational talk is an essentializing ideology to be debunked — along with one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. I want the tree to be honest rather than tidy: mark any combination of answers that no one occupies and say whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any two positions the tree cannot genuinely separate.
