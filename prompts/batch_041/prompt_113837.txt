I'm a second-year PhD student in a comp-cog lab and I've somehow been voluntold to run next month's session of our reading group, which is annoyingly interdisciplinary — theres a roboticist, someone from the semantics side of linguistics, two RL students from the CS department, and one economist who does mechanism design and will definitely ask whether this is just revealed preference with a GPU bolted on. The topic I got handed is inverse reinforcement learning, specifically whether you can actually recover someone's preferences from their behavior. I do not want you to explain IRL to me — I can explain it. I want you to plan how I explain it to them, in about 70 minutes with a projector, and no reading list, I have to fight about those separately with the other organizer.

The thing I keep going back and forth on is the ordering. I know where I'd like to end up, which is a claim I've been nursing: that IRL is less like measuring a hidden quantity and more like reading — behavior only counts as evidence about preferences once you've supplied a model that makes it evidence, so the "inference" is downstream of a modeling choice you already made rather than upstream of it, and two defensible choices can hand you rewards that disagree about the exact thing you care about. I'm not sure that can be the payoff or whether it's too cute and has to be planted way earlier. And I'm worried the whole session collapses into 'more data / a better algorithm will fix it' for most of the room. So: what has to come first and what has to wait, what's the actual dependency structure — does the ill-posedness of the problem have to land before or after they've seen a concrete case of identical behavior under different rewards, and where does the standard potential-based-reshaping result fit, given that it's the one piece of math I'm confident enough to put on a slide?

For the carrying example I've been planning to use a robot that steers wide around the human in the room: behaviorally it looks identical whether the reward says 'the human is fragile, don't bump them' or 'bumping them gets you punished', and those two will generalize differently in a new room with a different-looking person in it. Is that strong enough to carry the whole session, and if not, what does a second example have to add that this one structurally can't? Also, which misconceptions will they form and in what order — my predictions are at least 'this is just supervised learning on trajectories', 'a better prior or normalizer fixes the ambiguity', 'if I drop the optimality assumption then I'm not assuming anything', and 'preferences sit in the head and IRL reads them out' — and where exactly in the sequence do I head each one off, bearing in mind that two of those are things I half-believe myself? Then a check for understanding that would show the reading-not-measuring point actually landed rather than them just nodding along, and one thing I should openly refuse to do at this level and say so out loud.
