I'm teaching the nonidentity problem in an undergraduate ethics course and want to use Frank Capra's It's a Wonderful Life as a companion case, so read the film through the lens of that problem. I want three things, and I want them argued rather than gestured at. First, the film's central device -- the angel showing George what Bedford Falls would be like had he never been born -- is a same-people-worse-off counterfactual, which is precisely the easy case that the nonidentity problem is built to bypass; say plainly what the film has to assume about personal identity across the counterfactual for its emotional payoff to land, and what it would have to become if the counterfactual were populated by different people instead. Second, the good George does is largely a matter of which people come to exist -- he pulls Harry from the ice, and the men Harry later saves, and their descendants, exist only because of that -- so show where the film trades on a person-affecting intuition that the standard arguments treat as untenable, and whether the film's moral survives being retold with different people in the improved world. Third, tell me where the film resists the reading: its real subject may be the worth of a single life and the ripple of ordinary decency, not the metaphysics of coming into existence, and I don't want the film reduced to an illustration of a puzzle. Close with what, if anything, the film makes vivid that the arguments alone do not. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Please write in American English.
