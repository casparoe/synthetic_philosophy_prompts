I'm not a philosopher, I'm just a guy who's been going to county council meetings for three years over a statue. It's a Confederate soldier on the courthouse square, put up in 1912 (which I didn't know before all this started — the date is on the base). The compromise the council finally voted through is this: the statue stays, and they bolt a new bronze plaque onto the base saying when it went up, who paid for it, and how many people the county enslaved in 1860, plus a QR code that takes you to the actual documents. Every spring there's a little ceremony where somebody reads the names of the county's war dead, both sides, and lays a wreath.

What's been chewing on me alot is that I can't figure out what everybody, including the compromise itself, is assuming about how a symbol means.

The people who want it kept say the meaning lives in whoever's looking at it — it's about heritage, not hate, and that's just my reading of it. Then those same people say the new plaque puts words in the statue's mouth and rewrites what it means. The people who want it gone say the statue means what it meant when it was put there and no plaque can launder that, but they'll also say a new statue on the other side of the square would send a message to kids walking past. And the council turned down that other statue on the grounds it would stir things up, which is definately a different theory of what statues do. So which view of how a public symbol means does the add-a-plaque compromise actually require — the maker's intention, the community's use over the years, the effects on the people who walk past, or none of those, with the meaning just being assigned by whoever has the power to assign it? Can you separate what the practice strictly needs from what it just happens to fit with? My gut says there's a version where the plaque is a thing the county can point at, and a version where the plaque genuinely changes the object, and nobody in that room knows which one they're defending.

The other half, and I know this sounds big: does keeping it and explaining it assume something about whether a town today is the same "we" as the town in 1912 — that it can be on the hook for what its grandparents did, or share in their honor? Is that a real position somebody could hold, or just a mood? And if the point of the plaque is repair, what would it have to do to count as repairing anything, rather than being therapy for the people who are alive now?

Please don't just tell me it's complicated, I know that. Also tell me how confident you actually are in what you're saying and which part you're least sure about — I'd rather have the soft spot marked than a tidy answer.
