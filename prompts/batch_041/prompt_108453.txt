A reading group I sit in has been circling the definition of death, and one member produced the following argument, which none of us could dismantle. (1) An organism is alive exactly when its parts are integrated into a single self-maintaining whole — when its subsystems operate as one system rather than as a collection of independent parts. (2) In a human being, that integration is the work of the brain, which coordinates respiration, circulation, hormonal signalling, and the rest. (3) When the whole brain has irreversibly ceased to function, the integrator is gone, and whatever persists is a set of parts no longer governed as a unit. (4) So a patient who meets the whole-brain criterion — no brainstem reflexes, no respiratory drive, maintained on a ventilator — is dead, notwithstanding the warm and circulating body. (5) But such bodies do not simply decay: circulation is maintained, wounds heal, infections are resisted, and in reported cases a pregnancy has been carried for weeks or months. (6) Each of those is a coordinated achievement of many organs, so the parts are still operating as one. (7) By (1), then, such a body is functioning as an integrated whole and is therefore alive. (4) and (7) cannot both stand, yet each appears to follow from premises we would all defend.

Tell me which step to give up, why it looked compelling, and what giving it up costs. I would rather keep the brain criterion — I am not prepared to say the ventilator patient is alive — but I want to know what I would be paying, and whether the honest move is to abandon the integration criterion at (1), to deny that what the body does at (5) qualifies as integration at (6), or to locate the trouble somewhere I have not thought of.
