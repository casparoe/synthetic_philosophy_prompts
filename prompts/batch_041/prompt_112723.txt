My father's dementia comes and goes within the same day: on his clear mornings he tells me he does not want to be kept alive by machines; by the afternoon he asks me to promise I will never let him go. I hold his medical power of attorney, my sister and I will have to authorize or refuse treatment within the year, and I have found myself defending a position I did not know I held. What I want to consult is neither of those statements but what I have come to call his settled will: the preference ordering he would have if he were fully informed about his diagnosis and prognosis, free of the confusion that now comes and goes and of the despair that has come with it, and had reflected for as long as he needed with his faculties intact. Give me your credence that the settled will is authoritative in cases of this kind -- that where a person's settled will and their presently expressed preferences conflict, a surrogate ought to act on the settled will, setting aside what the law happens to require. My own number is about 0.5, and I suspect I am averaging over a decomposition I have not done.

I expect the number to turn on at least three things, though you should replace any of them with a term you think carries more weight: whether idealized preferences constitute a person's good at all, as against the view that only actual experience or actual desire counts; whether idealization converges, or is path-dependent in the way his case makes me fear, since extrapolating from his clear-morning self and from his clear-evening self may yield two different stable orderings rather than one; and whether an idealized self's preferences have any authority over the actual self, given that the idealized self is someone who does not exist and whose choices are never the ones that get lived with.

Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. Item 1 is your overall credence with the terms combined; items 2 through 4 are the sub-questions that carry the most weight, each with its own probability and a one-line justification; item 5 is the term the overall number is most sensitive to, whether that is the same term you are least confident in, and what evidence or argument would move it most.
