Title: Molyneux's Problem and the Amodal Spatial Hypothesis

The traditional question is whether a person blind from birth, who has learned to distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch, could visually identify the cube and the sphere immediately upon gaining sight, without touching them. The usual debate treats this as a yes-or-no question about innate ideas or about the unity of the senses. I want to argue that the yes-or-no framing hides the more interesting issue: which properties can transfer across modalities, and under what conditions. My claim is that there is no single answer because touch and vision share a spatial format for some properties, such as extension, relative size, and perhaps number, but not for others, such as shape identity under rotation or object kind. Call this the amodal spatial hypothesis.

I came to this from a practical direction. I work with 3D point clouds and haptic rendering, and I have been thinking about how a haptic glove would need to encode a shape so that a user could recognize it without seeing it. The mapping is not obvious. Edges and corners are easy to render as forces; curvature sign and topology are much harder. This made me suspect that the philosophical debate often assumes a richer notion of shape than any single sensory modality actually delivers. If shape is partly a matter of features that are easy for touch to encode and partly a matter of features that are easy for vision to encode, then the newly sighted person might succeed at some visual tasks and fail at others, not because of innate ideas but because of the structure of the perceptual formats.

Sensory substitution devices seem relevant here. In some systems, a camera feed is converted into a tactile grid on the tongue or back. Users can learn to navigate and even reach for objects. Some report experiencing the stimulation as objects located in space rather than as sensations on the skin. I take this as evidence that a spatial format can be shared across modalities, at least after training. But the training matters: users often need active exploration and calibration, and they may be exploiting learned correlations rather than immediately seeing the object. So the device does not settle Molyneux's question. It does suggest that the question should be decomposed into a family of questions about specific properties and specific tasks.

The strongest objection I can see is that this is a psychological hypothesis, not a philosophical answer. Even if touch and vision share a spatial format, that does not tell us what the newly sighted person would actually experience. It also does not address the original concern about whether perceptual concepts are innate. I am not sure how to respond. I suspect the philosophical lesson is that Molyneux's problem is not one problem but a template for asking what kind of cross-modal transfer is possible. But I may be missing a standard distinction between recognition and identification, or between perceptual discrimination and conceptual categorization, that would make my decomposition less novel than I think.
