I'm in a small ethics reading group — four of us, we meet in the back of a cafe, and one of the members is an econ grad student who has a view I can't budge and can't quite refute. He says compromise between people with different values is just trade, and trade doesn't need a common currency, only each side being able to rank its own options — so a deal can leave both parties better off by their own lights even if they think each other's values are wicked. His stock example: someone who cares above all about animals and someone who cares above all about global poverty each give to the other's cause as part of a bargain, and both come out ahead. He says the real skill is spotting the issues where you care little and the other cares a lot — he talks about it like comparative advantage but for morals — and he's got a further wrinkle I find sneaky, that the trade can be intertemporal: I concede something now that I expect to lose anyway and you owe me a concession later, which makes moral compromise look like a credit market with debts and defaults. He's not a relativist, which is what makes it so annoying — he insists the whole thing is about what to do under disagreement, not about who's right.

I want to practice taking this apart before next week, so play him for me: sharp, not a strawman, willing to give ground on small points but never on the main claim, first person, and don't break character to give me a summary or a reading list — I want the argument itself, one paragraph a turn, and you can press me and ask me things, just stay in it until I say stop. Jump straight into replying to my opening, don't ask me to clarify first.

Here's my opening: I don't see what the trade metaphor buys you. In real trade both sides want more of the same stuff — bushels, dollars — and thats exactly why a rate can exist. But if I think eating meat is a grave wrong and you think it's fine, there's no bundle of your concessions on anything else that adds up to one of mine; the "rate" is just whatever leverage we happen to have. So either the metaphor collapses into "each of us got some of what we wanted" — ordinary compromise with an economics vocabulary pasted on top — or you're quietly assuming values are just preferences, which is the very thing in dispute. And even granting that we both come out ahead by our own lights: we've settled a question about what's actually right according to who could threaten more. A deal can make both parties happier and still leave a wrong standing. So which is it — is gains-from-trade doing any real work here, or is it a fancy name for splitting the difference?
