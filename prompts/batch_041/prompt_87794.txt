I am assembling a unit on philosophy of mind and want to build it around a reading of Daniel Keyes's Flowers for Algernon through Daniel Dennett's four kinds of creatures — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian — as he sets them out in Kinds of Minds. What I am after is a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the story to an illustration of the taxonomy, and it should not bend the taxonomy to fit the story.

Charlie Gordon begins as a creature whose responses are largely fixed and who learns by reinforcement and trial and error; by the middle of the book he is running experiments on himself, forming and discarding hypotheses, reading the scientific literature, and extending his mind through writing, language, and the apparatus of the laboratory. If the four kinds are best distinguished by where testing happens — in the genome over evolutionary time, in the world by trial and error, in the head by simulation, and in the culture through thinking tools — then Charlie's progress reports are the record of a single mind climbing the ladder of kinds, and his regression is the record of it descending. A mouse named Algernon, the very image of a Skinnerian creature running a maze, dies first. Does that descent show that the kinds are stages of one mind rather than four distinct natural kinds? Is a Gregorian creature defined by the thinking tools it depends on, so that losing the tools is losing the mind?

Then press on the places where the story resists the scheme and an honest critic has to say so. Dennett's four kinds are easily read as a ladder of cognitive power, and the story tempts us to read them as a ladder of worth; but Keyes treats the early Charlie and the late Charlie as the same person, fully owed our regard, and he treats Algernon's life as mattering even though its mind is of the humbler kind. Does the framework have the resources to explain why moral standing does not track the kind of mind, or does the story expose a gap in it? Point me to the scenes where this pressure is sharpest — the bakery, the laboratory, the classroom with Alice Kinnian, the final progress report — and tell me what the story shows about the difference between a mind's powers and its standing that the four kinds on their own leave out. I want a reading that says where Dennett is right about Charlie, where the story corrects him, and what the story adds to the debate that the taxonomy by itself does not. There is no need to quote the text exactly; I am after the argument and the reading, not the wording.
