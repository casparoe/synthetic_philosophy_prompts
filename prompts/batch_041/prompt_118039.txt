A colleague of mine, a philosopher of language whose judgment I usually trust, recently handed me a one-paragraph summary of David Lewis's counterpart theory, and although I cannot quite pinpoint the problem, something about it strikes me as off. I am a PhD student in cognitive science; I took a single metaphysics seminar years ago and have never worked through Lewis's modal metaphysics in any detail, so I would rather have a careful audit than a summary verdict.

Here is what my colleague wrote:

"Counterpart theory is Lewis's way of avoiding transworld identity. For Lewis, no single individual exists in more than one possible world; each of us is confined to the world we inhabit. So when we say that you could have been a banker, we are not describing you in any other world. We are describing a different individual -- your counterpart -- who lives in some other world and resembles you closely in the relevant respects, but who is not you. It follows that, strictly speaking, you have no modal properties at all: every claim about what you could or could not have done is really a claim about some other person. That is why critics like Kripke complain that counterpart theory turns you into a stranger to your own possibilities, and why the view is sometimes read as a deflationary account on which modal talk about an individual is not really about that individual at all."

Please take this apart sentence by sentence. Which sentences would a committed counterpart theorist endorse as written? Which are loose but recoverable? And which simply misstate Lewis's position? I am especially unsure about the inference from "the counterpart is not you" to "the could-have-been claim is about someone else," and about whether, on Lewis's own view, the sentence "you could have been a banker" comes out true of me or false. Beyond the sentence-level audit, I would like you to say whether the closing charge -- that counterpart theory makes me a stranger to my own possibilities -- is a fair statement of a genuine objection or a misdescription that a Lewisian can simply reject, and whether the accompanying deflationary reading survives any of that.
