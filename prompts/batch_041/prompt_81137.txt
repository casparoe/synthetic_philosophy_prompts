I spent thirty-five years as a field ecologist, much of it on long-lived species, and since retiring I have been reading my way into moral philosophy and into the economics of the very long term. One thing in the latter keeps catching my attention, and I have not managed to find a name for it. Perhaps you can tell me whether one exists, and if not, whether the thing deserves one.

Here is the pattern. A single number — a discount rate — is put forward to settle how much the welfare of people a century or three from now counts relative to our own. Asked to justify the number, people reach for reasons of quite different kinds. One is that markets pay interest, so three per cent is simply what the world charges. Pressed on why the yield on a thirty-year bond should govern the weight of a stranger's welfare in the year 2200, they answer that a person simply does prefer a good sooner to the same good later, and that this preference is a fact about us. Pressed again, on why a fact about our preferences should be a reason, they answer that the future is uncertain and we may not be there to enjoy it. Each reply is of a different sort — about markets, about psychology, about risk — yet they are offered as interchangeable supports for one number, and that number then does the entire moral work. My impression is that the reasons are being pooled, so that the parameter looks better supported than any one of them alone, and that something about what matters — that later welfare is worth less in itself — is carried in on the back of considerations that are only about risk, or about what we are in a position to do.

In my own trade we should have called such a quantity phenomenological: a coefficient that mops up several unrelated effects, and therefore cannot be given a single interpretation, and whose value will drift whenever the balance of those effects does. But I do not think that is the whole of it here, because the pooling is not merely an epistemic failing; it is how a value judgement is made to look like a technical assumption.

So: does this have a name? Is it an instance of something already well described — the fact/value gap, the naturalistic fallacy, hidden premises, or the familiar point about the difference between discounting a benefit because we may never receive it and discounting it because it arrives later — or is it a distinct move that wants a term of its own? If the latter, please coin one and define it: what exactly the move is, how it differs from each of its nearest neighbours, and where its edges lie. Does it turn up elsewhere — in health economics, in safety regulation, wherever a technical parameter is asked to bear a moral load? And how should one reply to it: by keeping the reasons strictly apart, or by insisting that the question of what matters be settled on its own terms before any number is chosen?
