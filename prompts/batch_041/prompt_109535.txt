I'm writing the take-home half of the final for my upper-level seminar on autonomy and the ethics of influence, and I need one essay question on where manipulation stops and legitimate persuasion starts — 90 minutes, 20 marks, open book. My problem is that everyone who's read the obvious stuff writes the same essay: "manipulation bypasses rational deliberation, persuasion engages it," plus a paragraph saying it's a spectrum. I want the question built so that sentence is the opening move and not the answer. So use this case, which I use in lecture and which isn't from any paper:

The Adviser. A financial adviser has to present a client with two funds, and she thinks one is clearly better for him. She never says anything false, never discourages questions, never rushes him, and the client understands every word she says. What she does is present the fund she favours first and in concrete, easy-to-grasp terms, and describe the other one only in perfectly true but abstract terms ("a different risk-return profile, with more moving parts"), knowing he'll default to what he can picture. He decides by reasoning about what she told him, and afterwards he'd endorse the choice. It still looks like she manipulated him.

The Uncheckable Premise. A friend talks you out of a treatment using a valid argument built on a statistic you have no way to evaluate. He knows you can't evaluate it and that's precisely why he's using it. Nothing is hidden, no bias is being exploited — the premise is just sitting there on the table where you can't get at it. Same intuition.

What I want in the question: (1) what each of the three standard families of accounts — the bypassing/non-rational-influence one, the deception/trickery one, and the pressure one — is forced to say about the two cases, and exactly where each breaks (I expect the bypassing view to have to either call the Adviser case innocent or re-describe it as bias-exploitation, which looks ad hoc); (2) take the strongest account and repair it so it handles both cases, and say what the repair costs — in particular whether it forces the repairer to appeal to a norm about what the target was owed rather than to a fact about how the influence worked, and what that does to the whole approach; (3) one case where their repaired account gives the wrong verdict.

Then give me a model answer of about 650 words and a marking scheme of five must-hit points, 4 marks each, with a one-line note under each on how candidates typically lose those marks, and a sentence at the top on what separates a first from an upper second. Don't let them get away with "it's all on a spectrum." Also, please don't quote anything at them, no citations needed — open book but no internet. Finish the whole thing with one last line beginning "Crux:" that names in a single sentence the one move that separates a first from an upper second.
