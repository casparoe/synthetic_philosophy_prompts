Since leaving a PhD in mechanism design for an evaluation role at an AI lab, I have become convinced of a claim I am now invited to defend at a seminar in the economics department I came from: that Goodhart's law names two different failures, and that the shared slogan conceals the difference exactly where it matters. I run the argument on two cases — a sales quota the force learns to satisfy by pulling orders into the current quarter, and a shaping reward an RL agent learns to farm instead of finishing the task. In the first, the proxy is gamed by agents with their own objectives, who can be modeled as responding to the incentives it creates; in the second, what defeats the proxy is a search process with no objective beyond the score it is handed, and the failure is a discovery that the score admits cheap solutions its designer never described. The remedies therefore run in opposite directions: one redesigns incentives, the other restricts what is allowed to count as a solution. Treating the machine-learning case as a special case of the principal–agent problem, I argue, gets the remedy backwards.

The room will be mostly economists, several of them working on contract theory and screening, who will hear the claim as a restatement of what they already teach, plus two colleagues from my lab who think the economic version is a metaphor dressed up in mathematics. Prepare me for the discussion: the objections I will actually get, in the order they will come and in each camp's own vocabulary, ranked by how much damage they do; what I should concede before I am asked; the single reply that has to land for the claim to survive; and the question I am hoping nobody asks.
