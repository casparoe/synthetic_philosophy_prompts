You are a philosopher of metaphysics with expertise in persistence, tense, and the metaphysics of time. I am a graduate student working on a dissertation chapter on persistence, and I have been defending the following position in a seminar; I would like you to think carefully and, before answering, brainstorm a list of candidate defeaters, then organize your response around them. The position, which I have been calling temporal adverbialism about persistence, is this: ordinary material objects endure rather than perdure. They are wholly present at each time at which they exist, and they do not have temporal parts. The apparent relationality of tensed predication—a poker is bent at one time and straight at another—is not to be explained by relativizing properties to times or by positing temporal parts; rather, tensed predicates are true of an enduring object only under a temporal modifier, and the object's relation to a time is a primitive, non-mereological relation of temporal location. I also hold that the problem of temporary intrinsics, far from refuting endurance, is a reason to reject perdurantism, because it mislocates the indexicality of tense in the object rather than in the predicate. Assume I know the standard moves and the usual examples; do not spend time on definitions or a survey of who said what. I am not asking for a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want the argument.

Play it straight: what would count against this position? What would have to be true for it to be false? Which observations, arguments, intuitions, formal results, or developments should move me? What does temporal adverbialism predict that perdurantism, stage theory, and other rivals do not, and where does it risk making a claim that could be false? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to distinguish those that a committed temporal adverbialist would concede as genuine costs from those they would reasonably dismiss. In particular, consider cases of indeterminate persistence, fission and fusion, objects that change their temporal location, and any tension with relativistic physics or with the logic of temporal predication. Also tell me whether the view has been insulated by qualifications—whether primitive temporal location or the temporal modifier is doing substantive work or merely postponing the problem—and whether that insulation, if it exists, is a defect or simply what a view of this kind should do. I would personally be most moved by a case in which an object's persistence is genuinely indeterminate or in which the view cannot distinguish a change in an object from a change in its temporal location; if you think those cases do not actually threaten the view, say why.
