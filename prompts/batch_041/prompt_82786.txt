I write a small satirical newsletter for friends and colleagues — nothing professional, a few hundred words a fortnight that I circulate by email — and I have been trying to work up a piece about a philosophy idea I came across recently, without much luck. The idea, as I understand it, is that no individual exists in more than one possible world: there is not one person, you, who turns up in several versions across different worlds, but rather each world has its own inhabitants, and when we say you could have been a doctor, what is meant is that some inhabitant of some other world is your counterpart — not identical with you, merely similar to you in the relevant respects. The counterpart relation is just similarity, so it is vague, it shifts depending on which respects we are comparing, it need not be one-to-one (a person might have several counterparts in another world, or none at all), and it is not transitive: your counterpart's counterpart need not be your counterpart. And yet the whole point of the view is supposed to be that it saves ordinary modal talk — that you could have done otherwise, that an election could have gone the other way — while denying that anything crosses between worlds. I am told that one famous objection runs roughly: if the person who wins in the other world is not the same person as the one who lost here, then it is not true that the loser could have won; and that the reply is that this only sounds like a problem because we are still smuggling in the idea that the counterpart relation is identity. I have probably mangled it, but those are the parts I want the piece built on, and I would rather you told me at the end where I have gone wrong than quietly corrected me in the middle.

What I would like you to write is a mock product review — the sort of thing you find on a shopping site, with a star rating and an exasperated customer doing the talking — for a consumer app that claims to show you the lives you could have had: the version of you who took the job, who stayed, who said yes. The comedy should come from the app working exactly as the theory says it does, which is precisely the problem: it can only ever show you near-strangers rather than yourself, so the promise on the box is false advertising; the "family tree" feature branches in ways that never close up because the relation is not transitive; some years display three of you and some display none, because the relation is not one-to-one; and somewhere in the settings there is a control for which respects count as relevant, which means that "the real you" turns out to be a slider. Keep it deadpan and never explain the philosophy inside the review — the reviewer should be an ordinary, slightly aggrieved customer who keeps almost, but never quite, getting the point — and do not let it drift into generic parallel-universe science fiction: every joke should depend on the counterpart relation being similarity rather than identity, and on its being vague, context-sensitive, many-to-one and non-transitive. About 650 words, in British English, with the humour aimed so that someone who knows the literature laughs rather than winces while a general reader can still follow it.

Please set it out in this order, under these headings: Headline and rating (a title and a star rating out of five, two lines); What it claims to do (about 150 words); What it actually does (about 300 words, and this is where the real complaints should pile up); Verdict (about 150 words, including whether I would recommend it to someone grieving a choice they did not make); and finally Note on the philosophy I was after (about 150 words, telling me which commitment each of the jokes rests on, and flagging anything I have misunderstood about the view).
