I've just moved onto the safety side of a company that builds agents, first proper job after my masters, and I can't stop thinking about what our whole set-up assumes about the model. We run a second, much weaker model over the strong one's reasoning traces and have it flag anything that looks like the strong one working around its instructions. We keep the strong one off the network. In our red-team runs the attacker is the model itself, briefed to go after the worst outcome it can with the capabilities it already has. And we treat anything it says about itself, that it can't do something, that it's unsure what we meant, as just more output to be checked rather than a fact about the model — though we happily lean on its own claims about what it can and can't do when we're deciding whether one of our tests counts as passed. I sort of beleive in the whole thing but I couldn't tell you what it's committed to.

What theory of misalignment does that stack presuppose? It feels like it assumes the model is a persistent, strategic sort of thing with goals it's hiding, so that being misaligned is a matter of bad intentions rather than bad generalisation or plain confusion. Does the practice actually need that, or would the same protocol suit someone who thinks the failure mode is sycophancy, or muddled generalisation, or nothing much like an agent at all? And what does it quietly assume about the humans — that the weak monitor is trustworthy, that its blind spots aren't ours too? What I want is the implicit theory written out, with a clear line between what the process strictly requires and what it merely happens to fit, and then an honest account of what would have to change if the adversary picture were swapped for the misgeneralisation one.
