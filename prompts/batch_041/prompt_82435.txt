Two members of a reading group I sit in on spent most of last week's session arguing about whether the simulation argument takes the wind out of longtermist fanaticism — the claim that because the far future could contain astronomically many lives, even a small chance of steering it outweighs everything else we might do.

Ravi put it this way. Fanaticism gets its force by multiplying an enormous future value by whatever probability we can influence it. If there is a decent chance we are simulated, then the far future we imagine ourselves shaping is either not the real one, or belongs to a world whose continuation rests with whoever runs it. Either way the chance that our actions shape a real, vast future is far lower than the fanatic assumes, and the expected value shrinks with it; the far-future argument loses its grip.

Nadia answered that this only lands if you have already given up on fanaticism. Scaling by a smaller factor proves nothing when the factor is a civilization-scale future — half of an astronomical number is still astronomical — and the fanatic's whole position is that she will not be moved by probabilities once the stakes are large enough. Multiplying by any constant, however small, leaves the expected value astronomical. If anything, she said, the argument cuts the other way: simulations are cheap, so there may be vastly many simulated futures, and if the beings in them count, the value at stake grows rather than shrinks.

Ravi replied that she was treating the simulation hypothesis as one more independent branch to multiply by, when it actually changes what is in our option set. Reasoning conditional on being simulated, we may have no causal purchase on the real far future at all — the trouble is not that our odds are long but that the target is out of reach. And if simulations tend to be short-lived, the world we can affect may have little future in it to begin with.

Nadia said that reasoning conditional on being simulated was doing illegitimate work: Ravi was proceeding as though we knew we were simulated, whereas the argument's entire premise is that we do not. In the worlds where we are not simulated, nothing has changed, and Ravi owes an argument that the simulated worlds subtract value rather than merely adding branches. Ravi said the same charge could be turned around on her.

They asked me to referee and I could not. Please adjudicate move by move — say what is right and what is wrong in each of the four moves — and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing about how the simulation argument bears on fanaticism about the far future.
