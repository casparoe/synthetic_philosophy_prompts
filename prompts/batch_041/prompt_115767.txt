I teach an upper-level seminar in applied ethics (18 students, one 90-minute session) and I want a runnable activity that makes the net-welfare view of wild nature feel like a live position rather than a curiosity. The design I have in mind, which you should feel free to revise: students are staff of a regional development board operating under an unusual statutory mandate. Every land-use decision must be scored solely by its effect on the summed net welfare of the sentient animals in the affected area; species richness, ecosystem integrity, and the persistence of any particular population are explicitly given no weight. The board is about to approve the clearing of a parcel, and each team must produce a signed welfare impact statement.

Each team receives a one-page scoring rubric of the kind a bureaucracy would actually write, plus a table of invented but biologically plausible figures for a handful of groups on the parcel: soil invertebrates, insects, small rodents, songbirds, and one large mammal. The table should give per-hectare densities, reproductive strategy, typical lifespan, juvenile mortality, and an assumed fraction of individuals whose lives contain more suffering than enjoyment. Students do the arithmetic and reach a verdict themselves.

What I want them to run into: the groups they intuitively identify as the victims, the mammal and the birds, come out negative, while the vast numbers of short-lived, r-selected invertebrates come out positive, so that on the supplied figures the clearing scores as a welfare improvement. The uncomfortable result should look like the output of competent bookkeeping rather than a trick, and nobody should hear a name for the thesis until the debrief.

Please give me: the rubric and the figure table, with instructions to keep the numbers invented rather than drawn from real datasets; the roles, materials, and a minute-by-minute timeline; and a debrief that maps what happened onto the underlying idea, covering the shift from harm to ecosystems and species to summed welfare of individuals, the aggregation question of whether individual lives can simply be added and how much turns on which taxa are sentient, and the empirical hinge on which the sign of the verdict actually depends. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle it, and be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce. My worry is that it manufactures its conclusion by stipulating the figures, so I need the debrief to make clear which parameter would have to move for the verdict to flip, and which parts of the real dispute the exercise leaves untouched.
