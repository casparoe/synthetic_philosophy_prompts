Two people I know had an argument last week about a puzzle I had brought up, and I have not been able to decide who had the better of it at each stage. I should say at the outset that I have no academic background in this area; I enjoy puzzles and probability problems, and what follows is my own summary of what the two of them said.

The puzzle is this. A fair coin is flipped until the first heads appears. If the first heads falls on toss number n, you win 2^n/n dollars when n is odd and lose 2^n/n dollars when n is even. So you win $2 with probability 1/2, lose $2 with probability 1/4, win $8/3 with probability 1/8, lose $4 with probability 1/16, and so on indefinitely. To get the contribution of each outcome to the game's expected value, you multiply each payoff by its probability, which gives +1, -1/2, +1/3, -1/4, and so on. Added in that order, these come to about 0.69 (the natural logarithm of 2). But the winning contributions by themselves — 1 + 1/3 + 1/5 + ... — add up to infinity, and so do the losing contributions.

Rafael's opening claim was that the game simply has no expected value: not a number, and not infinity either. Since the gains and the losses each run off to infinity on their own, he said, there is no fact of the matter about what the game is worth, and anyone who answers "about 69 cents" has merely added the terms in one order rather than another.

June replied that the order is not a free choice. The game unfolds in stages, she said, and if you cut it off after N tosses — no payoff if it runs longer — then the value of that shortened game is exactly the partial sum, and those partial sums climb toward about 0.693. So 0.693 is a fact about the game, not an artifact of how we write the arithmetic. She added that the terms cannot be shuffled in the first place, because each one is attached to a particular outcome, a particular number of tosses, and pulling it loose is not an operation the game authorizes.

Rafael answered that the cutoff is just as arbitrary as the shuffle. Keep the same outcomes with the same probabilities and add the gains and losses in a different order, he said, and the total will come out to any number you like: positive, negative, or zero. Same outcomes, different "value," so there is no fact of the matter, and the arithmetic has simply failed to produce an answer for this game.

June said that proved too much. If there is no fact about what the game is worth, she said, then it cannot be irrational to buy it for a penny or sell it for a million dollars, and that is plainly absurd; so there must be a fact, and the limit of the shortened games is the best candidate. She also suggested the question might be moot, since a game that could require an endless run of tails is not something anyone can actually play.

Rafael answered both points. He said that "no fact about its value" does not mean "any price is as good as any other": it means the question has no answer, and a rule that returns no verdict on a case is silent about it rather than permissive. And the playability objection, he said, was a mistake — the chance of never getting heads is zero, so the game does end, and every payoff is perfectly well defined. What is missing is the value, not the game.

June came back the next day with an envelope of scribbles. She said the trouble seemed to be specific to this payoff schedule rather than to long-shot games as such: change the divisor from n to n squared and the contributions become 1, -1/4, +1/9, -1/16, and so on, whose winning and losing parts are each finite, so the same shape of game has an unambiguous value of about 0.82 no matter how you add it up. She added that, one way or another, she would rather say the rule was never meant to rank a game like this than say the rule is broken.

Rafael said that was precisely the disagreement: she wanted to fence the case off as an exotic monster, while he thought it showed that the rule we use to rank every ordinary gamble simply gives no answer here, with nothing obvious to put in its place.

I have turned this over and still cannot say who is right about which point. Would you go through the exchange move by move, rating each move on a scale that runs from plainly wrong to plainly correct and giving a short justification for each rating? I would also like to know what each of them is missing and who has the better of it overall. I am not looking for citations or a reading list, just the reasoning. Please write your answer in American English.
