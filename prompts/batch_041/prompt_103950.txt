I teach a seminar on the philosophy of criminal law, and this term's unit on consent has produced a running argument among the students that I would like to hand to you. One group has settled on the idea that consent is better modeled on a conversation than on a contract: it is continuous rather than punctual, negotiated and repaired turn by turn rather than agreed once and sealed, and undone by the wrong kind of responsiveness rather than by a defective signature. They use this to argue that our institution's affirmative-consent policy, which asks for a discrete verbal yes at the outset, is both under- and over-inclusive, and that the real question in a contested case is whether the encounter was conversational. A smaller faction, led by a student who spent two years in a commercial litigation practice, insists that consent simply is contractual: there is an offer and an acceptance, there are parties whose capacity is a threshold matter, and there are the familiar vitiating conditions -- duress, misrepresentation, unconscionability -- which is exactly why the three questions that occupy our syllabus are coercion, deception, and competence. On his view the conversation model is worse than useless, because a conversation has no moment at which one becomes entitled to proceed, and a standard with no moment of entitlement gives no guidance to anyone who wants to act permissibly.

What I want from you is an audit rather than a verdict between two slogans. Set out the correspondence explicitly for both models -- what plays the role of offer, acceptance, terms, the moment of formation, breach and remedy, and the vitiating conditions -- and then tell me where each breaks down, distinguishing the breaks that matter for the uses these two students are putting the models to from the ones that are merely picturesque. I am especially interested in three pressure points. First, whether the contractual baseline for duress can survive contact with intimate relationships, where the entitlements that would fix a baseline are precisely what is in dispute. Second, whether contract law's default of no duty to disclose, and the line it draws between fraud and mere non-disclosure, is the right line for deception about one's intentions, one's fertility or health status, or the nature of the act. Third, whether capacity, on either model, is a threshold or a gradient, given that the same person may be competent to agree to some things and not others, and at some moments and not others. Then tell me what survives: which model, suitably repaired, should govern how a campus disciplinary office and a prosecutor should think about consent, and whether some third analogy -- consent as a revocable license, as an invitation, as an ongoing permission rather than a transfer of something -- does better than either. Take a definite position on that; do not lay out the options and leave the choice to me.

Please organize the answer in the following sections, in this order: "The two models as my students use them" (~150 words); "Correspondence table" (a single table giving the mappings for both models side by side); "Where the contract model breaks" (~350 words); "Where the conversation model breaks" (~350 words); "The three pressure points: duress, disclosure, capacity" (~500 words); and "Verdict and a better picture" (~300 words).
