My flatmate's a second-year bio PhD and we ended up arguing for like three hours last night after I made the mistake of telling her what my seminar on evolution and ethics is about. Her line, which she's alot more pleased with than she should be, goes like this. (1) Our moral beliefs are the output of a faculty that natural selection built, and what selection was rewarding was behaviour that got our ancestors' genes into the next generation. (2) Nothing in that story suggests the fitness-relevant features of an action line up with its moral features — the pressure was on what we did, not on what we got right. (3) So we've got no way to check whether the faculty is reliable, and any check we could run would have to use moral judgments, so it'd be circular. (4) A belief that comes out of a faculty you can't independently certify is unjustified. (5) So none of our moral beliefs are justified. And here's the bit she really likes. (6) Everything I just said is true of perception, memory, induction, logic — you can't certify any of them without using them, and there's no neutral standpoint to check them against. (7) So nothing is justified, including the premises of her own argument. I said that last step is a reductio and she said no, it's the conclusion, welcome to the club, and then she made tea and went to bed.

I have to write a response paper thats due Monday and I want to keep (1) and (2) — the evolutionary story convinces me and I'm not going to pretend it doesn't — but I can't accept (7), and I genuinely can't tell whether that's because (7) really is a reductio or because I'm just squeemish about it. So I need you to pick a premise and cut it. My suspicion keeps sliding between three places and I can't get a grip on which is the cheap cut and which is the expensive one. One option is (4): maybe justification just doesn't require being able to certify the faculty, in which case the whole thing collapses, but I'd have to live with a view on which a creature can be justified by a process it has no access to and no evidence for. Another is (6): maybe moral beliefs are special because, whatever moral facts are, they don't explain why we hold the beliefs we do, whereas in perception the object does explain the percept — but then I owe an account of why that difference matters, and it looks like I'm rescuing morality by fiat. The third is the step from (2) to (3): "no reason to think they line up" is just agnosticism, and I can't see where the positive reason to doubt creeps in, unless (4) is already doing all the work — and if I plug a third-factor story in at that point (the same selection pressures that made us cooperative also made us responsive to some moral truths, so the two tracks get welded together at least in part) I don't know what that costs me either.

Don't hand me a menu of positions and don't tell me it depends on my metaethics — I want a verdict: name the premise you'd give up, say why it looked fine before, and tell me honestly what giving it up costs and who ends up paying. If there's a further case or thought experiment that would actually tell the two or three best diagnoses apart, give me that too, since that's what I'd rather put in the paper than a survey.
