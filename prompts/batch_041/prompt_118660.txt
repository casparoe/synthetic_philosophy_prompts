I run the taxonomy team at a mid-sized company — we own the category system that search, the data warehouse and the pricing models all sit on, about 4000 nodes and growing. Every quarter we have the same fight and it never resolves: is this category real, or did we just make it up? Someone says of course its real, look at the world; someone else says every category is a decision, pick whatever makes the downstream numbers work; then we compromise by adding another node that nobody uses.

I know philosophers spent an absurdly long time on exactly this — whether there are real universals, or whether properties are just names, or classes of resembling things, or bundles of particular features, and there's that useful-sounding split between the properties that carve nature at its joints and the ones that are only convenient groupings. I want to know if any of that survives translation into something my team can actually run on a Tuesday afternoon.

So: write me a decision procedure. It takes a proposed category (or a proposed split of an existing one) and walks it through a series of questions in order, saying what counts as a good enough answer at each step, and it ends either in a verdict — treat as real, treat as administrative-only, split, merge, delete — or in an explicit hand-off to a human because the question has turned into a values call rather than a facts call. The tests have to be things a smart non-philosopher can apply, so no jargon that isn't defined inside the procedure itself.

Constraints on how we actually work: maybe an hour per category, the person running it is a product analyst and not me, and any change to a category that feeds pricing needs sign-off from legal, which mostly means the procedure has to be able to say why a category is or isn't defensible, not just that we like it.

Run it on three cases, and I want it to have to sweat on all three:
1. The eternal one — whether our hot dog SKUs belong under sandwiches or get their own top-level node.
2. A category that's half biological, half commercial: we sell pet food and have to decide whether to key the tree to species, breed, or life stage, and those three cut across each other.
3. A people-category, which is where I actually lose sleep. We segment customers into a health-and-lifestyle bucket that marketing uses, and after a couple of years the customers in that bucket start behaving more like the bucket than like how they started out. So the category is doing something to the world it's supposed to be describing, and I dont know if that makes it more real or less.

For at least one of the three I expect the procedure to give an answer I won't like. Say so plainly when it does.

Then be honest about the limits: where the procedure is really just a judgment call wearing a checklist, what kinds of category it will get wrong, and whether there's a class of disputes it can't touch at all because the disagreement was never about metaphysics in the first place but about who pays for the mistake.

Skip the citations, I'll dig those up myself if I need them.
