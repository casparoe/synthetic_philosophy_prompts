Thesis: the proof is better read as a successful conversational move than as a failed deductive proof. Moore holds up one hand, then the other, and concludes that at least two external objects exist. The standard objection is that he cannot know his premises with the certainty needed to refute the skeptic, so the argument begs the question. I want to argue that this objection assumes proof must transmit warrant from premises to conclusion. On an alternative picture, proof can also redistribute the burden of proof by making a demand look unreasonable: the skeptic who insists on a proof of the external world while refusing to count any ordinary perceptual knowledge as a premise is not raising a hard epistemic standard but changing the conversational score. The essay would distinguish warrant-transfer proof from burden-shifting proof, use the brain-in-a-vat demand as a test case, and conclude that Moore's proof succeeds in the second sense and fails in the first, which is why it feels both compelling and unsatisfying. I also want to address the counter that this makes the proof merely rhetorical and no longer a proof.
