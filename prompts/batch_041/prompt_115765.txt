I came up through the rationalist blogs and forums, so my native dialect is decision-theoretic: policies and environments, priors and likelihoods, utility and reward functions, optimization targets, Goodharting, terminal versus instrumental value. I can follow a philosophical argument, but I understand a claim best when it can be put as a claim about an agent, a state of that agent, and what the state is answerable to.

There is a debate in value theory I have never been able to get fully inside, and I would like you to translate it into that dialect, carefully and without flattering either side. The topic is the intuition that some reasons for having an attitude are the wrong kind of reason. The stock cases run like this. A demon threatens to strike me unless I admire him. A benefactor will make me rich if I believe a proposition for which I have no evidence. A billionaire will pay me a large sum for sincerely intending, tonight, to drink a vial of poison tomorrow, whether or not I actually drink it. A pill would make me care about something I currently find worthless, and I would be happier for it. In each case the consideration plainly gives me something: I want to be in the state, I would pay for the pill, I would take a pill that made me believe. The claim is that it is not a reason for the attitude itself, only a reason to want the state, or to engineer it in myself. That distinction then gets put to work. Some views hold that for something to be good just is for there to be reasons to favour it; the objection is that such views overgenerate, since the demon's threat is a reason to favour the demon in some sense, and no one seems able to say what makes a reason the right kind without either arguing in a circle or quietly admitting that the restriction does no work.

What I want is two things.

First, the translation, claim by claim and case by case. In my dialect an attitude is a state of an agent that influences what it does; reasons are considerations that move expected value around; a belief is a state that ought to track the world, and a desire is a state the world ought to be made to match. Does the object-given versus state-given distinction survive as something like the difference between a fact about the environment that makes an outcome actually valuable and a fact about the agent that shifts the reward signal it is chasing? I suspect a fair amount of it survives, and I want to be told where that suspicion is right and where I am just pattern-matching to reward hacking and to the letter versus the spirit of an objective. Do not let the translation smuggle in my dialect's assumptions and present them as neutral renderings.

Second, and this is the part I actually care about, the residue report. Which claims come through cleanly, which come through only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all? In particular: for belief my dialect seems to have a clean counterpart, since evidence is the right kind of reason because it bears on accuracy and the wager is the wrong kind because it bears on payoff. But my dialect has no notion of a desire being correct, only of its being useful, so does the conative half of the debate survive translation at all, or does it collapse into a restatement of the thought that my final values are not up for grabs? If it collapses, is that a defect of my vocabulary, which cannot express the fittingness of an attitude, or a sign that the original distinction was doing less work than advertised, and that what looked like two kinds of reasons is really two kinds of explanations of why something is a reason? And where does the fact that I cannot adopt beliefs at will fit: does it fall out of the translation as a restriction on the action space, or is it what makes the whole puzzle look like a confusion about what can be chosen? I would also like to know whether the translation threatens to dissolve the puzzle entirely, in which case I want to be told that plainly rather than handed a both-frameworks-have-their-truth move.

No reading list and no citations; I cannot look anything up, and I would rather have your reasoning than your guesses about who said what. Do not smooth the seams. If the honest answer is that something important is lost in translation, say what it is and say whether the loss indicts the target vocabulary or the original.
