Ok, I need help with something and I think the only way to see it clearly is to make two people argue about it out loud.

Background: I'm a third-year in a phil of mind / cog sci program, and my prospectus is on teleosemantics, the whole project of squeezing content out of biological function. I've read the usual stuff — proper functions, the frog-and-fly detector, the asymmetric-dependence reply to the disjunction problem, swampman — and at this point I can recite all the moves, which is probly the problem. I want to see the two views actually pressed against each other on a case that isn't the frog.

So: write me a dialogue between two philosophers. Both good, neither a strawman, neither one an idiot who just doesn't get it. Both of them are naturalists who think content is a real phenomenon that needs a naturalistic account — they are not fighting about whether representation exists.

One of them, call her R, is a selectionist. For her a state represents X only if the mechanism that produces that state was selected for producing it when X obtained; content is fixed by history, by the proper function of the state-producer. No selection history, no content, full stop. She's happy to be statistical about it: proper functions are fixed by what was selected for across a lineage, Normal conditions are not the actual conditions, and misrepresentation is just what happens when Normal conditions fail.

The other, call him T, thinks history is a red herring and that what does the work is the current causal-informational link together with the way the rest of the system depends on it. His answer to the disjunction problem is asymmetric dependence: the state means X rather than X-or-Y because the Y-causing link depends on the X-causing link and not the reverse. He thinks you can have content without any selection history — a molecule-for-molecule duplicate that popped into existence by accident has the same content as the original — and, more aggressively, that a state can have a long selection history and still represent nothing at all.

Now the case, which is made up, so don't go looking for it. There's an ant species where a body-surface compound, call it S, is monitored by a sensory neuron that fires when S concentration crosses a threshold. Ancestrally this is pure physiology: S is a byproduct of the ants' own water balance, the neuron firing triggers a water-retention response, and that arrangement has been under selection for a very long time. Much later in the same lineage a new behavior shows up: foragers who find food lay down S as a trail, and nestmates follow it, using the same sensory neuron and roughly the same downstream pathway, except now the firing drives trail-following instead of water retention. Then, later still, a parasitic beetle shows up that secretes S on its back, so ants pick it up and carry it into the nest, which is very bad for the ants.

Things I want them to actually fight about:

Following a trail, what does the forager's sensory state represent — S itself, the food, or just that S is high? Does it matter that the trail use is layered on top of the water-balance use rather than the other way around?

The beetle. Does the state misrepresent, and if so, misrepresent what as what? R has things to say about Normal conditions here. T should have to say whether his asymmetric-dependence story even gets off the ground when the beetle-caused link is the one the ant is currently acting on.

A duplicate ant: same wiring, assembled by a freak accident, no lineage at all. What does each of them say, and where does T's view cost him something he doesn't want to pay?

Both uses in the same individual: if the same neuron is doing water balance on Tuesday and trail-following on Wednesday, is that one state with two contents, two states, or a state whose content is the disjunction? Neither of them should get to just declare it and move on.

Rules: they respond to each other's actual points, they concede where the other has landed a good objection, and each states the other's view in its strongest form at least once. Neither of them gets to end on "well, it's indeterminate" and go home. I don't need a winner, but I do want them to say what further fact — about the ants, about the selection history, about the wiring — would settle it, and if the honest answer is that nothing empirical could settle it, say that and say why. No citations, no reading list.
