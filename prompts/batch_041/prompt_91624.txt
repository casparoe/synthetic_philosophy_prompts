Here is a claim: there exist genuine moral dilemmas, that is, situations in which an agent, through no fault of her own, is under two all-things-considered moral requirements that cannot both be fulfilled, so that she does something morally wrong no matter what she does. Give me your credence that the claim is true, and lay out the reasoning that produces it. Decompose the question into at least these terms: the probability that conflicts arise at the level of all-things-considered obligation rather than only among prima facie duties; the probability that 'ought' agglomerates, in the sense that if one ought to do A and ought to do B then one ought to do A and B; the probability that 'ought implies can' holds in the form that licenses the inference from the impossibility of doing both to the denial that one ought to do both; and the probability that, whenever a conflict appears, some tie-breaker, permission, or lexical priority resolves it so that no wrong is done either way. Assign each term a number with a single line of justification, show how the terms combine into your final figure, and then tell me which term the final number hangs on most and what argument or case would move it furthest.
