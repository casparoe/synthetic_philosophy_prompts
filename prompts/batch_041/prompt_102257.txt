A friend of mine who is finishing a dissertation in value theory recently gave me his two-minute version of the heterogeneity problem for hedonism, and I would like to know how much of it I should trust. Reconstructed as faithfully as I can manage, here is what he said:

"The hedonist says pleasure is the one thing that is good in itself. The standard objection is that the things we call pleasures have nothing in common — the pleasure of a hot bath, of finally seeing a proof click into place, of hearing that a rival has failed, of a headache lifting — there is no single feeling running through them. Bentham thought he could get around this by saying that all pleasures differ only in how much and how long, which was always absurd, so Mill patched things up by distinguishing higher and lower pleasures. What this really exposes is a dilemma: either 'pleasure' names one specific sensation, in which case hedonism is plainly false, because most of the good things in life are not that sensation; or 'pleasure' just means 'whatever a person happens to like,' in which case hedonism stops making a substantive claim and collapses into preference satisfaction. And the modern dodge — saying that a pleasure is just the attitude of liking that something is the case, rather than any sensation — is a relabeling that quietly gives up everything distinctive about the view. So nobody is a hedonist anymore: you cannot even measure pleasure, which leaves the theory with nothing to get a grip on. It is a refuted position, the way phlogiston is."

I would like an audit rather than a verdict. Take his summary apart: which of its claims a philosopher who works on hedonism would accept as a fair statement of the objection, which are compressed or subtly mistaken, and which a hedonist would reject outright. In particular, I want to understand whether the heterogeneity objection is genuinely a refutation or something weaker; whether he has correctly described what Bentham and Mill were each doing; whether the "attitudinal" account he dismisses is really the same thing as preference satisfaction; and whether the slide from "you cannot measure pleasure" to "the theory has nothing to get a grip on" is a legitimate move or a change of subject. Please do not simply assure me that hedonism is defensible. Tell me what a competent hedonist would say in response to each part, and flag anything in his summary that is actually a confusion about what the position is, as opposed to a real objection to it.
