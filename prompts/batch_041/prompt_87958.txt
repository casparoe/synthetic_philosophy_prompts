I run grantmaking at a small family foundation — about $3M a year, I'm the only program officer, and I write the one-page memo that the board signs off on every quarter. So that's thirty-odd funding decisions a year, made mostly alone, on maybe a day's notice each, no researcher, no way to trial anything.

What keeps eating at me is cluelessness. Not the ordinary 'we don't know yet' kind that more research could fix (I've seen this called simple cluelessness), but the other kind, where the ripples of a grant are so diffuse and chaotic — effects on people far downstream, on institutions, on people not born yet — that no amount of study would hand me a determinate total. I've seen that called complex cluelessness. Every framework I read either pretends the problem away by telling me to multiply made-up probabilities, which is exactly the move cluelessness is supposed to be an objection to, or it shrugs and says fund what's tangible, which is what two of my trustees already believe and I don't want to just cave to them.

So write me a procedure I can actually run, in order, on a proposal that lands on my desk. I want the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the thing terminates in a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is about values and not facts. It needs to tell me, at each stage, whether I'm looking at the fixable ignorance or the unfixable kind and what changes about my decision depending on which — including what to do when I can't even sign the effect (a grant that might help, might backfire, and the evidence won't settle which). It should say when paying for more research is worth the money and when it's just procrastination in a lab coat, when to fall back on rules like 'don't do the intrinsically skeevy thing' because the arithmetic can't guide me, and when to fund a smaller amount as a hedge instead of yes/no. And it should flag the steps that are judgement calls wearing a procedure's clothes.

Then run it on three: (1) a boring renewal — school health program, cheap, decent evidence, no controversy; (2) a new request to pay for a media-literacy curriculum in local high schools, where I genuinely can't tell if the long-run civic effect is positive; (3) a documentary my board chair is excited about, where the procedure tells me to decline and I don't want to hear it. For that last one, tell me straight, and tell me what the procedure can't do at all.
