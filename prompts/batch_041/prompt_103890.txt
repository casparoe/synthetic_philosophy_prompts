I'm a backend engineer, zero philosophy training, and for the last two years I've kept a running note in my phone of gut calls I've made at work, mostly because a friend told me my ethics were "weirdly consistent" and I wanted to check if that was true. Here's basically what's in the note, with the verdicts I actually had at the time:

- A teammate pushes a bug, I fix it silently, push it, and never mention it. Fine.
- A teammate pushes a design I think is wrong, I silently revert it. Not fine. (I know these look like the same move and I can't fully say why they aren't.)
- I read a teammate's public commits and blog to figure out their code style. Fine.
- I read a teammate's personal notes sitting in a shared drive they clearly didn't mean to share. Not fine.
- I tell a recruiter I'm "really excited about the mission" when I'm not. Fine.
- I tell a recruiter I have a competing offer when I don't. Not fine.
- I take a branded water bottle from the office kitchen. Fine. I take a monitor home. Not fine.
- I quietly clean up a mess a teammate made, before they find out they made it, so they never find out. Fine, but this is the one I flip on most.
- Same teammate's mess is about to blow up in their performance review. I can fix it silently or tell them first. I genuinely cannot decide this one and it bothers me.

My best guess at the rule behind all this: something is fine when the other person, if they later found out, could still hand me the exact same thing again without hesitating — and not fine when finding out would cost them that. Silent bug fix, they'd still trust me with bugs. Silent revert of their design, they wouldn't trust me with designs. Water bottle, sure, take another. Monitor, no. Telling the recruiter I'm hyped, whatever. Telling them about an offer I don't have, no.

But two things nag at me. First, this sounds a lot like "fine as long as I wouldn't get caught / as long as it wouldn't blow up," which as a moral principle sounds kind of gross, so tell me if that's what it actually amounts to. Second, maybe there is no rule at all and I've just been pattern-matching my moods for two years — I want to know if that's the honest answer.

So: give me the principle, or the two or three candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply them to a case you invent. For each clause, tell me which of my verdicts it's doing the work for. Tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and whether a simpler principle that drops exactly one verdict is actually better than a complicated one that saves them all. And say what it predicts for the case I can't decide, plus one case I haven't listed. Most of all I want to know where this reasoning is most likely to be wrong — what argument or consideration would flip you to a different verdict, because I'd rather find out now than in a review meeting.
