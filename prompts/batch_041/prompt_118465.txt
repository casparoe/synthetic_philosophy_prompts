Two people in my Thursday reading group, Nadia and Sam, got into an argument over a case I'd brought up, and I've been chewing on it ever since. The case: a guy, Dan, has a preference cycle. Right now he'd swap his concert ticket even-up for a museum pass, the pass even-up for a dinner reservation, and the reservation even-up for the concert ticket. So a trader holding all three can walk him around the loop — ticket for pass, pass for dinner, dinner for ticket — and land him back where he started, minus a small fee on each swap, forever.

(1) Nadia said that shows Dan's preferences are irrational, full stop: they're inconsistent, and the inconsistency is exactly what makes him exploitable. A coherent ranking can't be pumped.

(2) Sam said only a naive Dan gets pumped. A sophisticated Dan sees the loop coming and just declines. Having a preference doesn't compel you to accept a trade — he can say no to all three offers and keep his ticket. So the pump shows he'd be a fool to keep playing, not that his ranking is broken. He added that exploitability is a pragmatic fact about which offers happen to be on the table, not a property of preferences — you can build a pump for plenty of perfectly transitive people if you get to pick the fees and the timing.

(3) Nadia: if he declines, he's turning down an option he ranks higher, available at no cost, which is already acting against his own preferences. And he can't just announce a standing policy of refusing, because the trader can design the offer around whatever rule he announces, or space the three offers over three weeks so no single decision ever looks like a loop. A rational agent shouldn't need a seatbelt against himself.

(4) Sam: that treats a preference like a command. Declining a trade whose only point is to walk you in a circle is something he can endorse for his life as a whole, and endorsing it is itself a preference — so he isn't overriding anything, he's just ranking at a higher level. As for the redesigned pump: if exploitability were the test of coherence, transitivity would fail it too, so it can't be the test. And anyway transitivity is a property of one ranking; the puzzle only bites because his ranking shifts as his holdings shift, which is a differant phenomenon.

(5) Nadia: fine, drop the cycle. Take someone whose preferences are merely opposite at different times — Dan will pay five bucks on Sunday to be locked out of cigarettes and five bucks on Friday to be let back in. Same structure, no cycle at any single moment, time does the work of the loop. If Sam says that's fine, then a person can be a run of time-slices that undercut each other with no fact of the matter about what he wants, and "irrational" stops meaning anything. Either the whole life is the unit and Dan is incoherent, or each moment is the unit and nobody is ever incoherent.

(6) Sam: that's the real fork, and he'd take the moment-by-moment side, but he doesn't think it kills diachronic rationality — it just makes the requirement conditional. If you want to be a planner, someone whose life hangs together as one project (the Parfit successive-selves stuff, Bratman on being a planner), then you take on constraints like not paying twice to reverse yourself. But those are constraints you adopt for reasons, not logical consequences of having preferences at all.
