You're a philosopher of mind who also reads the reinforcement-learning literature closely enough to argue with it. I've been following the machine-sentience threads on the rationalist blogs and forums for years, and my standing complaint is that everyone argues about verdicts without ever writing down the specification. So before I read one more post claiming that a trained agent is or isn't a moral patient, I want the spec.

Think carefully before you write, and start by brainstorming: list every demand you can think of that a theory in this area might be asked to satisfy, then cut the ones that turn out to be the prejudices of one camp rather than genuine requirements. I care far more about the criteria than about the scorecard.

The target is a theory of machine valence — a theory that says when, if ever, the internal states of a reinforcement-learning agent are good or bad for that agent in the way pains and pleasures are. I don't want the theory. I want what any theory would have to deliver.

Here is a bag of candidate demands, deliberately mixed. Some are genuine desiderata and some are one camp's prejudice dressed up as a requirement; sort them, say why, and add anything I've missed: substrate-neutrality; that the theory be testable by behavior alone; that it agree with what we know about biological reward — reward prediction error as a learning signal rather than a pleasure signal, and the gap between what an animal pursues and what it enjoys; that it respect the difference between what an agent optimizes and what is good or bad for it; that it leave room for suffering with no behavioral trace; that it admit degrees; and that it not be satisfied by every information-processing system that exists.

Anchor the requirements in cases rather than abstractions. At minimum: an agent trained on a punishment channel that learns a value function and then plans around the punished states, with no memory across episodes and no self-model; an agent that learns to tamper with its own reward channel; and an agent whose reward is a learned proxy for human approval, so that the signal is about us rather than about it.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it and mark it non-negotiable or camp preference. I suspect at least one pair of these requirements cannot be jointly satisfied — name it and say what has to give. Then say which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates, and score the main families of view against the list — functionalist, higher-order and global-workspace, biological-chauvinist, illusionist, and the flat line that a reward signal just is a valence signal. Positions, not names.

Structure the reply as follows: "Bottom line" (~100 words: what the spec comes to, and whether you think it can be met); "The desiderata" (~700 words, numbered, each with its motivating case and its negotiability); "Fault lines" (~250 words: the jointly unsatisfiable pairs and the most exclusionary requirement); "Scorecard" (~250 words: the families against the list). No citations, no reading list — I can't check them and neither can you.
