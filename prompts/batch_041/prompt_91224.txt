I am a novelist, and I am assembling a collection I have taken to calling bureaucratic apocrypha: institutional documents — review files, minutes, memoranda, denial letters, screening forms — written entirely straight, about events that institutions are constitutively unable to process. The comedy, where there is comedy, has to come from the document's competence rather than its stupidity. The office should be diligent, well drafted, and humane in tone, and it should nevertheless be unable to reach the thing it is adjudicating. I would like you to draft one of these, at roughly nine hundred words, with headed sections.

The event is the binding of Isaac, read against the account of Abraham in Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling (1843, published under the pseudonym Johannes de Silentio) and its question of a teleological suspension of the ethical. I am not asking for an essay about that book. I am asking for a modern case file in which a contemporary institution reviews the incident, and in which the philosophical machinery does its work inside the document rather than above it.

The document is an internal review by a body whose remit is the prevention of private violence committed under the cover of an unwitnessed command. Give the body a name that sounds like a real one — no puns, no winks — and let its founding principle, stated once in the front matter, be the position you are satirising: that whatever cannot be justified before a public tribunal of reasons may not be done. Set the file in the present. No one in it may remark that the case is ancient, that the parties are scriptural, or that anything about the matter is strange; the office handles such referrals routinely.

I would like the file to contain the following, in this order. A referral summary and a risk-scoring page, whose categories — capacity, insight, motivation, risk to self and other, the subject's understanding of the wrongness of the act — are exactly the ones that cannot get purchase on the case, and which the file treats as an ordinary gap in the evidence. Two or three excerpts from the interview, in which the interviewer asks precisely the questions the office must ask: what was said, in what words, in whose voice, who else heard it, what was to be gained, what was expected to happen at the end. The subject answers with silence, or with sentences that are formally responsive and materially useless; he is courteous, and he never sounds like the author. A cross-reference to an earlier, similar referral in another jurisdiction, which the office reads as evidence of a pattern, and whose disposition differed only because the outcome did.

Then the memorandum of the theological consultant, which is where the precision belongs. He should lay out, in his own vocabulary and without quoting anything, the distinction between the ethical as the universal — binding on everyone, statable in general terms, and therefore the only register in which a deed can be justified or mourned — and the position of the single individual who is addressed alone. He should distinguish the subject from the recognisable tragic hero, the commander who sacrifices a daughter so the fleet may sail or the judge who executes his sons for the republic, who gives up what he loves for a purpose the community can state and can grieve, and who can therefore speak. The subject cannot, and the memo should locate the reason for his silence in that fact rather than in secrecy or shame. He should name the two movements: the resignation that gives the beloved up in this life, and the further movement, by virtue of the absurd, that receives him back. And he should raise, without settling, the objection that the structure of the subject's warrant is formally indistinguishable from the warrant claimed by a deluded or malignant man, and that nothing available to the office sorts them. Finally, the finding, in the office's voice: unqualified, properly reasoned, adverse, with the ordinary remedy attached.

The consultant must not be your mouthpiece and must not resolve anything. He should concede that on any public standard the act is attempted murder, admit that he cannot perform the movement he describes, and end by observing that the office's question is well formed and unanswerable. The office must not become wise at the end, and no one may step outside the document to address the reader. When the file closes, I want the reader to see two things at once: that the office had no alternative, and that its finding does not reach the thing it condemns.

No citations, no bibliography, and no quotations from Kierkegaard; the consultant writes in his own words. Do not let the humour announce itself.
