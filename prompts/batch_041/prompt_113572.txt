I teach the second half of a graduate seminar on rational choice and epistemology, and I'm writing the final exam. My old question on the common prior assumption has stopped discriminating: students recite that Aumann showed rational agents can't agree to disagree, then pad for a page. What sent me back to the drawing board was an argument I had last week with a friend who works in finance; he insisted that because of Aumann's theorem, any time two people look at the same data and reach different conclusions, at least one of them must be irrational — that, he said, is just what Bayesian rationality means. I think he is folding together three separate claims that need to be pulled apart, and I want the exam to make students do that work rather than me.

Draft one essay question, answerable in 90 minutes, that cannot be passed by simply stating the agreement theorem. It should force students to (a) state precisely what Aumann's result assumes and what it concludes, (b) explain why it does not license the claim that disagreement is irrational, (c) say what work the common prior assumption is doing and whether it is a requirement of rationality or a substantive assumption about where beliefs come from, and (d) work through a case in which two agents' posteriors are common knowledge but their priors differ and are common knowledge as well — does the theorem say anything here, and does this case refute the slogan that rational agents cannot agree to disagree?

Then give me a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme worth 25 points that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain, with a one-line note on how candidates typically get each wrong. Please write in American English.
