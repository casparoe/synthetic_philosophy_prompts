Our school's philosophy society has given me the forty-minute slot next Thursday, and I've said I'll do indicative versus counterfactual conditionals. I'd like you to plan how I should teach it, not to explain the distinction to me — I can state it if pushed, and that's precisely the problem. There'll be about fifteen of us, a whiteboard and no slides, and the audience is an awkward middle: they have all met truth tables in maths, so they will walk in assuming that 'if' is a truth function and that a conditional is false only when the antecedent is true and the consequent false, but none of them has done any linguistics or any formal semantics, and several will be quietly convinced that this is a grammar lesson. So: the order the pieces have to go in and why that order rather than another, what depends on what, the single case or pair of cases that should carry the whole session and what it must have and lack, the misconceptions I should expect with the exact point in the sequence at which each gets headed off, and one question I can put to them at the end that would show the distinction has actually landed rather than merely been recited back.

The pair I'd drafted to build the session around is one I wrote on the bus: 'If the coach leaves at seven, we'll be in York by ten' and 'If the coach had left at seven, we'd have been in York by ten.' My worry is that it varies two things at once — the time of the scenario and whether the antecedent is being taken as false — so if they feel a difference between the two sentences they will have no way of telling which contrast is doing the work, and I'll have taught them something other than what I meant to. Tell me whether it can bear the weight with a tweak, what a replacement would have to have and lack, and which single example you would actually build on. I'm also expecting a cluster of misconceptions, and I want to know which are real, which I would merely be planting by mentioning them, and where each belongs: that the counterfactual is the one whose antecedent is false; that the past tense in something like 'if it rained tomorrow' means the rain is past, or that it signals the speaker thinks it won't rain; that the whole contrast is one of politeness, hedging or likelihood; that the indicative is the logical one and the counterfactual the loose everyday one; and that both are just material implication with something bolted on. I'd like the plan to finish on a live puzzle rather than on false comfort — they should leave with a question they cannot yet answer — and to be explicit about what I should refuse to go into at this level and say out loud that I am refusing. Please write it in British English, as I'll be circulating it to the others and our society's style is British.
