My housemates have been circling eachother for two weeks over a bylaw our city council is considering, and I've somehow ended up as the referee, so I'd like an outside opinion before I have to actually say something at dinner on Friday. The bylaw would shut down a members-only club on the east side. Adults pay dues and take part in what's basically theater: members volunteer to be "auctioned off" and "owned" for an evening — costumes, contracts written out on parchment, elaborate rules, and anyone can walk out at any moment and apparently everyone does, without complaint. Nothing sexual is required, no money changes hands except the dues, nobody gets physically hurt, and there's a waiting list. The council's stated reason isn't that anything bad has happened there; it's that the club is a public celebration of treating people as property, and the city shouldn't be seen to permit that. It's basically the old Hart–Devlin fight again, and my two housemates have reproduced the whole thing in my kitchen.

Nadia is for the ban. (1) Her first move: a society is held together by a shared moral life, not just by a police force, and law is one of the ways a society keeps that life alive; a city that shrugs at a public club devoted to "owning" people is telling everyone that the norm against it is optional. (2) Sam said that's an empirical prediction nobody has ever tested — moral liberalization on a dozen fronts hasn't dissolved anything — and that the "shared morality" Nadia wants to protect is just the fact that most people disapprove, so her principle licenses any majority to criminalize any disliked minority. (3) Nadia said she isn't appealing to disapproval; she'd happily concede that disgust is not a reason, and she'd keep the law out of genuinely private conduct. Her claim is narrower: the law may protect a shared practice of mutual respect, and she'd set the threshold high and demand a real consensus rather than a manufactured one. (4) Sam said fine, but look at what's actually doing the work — the act gets banned because its meaning contradicts a norm most people hold. Strip the rhetoric and that's legal moralism with a publicity filter: norms protected because they're norms. And the error costs are asymmetric. If the ban is wrong, real people's lives get wrecked; if the permission is wrong, some people are offended. So the burden should sit almost entirely on the ban. (5) Nadia came back with two things. First, Sam's harm principle can't be applied without begging the question, because "harm" is contested: if the club's existence makes life harder for people who have actually been trafficked — who now get to watch their worst experience staged as a hobby — that's a harm even though nothing is broken. Second, the club doesn't stay in its box: ex-members say the role-play changed how they imagine other people, and a city may legislate against a practice that cultivates a habit of seeing persons as things. Sam said that's speculation plus a slippery slope, and by that standard you'd have to ban violent games and half the internet, which Nadia doesn't want. Nadia said she's happy to defend each case on its merits, and that the point isn't a general rule but that a city has a legitimate interest in its moral environment the way it has an interest in clean air — you don't have to name the person whose lungs are damaged to justify stopping someone from dumping. Sam said the pollution analogy does too much work: air is a resource whose value doesn't depend on anyone's moral views, whereas the "moral environment" is only a resource if you've already decided the current majority norms are worth keeping. If the shared moral currency loses value, the only person harmed is someone who already accepted it at face value — that's nostalgia, not harm. Then Sam proposed a test — a law is justified only if it can point to a burden that would still be a burden to someone who rejected the majority's moral outlook entirely — and Nadia proposed her own, that a law is legitimate if an ordinary decent person, after hearing the strongest case for the practice, still finds it a serious wrong. Sam says Nadia's test just launders majority feeling; Nadia says Sam's test can't be applied by anyone, since nobody stands outside their own moral outlook.

I've been staying out of it, but I'm expected to say something on Friday and "you're both right" isn't going to fly. Can you go through the exchange move by move — say which claims are sound, which are overstated, and what each of them is missing — and then say who's closer to right overall? I'd also like to know whether either of the two tests at the end is actually workable, or whether the standard has to be something neither of them has said yet.
