I am an actuary on the intergenerational-risk desk of a pension fund, and for some months now a close friend who works in moral and political philosophy has been trying to bring me round on the question of inheritance tax. I follow the arguments well enough in the abstract, but I keep wanting to re-express the whole business in the idiom I actually think in — discount rates and present values, compounding, expected utility and risk aversion, cohort effects, stochastic processes that start from different initial conditions, option value, tax incidence and deadweight loss. My suspicion is that forcing the debate through that vocabulary will show me which parts of the disagreement are substantive and which are merely a difference of framing, and I would like you to be scrupulously honest about that rather than flatter my idiom. In particular, my friend keeps insisting that equal opportunity means the race must not be decided at the starting gun, and I want that made precise: does it require that a person's outcomes be statistically independent of their parents' endowment, merely uncorrelated in the mean, or something weaker still, and which of those readings does the underlying moral claim actually need?

Please restate the principal positions in the debate over inheritance tax, dynastic wealth and equal opportunity — the broadly libertarian case that a bequest is simply the transfer of justly held property and that taxing it wrongs someone; the broadly Rawlsian case that inherited advantage defeats fair equality of opportunity; the public-finance case that we should care chiefly about behavioural distortion and the optimal rate; and any view that treats dynastic wealth as a self-reinforcing compounding process — as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary, being careful not to smuggle in my framework's assumptions and call that translation. Then give me the residue report: what carries over without loss, what carries over only with distortion (say exactly what has to be bent to make it fit), and what has no counterpart in my idiom at all — and, for that last category, tell me whether the gap shows that my vocabulary is missing something or that the original claim was less clear than it looked.

Please give the answer as exactly five numbered points, each no more than a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion, and write it in British English.
