ok so im working my way through Aquinas on killing — one of those old public domain translations — and the article on self-defence has me stuck. Heres the paragraph I keep rereading:

> Nothing hinders one act from having two effects, only one of which is intended, while the other is beside the intention. Now moral acts take their species according to what is intended, and not according to what is beside the intention... Accordingly the act of self-defense may have two effects, one is the saving of one's life, the other is the slaying of the aggressor. Therefore this act, since one's intention is to save one's own life, is not unlawful... And yet, though proceeding from a good intention, an act may be rendered unlawful, if it be out of proportion to the end. Wherefore if a man, in self-defense, uses more than necessary violence, it will be unlawful: whereas if he repel force with moderation his defense will be lawful... Nor is it necessary for salvation that a man omit the act of moderate self-defense in order to avoid killing the other man, since one is bound to take more care of one's own life than of another's. But as it is unlawful to take a man's life, except for the public authority acting for the common good, as stated above (A[3]), it is not lawful for a man to intend killing a man in self-defense, except for such as have public authority...

Two things are tripping me up. First, if the intention is what gives the act its species, and my intention in defending myself is fine, then why is it suddenly not lawful for me to intend killing the man when the killing is precisely whats saving my life? Does "intend" here mean something narrower, like aiming at it as an end in itself, as opposed to choosing it knowing it has to happen? Second, the line about the act being unlawful if its out of proportion to the end — is that doing the same work as the modern rule that you cant use someone's death as a means to save others, or is it a seperate constraint that just happens to sit next to it? A friend told me the "species" talk is only about how you classify the act, not about whether its permissible, and I cant tell if thats a real distinction in the text or him importing modern jargon.

The case I keep testing all this against, since it seems to break the intended/foreseen split: a runaway trolley is headed at five people. I can flip a switch that sends it down a side track, but that side track loops back and rejoins the main line just before the five, and theres one person standing on the side track. If I flip the switch, the trolley hits him and his body stops it, so the five live; if I do nothing, the five die. So his death isnt a side effect of saving them — its the lever that saves them. Does Aquinas's two-effects machinery have any room for that case, or does it only work when the bad effect is genuinely off to the side? And would he say my intention in flipping the switch is to kill him (which he says is off limits) or to save the five (which he says is fine)?

I dont need the secondary literature or who said what about this later — just help me parse whats actually going on in this paragraph, in plain language, and tell me where I'm reading modern double-effect doctrine into a text that might not have it.
