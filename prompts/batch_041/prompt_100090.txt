I built the following argument for a reading group that mixes epistemologists with people who know complexity theory, and I have come to suspect that it proves too much. I cannot decide whether it is invalid or whether it is valid and I simply have to pay the conclusion. I would like a diagnosis.

The argument:

(1) For an agent to be justified in believing that p, the agent must be in a position to determine whether p is true.
(2) To determine whether p is true is to execute a computational procedure that returns a correct verdict on p.
(3) A great many propositions we take ourselves to be justified in believing correspond, on this reading, to intractable problems: whether a given logical formula is satisfiable, whether a given planning problem has a solution within a given length, whether a given set of constraints is consistent, whether a given program halts. These problems are NP-complete, NP-hard, PSPACE-hard, or undecidable.
(4) If P ≠ NP, no procedure running in polynomial time decides an NP-hard problem correctly on every input; the harder classes are worse, and the undecidable ones admit no deciding procedure at all.
(5) Every physically realizable procedure is bounded, in time and memory, by a polynomial function of its input size: a finite system has finite resources, and there is no physical operation by which a bounded system outruns a polynomial bound.
(6) Therefore, if P ≠ NP — and a fortiori if the harder classes are as we think — no physically realizable agent can determine the truth of the propositions in (3).
(7) By (1), no such agent is justified in believing them.
(8) But we are plainly justified in believing at least some of them: I am justified in thinking that this small satisfiability instance is satisfiable, that my itinerary is feasible, that the toy program I just traced halts.
(9) Therefore either P = NP, or (1) is false.

I want to keep (8). I am not going to become a skeptic about whether my itinerary is feasible, and (9) is absurd, so if something has to go it will be (1) or (5). Tell me where this goes wrong, or whether it does.

If you think a premise or step is faulty, name it, explain why it looked true, and price the alternatives: for each candidate repair, say what else it forces me to surrender, and whether the repair remains available to someone who wants to keep both (8) and the standard conjectures of complexity theory. If instead the argument is valid and the conclusion is the real cost, say so, and tell me which of (1) or (5) is the cheaper thing to abandon. Be explicit about how confident you are in your diagnosis, and identify the single step in your own reasoning that you are least sure of.
