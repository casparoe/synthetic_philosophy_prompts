Ok, so I keep having the same arguement with my officemate and I want to actually settle what I think. I'm a 4th-year in phil of sci who mostly works on models in econ/epi, and here are my gut calls on when a false assumption in a model is fine:

Frictionless plane: fine. I know exactly how to put friction back and the answer moves continuously.
Infinite population in a pop-gen model: fine, same reason, finite N is just a perturbation.
Spherical cow: fine. It's a joke but it's a real method — I'm after a qualitative result and the falsity is doing no work.
Ideal gas: fine.
Well-mixed SIR whose herd-immunity threshold goes straight into a policy memo: the model is fine, using the number that way is not, and I feel like the difference is the thing I'm trying to name.
Representative agent in macro: not fine, even though it's also just a false assumption that makes the math go.
Common knowledge of rationality in a one-shot game among real people: fine as a benchmark, not fine when the paper's claim is about the real people.
Transitive, stable preferences to run a welfare calc: fine until someone cites the calc in a policy context, then not fine.
A model of discrimination that assumes no discrimination and concludes the data show no discrimination: not fine, obviously, and I think it's a different kind of bad from the others.
A model stuffed with free parameters that have no interpretation, which nonetheless predicts well out of sample: I want to say this is fine and it bothers me, because it's the one case where I can't say anything at all about the assumptions.

My tentative story is: an idealization is ok iff you can specify the de-idealization — say what happens as the false assumption gets relaxed — and the thing you care about is continuous in that relaxation, or at least you know exactly where it isn't. But that doesn't get the representative agent (I can relax it, I just don't like where it goes) and it doesn't get the tuned model (no relaxation to speak of, and I still want to complain).

So is there a principle that gets these, or close? Don't hand me a taxonomy of the options and let me pick — tell me which one you'd actually defend, state it precisely, say what each clause is doing, and tell me which of my calls it forces me to throw out. Also say what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: assuming a continuum of agents when the real population is 30 people, which I genuinely don't know what to think about.
