For some weeks now a friend and I have been circling a move that we both keep making in ethical arguments and neither of us can name. The shape of it is this. Someone proposes a rule and asks whether it could be willed as a rule for everyone. Set 'never break a promise' against 'break your promises whenever keeping them becomes inconvenient.' Universalize the second and you seem to get a world in which promising has collapsed, so the very act of breaking a promise has nothing left to be, and the rule fails the test; universalize the first and nothing obviously goes wrong. But now write the second rule more narrowly — 'break this promise, to this person, on this occasion, in order to avert something worse' — and the universal version is merely a world in which a great many small predicaments get handled pragmatically, which is not incoherent at all. Same action, same agent, same test; the verdict turns entirely on how finely one chops up what is being universalized. And in the arguments I keep having, whoever is attacking a rule chops coarsely, whoever is defending it chops finely, and neither party admits to having chosen a description.

There is a second version of the same unease, and I cannot tell whether it is the same problem or a different one. When I ask whether I could will that everyone act as I am about to act, I am never sure which world I am being asked to imagine: one in which everyone in my exact situation does this, or one in which everyone everywhere has adopted this as a standing policy. Those two worlds give different verdicts whenever my action depends on most other people not doing the same thing — queuing, voting, overfishing, taking the last seat on the train — and the test seems to slide between the two depending on which answer is wanted.

What I want to know is whether any of this has a name. I suspect it sits near the categorical imperative, and I am fairly sure it is not simply the Golden Rule, but I am not after an exposition of either doctrine; I want to know whether the specific difficulty of fixing the level of description is a recognised problem with a settled label, or whether it is usually handled as two separate complaints with no single name. So: if there is a name, give it to me and say precisely which parts of what I have described it covers and which it leaves out, comparing it to the nearest things it might be confused with — the zealot who universalizes his own ruthlessness without flinching, the charge that treating rules as sacrosanct is a kind of fetish, and whatever the philosophy of action calls the business of describing what someone did at the right grain. Tell me also whether Kant's several formulations of the imperative all face the same version of this difficulty or whether the level-of-description problem bites one of them harder than the others. If nothing established fits, then coin a term and define it, and give me the boundary cases: one where the verdict flips and should count as the same phenomenon, and one nearby where it flips but should not.
