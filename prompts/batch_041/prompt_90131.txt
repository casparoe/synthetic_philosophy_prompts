Here's something I keep noticing in my own city and can't square. For the big constitutional stuff we run a citizens' assembly — like a hundred people pulled at random off the electoral roll, they hear from experts, deliberate for a few weekends, and whatever they land on gets adopted (or at least put to a binding vote). For the smaller stuff — a tax levy, a zoning change, renaming a street — we just hold a referendum, simple majority, whoever bothers to show up. And then either way the mayor gets on TV and says "the people have spoken."

What I want to know is what each of these two practices is presupposing about "the will of the people." The referendum seems to assume there's a will already sitting out there, one person one vote, and the count just reveals it. The assembly seems to assume there's no such will until people actually talk it through, and that the talking changes what's there rather than uncovering it. Can both be true at once? Does one of them quietly rule the other out, or are they just answers to different questions? And when the mayor says "the people have spoken" after a 52–48 result, what is he actually committing himself to — that the 52% just is the will, that there's a will and this is our best available proxy for it, or that the whole phrase is theater that presupposes nothing at all? Tell me what these practices strictly have to presuppose, what they'd survive without, and what would have to change if you swapped in the opposite view.
