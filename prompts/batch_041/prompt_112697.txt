I'm running a reading group next week on All Quiet on the Western Front, and I want to pair it with the literature arguing that violence has been declining for centuries. So read the novel through that debate. Remarque gives you one cohort, one front, four years, and treats the slaughter as a rupture; the trend thesis is about rates across millennia and claims we're living in the calmest stretch ever. My suspicion is that the two aren't even describing the same object, and I want you to test that against the book. Push on at least three things: whether what fell was violence or just lethality (a gut-shot man in 1916 dies, the same man in 2016 gets evacuated and lives -- did that encounter stop being violence because he survived?); per capita vs absolute, and what the denominator is even supposed to be; and whether the trend is partly an artifact of the state getting better at counting and classifying, so that colonial wars, famines, deaths in custody, anything falling outside the operative category, quietly drop off the graph. Then the harder one -- does violence name a natural kind or a moralized cluster concept, and if its the latter, is there a fact of the matter about the trend at all, or only rival stipulations? Don't just use the novel to illustrate: I want the places where it resists the framing, a verdict on what a novel can show here that a table of rates can't, and which scenes you'd hand the group to argue about.
