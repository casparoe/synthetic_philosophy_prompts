So I moved six months ago and it's the first local election where I actually know anything about the candidates, and now I've got a mail ballot sitting on the kitchen counter that I keep picking up and putting back down. The incumbent on the city council is basically a bagman, everybody knows about the developer stuff, and the challenger is clean as far as I can tell but she wants to gut the library budget and cut the arts program at my kid's school. My roommate and I have been arguing about this at dinner for two weeks and I want to actually hear the best version of the argument instead of us just rehashing it.

So write me a dialogue between two people who disagree about it. Not a debate where one of them is there to lose — two sharp people who've each thought about this more than I have.

One of them (call her Dara) thinks there's no duty to vote at all, and more than that, that voting for the lesser evil is actually worse than leaving the bubble blank — that it launders your complicity, that it teaches parties they can win by being merely less bad, and that if you wouldn't be willing to be the sole reason this person got the seat, then voting for them is a way of getting the outcome while dodging the responsibility for it.

The other (call him Miguel) thinks abstention isn't innocence, that the lesser evil really is less evil and real people get hurt by the worse one, that refusing to pick is still picking only with your hands rhetorically clean, and that you take benefits from the system so you owe it something.

Neither one should be a dummy. Miguel can't just be "civic duty, rah," and Dara can't just be "both sides are the same." They should actually answer each other — pick up the other's specific point and turn it over, concede where they have to, and press on the spots where the other's view gets uncomfortable. Let them get into the weeds: whether a vote is best understood as a cause or as a signal, whether abstaining is itself a speech act or just an absence, whether a voter is more like a juror (an office with a duty of care) or a shopper (no office, no duty), the difference between what one vote causes and what the practice of people like you voting causes, and whether "clean hands" is a moral position or a vanity.

Please don't reach for the worn-out hypotheticals — no "what if one candidate is literally Hitler," no trolleys. Use ordinary stuff: a corrupt councilman, a library-cutting reformer, the judge retention question on the back of the ballot, whatever fits.

At least four or five exchanges each, so it's a real back-and-forth and not two monologues stapled together. It's fine if they land somewhere in between or if they just sharpen the disagreement, but don't tie it off with a bow or have somebody suddenly see the light. I'd rather this get too subtle than too obvious. 900-1200 words, and American English please.
