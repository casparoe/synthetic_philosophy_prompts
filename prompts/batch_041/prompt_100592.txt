Read The Island of Doctor Moreau through the philosophical literature on the species problem. Wells's Moreau is a physiologist who remakes living beasts into human shape and then holds them to the result by a code of prohibitions that the Beast Folk recite and dread; his creatures are hybrids who revert, and the narrator, back in England, finds he can no longer see other men as anything but animals. I want a reading that does justice to both the novel and the problem.

Assume the following as the philosophical frame. The species problem is that no single species concept sorts all of life the same way: reproductive isolation, common ancestry and monophyly, ecological role, and morphological similarity each break down somewhere — on asexual organisms, on hybrids, on ring species. Alongside this sits the anti-essentialist view associated with Ghiselin and Hull, that species are not kinds but individuals: particular, spatiotemporally extended lineages rather than classes with members, so that a species name behaves more like a proper name than a common noun, and a species has no defining essence to be discovered. Against both stands the stubborn essentialism of ordinary thought, which treats each kind of organism as having a hidden nature that makes it what it is.

Then ask: does Moreau's island dramatize the collapse of that essentialism, or does it quietly depend on it? Where does the novel's handling of hybridity and reversion support the anti-essentialist, species-as-individuals picture, and where does it undercut that picture by implying that some underlying animal nature reasserts itself, so that the line between human and beast is a discovery rather than a convention? Note that Moreau enforces his code by terror, which makes the species boundary look normative before it is natural; say whether that is Wells's point or your own imposition. Identify the scenes that carry the weight of your reading. Where the novel resists the reading, say so plainly: do not reduce the book to an illustration of a thesis, and do not bend the philosophy to fit the book. Conclude with what the novel shows about the species problem that the arguments alone do not. No quotations from the novel are needed; I care about the reading, not the text.
