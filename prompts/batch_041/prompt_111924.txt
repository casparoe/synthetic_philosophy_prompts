I am a retired behavioral biologist — my working life was spent on the sensory guidance of insect locomotion — and I have spent the last several years reading philosophy on my own, which I mention only so that you will not overestimate what I already know. There is a notion in the philosophy of mind that I keep circling, called sphexishness. It takes its name from the digger wasp that, having paralyzed its prey and dragged it to the mouth of the burrow, leaves it there while it inspects the interior; if the prey is displaced a few inches during the inspection, the wasp carries it back to the mouth and inspects again, and by some accounts can be made to repeat the cycle indefinitely. The wasp is offered as the image of behavior that is fixed, unrevisable, and in that sense mindless, and the term is then turned on us, to ask whether anything in our own conduct is different. I should say at once that I have doubts about the anecdote itself — I have never seen the loop run as cleanly as it is reported, and it would not surprise me if the repeated inspection were simply the right thing for the animal to do — but my question does not depend on the wasp.

What interests me is a difficulty that the story is used to raise and, in my reading, rarely carries through. Any finite creature must have a finite repertoire of responses; any finite repertoire can be written down as a system of rules; and so the wasp's rigidity and ours would seem to differ only in the length of the list and the intricacy of the rules that generate it. Yet we want to say that the wasp is not merely less flexible than we are but inflexible in a different way, and that the charge of being mechanical applies to it and not to us. Give me an example of a conceptual issue — one definite puzzle, worked out rather than gestured at — that arises here. I am not asking for the familiar question of whether a machine could think; I want something narrower, and I would be glad if it concerned the way a rule may be applied flexibly at one level while remaining fixed at another, since that is where I lose my footing. Show me why the issue is not merely a matter of terminology, what the live answers to it are, and what, if anything, it implies about whether our own flexibility differs in kind from the wasp's or only in degree.

If I have set this up badly — if flexibility as I have used it does not name a property that a system can have more or less of, or if the picture of nested rules is confused in some way I have not noticed — then say so plainly and explain why, rather than supplying an example that quietly accepts my framing. I would rather be told that the question is malformed than be handed a well-shaped answer to a bad question.
