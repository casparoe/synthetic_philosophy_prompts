I am a high school senior, the editor of our school's science-and-society journal, and a member of the debate team. In both roles I keep encountering claims about heritability: that intelligence is roughly seventy percent heritable, that height is roughly eighty percent heritable, that depression is roughly forty percent heritable, that because a trait is heritable it cannot be changed by schools or therapy, and that because a trait is heritable within a group, differences between groups must also be genetic. Our debate team is preparing for a tournament on whether districts should use polygenic scores for advanced-course placement, and my column has to evaluate these claims on a weekly deadline. I need a reusable procedure, not an essay.

Please turn what heritability actually means, and what it does not mean, into a decision procedure I can run in about twenty minutes, without internet access or the original studies, using only a headline, a classmate's summary, or a dataset description. The procedure should be a checklist or flowchart in words: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops because the claim cannot be evaluated, where it stops with a clear no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values or missing data rather than statistics. It must keep the distinctions that matter: heritability as a proportion of variance in a population versus a claim about an individual's trait; heritability of a trait versus heritability of a difference between groups; heritability as dependent on the range of environments studied and on reaction norms; gene-environment correlation and interaction; the assumptions behind twin and family studies, including equal environments and representativeness; the difference between twin-based heritability and SNP-based heritability; broad-sense versus narrow-sense heritability; additive genetic variance versus non-additive effects; and the difference between explaining variation and predicting the effect of an intervention. It should say which of these distinctions can be dropped when writing for a general audience and which must survive. It should be honest about where a number is being used as a rhetorical prop rather than evidence.

My constraints: I have AP Statistics but not genetics or advanced psychometrics; my faculty advisor, who teaches statistics, has to sign off before publication; I cannot run code or consult paywalled papers; and the procedure has to be short enough to use during layout. It should include a one-page field version for the column and a slightly longer technical appendix for the debate team. Please mark the points where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment, and explain what a user should do when the answer to a step is unknown.

Then apply the procedure to at least three cases: (1) a classmate argues that because intelligence is about seventy percent heritable, schools cannot do much to raise achievement; (2) a headline reports that height is about eighty percent heritable and concludes that a basketball program should screen children genetically; and (3) a student argues that because test scores are highly heritable within our district, the gap between our district and a poorer neighboring district must be genetic. Include a fourth case if it helps, such as a proposal to use polygenic scores for course placement. At least one application should give an answer I will not like: either it should block a conclusion I was hoping to draw against a heritability claim, or it should show that a claim I want to debunk cannot be debunked without environmental data the procedure does not have. End with a short section on what the procedure cannot do, where it is most likely to misfire, and how I should phrase the limits in the column so that I do not overstate what the science says. No citations, reading lists, or bibliographies are needed; I need the reasoning and the procedure in plain language.
