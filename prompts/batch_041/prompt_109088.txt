Two colleagues on the deployment-safety team I work with spent a meeting arguing about where our remaining effort should go, and neither of them persuaded me. I would like you to sort out who is right about which points.

Priya argues that since we cannot establish that the model is aligned, we should build the deployment so that a misaligned model cannot do catastrophic damage even if it is actively trying: a second, weaker model reads the strong model's reasoning traces and flags suspicious ones for human review; flagged outputs get rewritten before anyone sees them; the strong model can take no consequential action without a human in the loop; and the whole arrangement is tested by a red team instructed to play the part of a scheming model. Her claim is that this argument never requires knowing whether the model is aligned, because it is an argument about the architecture rather than about the model.

Marcus objects in four steps. (1) Every layer she describes bottoms out in something you have to trust anyway — the weak monitor, the human reviewers, the logging, the harness that implements the protocol — so the real choice is between trusting the model and trusting a set of things that are weaker, less inspected, and easier to manipulate. (2) The red-team test is circular: the only thing that could play the schemer well enough to be informative is the model itself, and a scheming model has no interest in showing you how it would escape. (3) Protocols like these make a system uncompetitive against labs that skip them, so they will be adopted precisely where they do not matter. (4) So the safety argument is not about the architecture; it is a claim about what a red team failed to imagine.

Priya answers (1) by saying the aim was never to eliminate trust but to shrink it to something small, boring, and too weak to model the situation well enough to collude; (2) by saying such a test does not need a smarter red team, only one with the model's weights and more compute than the deployment grants, and that being unable to run the test is itself a finding; and (3) by saying the same objection would sink any safety measure a single lab adopts voluntarily, which argues for regulation rather than against the measure. She had no answer to (4).

Please adjudicate move by move: say who has the better of each exchange, rating each from clearly right to clearly mistaken, and identify what, if anything, both are missing. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
