I teach a third-year metaphysics seminar on dispositions and laws of nature, and I am drafting the final examination. I want one essay question on the conditional analysis of dispositions, and I want it to be a question that cannot be answered by reciting the standard catalogue of counterexamples. My students can already reproduce, on demand, the usual material — finkish cases, in which the trigger removes the disposition or its causal basis, and masking cases, in which the manifestation is blocked while the disposition and its basis survive — and they can rehearse the usual repair, on which an object is disposed to manifest M in response to a stimulus C just in case it possesses some intrinsic causal basis such that, were the basis and C both to obtain, M would occur. What they cannot yet do is adjudicate a case they have not seen before. That is what the question should make them do.

The case I want the question built around is one I have invented, and as far as I know it does not appear in the literature.

X is a lump of material whose microstructure is, in the relevant respect, exactly the kind that ignites on impact: it is the same kind of microstructure as that of an ordinary match-head compound. But X's microstructure also has a second feature. The instant X is struck, it releases a gas that suppresses any flame at X's surface, and the release is faster than ignition would be. The gas leaves the ignition-capable part of X's microstructure untouched, and if the gas-releasing feature were somehow disabled while the ignition-capable microstructure was left intact, X would ignite when struck. Both features are intrinsic to X, neither ever fails, and nothing outside X ever interferes.

I want the question to require four things of the student. First, a verdict on whether X is disposed to ignite when struck, with a defence of it. Second, an account of what the case shows about the simple conditional analysis. Third, an assessment of whether the basis-preserving repair survives the case, including whether it matters that the interfering feature is intrinsic to X rather than an external circumstance. Fourth, an engagement with the temptation to rescue the analysis by adding a clause to the effect that the manifestation occurs provided nothing interferes, and an explanation of why that clause either trivialises the analysis or makes it untestable. I should also like the question to require the student to distinguish this case from a finkish case and from a case in which the behaviour would be produced even though the disposition is absent, so that I can see whether they can tell the kinds of case apart rather than merely applying labels to them.

Please write the examination question — the students have fifty minutes and a limit of 800 words — together with a model answer of about 800 words and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves a strong answer must make and the specific errors that ought to cost marks. In the model answer, take a definite position: state whether X has the disposition, and state whether any counterfactual analysis of dispositions can be made to work, and argue for that position, rather than surveying the options and leaving the verdict to me. There is no need for citations, a reading list, or a bibliography; I am interested in the argument, not the sources.
