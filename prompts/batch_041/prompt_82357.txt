I am a second-year doctoral student, and I need to turn a half-formed idea into a short research proposal — roughly four pages — for a workshop our department runs on decision theory and rationality. The audience will be mixed, economists and philosophers, and I have twenty minutes to present, so the document has to carry a single sharp question that people can disagree about, not a survey of the field. I have no need of a bibliography or a reading list; I will attach citations myself once the argument is fixed, and I would rather the proposal not lean on any particular piece of literature for its framing.

The idea I want to turn into a proposal is this. In the tradition running back to Herbert Simon, "satisficing" is used for at least three quite different things, and I suspect only one of them is a genuine alternative to maximizing. First, there is the search rule: stop at the first option that clears an aspiration level. Second, there is the cost-sensitive rule: stop when the expected gain from further search no longer covers its cost — but this is simply maximizing over a wider domain, so it is a special case of the orthodoxy rather than a rival to it, and its defenders should stop advertising it as a departure from rationality. Third, and this is the reading I want to develop, there is the case of incomplete or incommensurable preferences: where one cannot rank the options at all, an aspiration level is not a proxy for a value one already holds but a rule for acting in the absence of a ranking. My tentative thesis is that only the third reading gives satisficing a distinct and defensible normative standing, and that once it is read that way the interesting question shifts from which option one chooses to when one stops deliberating: satisficing becomes a stopping rule for inquiry rather than for choice. I am aware this may be a distinction in search of a difference, and I would rather discover that in the proposal than after a year of work.

Please write the proposal. It should state the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; explain why the ordinary ways of setting up the satisficing-versus-maximizing contrast leave the question open rather than settling it; describe the method concretely — what I would actually do, in what order, and why that approach rather than, say, building a formal model or surveying experimental work; say what a positive result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and give an honest assessment of whether this is a workshop paper or a dissertation chapter, and what would have to be dropped to fit the time available. One case I keep returning to, though I do not want the whole thing to rest on it, is choosing between two careers whose goods seem to me not commensurable. Take a definite position: I want a verdict on whether the three-way distinction is real and on which reading of satisficing survives scrutiny, with your reasons, rather than a layout of the options and the choice left to me.
