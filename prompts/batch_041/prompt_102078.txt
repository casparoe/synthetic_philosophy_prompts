I retired from experimental physics a few years ago and have since been convening a fortnightly reading group on the philosophy of mathematics: ten regulars, mostly scientists with a couple of philosophers, ninety minutes a session. I should like one of those sessions built around a game that makes mathematical constructivism felt as a live constraint on what one may actually do, rather than a fussy scruple about the law of excluded middle. What I want the group to run into is the gap between being unable to refute a claim and being able to exhibit what the claim asserts — and the pull of a rule that lets you settle any either/or for free.

Concretely, I have in mind a hidden-sequence setup. One player holds a concealed finite string of zeros and ones; the others must reach conclusions about it, and may assert a disjunction only when they can point to the disjunct they are committing to, and may assert that something exists only when they can produce it. Build in a referee card that, once per round, may declare of any such string either that it is all zeros or that it contains a one — no matter which, and without ever showing the string. Arrange the rules, the win condition and the payoff (points will do) so that the players first feel the power of the referee and then the emptiness of what it buys them, and so that they construct the difficulty themselves before anyone names it. I want a debrief that maps each moment of play onto the corresponding part of the constructive critique — witness conditions, double negation, the decision principle the referee embodies, and what the classical mathematician would say the referee is entitled to — together with an honest account of what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it, and which feature of constructivism the game cannot reproduce and I shall have to supply in speech.
