I have been working through the old puzzle about whether a green apple gives any support to the claim that every raven is black. Every discussion I can follow ends the same way: yes, it technically counts as a confirming instance, but the amount of confirmation is so small that no sensible person would change their mind. I would like you to construct a case that breaks with that ending — a case in which a single observation of a non-black non-raven does real work, enough that a reasonable person's confidence in the raven claim should visibly rise. Please build it to the following specifications.

First, the observation has to be something an ordinary person could actually make; no inspecting the entire universe, and no magical sampling device. Second, the case may not rest on an unstated assumption that the observed object was pulled uniformly at random from all the objects in existence. Whatever process produced the observation has to be stated plainly and has to be believable within the story. Third, the hypotheses in play have to be logically equivalent in the way the puzzle demands — the claim that every raven is black, and its contrapositive, that every non-black thing is not a raven — and the observation has to be a non-black non-raven. Fourth, give me the arithmetic: state the starting probability of the raven claim, the probability of seeing what was seen under each hypothesis, and the calculation that leads to the updated confidence, so that I can see the size of the shift rather than take your word for it. The shift should be large enough to notice; if you can make the observation decisive without cheating, so much the better, and tell me whether the non-black non-raven is doing more work than a black raven would under a comparable procedure. Fifth, once the case is built, take away the one feature that is carrying the most weight and show that the case falls back to the negligible result everyone starts with.

I should say what the case is for, because I do not want a case that simply stipulates its own answer. My real question is whether the asymmetry in how much these observations confirm is a fact about logic or a fact about what we happen to know already — about background knowledge and the way the observation was gathered. If the case only works because you quietly wrote the answer into the setup, it is no use to me.

After the scenario, walk me through the reasoning explicitly: what the starting probability is, what each observation does to it, and why. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work. I have a suspicion that it is the sampling procedure together with how rare ravens are relative to everything else, but I want you to say whether that is right or whether something else is carrying the weight. Tell me whether the case shows the puzzle is really about logic or really about background knowledge, and do not let those two blur into each other. Then tell me how the strongest opponent of the case would attack it — someone who thinks the whole puzzle is a triviality, or someone who thinks confirmation is a purely logical matter — and answer that attack if you can. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which consideration or argument would lead you to a different verdict, and where in your reasoning you are most likely to be wrong. I would rather have an honest account of the weak points than a confident one. I have no formal training in this area, so keep the arithmetic simple enough to follow, but do not water down the substance.
