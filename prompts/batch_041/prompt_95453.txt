I'm a third-year PhD student in political theory, and in a fortnight I have to run a ninety-minute session on Machiavelli's fortune/virtù pair for a reading group made up mostly of graduate students from neighbouring fields — early modern historians, two philosophers, one economist — none of whom read Italian and most of whom arrive with the word "Machiavellian" already doing a great deal of quiet work for them. The session is anchored to the closing chapters of The Prince and to the Discourses' scattered treatment of fortune, but I don't want a plan for lecturing on those texts. I want a plan for teaching the concept. What I need is the order in which the components must come so that the whole thing registers as a single position rather than a heap of aphorisms: the dependence relations among the claims, which piece has to be in place before the next can even be stated intelligibly, and where a premature formulation would do lasting damage.

To make this concrete, here is the reading I want the session to land on, and I'd like you to treat it as a proposal to be argued with rather than a settled gloss. I think Machiavelli runs three different notions under the single word fortuna, and that the interest lies in their interaction. First, fortune as variance: the share of an outcome not attributable to the agent's dispositions — floods, the deaths of popes, the arrival of the French. Second, fortune as fit: not a quantity of chance at all but a relation between the agent's fixed mode of proceeding and the temper of the times, so that the same caution which made a man's career ruins it a decade later. Third, and this is the one I care about, fortune as the unavailability of the reflexive capacity: the agent who would need to change his mode of proceeding with the times cannot choose the temperament from which such flexibility would issue, so the highest requirement Machiavelli places on virtù turns out to be something the agent cannot be answerable for — which is why the discussion can end by praising the impetuous and conceding that a man cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to. I want the audience to see why that third move is not a lapse into fatalism but the sharpest thing in the text, and why it makes virtù an unstable achievement concept rather than a trait. Along the way I want the predictable misconceptions mapped and assigned to specific moments in the sequence: that fortuna is luck in the everyday sense; that virtù is moral virtue (or, in the other direction, mere ruthlessness); that the river-and-dikes image licenses confident engineering of contingency; that the roughly-half remark is a quantitative thesis about determinism and free will; that the man of virtù is guaranteed success; and that the text is simply inconsistent between the prince who leans on fortune being lost when she shifts and the claim that fortune can be resisted. Tell me which misconception the ordering should pre-empt and which must be provoked and then corrected, and give me one closing question or case that would show whether they have the concept rather than a summary of it — something that would expose an audience that has learned to say "virtù" and "fortuna" in the right places without grasping the dependency. Say too what I should explicitly refuse to cover at this level and flag as out of scope. Finally, since I'm asking you to adjudicate my three-way reading rather than merely dress it up: tell me how confident you are in the claims you make about what Machiavelli is up to, and single out the point in your plan that you are least sure of, whether because the textual basis is thin or because the pedagogical bet might not pay off.
