I'm in a seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and I've been trying to work out what I actually believe by going case by case rather than by adopting a theory and defending it. I've written up my verdicts on a set of cases that all have the same basic shape — a backup mechanism that would have produced the act if the agent hadn't produced it herself — and I want to know what principle, if any, unifies them, what that principle says about the cases I haven't decided, and which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. The cases are these. (1) Nadia deletes a colleague's dataset out of spite; unknown to her, the colleague had installed a program that would have seized her hands and deleted the file anyway had she shown any hesitation, but she never hesitated, so it never fired. I say she is fully responsible. (2) Same as (1), except that the program, had it fired, would have deleted the file by making Nadia believe it was corrupted, so that she would have deleted it while thinking she was doing something good. She in fact deleted it out of spite. Still fully responsible, I think; the counterfactual motive strikes me as irrelevant, since the act itself is the same. (3) Same as (1), but the program had only a forty percent chance of firing. Still responsible; the probability of the backup seems to me to make no difference. (4) Nadia knows about the program and knows it will fire if she doesn't act first. She deletes the file anyway, out of spite and partly to beat the program to it. I want to say responsible, but this case feels different from (1) and I can't articulate why. (5) Priya was raised in a way that left her incapable of caring about other people's property; no intervener, no program, only her upbringing. She takes the money. I blame her. (6) Stipulate that the universe is deterministic, so that nothing other than what happened could have happened; Priya takes the money out of ordinary greed. I blame her here too — I'm a compatibilist about determinism. (7) A neuroscientist writes the intention directly into Priya's brain, and she takes the money. I do not blame her. (8) Here the outcome was unavoidable but the act was not: the program would have deleted the file by itself at midnight, and Nadia's act is at five o'clock, so had she decided differently at five the file would still have been deleted at midnight, but the five o'clock deletion would not have occurred. I say responsible.

What I keep noticing is that (5) and (6) sit badly with (7): if an upbringing that leaves someone unable to do otherwise does not get her off the hook, why does the neuroscientist's intervention? And I notice that in (8) I seem to be leaning on the thought that she *could* have refrained, which is exactly the thought I set aside in (1). My tentative principle has been this: a person is responsible for an act when the act issues from her own reasons-responsive mechanism, whether or not any other mechanism was available, since a backup that never fires never enters the actual causal history and so should be irrelevant. But I can see that this principle is in trouble with (7), where the manipulated agent's mechanism is in some sense still hers, and possibly with (4), where awareness seems to do something I can't name. So: is there a principle, or a small set of them, that captures these verdicts or nearly all of them? State it precisely, tell me which of my cases each clause is doing its work for, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot keep. Then apply it to two or three further cases of the same shape that I haven't listed — I am especially curious what it says about a case in which the backup, had it fired, would have operated through exactly the same neural pathway as the decision she actually made, so that her actual decision and the counterfactual one would have been indistinguishable in kind, and about a case in which the backup would have made the act merely more likely rather than certain. Please don't send me to the literature; I want the principle and the verdicts, not a reading list.

Structure the reply as follows, with these headings in this order: "The Cases, Restated," a compact numbered restatement of my verdicts, no more than 150 words; "Candidate Principles," two or three principles each stated precisely enough to be tested, about 400 words; "What Each Principle Costs," which of my verdicts each candidate preserves and which it has to abandon, about 300 words; "Further Cases," your two or three new cases with your verdicts and a sentence of rationale each, about 250 words; and "Bottom Line," the principle I should adopt and the verdict I should give up, no more than 150 words.
