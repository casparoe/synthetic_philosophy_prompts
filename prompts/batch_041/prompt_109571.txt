Write an essay on whether the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem—the result that any strategy-proof social choice function whose range contains at least three alternatives is dictatorial—should be read as a fundamental limit on democratic decision-making or as a narrow result about a particular class of mechanisms. To focus the issue, consider a proposed procedure: each voter submits a ranking of the alternatives and a separate list of alternatives that she deems acceptable; the procedure first eliminates any alternative that fails a threshold of acceptability, then applies a Borda count to the remaining alternatives, and if a Condorcet winner exists among the remaining alternatives and is not the Borda winner, it holds a lottery between those two; otherwise it elects the Borda winner. An advocate of the procedure claims that it escapes the theorem because the lottery is probabilistic and because the acceptability stage restricts the domain of preferences; a critic replies that the theorem still applies, or that any apparent escape is purchased at the cost of other democratic desiderata. Weigh the strongest versions of both positions. Consider whether strategy-proofness is best understood as a property of a rule, as a property of an equilibrium, or as a constraint on a voter's practical reasoning. Reach a definite verdict on whether the theorem should make us pessimistic about the possibility of a genuinely non-manipulable democratic procedure, and justify that verdict. Argue from the concepts rather than citing literature; do not supply a bibliography or reading list.
