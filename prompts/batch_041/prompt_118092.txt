hey — I'm a 4th-year PhD student in philosophy (I work on ethics of behavioral public policy) and I'm the grad student rep on our county's Behavioral Insights Review Board. We vote Thursday on a proposal from the Department of Health and Human Services and I have to circulate the written opinion beforehand, so I'd like your help drafting it.

The proposal: the county wants to mail about 6,000 households that are eligible for but not enrolled in a food-assistance program. The letter will be signed by someone described as the household's "neighborhood benefits coordinator." There is no such person — it's a county employee writing from a central office, and the county's position is that the title is just a friendlier face and isn't, strictly, a lie. The letter also says that most households on the block have already applied, which is true-ish (they're reading it off a matched pilot), and it never mentions that it's coming from the government or that applying is optional. In the pilot this version got 22% uptake vs 9% for a plain letter that said who it was from and that the program is voluntary.

The catch is our charter says all interventions have to be "transparent and non-manipulative" and nobody has ever defined those. I wrote up a two-part test I want the memo to use, though feel free to argue with the test itself, honestly I'm not sure it survives scrutiny: (1) disclosure-survival — if the county published a plain-language description of the letter and its intent a month ahead, and it got normal local news coverage, would the effect mostly survive? (2) belief-licensing — does the letter do its work by leaving recipients with a belief the county's own records don't support and that the county wouldn't put in a sentence on the record?

Some other stuff that's come up: the county ran a survey where 78% of people said they'd be fine with the letter once it was explained to them, and they are definately treating that as a kind of consent. Also the pilot only measured who applied, not whether people stayed enrolled or got discouraged after a first rejection. And one member wants to approve as-is because the letters go out March 3 and a delay burns a mailing cycle.

The thing I keep going back and forth on, and want the memo to actually take a position on: the county's line is that they never lied, they just declined to correct an assumption the recipient was already making. Does that distinction do any real work here, or is it lawyer stuff? I'd rather the memo answer that than just gesture at autonomy.

Can you draft the memo? Recommend a concrete course of action with a timeframe (approve / reject / approve with conditions), engage the strongest argument on the county's side honestly rather than dunking on it, and keep it tight — a page or two. No citations or reading lists, I know the literature. American English please.
