A colleague of mine — sharp, widely read, the sort of person who can hold forth on anything — summed the whole matter up for me over lunch the other day, and it went more or less like this: 'Douglass began as a Garrisonian and denounced the Constitution as a pro-slavery compact, but during the Civil War, once emancipation was in the air, he changed his mind and came to hold that the Constitution, as it actually operated in the United States, was an antislavery document. He was persuaded chiefly by Lysander Spooner, who had shown that the framers never once used the word slave and that the document's principles were incompatible with bondage. From then on Douglass argued that the Constitution gave the federal government the power to abolish slavery in the Southern states straight away, so that the slaveholders never had a legal leg to stand on and only bad faith kept slavery alive. That is why Douglass could praise the Constitution as a charter of liberty while Garrison regarded it as a pro-slavery compact.' My own instinct, for what it is worth, is that the Constitution plainly did protect slavery, so I am inclined to distrust the liberty-document line altogether — but I would rather not trade one confident summary for another.
