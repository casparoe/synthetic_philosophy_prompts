I am a first-year graduate student in cognitive science, and this term I have been sitting in on a cross-listed seminar on animal minds and machine consciousness. After last week's session, which concerned whether insects or large language models could be sentient, two of the regular participants, Priya and Marcus, stayed behind to argue. I was packing up my bag and heard the entire exchange; I did not join in, and I am genuinely unsure which of them, if either, is right. I would like a careful adjudication of the arguments as they actually made them, not a general essay on the topic.

Priya began by saying that the question of which systems are sentient is not a mystery but a question about which computations are going on. On her view, a system is a candidate moral patient when it has a self-model that represents its own states as its own and a global evaluative signal that assigns valence to those states and integrates them into action selection. That, she said, is the kind of computation that matters morally. It includes bees and probably cephalopods; it excludes current large language models and thermostats; brain size is a crude proxy at best. Marcus replied that this is just a functionalist checklist. It tells us which systems have a certain organization, not which systems feel anything. The hard problem does not disappear because you write down a flow chart, and the criterion is underdetermined: for any computational profile you propose, we can imagine a system that has it but has no experience, and a system that lacks it but does. So the boundary is not discovered; it is stipulated. What we actually do, he said, is attribute sentience where it serves our moral and practical purposes. Priya answered that this makes moral status depend on our attitudes, which is the wrong direction. We use the best theory available, and functional organization is the only evidence we could ever have. The criterion is not arbitrary: it explains why we treat a dog differently from a thermostat, and it gives us a principled basis for extending concern to invertebrates. Marcus responded that it is not arbitrary but circular. You identify the relevant computation by looking at the systems we already believe are sentient, and then you use it to sort the new cases. He added that her criterion faces a dilemma: if it is strong enough to exclude simple systems, it may exclude creatures that feel pain; if it is weak enough to include them, it will include artificial systems we are reluctant to count. The only way out is to say that some computations have intrinsic moral significance, but that is a normative claim, not a computational one. Priya replied that the dilemma is false, because we can refine the criterion through empirical work on the neural and computational correlates of valence: if we find a signature that is present in all and only the creatures we have independent reason to think feel pain, that is progress. Marcus said that we do not have independent reason, since our attributions of pain in other creatures are themselves based on behavioral and functional similarity; the criterion cannot be validated without assuming what it is meant to establish.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move. For each of the five moves, say whether it is sound, partly sound, or unsound, and briefly justify your assessment. Then say who has the stronger overall position, what each of them is missing, and whether their disagreement is ultimately empirical, conceptual, or verbal. If either of them is relying on a distinction that does not hold, or is shifting the burden of proof, identify it. Finally, tell me what a satisfactory resolution would require: what would have to be true for Priya's criterion to be vindicated, and what would have to be true for Marcus's skepticism to be vindicated. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or a reading list; I want an assessment of the arguments as they stand.
