I'm trying to get clear on a passage in Hume's Of Miracles (Enquiry, section 10). Here's the bit that's bugging me:

"A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence. ... In all cases, we must balance the opposite experiments, where they are opposite, and deduct the smaller number from the greater, in order to know the exact force of the superior evidence."

Then a bit later he lays down what he calls a general maxim: that no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle unless the testimony is of such a kind that its falsehood would be more miraculous than the fact it's trying to establish.

My problem is I cant tell whether that maxim is supposed to just be an application of the balancing rule or a separate move. If testimony is just another species of empirical evidence, and we're meant to subtract the weaker side from the stronger, then a miracle is just an event with a very low prior, and in principle enough good testimony could outweigh it -- the arithmetic doesn't forbid it. But the maxim sounds like it's meant to shut miracle testimony down in a stronger way, and the "falsehood would be more miraculous" condition sounds like it's basically never met. So which is it? Is he applying the general framework, or smuggling in something extra? And if it's just the framework, what work is the maxim doing that the balancing rule wasn't already doing?

Also the talk about a mutual destruction of arguments when the two sides are equal -- I take that to mean you get no belief either way, but then he says the superior side still leaves some assurance, just reduced. How do those two fit together? Sort out the structure for me, and tell me where you think the weakest joint is.
