You are a philosopher who also drafts regulatory filings for a living, and you have been asked to write a piece of satire for a dataset on philosophical reasoning. Your target is not a person but a practice: the habit, common in effective-altruist and longtermist cost-benefit work, of attaching precise expected values to interventions whose long-run consequences are not merely uncertain but indeterminate — the condition usually called consequentialist cluelessness.

Produce the Risk Factors section of the private placement memorandum for the Palimpsest Far-Future Impact Fund, LP, a fictional vehicle that pools donations and claims to maximize expected moral value per dollar, reported in Impact Units (IU) to four significant figures. The register is deadpan corporate legalese: the document never winks, never names its own joke, and never breaks character. Every risk factor must be a straight-faced disclosure that, read carefully, concedes one facet of the fund's cluelessness, so that a philosopher who works on this problem recognizes each item as a genuine difficulty rather than a strawman.

Before you draft, decide which five facets you will use and check that no two overlap. The three-way distinction between risk (a known distribution over outcomes), uncertainty (an unknown distribution over outcomes), and cluelessness (no determinate distribution, because the outcome space itself is not fixed) should be the spine of the piece, and at least one item should turn on the point that a fund of this kind alters which future people exist, so that the familiar promise of making the same people better off is unavailable. Invent whatever internal machinery you need — committees, valuation conventions, a Counterfactual Baseline Subcommittee — but keep it internally consistent.

Format: exactly five numbered risk factors, each one or two sentences, with no preamble and no concluding paragraph; the result should read as a single uninterrupted document.

If my framing is confused — if cluelessness is not genuinely distinct from ordinary uncertainty, or if a risk-factors parody cannot be made funny without misrepresenting the position — say so plainly rather than writing the piece anyway. And if the satire does lean on a contested reading of the idea, say which reading, in a single bracketed note after the fifth item.
