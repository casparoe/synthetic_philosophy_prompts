I'm a graduate student in philosophy, and next week I'm presenting a paper at our decision theory reading group. The paper argues that the counterfactual mugging scenario and the XOR blackmail scenario come apart, and that the standard updateless treatment of both is mistaken. In counterfactual mugging, a perfect predictor flips a fair coin; if it comes up tails, it asks you for $100; if it comes up heads, it pays you $10,000 if and only if it predicted that you would have paid had the coin come up tails. My argument is that you should pay in the tails case, because the predictor's prediction is a function of your decision procedure, and so your decision procedure causally influences whether you receive the $10,000 in the heads branch. In XOR blackmail, a blackmailer flips a fair coin; if it comes up heads, he will torture you unless you pay him $100; if it comes up tails, he will not torture you, but if you pay him, he will torture you. The blackmailer is not a predictor, and the coin flip is independent of your decision procedure. My argument is that you should not pay, because your payment is causally ineffective with respect to the coin, and the torture is a function of the coin and your payment; there is no predictor whose prediction could be influenced by your decision procedure. So updateless reasoning is warranted in the first case but not the second.

Next week I'll present this to our reading group, which is mostly causal decision theorists and evidential decision theorists, plus a couple of philosophers of probability who are skeptical of updateless reasoning and think perfect predictors are incoherent. They care about the formal distinction between causal and evidential correlation, the role of precommitment, and the coherence of prediction under indeterminacy. They will not be impressed by appeals to intuition; they will want to know exactly where the causal structure differs between the two cases. I need a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this audience, not the ones I'd get from a LessWrong meetup, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way a causal decision theorist, an evidential decision theorist, or a philosopher of probability would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. My replies should not rely on citations or reading lists; I want to be able to defend the position from the armchair, using only the structure of the scenarios as described above. Also, please flag any objection that would require me to concede a premise of my argument, and tell me which concession I should make up front rather than fight.
