I've been arguing about a puzzle with my brother for weeks, and I have no training in philosophy or economics, so I'm hoping someone can explain what the real conceptual issue is rather than just repeating the standard answer at me. Two people sit across a table from a pile of money. There are twenty turns. On any turn, the person whose turn it is can take the pile — she gets four fifths of it and her partner gets the rest — or pass, which doubles the pile and hands the turn to the other player. If the twentieth turn is passed, the game ends and they split whatever is on the table evenly instead. The argument everyone makes is that whoever holds the twentieth turn will take, since taking beats an even split; the person holding the nineteenth turn sees that and takes; and so on back to the first person, who takes immediately and ends the whole thing within seconds. I can follow every step, and yet the conclusion is plainly bad for both of them. I can't tell whether the argument has a hole in it or whether my discomfort is just sentimentality.

So I tried to build a version where a hole, if there is one, would have to show. Suppose the two players never meet. Each privately submits a written set of instructions — a program — saying when to take and when to pass, and a referee runs the two programs against each other and pays each player according to how his program did. Now consider two programs. The first takes at the earliest opportunity. The second passes until the final turn and then takes. When these two meet, the first program always ends up with the larger share, since it takes the pile rather than letting it grow. But run the greedy program against a copy of itself and the game collapses in a second, leaving both players with almost nothing, while run the patient program against a copy of itself and both players walk away rich.

What I want explained is what the underlying issue actually is. Does "the rational move" mean something different when you are choosing an action inside a game you are already in than when you are choosing the instructions you will follow before the game starts, and does the original argument quietly assume one of those and not the other? Is the puzzle really about rationality at all, or is it about something else — what it means to know that the other player is working through exactly the same reasoning you are? And does the answer depend on who else is in the contest, that is, on whether I am facing a copy of myself or a stranger whose instructions I will never see?
