I'm on my university's research ethics committee — I'm the philosopher on it, which mostly means I get handed the protocols nobody else wants to touch — and there's one up for decision next Thursday that I have to circulate a written opinion on. It's from a colleague in my own department and one of her grad students, and it's an experimental-philosophy study built around the side-effect effect (the Knobe thing: people say a CEO who knowingly causes harm as a side effect of a profit-seeking plan brought the harm about intentionally, but won't say the same when the side effect is a benefit). What they want to do isn't just measure it, it's intervene. After participants give their initial intent judgment, the study shows them an explanation of why that judgment is probably a distortion — something about the harm case breaking a norm and the benefit case not, and about the inconsistency in their answers — and then has them judge a fresh vignette to see if the intuition moves. Since the entire point is to see whether you can defuse the effect, they can't tell participants upfront that the study is trying to change their moral judgments; that would kill it. Half the committee thinks this is fine, it's basically debriefing moved a bit earlier, and the other half thinks we'd be running a study whose real aim is to talk people out of their considered moral views without their consent, which is a different animal from measuring those views. There are also a couple of design quirks I want us to weigh, because they bear on whether the science is even worth the ethical cost: there's a condition where the side effect is described as what almost every comparable institution does (so the norm-violation story should predict the effect shrinking or flipping), and a condition where the participant is asked to imagine they are the administrator rather than judging him, and honestly I can't tell whether the intervention is testing their hypothesis or just testing their own persuasive skill.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before the meeting: a clear recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with conditions, and if it's conditions, spell them out concretely (consent language, a second debrief, withdrawal window, who signs off and by when) — plus the reasoning and an honest engagement with the strongest argument on whichever side I end up going against. Keep it to something I can read in five minutes and that the researchers could actually respond to; don't just recite the usual human-subjects checklist at me, and don't alot a paragraph to how important informed consent is in general.
