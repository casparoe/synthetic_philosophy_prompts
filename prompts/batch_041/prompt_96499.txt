I'm a novelist midway through a book whose protagonist is defined less by what she does than by what she would do under conditions the narrative never quite supplies — a woman whose generosity, courage, and cruelty are all, in some sense, never given their chance. To keep the metaphysics honest I've been reading around the philosophy of dispositions, and I've become stuck on the pair of problems usually filed under finks and masks: a fink is an interfering circumstance that stops a disposition from manifesting by altering the disposition (or its bearer) rather than merely obstructing its exercise, whereas a mask blocks the manifestation while leaving the disposition intact. I can state the difference; I cannot say how much of the surrounding debate is load-bearing and how much is scaffolding. My constraints are these: I have no library access worth the name, no appetite for a reading list or citations, and no interest in being told what has already been published — I want to think, from the armchair, about what is actually unresolved and what is worth resolving first. Assume I have roughly a year of early mornings before I must start drafting and can genuinely work through two or three problems, not twenty.

What I'd like from you is an agenda, not a survey. Among the open questions in this area, which deserve the most attention, and in what order? To make the request concrete, here are the candidates I've been circling, though I suspect the right answer rearranges them: whether the fink/mask distinction tracks a joint in nature or only a difference in how we describe an intervention; whether the stimulus and manifestation conditions of a disposition can themselves be analyzed dispositionally, and whether that generates a vicious regress or merely an infinite but benign one; whether there could be a disposition whose sole mask is the very disposition to test for it — a disposition that the act of probing reliably defeats — and what that would do to any conditional analysis of disposition ascriptions; whether a disposition can be masked by its own manifestation, and whether such self-masking dispositions are coherent or a contradiction in terms; and whether the whole apparatus is an artifact of a Humean picture that a thoroughgoing dispositionalism would simply dissolve. For each item you rank, tell me why it sits where it does: whether it is upstream of the others, whether it is tractable for someone reasoning rather than experimenting, and — most important for me — whether settling it would visibly change how the downstream questions are answered, or whether it is the kind of dispute that can be settled either way without anything else moving.

Then defend the ordering against the obvious objection that I'm optimizing the wrong thing. I care less about which question is intrinsically deepest than about which one, once answered, makes the others easier or moot — and I want to know what would move an item up or down my list: a counterexample that survives scrutiny, a proof that some proposed analysis collapses into circularity, a demonstration that two of my candidate questions are really the same question wearing different clothes. Finally, since I do eventually have to write the novel rather than the treatise, say which one or two of these I should simply stipulate for fictional purposes and stop worrying about, and what the cost of stipulating them would be — that is, what a reader who knows the literature would find implausible in a character built on the stipulation.
