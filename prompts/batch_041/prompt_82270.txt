A colleague and I have been running the same argument for two years in our political theory reading group and neither of us has moved. I hold that the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the Great Leap Forward famine were political crimes: in each case the deaths were not simply a matter of there being no food, they fell along lines that prices, requisitioning, and relief policy drew — those who bought their food starved while grain went out of Ireland, harvest failure coincided with procurement quotas and labour diverted to backyard furnaces — and people with the power to stop the dying did not stop it. She holds that calling these events political is a moral overlay on a natural one: the triggers were a cyclone and wartime disruption, a potato blight, a bad harvest, and unless I can show that the dead would have lived under a different politics, I am blaming people for the weather. We have read Sen on entitlements, we do not dispute the harvest figures, and we cannot get past this.

My hypothesis — and I want it tested, not confirmed — is that we are not really disagreeing about causes. I think we are using 'natural' in two registers at once: as a description of a trigger, and as a verdict that nobody is to blame; and that the argument is stuck because each of us keeps answering the question the other is not asking. Work through the three cases rather than the general thesis. Is this a verbal dispute about the words 'famine' and 'natural', a disagreement about which facts count as the relevant ones (yields and rainfall versus wages, prices, and entitlements), a dispute about a counterfactual that no evidence could settle, or a moral disagreement about blame wearing the costume of a causal one? Then tell me, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress between us — a distinction, a piece of evidence, a change in the question — or whether the impasse shows that 'natural disaster or political crime' is simply the wrong way to cut these cases.
