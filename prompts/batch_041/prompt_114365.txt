I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have reasoned myself into a position I cannot get out of on my own. I would like you to diagnose the argument below rather than restate it.

Some background, since it matters to the argument. I have been generating Bongard problems programmatically: the format from Mikhail Bongard's 1967 collection, in which six figures sit to the left of a dividing line and six to the right, and the solver has to state the concept that separates them. My generator picks a rule first and then samples figures that satisfy it on one side and violate it on the other. Here is a recent problem, described in words because I cannot paste the images. Left: a triangle above three dots; a square above four dots; an irregular quadrilateral above four dots; a pentagon above five dots; a hexagon above six dots; a smaller triangle above three dots. Right: a triangle above four dots; a square above three dots; a pentagon above seven dots; a hexagon above five dots; a quadrilateral above two dots; a triangle above six dots. The generator's rule was: left if and only if the number of dots equals the number of sides of the polygon.

Here is the argument I built and can no longer assess.

(1) A Bongard problem is fully specified by its twelve figures. The solver is given nothing else.

(2) Call the generator's rule R. R agrees with all twelve figures. So does R-prime: left if and only if the dot count equals the side count, or the figure contains exactly eleven dots. So does R-double-prime: left if and only if the dot count equals the side count, or the polygon is a heptagon. Each of these differs from R only on figures that do not appear in this problem, and there are infinitely many such variants.

(3) Two rules that agree on all twelve figures cannot be told apart by any amount of inspection of those twelve figures.

(4) The answer to the problem is supposed to be determined by the figures. That is the point of presenting exactly those figures and no others.

(5) If the figures cannot distinguish R from R-prime from R-double-prime, then none of them is the answer the figures determine.

(6) So either all of them are equally correct or none is correct. Either way there is no fact of the matter about which rule is the answer.

(7) Nothing in the argument turns on this rule or these figures. So the same holds for every Bongard problem, and, more generally, for any concept to be induced from finitely many examples.

(8) But (7) proves far too much. People solve these puzzles in seconds, they agree with one another, and the disagreements that do arise get settled by looking harder at the puzzle rather than by declaring the question empty. My own classifier, trained on a few hundred generated problems, generalizes to held-out figures well above chance, and I have ground truth to check it against. If there were no fact of the matter, both of those would be inexplicable.

(9) Therefore one of (1) through (6) is false.

I have tried patching the problem statement instead of the argument, and it did not work. I added a convention requiring the rule to be stated in terms of the drawn features, and banned rules that refer to a figure's position in the sequence. My counterexample rules survive both restrictions: eleven dots and heptagons are drawn features.

Which numbered step should I give up, why did that step look so plausible when I wrote it, and what does giving it up cost me? If the repair requires the answer to be fixed by something other than the twelve figures, I want to know what that something is, whether it is a fact about the world or only a fact about the solver's prior, and whether the same repair rescues concepts that have no author behind them. I would rather abandon a premise than abandon (8), but I want an honest account of the price. And I would like to know whether any part of the argument survives intact, since it still feels to me like it is pointing at something true about learning a concept from a handful of examples.
