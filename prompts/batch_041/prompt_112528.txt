Question 3 (10 points).

Mira has credences over the partition {A, not-A} of 0.4 and 0.4. She regards a unit ticket that pays $1 if X as fairly priced at exactly her credence in X, and she'll trade at that price. A bookie is allowed to buy tickets from Mira, but is not allowed to sell her any.

(a) [3] Show that the bookie can make a sure profit. Say precisely what she buys, and say what feature of Mira's credences is being exploited.

(b) [3] The sure-loss result shows that Mira is exploitable. Is the sure-loss result on its own enough to get you to the inaccuracy conclusion, or does it need help?

And here's what the student wrote:

(a) Mira treats 40 cents as a fair price for a unit ticket on A, and 40 cents as a fair price for a unit ticket on not-A. So the bookie buys both tickets from her at those prices. The bookie pays her 80 cents up front and gets back exactly $1 whichever of A or not-A turns out to be the case. The bookie therefore clears 20 cents for sure, and Mira is 20 cents down no matter what. What's being exploited is that her credences over the partition don't sum to 1: since the two tickets are complements, buying both at her own fair prices has to be a sure loss for her whenever those prices sum to less than a dollar. (The bookie does need to know her credences, and Mira does need to be willing to trade at the prices she calls fair; those are the standard idealisations.)

(b) I'd say the result does show her credences are inaccurate, and not just that she'll lose money. It's true that the sure-loss fact is stated in terms of what happens when she trades, so at first glance it looks like a fact about her behaviour rather than about how things are. But a credence that can't survive being read as a fair price isn't tracking anything. If both of her prices were correct, then the world would have to make both tickets worth what they cost, and it can't, because exactly one of the two pays. So the inconsistency in her prices is already a misrepresentation of the world, and I don't think you need to bolt on any extra premise (say, one about accuracy being the aim of credence) to get from the sure loss to the inaccuracy.

(c) I think the argument is much weaker than it looks here. If Mira is risk-averse then her utility for money is concave, so she won't sell a ticket at a price that's merely fair in money terms: she'd be taking on a 40% chance of having to pay out a dollar in exchange for a certain 40 cents, and the disutility of the payout outweighs the utility of the premium. So the book only works against someone who is risk-neutral about money, which is an idealised agent. That makes the Dutch book argument a result about perfectly rational, risk-neutral agents rather than about actual people, and to get it to apply to real agents you'd have to add a further assumption that they're willing to take small fair bets, which is exactly the assumption that risk aversion denies.
