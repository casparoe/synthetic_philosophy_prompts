I'm a second-year medical ethics student, and for the past few months I've been trying to write down what I actually think about choosing children. The occasion is that a friend of mine works as a genetic counselor at a fertility clinic that has recently started offering a service I had never heard of until she explained it to me: for each embryo in a batch, the lab computes a score for traits like adult height, body mass, and years of schooling, and parents may look at those scores when they decide which embryo to transfer. I have no settled view about the service as a whole, and I am not asking whether it should be legal or how it should be regulated. What interests me is that my reactions to particular choices are quite consistent, and I would like to know whether there is a principle behind them or whether I am simply improvising. Here is where I actually land. Two embryos, one of which will develop a degenerative neurological condition that means years of pain and death in childhood: choosing the other is not merely permitted but required, and I do not find that a hard case at all. An embryo that will very likely develop moderate hearing loss in middle age, against one that will not: choosing the one that will not is fine, and I would think badly of a clinic that refused to let the parents weigh it. A variant that the clinic's data associate with roughly two additional years of schooling on average and with a modestly elevated risk of a mood disorder in adulthood: choosing that embryo is fine, since the parents are betting on a whole life and mild risks are everywhere. A variant associated with being about two inches taller than the alternative: I squirm, but I cannot bring myself to call it wrong, though if the service became universal and the average rose and no one's life was any better except in relation to everyone else, I would think something had gone badly wrong. A variant associated with lighter skin, which in the country where these particular parents live predicts, on average, better treatment by teachers, doctors, and police: not fine, even granting that the child's life will go better; it looks to me as though the parents are buying into an injustice rather than shielding the child from one. An embryo selected in part because it will be a good tissue match for an older sibling who needs a transplant, where the parents say, and I believe them, that they wanted a second child anyway and will love this one no less: fine. And an embryo selected for a variant associated with a very even temperament and a strong preference for routine, which the parents want because they think it will make for a calmer household: not fine, and I notice I am considerably more bothered by this than by the tissue match, even though in both cases the child is chosen partly for someone else's sake.

The best I have come up with is this: choose the embryo you expect to have the better life, unless the advantage you are buying comes at someone else's expense, or unless the child could rightly resent you for having chosen as you did. I cannot tell whether that is one principle with two escape hatches or three principles wearing a trench coat, and I suspect the resentment clause is doing work the welfare clause cannot do.

You are a philosopher who works on the ethics of reproduction and who is good at stating a principle precisely and then finding the case that breaks it. Please brainstorm the candidate principles and the cases that pull against each before you commit to anything. Tell me which of my seven verdicts the principle requires me to abandon; if it saves all seven, tell me exactly what further clause buys that and what it costs me elsewhere. Then apply the principle to two cases I have not made up my mind about. First, parents select the embryo that is the better tissue match even though the clinic's scores suggest it will have a somewhat harder life than the alternative, so the interests of the two children conflict — does my sibling verdict survive that? Second, parents choose the embryo with the lower expected welfare because they are confident they would be better parents to that child and the family as a whole would flourish — I want to know whether your principle treats the child's own welfare as something that can be traded against other goods or as a constraint that cannot be traded at all. Be explicit about the difference between a principle that tracks what the parents intend and one that tracks what the child ends up with, since I think my verdicts pull in both directions. Where you think I am simply inconsistent and should change a verdict rather than complicate the principle, say so plainly. Write in American English, keep the answer focused on the principle rather than a survey of the literature, and flag any point at which you think the question itself is confused.
