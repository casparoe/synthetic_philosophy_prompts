I am grading the final problem set for a graduate seminar in social choice and formal epistemology, and I want a second opinion on one student's answer before I return the scripts. Below is the question as it was posed, followed by the answer the student submitted.

Question 3 (30 points). Consider a three-member appellate panel deciding a tort claim. Under the governing rule the defendant is liable if and only if both of the following hold: (P) the defendant breached a duty of care owed to the plaintiff, and (Q) that breach caused the plaintiff's injury. The panel resolves the case by voting separately on three propositions, P, Q, and R, where R is the proposition that the defendant is liable, so that R holds just in case P and Q both hold. Each judge's three votes are logically consistent. The votes are:

Judge A: P yes; Q yes; R yes.
Judge B: P yes; Q no; R no.
Judge C: P no; Q yes; R no.

(a) Determine the outcome of majority voting applied to each proposition independently, and say whether the resulting collective set of judgments is logically consistent.

(b) Distinguish the premise-based and the conclusion-based procedures for aggregating the panel's judgments, and state what each yields in this case.

(c) Explain what the case shows about the possibility of consistent collective judgment, and discuss how the difficulty relates to Arrow's theorem.

Answer all three parts.

The student's answer:

(a) Majority rule applied proposition by proposition gives: P is affirmed by two judges (A and B); Q is affirmed by two judges (A and C); R is affirmed by only one judge (A). The collective set is therefore {P, Q, not-R}. This set is inconsistent, because P and Q jointly entail R, so the panel collectively affirms premises that entail a conclusion it denies. It is worth noting that no individual judge is inconsistent: A affirms all three, B denies Q and hence R, C denies P and hence R. The inconsistency appears only at the level of the aggregation. This is the doctrinal paradox.

(b) The premise-based procedure applies majority rule to the premises P and Q and then infers the conclusion logically. Here P passes 2–1 and Q passes 2–1, so the procedure returns R. The conclusion-based procedure applies majority rule to R alone and disregards the premises. Here R fails 1–2, so the procedure returns not-R. The two procedures therefore disagree on the very same votes. That disagreement is the substance of the dilemma: a procedure that is guaranteed to be consistent need not track the majority's verdict on every proposition, while a procedure that tracks majorities everywhere need not be consistent.

(c) The case shows that proposition-by-proposition majority voting can deliver an inconsistent collective judgment set even though every voter is perfectly consistent. This is in essence the same impossibility that Condorcet's cyclical-majority example illustrates and that Arrow's theorem states in general: the cycle of preferences in Condorcet's case and the inconsistent set of judgments here are one and the same phenomenon, dressed in different objects. And because the premise-based procedure always returns a logically consistent set of collective judgments, adopting it removes the difficulty: if the panel fixes the premises by majority vote and lets logic supply the rest, the collective judgment cannot be inconsistent, and the dilemma is dissolved.
