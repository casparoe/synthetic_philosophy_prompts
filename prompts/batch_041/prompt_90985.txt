So I just got handed the "uncertain evidence" week in the upper-level epistemology course I'm TAing, and the timing is honestly a little cursed because my roommate did one of those rapid tests last week and the line was so faint neither of us could tell if it was a positive or just a shadow, and we spent like forty minutes arguing about what we were even allowed to conclude from it, and I realized somewhere in there that I did not actually know how to do the math on evidence I'm only, say, seventy percent sure of. So now I have to teach it. I need a 90-minute in-class activity for about 16 juniors/seniors (they've all had a little probability, nothing fancy) that makes them feel why conditionalizing on evidence you're unsure about isnt the same as just conditionalizing on the proposition as if it were certain. I want them to run into it with their hands and only hear the name at the very end.

Heres my idea, please flesh it into something runnable and tell me where it breaks. A hidden jar is either mostly red (70 red / 30 blue beads) or mostly blue (30 red / 70 blue), 50/50 going in. One student, the looker, draws a bead and views it through a frosted tube so they can only partially make out the color — we calibrate the frosting so they come away about 70% sure it's red. Nobody else sees the bead. Now everyone, looker included, writes down two numbers: how sure they are the bead was red, and how sure they are the jar is mostly red. The naive move is to conditionalize on "the bead is red" as if certain; the right move is to mix the two conditional credences (jar-red given bead-red, jar-red given bead-blue) with weights 0.7 and 0.3. I want the rules to make that fall out rather than be announced.

For the force: before the draw, the group computes the two conditional credences by plain conditionalization (easy, they can do it on index cards). Then a designated student plays bookie, offering to buy and sell tickets on the jar's composition at prices set by those conditional credences. Whoever states a jar-credence that isnt the 0.7/0.3 mixture of them can be traded against for a sure profit, and the bookie's job is to actually find the trade in front of everyone. Payoffs in tokens or candy, nothing real. Maybe run three rounds with different lookers and different calibrations (0.6, 0.9) so they see the weights move and the answer still has to be a mixture. I also want the moment where the looker feels they should have some private edge and then discovers that once they've announced their 0.7, everyone ends up in the same place — that convergence feels important to me.

Then a debrief that maps each beat onto the actual idea: the two numbers they wrote, why the second one cant be gotten by treating the first as a fact, what the "rigidity" of the conditional credences is doing, and why the bookie works. Tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time — my guesses are that half of them conditionalize on the proposition anyway and come out overconfident, and a couple mix with 50/50 weights instead of their own 0.7/0.3, and one person refuses to give a number because the frosting is vague. How do I handle those without lecturing? And be honest with me about what the activity cant do: it's only a two-cell partition, so it wont show the general rule for finer partitions, and I assume it also cant touch the objection that you're always certain of something (namely that you had that experience) so you should just conditionalize on that instead — if that's right, tell me what I have to supply myself and when to bring it in.
