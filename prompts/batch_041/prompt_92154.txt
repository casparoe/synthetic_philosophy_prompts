In my seminar on analogy and categorization we have just finished the Copycat material — the letter-string model in which a description of the problem is not retrieved from a fixed taxonomy but assembled on the fly by many small competing processes, with a global "temperature" that rises when the current description is weak and makes the system readier to abandon it and slip to a neighbouring concept in the network. The assignment is to carry the model out of its native microdomain and test it against a real episode, in both directions, and to say plainly where it runs out.

The episode I have chosen is the American tariff litigation decided by the Supreme Court in 1893, in which importers who had paid duty on tomatoes under a schedule taxing "vegetables in their natural state" argued that tomatoes are fruit and so belonged on the free list. The only testimony offered was that "fruit" and "vegetable" had no special meaning in trade, so the Court applied what it treated as the ordinary meaning of the words and held for the collector. Justice Gray conceded the botanical classification and then set it aside: "Botanically speaking, tomatoes are the fruit of a vine, just as are cucumbers, squashes, beans, and peas." What decided the case was the description the words carry "in the common language of the people" — the description under which a tomato appears at dinner rather than at dessert.

Two things I want from you. First, a disciplined mapping, and an honest verdict on whether the theory applies at all. My worry is that Copycat's central claim is that the description is constructed and that its boundary moves under pressure from the analogy being drawn, whereas the Court appears to be choosing between two boundaries already in place, botany's and the kitchen's; if all the model can say is that the description depends on context, it says nothing the Court did not say itself. Is the culinary reading a slip within one conceptual network, an activation of a different region of it, or neither?

Second, treat the case as a test of the model. It has no representation of an authoritative text that must be applied, of a community that needs one answer rather than a distribution of answers, or of the interests — here, the protection of domestic growers — that make one description more serviceable than another. Does it need a notion of a description being frozen by a decision or a convention, and can that be added without surrendering the claim that concepts are fluid? What, if anything, plays the role of temperature in this episode?

Close with what the case shows about the model: a confirmation, an anomaly, or an episode it never anticipated — and be explicit about where it is silent rather than making it speak. I am not asking whether the decision was correct as law.
