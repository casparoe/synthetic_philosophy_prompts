And here's what the student wrote:

Lakatos starts from the failure of Popper's naive falsificationism: on the naive view a single basic statement that clashes with a theory refutes it, so science advances by conjecture and refutation and the history of science should be a graveyard of dead theories. Lakatos objects that no theory ever faces the tribunal of experience alone. There is always a belt of auxiliary assumptions between theory and observation, so the picture in which one recalcitrant observation kills a theory is a fiction. Hence he replaces the isolated theory with the research programme as the unit of appraisal.

A research programme has a hard core of assumptions that its practitioners treat as non-negotiable, and a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses, initial conditions and observational assumptions that take the strain of the tests. The negative heuristic is the programme's long-range research policy: it tells the scientist which anomalies are worth pursuing and roughly how to patch the belt when something goes wrong. The positive heuristic is the set of methodological prohibitions that keeps workers from aiming modus tollens at the hard core. Because the belt absorbs the shocks, the hard core is never refuted by any single experiment; it can only be abandoned by a methodological decision, and Lakatos thinks that decision is never forced by the evidence alone.

A programme is theoretically progressive if each new version of it has excess empirical content over its predecessor — that is, it predicts novel facts that the predecessor did not predict and that were not used in constructing it. It is empirically progressive if some of that excess content is actually corroborated. And so long as a programme keeps accommodating the facts it was built to explain and solving the puzzles it sets for itself, its practitioners are entitled to keep going. When theoretical growth falls behind the growth of the belt — when the programme is only patching up after the fact, adding ad hoc hypotheses that predict no new facts — it is degenerating, and Lakatos says a degenerating programme should be given up, since continuing to work on it is no longer rational; he admits that scientists often keep going anyway but treats that as a lapse.

The classic illustration is Prout's hypothesis that the atomic weights of the elements are whole-number multiples of the weight of hydrogen. Careful measurements clashed with it — chlorine came out around 35.45 — and the hypothesis was widely regarded as refuted. But the anomalies were absorbed by the belt (assumptions about impurities in the samples, about how atomic weights should be measured) and later explained by the existence of isotopes. Lakatos's point is that the apparently decisive refutation was not decisive: what had been refuted was one version of the programme with a particular belt, and a programme can be revived when the belt is rebuilt. This is what separates his sophisticated falsificationism from the naive variety: for Lakatos a theory is falsified only when a rival theory has been proposed that has excess empirical content over it, explains its previous success, and has some of its excess content corroborated. Falsification is a relation between rival theories, not between a theory and a basic statement. It's also why he's more lenient than Popper with budding programmes — you can't judge a young programme by its early failures, and a programme shouldn't be killed off by a single crucial experiment.
