I write about science and ideas for a general readership, and there is a pattern I keep meeting in my reporting for which I have no proper term. I should like the term if one exists, but what I really want is the analysis, because I have to explain the thing in print and cannot do it with hand-waving.

Here is the sort of case I mean. A biologist tells me that the larva of a certain beetle gives off a scent that dupes a particular species of ant into carrying it into the nest, and calls this the larva's way of getting itself fed and protected; the larva, needless to say, understands none of it. Or someone explains that a gazelle stots — springs vertically rather than running — in order to advertise to the pursuing cheetah that it is fit and not worth chasing; the gazelle is not trying to say anything to anybody. Or a botanist observes that the arrangement of an orchid's petals ensures that the single moth which pollinates it cannot avoid picking up a packet of pollen on the way in.

In each of these the purpose-language is not decoration. Strike out the 'in order to' and something is lost from the explanation: you can no longer account for why the trait is there, or why it has the shape it has. And yet there is evidently no one, anywhere, doing the intending — no mind holding the reason. The rationale appears to be real, and to be doing explanatory work, without belonging to anyone. I have a suspicion that this is a recognised problem in the philosophy of biology and that it has a name, but I have never been able to recover it.

What I need, then, is the concept, and its edges. If there is an established term, give it to me with a definition; if there is none, coin one and define it. Either way I want to know precisely what the phenomenon is and what it is not, and in particular how it is to be distinguished from each of the following:

- the merely metaphorical use of purpose-language, where saying that the sun 'wants' to set explains nothing;
- the ascription of a function to a part or a process, as when we say that the heart's job is to pump blood;
- the invisible-hand sort of explanation familiar from economics, where an outcome is nobody's intention but every party to it is nonetheless an agent with reasons of their own;
- and the reasons we might attribute to a thermostat or a chess program, where there was a designer who had the reason first.

Does the concept require natural selection, or does it extend to artefacts? Is the rationale supposed to be a genuine fact about the world, or a useful fiction we tell ourselves? Where does a trait that once did something and no longer does — a vestige — fall? And what would be a case right at the boundary, so that I can tell in my own writing when I am using the idea legitimately and when I am smuggling in a purpose that is not there?

I do not need a reading list. Please answer in British English.
