I am a high-school junior and I have a fifteen-minute slot at our spring philosophy symposium, followed by about twenty minutes of questions, and I would like you to prepare me for that question period. Please take the role of a philosopher of science who has spent years coaching speakers through hostile Q&A; before you write anything, think carefully about what each person in the room will actually hear when I say the word confirmation, brainstorm their priors and their pet moves, and then give me a prep sheet rather than a defence. My audience, roughly in order of how much they will speak: our club's faculty adviser, a retired chemistry teacher and a committed Popperian who will say the whole thing is a confusion because a white shoe forbids nothing and therefore tests nothing; two club members who took an online Bayesian statistics unit last summer and are convinced a likelihood ratio makes the paradox evaporate as a mere illusion of salience; one member who has read Quine and thinks confirmation is a folk notion to be replaced rather than repaired; and, because we invited the science department, one physics teacher who will almost certainly announce that this is word games about grammar and that nobody doing real science has ever needed a theory of confirmation. There will also be two or three students with no background who will ask the one question I have no idea how to answer.

My thesis, which you should treat as the target for every objection, is that the raven paradox is not really a paradox about ravens but a symptom of forcing confirmation into two places. I claim that a statement of the form "this evidence confirms that hypothesis" is always elliptical for something like "this evidence confirms that hypothesis rather than the alternatives in contrast class C"; that Hempel's conditions of adequacy, the entailment condition, the consequence condition and especially the equivalence condition, are each true for some choices of C and false for others; and that the paradox therefore trades on an equivocation about what the hypothesis is being contrasted with, since the white shoe does raise the probability of "all ravens are black" against a contrast class of colour-swapped hypotheses and does nothing against the contrast class "all ravens are black versus all ravens are not black". More aggressively, I want to argue that the equivalence condition cannot be kept in its unrestricted form, and that this only looks like cheating because we are used to treating a hypothesis as a single sentence rather than as a partition. My private worry is that I have stated all of this too quickly, that a clever Bayesian will show that my contrast class is doing all the work while itself being unprincipled, and that the Popperian will say I have merely relocated the problem into a parameter I invented.

Please give me the six to eight objections I will actually get from these particular people, ordered by how much damage they would do if I fumbled them, each phrased the way that person would phrase it in their own vocabulary, each with a reply I can deliver in two minutes or less, and each with a candid note on where the reply is soft or where I am bluffing. Tell me what I should concede up front before anyone forces me to, which single reply has to land for the talk to survive, and finish with the objection I am hoping nobody asks, together with my best honest answer to it. Where someone in the audience is likely to be right and I am likely to be wrong, say so plainly instead of flattering my position.
