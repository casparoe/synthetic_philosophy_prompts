I got appointed as a lay magistrate in my town last year — I'm not a lawyer, no law degree, I just sit on the bench one day a week with two others and we handle the small stuff, shoplifting, drunk driving, little assaults, people breaching court orders. I have to decide sentances and write a short reason for each one, and a legal advisor looks over what we do afterwards. I get maybe fifteen minutes a case and there's often a dozen on the list.

Ive been reading around on my own about what punishment is even for, and I've landed on a rule I want to actually hold myself to, but I can't work out how to use it when I'm staring at a file. Here's the rule:

- Making someone suffer is only ever allowed because they deserve it. The fact that punishing them might scare off other people, or straighten them out, or make the victim feel better — none of that can ever be a reason to give someone MORE than they deserve. Those things can only push the sentence down, never up.
- What someone deserves is set by what they actually knew they were risking when they did it, from where they stood at the time — not by how bad it looks now with the whole file in front of me.

Two things I can't get past. First, when I'm actually doing it I can't tell the difference between following the rule and just going with my gut and dressing it up afterwards. Second, a friend who used to work in the courts told me that what really gets punished is the person, not the act — the priors, the attitude, whether they're a repeat customer — and I can't tell if that's a hole in my rule or just a hard part of applying it, since most of my list has priors.

Can you turn the rule into a procedure I could actually run in fifteen minutes a case? I want the questions in order, what a real answer to each one looks like as opposed to me waving my hands, the point where the answer comes out no and I just stop, and the point where I have to admit it's a value call and own it. Then run it on these, and don't soften it when the procedure lands somewhere other than where I'd have gone on my own:

1) a 19-year-old who nicked a bike, sold it for cash, two priors for the same thing, no job.
2) a 45-year-old nurse, first offence, blew over the limit driving home, nobody hurt — but she'll lose her licence and probably her job, and she's the one holding the family together.
3) a bloke who's been in front of us five times for petty theft, always pays the fine, never says sorry, and something about him makes me want to give him the maximum.

Also tell me straight what the procedure can't do — where it'll misfire, and the kind of case where I shouldn't pretend it's giving me an answer. I'm not going to read a textbook about this, I need something I can run at the bench.
