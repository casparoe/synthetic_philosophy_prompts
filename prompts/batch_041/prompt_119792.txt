I run a small thing at the local library once a month — about ten adults, none of us with any philosophy training, 75 minutes, a flipchart and bad coffee — and I've gotten myself into a hole. Last time someone asked about free will and it went surprisingly well, so I promised the group we'd do the question of whether your thoughts actually do anything. I've been chewing on it for weeks now and I can't get the shape of it straight. Here's the knot as I understand it: the physical stuff in your brain is already enough on its own to make your arm move — if you knew the whole physical story you wouldn't need anything else to say why the arm went up — and your thoughts and feelings are supposed to be somehow based in that physical stuff rather than floating free of it. But then what's left for the thought to do? The physical story was already complete without it, and you can't have two separate things both fully causing the same movement unless one of them is redundant. I don't know who first put this together, I read about it somewhere and it's been following me around. I want to teach it properly and I've realised I don't even know what order the pieces have to go in — the bit about the physical world being closed off and complete in itself, the bit about thoughts being based in the brain, the bit about the brain cause being enough by itself, the bit where you decide whether the thought is redundant or the physical thing is. Which of those has to come first for the last step to feel earned instead of sprung on them?

I had two things I was planning to lean on and I'm now suspicious of both. For the example that carries the whole session I was going to use the night I cancelled a trip because of a panic attack — the fear on one side, the racing heart and the adrenaline on the other, me not getting on the plane. But I worry it makes the fear sound like nothing more than a nickname for the chemistry, which lets everybody off the hook way too early. And for the analogy I was going to say it's like a firing squad, two shooters and either one would have done the job, so who cares which one pulled the trigger — except something tells me that's cheating, that the two shooters are genuinely separate things in a way my fear and my brain aren't. Tell me if either of those can carry it, and if not, what the example has to have and what it mustn't have (I have a feeling the answer involves the two causes not being independent, but I can't say why that matters). Also the thing I'm most worried about: they'll hear the conclusion as the mind being spooky and non-physical, or as this being an argument about determinism, or as the flat announcement that their thoughts don't matter so nothing matters — and I can see each of those killing the room dead if it lands at the wrong moment.

So don't explain the argument to me, I mostly have it — plan the teaching of it. The order the components have to go in and why each depends on the last, the one case to build the whole session on and what it has to include and leave out, the misconceptions they'll walk in with and the ones they'll form along the way, and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off before it sets. Plus one question I can put to the group at the end that would actually show me whether they've got the problem or just learned to say the words. And be honest about what I should just refuse to cover in 75 minutes and tell them I'm refusing — I'd rather leave a clean gap than a muddy one.
