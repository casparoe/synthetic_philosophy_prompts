I teach a small undergraduate seminar on free will and practical reason, and for the final session I like to set a piece of fiction that stages a philosophical puzzle rather than merely illustrating one. I would be grateful, therefore, if you would write me a short story of roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words. The puzzle I have in mind is the old one, commonly called Buridan's ass, about an agent who must choose between two options that are exactly equal in every respect that could give her a reason to prefer one of them; the standard telling leaves her paralysed, but I have never found the starving donkey itself especially interesting, so please do not retell it. I would like the story instead to be set in a world that has taken the puzzle seriously as a problem of administration. In that world there is a small and rather proud institution — call it the Bureau of Indifferent Cases — whose officers are summoned precisely when a decision must be taken and no reason favours either side: which of two identical claimants receives the last course of a scarce medicine, which of two identical routes a convoy takes, which of two identical bells is rung at noon. The Bureau's creed is that its officers must decide without preferring; they are forbidden to break the symmetry by any consideration of merit, and are trained instead to hold no motive at all. I would like the story to follow a single officer through a single case, and to let the philosophical difficulty emerge through the characters' arguments rather than through exposition. In particular, please include at least one scene in which two characters disagree about whether what the officer does can count as a choice at all: one insisting that any actual decision, however it is dressed up, betrays a preference — if not for one option, then for decisiveness, or for symmetry, or for the avoidance of some outcome — and the other insisting that a decision not settled by any reason is not a decision but an event, like a coin coming down heads.

A few constraints on how you handle the material. Please do not have characters cite, summarise or name real philosophers, and do not put arguments into the mouths of historical authorities: I want the positions to stand on their own, and I would rather not risk a story that misrepresents someone's view. The setting should be concrete and institutional — I picture something like a provincial customs house or a municipal registry, with ledgers, forms, waiting rooms and a hierarchy of clerks — and the stakes of the case should be real enough that the officer's cultivated indifference costs somebody something. Equally, the story should not resolve the puzzle too neatly; a neat resolution is exactly what the students will be asked to argue with.

After the story, please add a short note of no more than a paragraph indicating where the story takes liberties with the puzzle or leans on a contested reading of it — for instance, if it tacitly assumes that indifference is unstable, or that an undetermined decision must be arbitrary — and stating how confident you are in the philosophical claims the story ends up making, singling out the point you are least sure of.
