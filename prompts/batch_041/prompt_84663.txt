For the past four years I have been writing a novel set in the late Bronze Age Aegean, and the research has left me with a cluster of convictions about historical knowledge that I would like audited before I commit them to an essay. I will set them out flatly.

(1) There is a fact of the matter about the distant past. Statements about what happened are true or false independently of whether any trace survives; the past is determinate even where the record is not.

(2) The credibility of any report about the distant past is a function of its proximity, in time and in culture, to the events reported, and of its independence from the other reports I am using. Two sources that agree corroborate one another only once I have shown that they do not descend from a common origin; a late source that cannot be traced back to a contemporary one is worth nothing to me. I take this in the spirit of the old rule that one should proportion belief in testimony to what one can independently check.

(3) At the same time I am convinced that a society without writing can carry accurate historical information for many centuries — genealogies, boundaries, ecological knowledge, the memory of a flood or a migration — and that where such traditions have been transmitted under stable institutions they are evidence of the first rank, not picturesque filler.

(4) Where the record fails me, I invent. But I hold my inventions to a discipline: nothing I make up may contradict what is established, and in my notes I mark plainly what is invention and what is testimony.

(5) I have come to think that the historian and I are engaged in one activity rather than two. Both of us colligate — that is, both of us take a heap of surviving traces and group them under a story-form, a concept, or a plot that the traces themselves do not dictate. The difference between us is one of degree.

(6) And yet I insist that my novel asserts nothing. When I depart from the record I am not saying anything false, because I am not saying anything at all.

(7) I refuse to let my characters know what happened next, and I hold that the historian who writes as though the agents could see the outcome has committed a real error rather than a stylistic lapse.

I suspect this package does not hang together, but I cannot see where the fault lines run, and I am too close to it to trust my own sense of which parts are load-bearing. Please audit it. Tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, rank the genuine ones by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad, and for each say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would cost me. Where a pair is in apparent tension only, name the distinction that dissolves it. Finally, tell me which single revision would resolve the most.
