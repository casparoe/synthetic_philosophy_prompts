A colleague in our collective-action reading group leans on the following analogy and treats it as obvious: a public announcement is like a broadcast — one signal, received simultaneously by everyone in range, so that each listener knows that every other listener heard it too. That, he says, is all common knowledge amounts to, and it is why public acts (a televised pledge, a coronation, an announcement in the village square) secure coordination where private assurances fail; he extends the point further, claiming the same picture explains how the players' rationality becomes common knowledge in a game. I suspect the analogy is carrying conclusions it cannot support, but I cannot locate where the load is being borne. Please give me the correspondence table — what stands in for the transmitter, the signal, the audience, reception — then set out where the mapping breaks, including what the broadcast picture quietly assumes about the reliability of reception, about whether each listener knows who else is listening, and about how many levels of the regress actually have to be in place for his examples to work. Say finally whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether a better analogy is available for the work he wants done.
