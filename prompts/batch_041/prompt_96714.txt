Four years into a novel, I have reached the point where its premise has to stop being a mood and become a rule. The premise, as I am using it, is Hofstadter's: an "I" is not an object but a level-crossing pattern — a loop a brain runs — and such patterns are not confined to the brain that runs them. They get rebuilt, in degrees, in other brains, so that one person can be partly resident in another; this is the claim he pushes furthest in I Am a Strange Loop, written after his wife's death, where the dead are said to persist in proportion to how richly the living model them. I take that as true inside the fiction. What I need from you is a procedure, not an exposition.

I decide this question at my desk, scene by scene: which of my characters actually carries which other character, and how much. There are perhaps thirty such relationships in the book and more in the revisions, and the same call has to come out the same way twice. It matters because the ending turns on whether a particular death is a death, and because the moral accounting of the whole novel — what the survivors owe, what they are guilty of, whether anyone can be said to have killed more than a body — is currently being decided by whichever way I happen to be feeling on a given afternoon. I have read the book once, years ago, and I am not going to re-read it mid-draft; give me something I can run in ten minutes with the manuscript open. No citations, no reading list; my editor signs off on consistency, not on scholarship.

What I want:

- An ordered set of questions to ask about a pair of characters, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each and how the answers combine into a verdict. I do not want a yes or no: the whole point of the premise is that residence comes in degrees, so give me three or four graded verdicts and say what each licenses in narration and in what the characters can sincerely say to one another.
- Explicit stopping points: the questions whose answer ends the inquiry with a no, and the ones where the procedure must hand the decision back to me because it is a question about what I want the book to mean, not about what the theory implies.
- A test of the vocabulary I have been groping toward, which I suspect is half-right. I have been asking whether a host reproduces the other's pattern with fidelity, whether the modelling was reciprocal while both were alive (he modelled her back, so the loop was mutual), and whether the host can address the pattern in the second person — speak to it, be answered in its voice, be embarrassed in front of it. Tell me which of these three is doing real work, which is a redescription of another, and what is missing.
- Worked applications to three relationships from my draft: a widow who barely attended to her husband's mind while he lived and now reconstructs him constantly, more faithfully than she ever knew him; a hostile critic who despised the dead man, read every word of him with contempt, and cannot stop arguing with him in his head; and a machine — in my book, a scholar's device trained on a dead writer's letters and journals — that produces new sentences in his voice. I expect to dislike at least one of your verdicts, and I would rather dislike it now than discover it in print.
- An honest note on where the procedure will misfire. I can already see one trap: if residence is a matter of degree, the procedure will quietly tell me that killing a person richly carried by others destroys less than killing a solitary one, and I am not sure my book can live with that. Say plainly which steps are consequences of the premise and which are stipulations I am choosing for the fiction, and do not dress up the second kind as the first.

Under 1,000 words, including the worked cases.
