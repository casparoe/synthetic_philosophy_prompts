Last night a friend and I argued for the better part of two hours about whether moral disagreement is ever a positive-sum game, and I want to build a class around the version of the question we kept circling. Our disagreement was this. She claimed that whenever two people with different value systems have to share a decision, there is usually a trade available that leaves both better off by their own lights -- I concede on the thing you rank highest and you concede on the thing I rank highest, and we both end up ahead of the status quo, exactly as two countries gain from trade. I claimed that this picture smuggles in something false: that our commitments come in separable units with exchange rates, when in fact a commitment is often constitutive of the rest of one's outlook, so that trading it away does not merely cost you one item, it dissolves the ground on which you valued the item you received in return. We never resolved it, partly because we could not agree on what "better off by their own lights" even measures once the trade itself changes the lights.

I teach a graduate seminar in moral and political philosophy -- fourteen students, one ninety-minute session, and they will have read a little on value pluralism but nothing formal. Please design a runnable in-class activity that makes them feel the force of the gains-from-trade picture before they feel the force of the objections to it, so that the objections land as discoveries rather than as caveats. Concretely, I want the following to happen in the room, in roughly this order: (i) they should find, and be able to point to, at least one exchange that genuinely leaves every party better off by that party's own stated ranking, so that the Pareto-improvement idea feels earned rather than asserted; (ii) they should then run into the problem that the exchange presupposes their values are modular and commensurable, and that some of the things they most care about refuse to behave as tradable units; (iii) at least some of them should notice that a rule permitting such trades rewards anyone who inflates or radicalizes a position in order to have something to sell, and should have to decide whether that is a defect of the mechanism or of the people; and (iv) they should end up unable to say cleanly whether the trades they made were gains at all.

Design the whole thing: the setting and materials, how the value profiles or positions are assigned, the rules of the exchange and what makes an agreement binding, a way to represent a stake that does not require real money, and the sequence of rounds. The idea must show up in the mechanics -- the rules and incentives should drive them into the puzzle on their own -- and the names of any philosophical positions should be withheld until the debrief. Then give me the debrief: which moments in what happened map onto which parts of the gains-from-trade argument and onto which objections (commensurability, alienability, the endogeneity of preferences under trade, integrity, strategic misrepresentation), the order to raise them in, and the questions to ask so the mapping is drawn out of them rather than announced. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- which students will free-ride or refuse on principle, where the arithmetic will fail to convince anyone, how a coalition can quietly exclude someone -- and how to handle each. Finally, be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will simply have to supply in speech: I expect there is something about the first-person texture of selling out, and something about whether value systems are commensurable at all, that no set of rules can stage.

Format the response as a facilitator's plan I can run from directly, with the rounds as numbered steps and the debrief as a script with questions. End with a single final line of the form \answer{...} containing, in one short phrase, the one thing the facilitator must supply by talking because the activity cannot make participants feel it.
