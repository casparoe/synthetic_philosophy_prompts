I'd like a case built to order, and I'd rather it come out uncomfortable than tidy.

Three accounts of what makes an act corrupt, stated flatly so that we don't have to agree about any literature first:

- The office account: corruption is the misuse of a public office for private gain, judged against the duties the office formally carries.
- The impartiality account: corruption is the substitution of a personal tie for an impersonal standard in the allocation of something the allocator holds in trust. No private gain is required, and the standard need not be written down anywhere.
- The local-norm account: an act is corrupt only if it violates the norms the relevant community actually sustains around the office. Gift-giving that discharges a recognized social obligation is not corruption on this view, even when it breaks the formal rule, because the formal rule and the community's norm are two descriptions of a single practice.

The case is for settling something I can't settle by introspection: whether the standard I would apply to a gift between an office-holder and a supplicant is anything more than the norm of the place I happen to be from, wearing a universal-sounding name.

Specification. Build two cases in which the impartiality account and the local-norm account give opposite verdicts on whether the transaction is corrupt, each verdict falling out of its own account rather than being asserted. The cases must be identical in every respect but one: whether the community sustains a norm endorsing the practice. Hold fixed the transaction, its effect on the allocation of the office's resources, what each party expects in return, the office's formal duties, the actors' own conviction that they are discharging an obligation rather than evading one, and the fact that in both cases the exchange belongs to a standing network rather than being a one-off. Invent the setting: no real country's statutes, no ethnography, no citations. One paragraph per case, and keep the office a working civil service in a functioning state rather than a kleptocracy in a failed one. Say plainly who bears the costs of the practice and whether those people are among the ones whose norm it is.

Then do the check. Give each account's verdict and the step in its reasoning that produces it. Name the single feature doing the work, and say what happens when I remove it: do the accounts converge, or does the case only establish that corruption is everywhere? Say whether the impartiality verdict survives the version where the community endorses the practice, or whether it quietly reimports the community's norm as the standard. Say whether the network structure — as opposed to a one-off gift — moves either verdict, and if it does, which account is embarrassed by the move. Then give the strongest attack on the case from each side, marking whether it is a repair or an admission. I don't want the answer that the accounts are complementary; I want to see the case force a verdict.

Finish with the move I care about most. The local-norm account can condemn a practice only if the community already disapproves of it, but communities are not monolithic, and the practice may violate a norm the same community endorses elsewhere — say, that a gift creates a debt the recipient must be able to repay in kind, which the supplicant cannot. Tell me whether that internal-criticism move rescues the universalist or is relativism with extra steps, and what would have to be true of a community for the answer to go the other way.
