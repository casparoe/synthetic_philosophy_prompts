I'd like a poem that earns its keep by doing philosophy rather than by gesturing at it. The subject is causation by omission, and I want the form to carry the argument, not merely decorate it.

The knot is this. A child drowns because the people on the bank did not throw the rope; we say the omission caused the death and hold someone answerable for it. But an omission is an absence, and it is not obvious that absences can enter into causal relations at all — if they cannot, then either our causal talk here is loose, or something other than causation is doing the work of assigning fault. Suppose absences can be causes. Then the natural test — the death would not have happened had the rope been thrown — licenses far too much: it makes a cause of every absent condition without which the child would have lived, the rope's not being thrown alongside the whistle's not sounding, the current's not slackening, the passerby's not being a stronger swimmer. The counterfactual test alone cannot say which of these absences is the cause, and the answer we actually give seems to depend on a norm, on what someone was supposed to do — which threatens to smuggle the responsibility in ahead of the metaphysics instead of deriving it from them. One rival move is to make causation contrastive: not "the omission caused the death" but "the omission rather than the rescue caused the death rather than the survival," so that the relata were never the bare events. A third device, adjacent to the second, is double prevention: a cause that consists in disabling a preventer, so that the causal chain runs through an absence twice over.

Write a sestina. Let the form be the argument. The six end-words should be a small closed set of words of non-occurrence or deferral — absences, nothings, stills, waits — so that the poem can permute its whole vocabulary forever and never introduce the one term that would have saved the child: the form's lexicon is missing exactly what the scene is missing. Let no line-end name a positive action. Choose a scene concrete enough to be plain — a drowning, a fire, a patient left in a corridor — and keep it inside the grammar of what did not happen. Admit, once and only once, an explicit norm ("one ought to," "it was hers to"), and let that line be the turn of the poem: the moment the reader sees that an absence becomes a cause only after a rule has already told us which absence to look at. In the tornada, try to name the cause and either fail or name the norm instead; leave unresolved whether the omission was a cause or only the occasion for a judgment of blame. A reader who knows the debate should be able to tell which of the three positions the poem is leaning on and where it is straining them; a reader who does not should still find it a poem.

After the poem, add a short prose note — a few sentences — saying where it takes liberties or leans on a contested reading: in particular, whether it presumes that absences can be causes at all, whether it presumes that norms do the selecting among them, and whether the contrastive or double-prevention reading it uses is bearing more weight than the position can honestly bear. Close the note by saying what would change the poem's implicit verdict: which position on omission causation, if it proved correct, would make the central conceit false, and what the poem would have to become in its place.
