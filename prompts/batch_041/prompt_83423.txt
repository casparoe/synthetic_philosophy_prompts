I work on the metaphysics of time, and I am preparing a two-page proposal for my faculty's internal research fund, which would buy me one semester of partial release — roughly six months at a third of my time — to produce a single substantial paper. I would like you to write that proposal. The idea I want to pitch grew out of a metaphysics seminar I have been teaching. We spent a session on the argument, usually credited to Rietdijk and to Putnam, that the relativity of simultaneity gives us the block universe: if simultaneity is frame-relative, then any event space-like separated from here-now is simultaneous with here-now according to some inertial frame; each observer therefore has a different three-dimensional slice of spacetime as their present; and if each of those slices is real, we are already committed to a four-dimensional reality in which the future is as real as the past. A student pushed back with the reply I had been raised on: that "now" is an indexical, so the argument confuses the fact that every observer has a present with the claim that some one present is metaphysically privileged. I defended that reply badly, and afterwards I realised I do not believe it. "Here" is an indexical in the same sense, and nobody thinks that shows that only my current location is real, or that realism about distant places rests on a confusion about the word "here." So the indexicality objection cannot be doing the work it is advertised to do unless there is an asymmetry between the spatial and the temporal case; and if there is one, it is almost never stated explicitly. My working hypothesis — and I would be happy for the project to end by refuting it — is that the objection runs together two very different complaints: a deflationary point about the semantics of tensed sentences, which is harmless, and a substantive point about which regions of spacetime are "given" to an observer, which is precisely what is at issue. If the hypothesis holds, the argument is considerably stronger than its critics allow, and the usual talk of a "local" notion of simultaneity is a redescription of the problem rather than a solution to it; if it fails, I want to know exactly which premise of the argument the critic is committed to denying, and whether denying it costs anything elsewhere. There is a second strand I want the project to carry: the intermediate positions that keep the relativity of simultaneity and the reality of the past while treating the open future as more than an epistemic matter. In Minkowski spacetime there is no global surface to be the present, so these views struggle even to say what is open and open to whom, and I want the proposal to explain why that is a threat to the coherence of objective becoming rather than a technical inconvenience, and to sketch what a defensible version of such a view would have to look like.

Please write the proposal in five named sections, in this order: "The question," stating precisely what is to be decided and what would settle it (about 200 words); "Why the question is open," explaining why the familiar replies do not close it, described as positions and arguments in plain terms rather than by citation (about 350 words); "Approach and method," saying what I will actually do, with a rough division of the six months into stages and a note on how the two strands connect (about 400 words); "What success and failure would look like," giving the possible outcomes, what would decide between them, and an honest statement of what would make the project a failure rather than a success (about 250 words); and "Scope, timeline, and risks," including what would have to be cut if the project runs long (about 200 words). The panel that reads these is mixed — historians, a couple of scientists, and one economist — so the proposal has to make a case for why conceptual work makes progress here and why I am not promising empirical results I cannot deliver. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations; refer to views by description and I will supply the references myself.
