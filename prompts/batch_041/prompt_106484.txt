I'm in a human rights reading group and we keep having the same fight, so I'd like to settle it for myself. Someone raises an atrocity — usually one where a group was driven off its land rather than rounded up and shot — and someone else says: it's the intent that makes it genocide, not the numbers. The point being, they only wanted the territory (or to scatter them, or to punish them), they weren't trying to wipe the group out as such, so it's ethnic cleansing or a crime against humanity, not genocide. I've also seen the same line used the other way, to insist that a tiny case counts because the intent was there.

What I want is a breakdown of what that one-liner could actually be claiming. Some things I half-know and would like you to fold in, since I don't trust my memory on them:

- The definition says the intent has to be to destroy the group "in whole or in part", and I'm told courts have read "in part" as meaning at least a "substantial part" — which sounds like numbers creeping back in through the quantifier.
- The man who coined the word apparently treated the destruction of a group's culture and language as central, and that got dropped when the treaty was actually drafted. So "intent to destroy" may mean two different things depending on whether you're talking about the coined concept or the codified one.
- Someone in the group said tribunals don't agree on how strong the intent has to be — aiming at the destruction, versus seeing it as a near-certain consequence, versus merely knowing it would follow — and that when there's no smoking gun they infer intent from the scale and pattern of what happened, which again sounds like numbers doing the work.

So: lay out the distinct precise claims "it's intent, not numbers" could be making, from the trivially-true-by-definition ones up through the radical ones, say for each whether it's plausible and whether it would actually support the move it was used for in that argument, and tell me which reading the person probably means. If some of the readings make the slogan a tautology dressed up as a criterion, say so. Don't bother with citations or reading lists, I'll chase those down myself.

Finish with a last line beginning "Verdict:" that names, in one sentence, the reading you'd bet on.
