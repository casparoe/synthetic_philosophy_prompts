For a philosophy club project I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about rational stopping, and I would like you to help me find the principle behind my verdicts. I have been thinking about the idea of satisficing, that is, settling for an option that is good enough rather than hunting for the best, and about how much searching is warranted before one settles, and my intuitions do not line up the way I expected them to. Here are the cases, with my honest verdicts. Grabbing the first cereal box that is cheap and not too sugary: fine, and I would not think better of someone who compared all fourteen brands. Spending forty-five minutes comparing reviews before buying a six-hundred-dollar laptop: fine, though I would not insist on it. Spending two hours comparing cases for a twelve-dollar phone: not fine; it strikes me as a small failure of self-management even though nothing is harmed. A hiring committee that stops at the first applicant who clearly clears the bar, rather than reading every remaining file: fine. A person buying a house who takes the first one that meets their list without looking at a single other property: not fine, and not merely because the sums are large, since it feels like a failure to take the decision seriously. A doctor who orders three tests, decides the picture is clear enough to treat, and declines a fourth test that is cheap, quick, and would modestly raise the chance of catching something: fine; ordering it would not have been wrong, but I do not think the doctor was being irrational. A student who writes a college essay, revises it twice, and stops, though one more pass would probably improve it slightly: not fine, and I feel this more strongly than I can justify. A chess player with two minutes on the clock who plays a solid move rather than finding the best one: fine. A safety engineer who signs off on the first design that passes the minimum standard, when a cheap extra check would reveal a serious flaw: not fine. Taking the first parking space I see at the far end of the lot: fine.

My provisional principle is a marginal one: stopping is rational exactly when the expected benefit of one more look is smaller than its expected cost, so that further searching would not pay for itself. I like it because it seems to explain the phone case, where the possible gain is pennies and the cost is two hours, and the laptop case, where the possible gain is real and the cost is small, and the parking space. But it seems to invert two of my verdicts at once. The doctor's fourth test is cheap and has a real chance of mattering, so the marginal principle tells me the doctor should have ordered it, yet I think declining was fine. The student's third revision is also cheap, but the chance that it changes anything important is small, so the marginal principle tells me the student was fine to stop, yet I think stopping was not fine. I have tried patching this by saying that professionals answer to a standard of care while private individuals answer to themselves, or that the doctor's duty is to the whole clinic rather than to this one patient, but both patches feel like excuses invented after the fact, and the second one seems to make the doctor's stopping merely lucky rather than rational.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of principles, that yields all of my verdicts, including the doctor's and the student's, without collapsing into a list of special cases? Please state each candidate precisely, tell me which case forces each clause, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. I would also like the principle applied to two cases I have not described: a friend who accepts the first college offer that meets her criteria without waiting to hear from two schools she prefers but has not yet heard from, and a novelist who stops revising after the fifth draft. Finally, the case I keep going back and forth on is my own: last spring I spent most of a weekend reading reviews before choosing a twenty-five-dollar pair of headphones, and I still think that was fine, because I enjoyed the reading, but by every principle I can state it looks worse than the doctor's fourth test, which I also think was fine. Tell me whether I have to give up one of those two verdicts.
