I have been working through chapter 16 of Spinoza's Tractatus Theologico-Politicus and keep losing my footing in one stretch of it. Rather than risk reconstructing a translator's wording from memory, I shall set out below what I take the passage to be arguing, in my own words, and ask you to help me see what is actually going on in it. The passage is the part where Spinoza moves from his account of natural right to his account of the state, and then to his remark that democracy is the most natural form of commonwealth.

As I render it, the argument runs roughly thus. Spinoza begins from the claim that natural right is coextensive with power: whatever anyone has the power to do, and to the extent that he has the power, he has the right to do it, whether he is guided by reason or by blind appetite. He then says that this right is not abolished by the institution of a state but reconstituted within it. Men contract together and hand over their power to a common authority, and the right of that authority is measured by the power actually at its disposal — so that a sovereign whose commands outrun what his subjects can be brought to comply with has, to that extent, no right over them. From this he concludes that the transfer can never be absolute, since no one can genuinely strip himself of the faculty of judgement; and he observes that a commonwealth is held together not by fear alone but by the hope of advantage and the shared good that lead men to obey. He then argues that democracy is the most natural form of state, because in it the power of the state just is the power of the multitude, so that the state's right is least diminished by the transfer.

Three things about this stretch defeat me.

First, if right is coextensive with power, I cannot see what work the notion of transfer is doing. Power is not the sort of thing one can literally hand over; and if the sovereign's right is simply the power he in fact has, then the contract appears to be doing no explanatory work at all, and the whole apparatus of pact and transfer looks like a decorative way of restating the initial identity. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a sense of transfer that is doing real work here — and if so, what is it?

Second, I cannot decide whether the claim that democracy is the most natural form of state is descriptive or normative. If it means that democracy is the form in which the state's right is least diminished, then it is a claim about the maximisation of power, and it seems to sit oddly with the suggestion that natural right is indifferent between reason and appetite. If instead it means that democracy best answers to some standard of natural equality, then I do not see where in the argument that standard is established rather than assumed. I suspect Spinoza is running two thoughts together here, and I would like you to separate them, if they can be separated, and say which one the argument actually supports.

Third, the retained right of judgement troubles me. Spinoza seems to hold both that subjects are bound to obey, and that there is an inner domain of judgement that no transfer can reach. I cannot tell whether this is a substantive limit on sovereign authority, a merely psychological observation about what men can be made to believe, or a quiet concession that later becomes the argument for freedom of thought. Does the passage commit him to a right of resistance, or only to the impossibility of total obedience, and does the difference matter for how the argument should be read?

Please help me read this stretch properly. Say precisely where my paraphrase distorts the argument, distinguish the senses in which he is using power, right, nature and transfer, explain how the several claims I have listed are meant to hang together, and tell me what the passage commits Spinoza to that he might not have wanted to accept. I am not asking for secondary literature or for a list of things to read; I want the argument itself, reconstructed carefully, with the weak joints identified and the interpretive options stated in a way I can then assess myself. If some of my difficulties arise from my own paraphrase rather than from Spinoza, say so plainly. Please write your answer in British English.
