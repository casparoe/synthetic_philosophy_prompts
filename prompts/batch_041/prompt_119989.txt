A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the omnipotence paradox, and we each ended up defending a position neither of us had seen spelled out anywhere. I defended what I will call the maximality view: omnipotence is a property a being has at a time relative to a fixed repertoire of tasks, and it means that for any task that is intrinsically possible and not self-undermining — not, that is, a task whose own description is inconsistent, like making a stone one cannot lift, or a being more powerful than oneself — the being can do it. On this view the stone puzzle is not solved so much as dissolved: there was never a task there to be performed. My friend defended a self-expansion view: any account that fixes the repertoire at a time makes the being's power hostage to a contingent inventory of tasks, which is itself a kind of limit; better to say that omnipotence is not a maximum but an unboundedness — whatever limitation you can specify, the being can exceed it, including limits it used to have, so that it makes the stone and then makes itself able to lift it, then makes a heavier stone, and so on without end, and can even revise what counts as possible in the first place.

Write the dialogue between us, each character arguing the position above and responding to the other's actual points rather than talking past them. Let them press at least these questions: whether the maximality view makes omnipotence a fact about the world's repertoire of tasks rather than about the being, so that a being could be omnipotent by accident; whether an ascent that is never completed deserves the name of omnipotence at all, given that at every moment the being has some definite power level and there is something it cannot yet do — the way the natural numbers have no maximum while every one of them is finite; whether self-undermining tasks are contradictions or genuine tasks with impossible conditions; whether the self-expansion view covertly needs a resistant world for its ascent to be meaningful, which would undercut the claim that the power is unbounded; and whether a being that can shed its own essential properties is still a being with an identity. Neither side should be a strawman, and neither should cite any literature or historical figure — the argument should stand on its own.

End with a two-sentence verdict from the narrator naming which position came out ahead and the single consideration that decided it. Keep the whole thing under 850 words.
