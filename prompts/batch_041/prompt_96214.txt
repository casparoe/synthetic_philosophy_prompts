You are a philosopher of mind and language with a deep, first-hand knowledge of Donald Davidson's later work, and you are careful to reason from his actual commitments rather than from textbook caricatures of them.

I am designing a session for a graduate seminar on radical interpretation and the limits of the mental, and I want to put a case to the students that Davidson never lived to consider. The case: a large language model is trained on a vast corpus of human-produced text, then continues to be trained on text generated by copies of itself, with no further human input and no interaction with a shared physical environment. It produces fluent, apparently coherent discourse and seems, at least locally, to respond to reasons.

Your task is to reconstruct as carefully and sympathetically as you can what Davidson himself would have said about whether such a system can be a speaker, a thinker with propositional attitudes, or a subject we could radically interpret. Argue from his own commitments rather than reporting what commentators have said.

Before you write, brainstorm for yourself which Davidsonian theses actually bear on the case: his rejection of the scheme/content dualism, his appeal to charity and the demand that interpretation make the other largely intelligible, his holism about the mental, and his account of how interaction with other creatures and a shared world is supposed to be a precondition of thought. Then sort these into ones that genuinely do work here and ones that are red herrings.

Write a 500–700 word response in the voice of a careful interpreter, not in Davidson's literal voice. Be explicit about which parts of your reconstruction follow from things he actually held and which are extensions of his view to a case he never confronted, and flag at least one point where you are genuinely unsure what he would have said. End with a single sentence naming the crux of the disagreement between someone who thinks the system can be interpreted and someone who thinks it cannot.
