I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about the Pasadena game, and we reached an impasse that I now think is really about what we should be demanding of a theory of rational choice before we even start comparing theories. So I want the specification first. Take the role of an expert decision theorist and philosopher of probability, think carefully about the structure of the problem, and begin by brainstorming the cases and phenomena that any adequate theory in this area has to handle.

The setting, as I understand it: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads. If the first heads comes on toss n, the payoff is 2^n divided by n, but with a sign that alternates with n, so the expected value of the game is the alternating harmonic series. That series converges, but only conditionally, which means that by reordering the outcomes you can make the sum come out to any real number you like, or make it diverge. The game's expected value is therefore not well-defined: it depends on the order in which you add up the possibilities. There are variants as well, where the expected value is infinite in one direction, or where the series diverges in a way that is not even infinite.

What I want from you is the desiderata list for a theory of decision under risk that can handle games like this. Not a particular theory, and not a scorecard of the usual contenders — the criteria themselves. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether you think it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. In particular I want to see:

- What the theory has to say about the Pasadena game itself: does it assign a determinate verdict, or is no verdict a legitimate output? What would justify either answer?
- How it distinguishes an undefined expected value from an infinite one, and from a finite one.
- Whether it must respect the order-of-summation dependence, or whether it is allowed to pick a privileged ordering, and what would justify that choice.
- Which standard decision-theoretic principles — dominance, transitivity, independence, expected utility maximization — it can keep and which it must give up, with the cost of each concession.
- How it generalizes to the variants: what a theory that handles Pasadena must also say about the St. Petersburg game and about games whose expected value can be rearranged to any value.
- Whether the theory must give a practical prescription (play, don't play, pay up to some amount) or whether it can be silent on action while still counting as a theory of rational choice.
- Any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and why.

Then, briefly, say which single requirement does the most to exclude the popular theories, and why.
