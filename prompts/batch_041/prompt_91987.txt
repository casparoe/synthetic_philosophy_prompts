I report on science and philosophy for a general-readership monthly, and I am stuck on a dispute I walked into while researching a feature on the four-grade scheme of minds — the familiar sorting of creatures into Darwinian ones, whose behaviour is fixed by natural selection; Skinnerian ones, which can be reshaped by reinforcement within their own lifetime; Popperian ones, which can run a trial in imagination so that the hypothesis perishes instead of the creature; and Gregorian ones, which add to all this a stock of inherited thinking tools, language foremost among them. My two principal interviewees both accept the four labels and can each tell you what belongs in each grade; what they cannot agree on is what the labels are labelling, and each has asked me to give the other a fair hearing.

On the first view, the grades form a genuine ladder: each rung is a real increase in the creature's independence from its environment, and the scheme earns its keep through the further claim that every grade is implemented by the machinery of the one beneath it, so that a Popperian creature is still, underneath, a Skinnerian and a Darwinian one. On the second view, the scheme conflates two quite different questions — where the trial and error happens, and what kind of mind is doing it — and once those are prised apart the ladder dissolves into a continuum: on this reading, Popperian is just Skinnerian learning made cheap enough to run off-line, and Gregorian names not a further internalisation of selection but a change in the medium — culture, text, institution — in which the results of selection are stored and handed on. I would like you to write the dialogue I wish I could have recorded, in which each side presses the other exactly where they divide: whether the off-line trial is a difference in kind or only in cost, whether inheriting tools is a cognitive capacity at all or merely a fact about a population, and whether the whole scheme is a taxonomy of minds or a description of where selection happens to be lodged.

One thing I would like them to take seriously, because neither of my interviewees will, and because I suspect it is either the scheme's undoing or its crown: I keep wanting to add a fifth rung for creatures that never run their own trials at all but live off trials run by others, letting other creatures' hypotheses perish in their stead, with the selection lodged in norms, markets and archives rather than in any individual. I am told this is either already what Gregorian means or else a change of subject rather than a new grade. Have them settle which it is, and use as a test case the contemporary language models — things that appear to possess every inherited tool and no obvious lifetime of trial and error of their own — asking whether such a creature refutes the ladder or illustrates it. Neither speaker should be a strawman, and neither should concede a point merely to be agreeable; each should answer the other's actual arguments rather than restating a position.
