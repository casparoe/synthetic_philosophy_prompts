I am a trustee of a small charitable foundation that funds work on the long-term risks of artificial intelligence, and we have spent the last few months arguing about our mission statement. A colleague has proposed that we adopt coherent extrapolated volition as our guiding standard: that we should act not on what our grantees or the public now say they want, but on what they would want if they knew more, thought faster, were more the people they wished they were, and had grown up together. I am drawn to the ambition of the idea but uneasy about its application, and the OpenAI board crisis of late 2023 has become our test case. The board, after all, claimed to be acting not for shareholders but for the mission, and when it dismissed Sam Altman it cited a lack of consistent candour; when the staff and investors revolted, the board reversed itself. I would like you to analyse that episode through the lens of coherent extrapolated volition and the related family of idealized-preference accounts, rather than through the usual corporate-governance or power-politics reading.

What I want is a careful mapping: who are the subjects whose volition is to be extrapolated -- humanity, OpenAI's employees, the board itself, the beneficiaries of its mission? What is the extrapolation procedure supposed to be, and does the board's fiduciary duty to a nonprofit mission conflict with it or instantiate it? What would a CEV standard have told the board to do at each stage -- before the firing, during the staff revolt, and at the reinstatement -- and how does that differ from what an idealized-preference account (say, one that asks what the relevant parties would prefer under fuller information and greater coherence) would have told them? Then I want the reverse direction: what does the episode reveal about the feasibility of CEV as a decision standard? Does the board's failure show that CEV is indeterminate, or that it was simply misapplied, or that no institution can be trusted to extrapolate anyone's volition but its own? Finally, tell me which of the two standards -- coherent extrapolated volition or a more individualistic idealized-preference account -- is more defensible for a foundation like mine, and what the episode suggests about the limits of both. Please write your answer in British English.
