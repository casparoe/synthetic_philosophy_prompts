I had a long argument last night with a close friend, and it ended in the kind of stalemate where neither of us could say precisely what the other had got wrong. The subject was personal identity, and it started from something small: a mutual acquaintance of ours, a man who did something genuinely cruel in his thirties — nothing criminal, but the sort of thing that ought to follow a person — and who then, over about a decade, rebuilt himself. New city, new marriage, a great deal of therapy, and, as far as anyone can tell, a sincere and stable change of character. He now describes his earlier self in the third person, almost as a different man, and he is not being coy when he does it; he seems actually to have reorganized his life around a story in which that person is a character who is no longer him. Our friend circle has more or less accepted the story. But when the old business resurfaces — as it did recently, when someone from that period reappeared — nobody, including the man himself, treats it as a matter concerning a stranger. He answers for it. He is the one who is embarrassed, the one who owes something, the one whose account of himself is now in question.

What I kept noticing, and what I could not put a name to, is the gap between two things that both get called "the self." On the one hand there is the self as the protagonist of an ongoing story: something that is constituted by the telling, that can be revised, that is co-authored by the person and by everyone around him, and about which there may be no fact of the matter — two incompatible tellings of the same life can each be honest and neither simply false. On the other hand there is the self as the one who answers: the bearer of the deed, fixed by some kind of causal and historical continuity that no amount of narration can sever, such that a man who sincerely narrates himself as a new person is still, for purposes of owing and being owed, the old one. These two things are usually discussed as though they were the same self, and I think that is what made our argument go in circles. My friend's position was that if the first picture is right — if the self really is just the story, with no hidden thing underneath doing the being — then the second picture has to go too, and with it any notion that the man still owes anything to the people he hurt. We should, he said, stop telling the part of the story in which he is still on the hook. I found this too quick, but every time I tried to say why, I found myself appealing to the very "real self underneath" that I had just agreed does not exist.

So my question is whether the thing I am circling has an established name and an established treatment, or whether I have merely reinvented a distinction that already has a home. Is there a recognized concept for the specific divergence between the narrative self — the revisable, co-authored, possibly fiction-like protagonist — and the answerable self — the bearer of the ledger, whose continuity is not up to the story? Does any existing account of the narrative self actually secure the second without smuggling the first back in, or do the major views simply collapse the two, and if so, is that collapse a feature or the central problem? If there is a name, I want to know what it is and, more importantly, how the concept is carved: what exactly falls on each side, whether the gap is a genuine structural feature or an artifact of mixing two vocabularies, and what the boundary cases look like — the person with dementia who no longer recognizes the deeds attributed to her, the child soldier, the dissociative patient, the ordinary person who simply refuses to own something and is not thereby released. If there is no name, I would like a term coined and defined, with the boundaries drawn against the nearest things it is not: it is plainly not just the old problem of personal identity over time, since the continuity here is not in question; and it is not the ordinary distinction between identity and responsibility, because that distinction is usually drawn as though both terms were already settled. What I am after is the relation between them when the first is allowed to be a story all the way down.
