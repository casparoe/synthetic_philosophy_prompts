Three months ago I was drawn by lot to sit on my city's first citizens' assembly, and the experience has turned me from a skeptic into an advocate. Next week the council has invited me — as the assembly's designated spokesperson — to argue that the assembly should be given binding authority over a defined set of local decisions, rather than the purely advisory role it has now.

The proposal I'll put in front of them has three parts: a fixed list of questions the assembly decides (land use, the participatory budget's priorities, and any proposed change to the council's own procedures); a two-stage lottery in which one randomly drawn panel frames the question and selects the expert witnesses while a second, separately drawn panel deliberates and votes; and, standing in for electoral accountability, mandatory rotation, published reasoned decisions, and a council override requiring a two-thirds majority plus a written justification.

The room will be mostly long-serving elected councillors — several with fifteen or more years in office, whose sense of legitimacy rests entirely on having been voted in — together with a couple of council officers from legal and finance who will care about cost, feasibility, and whether a council can bind itself and its successors, and one or two members already sympathetic to mini-publics.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get from this room, not the ones I'd get from a political theory seminar. Order them by how likely and how damaging they are, phrase each the way these people would phrase it, and give me a two-minute reply for each with a note on where the reply is weak. Tell me what to concede up front, which single objection would sink the proposal if it landed cleanly, and include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
