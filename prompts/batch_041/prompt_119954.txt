My wifes family is Singaporean and we go over most years, and the one thing I still can't get my head around is the MP clinics. Every week, in every constituency, the MP sits in a room for a few hours and people queue up with their actual problems — my mums hospital bill is wrong, the ceiling in the flat above is leaking into mine, my kid didn't get the school we wanted — and the MP writes a letter off to the agency, and more often than not the thing gets sorted. When I ask people there why they vote the way they do, or why it doesn't bother them that the same party has run the whole show for as long as anyone can remember, this is what they bring up. A friend who lived in Beijing for years says there's something a bit like it there too, ordinary people writing letters and petitions up the chain, and every so often someone higher up actually acts on it, and that people point to that as a big part of why the system is thought to work even though nobody gets to throw the government out.

Here's what I'm trying to work out. If the whole case for a government like that is that it delivers — good roads, safe streets, money in your pocket, no chaos — then why bother with any of this? A government that's judged on what it produces doesn't need to hear from me about my ceiling. Building a whole machine for listening to individual complaints seems to say something different, something more like the government has to answer to the people it governs, and that its right to rule depends on being responsive and not only on being effective. So I want you to tell me what view of legitimacy this arrangement actually presupposes. Does it assume that delivery is what makes rule legitimate, that being answerable to the governed is what makes it legitimate, or is there some third thing going on — that the clinics are really just a way of buying loyalty and the honest description is 'whatever keeps us in power'? And I'd like to know whether the practice is even consistent with itself, because to me it looks like it's cashing out two different ideas at once and hoping nobody notices.

I'm not a philosophy person, but somewhere I read that people split this into a government having an actual right to rule, so that its people owe it obedience, versus a government merely being defensible or better than the alternatives, so that you have no good reason to overthrow it but it also has no claim on you. Which of those is this practice really assuming — or can it get by with neither, and just be a story about incentives? And push it the other way for me: if you decided the right answer was the opposite one, that only the consent or authorisation of the governed makes rule legitimate and that delivery counts for nothing, what would concretely have to change in how a place like Singapore is actually run? Would the clinics go, or would they just stop counting as a reason?
