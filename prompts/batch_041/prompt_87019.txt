I would like to know whether the following has an established name, though a name alone would not satisfy me: what I am really after is the correct division of the thing.

The pattern is this. In each of the following cases, someone arrives at a general verdict about the trustworthiness of a way of forming beliefs, and the evidence for that verdict consists of particular beliefs formed in that very way.

(a) A man concludes that he is a good judge of character. His evidence is his record: here he read someone correctly, there he was not taken in. Each item on the record is a deliverance of the very judgement being assessed, and he has no other route to how those encounters turned out.

(b) A woman is confident that her proofreading is reliable, because the documents she sends out contain very few errors. That a given document contains an error is something she knows by proofreading it.

(c) Asked how he knows he is not dreaming the conversation he is having, a man answers that he can see and hear his interlocutor, who is plainly there in front of him.

These three feel to me like a single shape, yet they are plainly not equally good or equally bad. In (a) the verdict is built by piling up many occasions; in (b) there is one document and one act of reading; in (c) nothing is accumulated at all and the answer is given in a single step. I am told that all three are simply circular reasoning, which cannot be right in the ordinary sense, since no premise restates the conclusion and in (a) every premise may be true. I am also told that the only real complaint is that the person cannot justify trusting anything at all, which strikes me as a different complaint.

What I want is the following. Give me the established name, if there is one, for this family — the move of establishing the trustworthiness of a way of forming beliefs by means of beliefs formed that way. If the name covers all three cases, tell me what makes them one thing despite the differences I have listed. If it covers only some, tell me where the family divides, what the parts are called, and which of my three is the odd one out. If nothing established covers the residue, coin a term: define it, say what it excludes, and give me one case that resembles the pattern without being it and one that looks innocent while being it. Finally, in the cases that really are defective, say whether the defect lies in the belief itself or only in the person's ability to answer a challenge, because I suspect those come apart and that this is what decides which of my cases belong together.
