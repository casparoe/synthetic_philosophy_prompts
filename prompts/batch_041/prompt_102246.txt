I work as an applied statistician and probabilistic programmer: I build and fit generative models, I think natively in terms of sample spaces, random variables, sigma-algebras, pushforward measures, reparameterization and Jacobians, and I have essentially no training in philosophy. I have run into Bertrand's paradox -- the "random chord" problem, in which an apparently innocent phrase turns out to admit the familiar three different answers depending on how the chord is generated -- and I want to understand it entirely inside my own vocabulary rather than in the philosophers'.

Please take the principle of indifference, together with the dispute over whether Bertrand's paradox shows that principle to be incoherent or merely in need of a more careful formulation, and restate that dispute as faithfully as you can in the language of measure-theoretic probability and probabilistic programming: sampling procedures, parameterizations and reparameterizations, the Jacobian of a change of variables, pushforward measures, well-posedness of a generative model, and invariance under a group action. Use whatever of that machinery is genuinely apt and say plainly where it runs out.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophical disagreement translate cleanly -- for instance, is "chosen at random" simply the demand that a generative model be fully specified, and is Bertrand's puzzle nothing more than the observation that "a random chord" names a whole family of models rather than one model? Which parts translate only with distortion? And which parts have no counterpart at all in my vocabulary? I am especially interested in one thing. The principle of indifference, as the philosophers deploy it, seems to carry normative force: it purports to tell you which prior you ought to adopt, not merely to catalog the ones you could adopt. My framework, as far as I can tell, is purely descriptive -- a measure either exists and is well-defined or it doesn't, and "uniform" only ever means "uniform with respect to some already-chosen measure." So I want to know whether that normative force has any expression in the translation at all, and, if the invariance requirement turns out to be underdetermined (say, because the symmetry group of the problem is not rich enough to single out a unique invariant measure), whether that underdetermination is a feature of the mathematics or of the original principle. Do not smuggle measure-theoretic assumptions in and then present them as translations of the philosophers' claims; keep the two vocabularies honest with each other, and treat whatever fails to carry over as the most interesting part of the answer.

Conclude with a single final line beginning "Verdict:" that states, in one sentence, whether the residue is a defect of the measure-theoretic framework or evidence that the principle of indifference was never well-posed to begin with.
