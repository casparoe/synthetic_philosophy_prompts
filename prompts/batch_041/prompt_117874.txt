I'm a third-year philosophy PhD student working mostly on formal epistemology and decision theory, and I'm supposed to lead a reading group session next week on the two-envelope paradox — just a handout and some discussion questions, nothing ambitious — but the more I try to write it up the less sure I am that I actually understand why anyone thinks it's a paradox. Here's where I've landed. The envelopes are symmetric, so before you open anything your expected value is the same whichever one you're holding; the famous 1.25x calculation therefore has to contain some fallacy, probably that it treats the amount in your envelope as a fixed quantity when it's really a random variable, or else it just hands itself probabilities it isn't entitled to. And since that symmetry is there from the very start, I can't see how opening the envelope could change anything — sure, you now know the number, but a second ago the two envelopes were interchangeable, so switching still can't be to your advantage. So my honest view is that it isn't a real paradox, it's a mistake that keeps getting made, and I'm a bit puzzled why its still written about. Is that too quick?

What's really bugging me is that I tried to make the setup concrete to check myself and got a number I don't trust. Say the experimenter flips a fair coin: heads, the envelopes get 1 and 2; tails, they get 2 and 4. You pick one at random and open it, and it's 2. If I do the conditioning carefully — the two ways of ending up holding a 2 look equally likely, one from the {1,2} pair and one from the {2,4} pair — then the other envelope is 1 half the time and 4 half the time, which averages to 2.5, so switching looks like it gains me 0.5. That definately can't be right, given the symmetry point above, so either I've set the posterior up wrong or the example is somehow rigged so that the symmetry argument doesn't apply to it. Can you tell me where the error is? And more generally, what is the actual resolution — is it just the symmetry observation, or does the real work get done by something about the prior over the amounts, and if it's the latter, what exactly does that prior have to look like for the switching argument to go through? I have a vague sense the answer is that no well-defined distribution makes switching favorable at every amount, but I can't tell whether that's a real result or just something I half-remember, and I'd like to know whether the paradox is fully defused once you point at the setup being ill-posed, or whether there's still something genuinely puzzling left over that people in decision theory actually care about.
