I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've arrived at a view about preferences and utility that I'd like you to put under real pressure. Please take on the role of an expert in decision theory and the philosophy of economics — someone fluent both in the formal results and in the interpretive disputes around them — and reason carefully and systematically rather than giving me a tour of the literature. I'm not looking for reading suggestions or a survey; I want the substance of the case against the view.

The view is this. A person's preferences just are the dispositions their choices reveal, and utility is nothing over and above a convenient numerical summary of a sufficiently consistent pattern of choices. There is no further fact about what someone "really" wants that floats free of how they choose; a utility function represents a choice pattern rather than describing a hidden quantity waiting to be discovered. Rationality, on this reading, is a matter of internal consistency — completeness, transitivity, independence, and the rest of the standard axioms — not of tracking some good that exists independently of the choosing. I came to this from building ranking and recommendation systems, where "what the user wants" is operationally defined as what the user clicks, buys, or comes back to, and where a stable choice pattern is simply treated as ground truth. I like the view because it's austere and testable: it tells me exactly what evidence would settle a question about someone's preferences, and it refuses to traffic in mental states I can't observe.

What I want from you is a map of the view's exposure. Which observations, arguments, intuitions, or formal results should count against it? Sort them by how decisive they'd actually be — fatal, seriously damaging, merely awkward — and for each one say whether a committed defender would concede it as a genuine cost or would dismiss it as question-begging or as changing the subject. I'd also like to know what the view predicts that its rivals don't, so I can tell whether it's risking anything at all. If you conclude that it has been quietly qualified into unfalsifiability, say so plainly and tell me whether that's a vice for a view of this kind or simply the price of doing business.

A few specific cases have been nagging me, and I'd like them handled individually rather than swept into a general objection:

1. I once built a tool that inferred a user's utility function from their choices and displayed it back to them ("by your own choices, you rank X above Y above Z"). A meaningful fraction of users said that wasn't what they wanted, and then made choices that were just as internally consistent as before but pointed the other way. Both patterns satisfy the axioms; the person disowns the first. Is the disavowal a fact about their preferences that the choices alone couldn't capture, or is the second pattern simply a different preference and nothing has been lost?

2. Consider someone who decides everything by flipping a coin — not because they are evenly torn between options, but because they have no preferences at all among them. Their behavior is a perfectly well-defined choice function. Does the view have to attribute preferences to them? If it does, that looks like a reductio; if it doesn't, what in the behavior is doing the work of separating them from a genuinely torn chooser?

3. Consider people who sincerely report having no preference between two options, but who, when forced to choose, pick one — and would pick the same way again. This is behaviorally indistinguishable from holding a weak preference. Is "no preference, but forced to choose" a coherent state that the view simply cannot represent?

4. Consider the same option under two descriptions, where choices go opposite ways — say, a medical treatment described by its survival rate versus by its mortality rate. If the options are the same, the behavior is inconsistent and the person is irrational; if the descriptions make them different options, the view starts to look unfalsifiable, since any reversal can be redescribed as a change in what was chosen. (I suspect the classic intransitivity and money-pump arguments live or die with how you answer this, so please connect them.)

For what it's worth, I'd be most moved by an argument that the view renders an entire class of ordinary questions unaskable — that it can't even state the things we normally want to know about what's good for someone — and least moved by appeals to introspection, which I don't trust. Feel free to tell me that my priors about what should count as evidence are themselves part of the problem.
