I referee for a small combinatorics-and-games journal and I also write the puzzle page for a maths magazine, and about once a month something lands on my desk whose whole engine is a strategy-stealing argument: the author doesn't exhibit a winning strategy, they just show that the second player can't have one, point out that the game is finite, perfect-information and drawless, so somebody's got to win, and it's the first player, done. Maybe half of these are sound and half are quietly broken, and at the moment my method is to squint at them for an hour and then write either an acceptance or a grumpy report.

I'd like an actual procedure I can run, in order: the questions to ask, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with 'no, that isn't a strategy-stealing argument', where it stops with 'the conclusion is probably right but the author owes me an argument for step X', and where it hands the decision back to me because no test settles it. Please mark which of those steps are real tests and which are just taste wearing a lab coat. It has to deal with the bit I keep tripping over, the step where an extra stone, move or turn lying around is assumed never to hurt you. That step is doing all the work, half the papers assert it rather than argue for it, and I've definately got a nasty feeling it fails in exactly the games I find hardest: inverted win conditions, games where you have to move and can't pass, games with more than two players, games that can be drawn or that can go on forever, games where your own pieces get in your own way. I'd also like it to say what a proof like this is worth when it does work, given that it hands me a strategy I could never actually play. Is that enough for a referee report? Is it enough for the puzzle page, where the reader is being asked to find the thing? Probably not the same answer, and I want to know why.

Then run the procedure on three cases so I can see the wheels turn: a Hex-style connection game on a finite board, where the first player colours some cell and then plays as if they were the second player; a misère take-away game, where someone has claimed the same argument goes through; and a third where the honest verdict goes against what I'd like it to be — you pick it, but I want to see the procedure tell me the argument is broken when the conclusion is true, or the reverse. Keep the cases concrete enough that I can follow the steps with a pencil.

Finish by telling me what the procedure can't do. I don't want to walk away thinking a clean checklist means a clean paper. No references needed, I'll sort those out myself.
