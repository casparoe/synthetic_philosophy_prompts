Last night a friend and I argued, well past the point of good judgment, about a third friend of ours, and I would like to know which of us had the better of it. The friend in question has what I take to be a straightforwardly circular set of preferences: over three options — call them A, B, and C — he prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A, and he will act on each of those preferences when the corresponding pair is put to him by itself. I insisted that this is not merely eccentric but self-contradictory, in the same way as believing both p and not-p, and I offered the standard demonstration. Begin with him holding C. Offer to exchange C for B for a small fee; since he prefers B to C, he pays. He now holds B; offer to exchange B for A for the same fee; again he pays. He now holds A; offer to exchange A back for C; he pays a third time. He is exactly where he started, holding C, having parted with three fees — a sequence of trades that he accepted at every step has left him strictly worse off, which, I said, is something that could never happen to a coherent agent, and so the cycle is not merely imprudent but impossible to hold without contradiction. I even tried to tighten the point: because he would accept each swap if it were offered in isolation, the inconsistency is already in him before any trader arrives, and the trades merely make it visible. My friend would not concede. He said our friend could simply decline the swaps once he saw where they led, or could resolve in advance never to trade around a loop, and that a person who does so is being prudent rather than incoherent. I replied that this merely changes the choice situation and so dodges the argument, since at each moment the swap still presents itself as a strict improvement, and refusing it would mean not taking what he most prefers — which is precisely what we mean by calling a preference irrational. We could not settle it, and I have been turning it over since.

So, two questions. First, was I right that the money pump establishes that such a cycle is self-contradictory — a genuine logical impossibility, on a par with a contradiction — rather than a merely practical or prudential failing? Second, supposing my friend is mistaken and the cycle really is a defect, how is it to be repaired? Which of the three preferences is the erroneous one, and which should our friend give up or revise in order to restore coherence?
