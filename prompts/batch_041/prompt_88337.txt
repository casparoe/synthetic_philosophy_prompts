At a house party last weekend I ended up pinned in a corner for about two hours listening to two friends of mine — Maya and Dev, who'd never met before — go at each other about futarchy, and I walked away genuinely unable to tell who'd won. I'm not giving you my own take because I honestly don't have one; I just want to know who was landing punches. The gist of what Maya was pushing: instead of voting on policies, you vote on a single measurable goal (she kept using median income and life expectancy as examples), and then you let prediction markets decide which policies get adopted. Her one-line version was that you vote on what you want and bet on what will happen. She thinks the current system is a joke because voters are terrible forecasters while markets are good at aggregating scattered information, and the split means you never have to ask a trader to have good values or a voter to have good information.

Dev's counter was that the split is fake. Round one, he said the hard part was never the forecasting, it's picking the metric, and any metric you can actually write down is a value judgment in disguise — you're deciding whose wellbeing counts and how to trade off health against freedom against whatever else. Maya said fine, then vote on the metric too; at least it's explicit and revisable instead of being hidden in whatever gets a politician reelected. Round two, Dev said the second you point the whole apparatus at a number, everyone's incentive flips from improving the thing to improving the number — basically Goodhart's law — and composite indices are the worst offenders because the weights are a value choice nobody ever voted on. Maya came back that this is exactly what markets are for: if you manipulate the price you're just handing money to arbitrageurs, and that's the whole reason prediction markets self-correct. Round three, Dev said that argument only works for markets on facts that happen anyway; here the price is what causes the policy, so it's not a bet on an independent fact, it's a bet on your own bet, and anyone who profits from a policy has a straight-up incentive to move its price. He also said that to even ask whether a policy beats the status quo you need a conditional market — how would the metric look if we did X versus if we didn't — and those are a mess, because you can only ever settle the branch that actually happens, so the people who wanted X never really get scored on the branch where they were wrong. Maya said that's a liquidity and design problem, not a conceptual one, and that arbitrageurs plus subsidized liquidity would clean it up. Round four, Dev said it was circular at bottom: the market is asked to predict what happens if we do X, but we only do X because the market said so, so the thing being predicted is partly created by the prediction. Maya said all forecasting is like that, and the alternative is pundits and politicians who are just as self-referential but worse at it and unaccountable.

Can you go through this round by round and tell me, claim by claim, who's right and who's overreaching — rate each move from clearly wrong to clearly right with a short justification — and then say who's more right overall and what both of them are missing? Please keep it to the actual exchange; I don't want a general summary of futarchy, I want a referee's decision on the seperate points they made.
