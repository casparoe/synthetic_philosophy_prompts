A philosophy podcast I listen to recently ran an episode on Lewis Carroll's short dialogue between Achilles and a tortoise, and the presenter's summary of it has been bothering me. As best I can reproduce it, he said:

Carroll's tortoise proves that deductive reasoning can never be justified. Achilles and the tortoise agree on two premises about a triangle; Achilles invites the tortoise to draw the conclusion by modus ponens, and the tortoise declines. Achilles therefore writes down, as a third premise, the conditional that if the premises are true then the conclusion must be true. The tortoise accepts this and declines again; a fourth premise is added, then a fifth, and the process never terminates. This 'premise shuffle' shows that you cannot justify any inference without already relying on it, so deductive logic is circular and rests on a leap of faith. The honest conclusion is that deduction is a useful habit rather than a rational requirement, and that logical rules are conventions we may revise whenever they become inconvenient. The regress is peculiar to deduction, incidentally; induction is in better shape, since Bayesians have shown how to justify it.

I suspect the presenter has turned a puzzle about what it is to follow a rule into a refutation of something, but I cannot locate the seam, and I do not want to dismiss the whole thing if the underlying point is genuine. Please go through the summary clause by clause and tell me what a philosopher of logic would accept, what is a defensible reading presented as the only reading, and what is straightforwardly wrong. I am particularly interested in whether the premise shuffle really shows that any attempt to justify deduction must fail — I have a vague sense that the crux is a distinction between a premise and a rule of inference, and I should like to know whether that is the right way to take it — and whether the closing moral about revising logic, and the aside about induction, follow from anything true in the summary.
