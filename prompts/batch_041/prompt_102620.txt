I have spent the better part of a year working through Wittgenstein's remarks on religious belief on my own; I have no philosophical training and no academic reason to be doing this, and in two weeks I am to lead a two-hour discussion circle on the subject. The others are thoughtful general readers, none of whom has studied philosophy of religion, and most of them arrive with one of two reflexes: that religion is a matter of faith rather than evidence, or that Wittgenstein is the thinker who licensed the idea that religious claims are beyond criticism. Neither is where I want them to end up. Please do not explain Wittgenstein to me; I want a plan for how I should explain him to them.

The distinction I keep circling, and that I want the whole session built around, is one I have arrived at myself and am not sure how to stage. It is not the familiar contrast between believing something on weak evidence and believing it on strong evidence. It is the difference between a belief held as a hypothesis about how things stand — something evidence could in principle count for or against — and a belief that instead functions as the frame within which a person lives, so that the demand for evidence does not so much get answered as fail to find a foothold. My sense is that Wittgenstein's talk of the groundlessness of religious belief belongs to the second kind, and that the standard telling quietly converts it into the first, which is what makes him sound as though he were defending wishful thinking. I want the plan to make that distinction land without the audience concluding either that religious believers are being let off the hook or that anything goes.

Give me the order in which the pieces must come, and why each has to wait for the one before it. I can already see two traps in the sequencing — if the notion of a language game arrives before the audience has any grip on what it is for, they will hear relativism; if the hypothesis/frame distinction arrives before they have seen a belief that is neither evidential nor arbitrary, they will hear irrationalism — but tell me where else the order can go wrong, and exactly where in the sequence each misreading should be headed off.

For the carrying example I have been trying to use two people who both affirm that there will be a final judgment: one who means it as a prediction about a future event, the other for whom it is instead something like a standing way of holding himself accountable. Tell me whether that example can carry the whole explanation or whether I should build on something else; if it can, what it must have and must lack for the distinction to come out cleanly, and if it cannot, precisely where it breaks. I am also unsure whether that one example can do double duty when I reach the point about the belief not being arbitrary, or whether I need a second, non-religious case for it.

Then the misconceptions. Assume the audience forms them in a predictable order; tell me what that order is, which ones they bring with them and which the explanation itself will breed, and where each should be met. I expect at least these: that Wittgenstein is saying religious beliefs cannot be criticised; that he is saying they are meaningless or merely emotional; that groundlessness means arbitrariness; and that his view amounts to being polite about religion rather than making a claim about what belief is. Correct me if I have left out the ones that matter.

Finally, end on nothing but this: one question I can put to the room that would show whether the distinction has actually landed or whether they have merely learned to repeat the vocabulary — and tell me plainly what I should refuse to explain at this level, and say so out loud rather than fudge it.
