Last semester a visiting speaker in our environmental history seminar ended a discussion of the cold, crop-failing summer of 1816 that followed the Tambora eruption with a line I have not been able to shake: "the volcano is as much a historical actor as the general." What he wanted from it was a demotion of human intention — the harvest failures, he said, were the eruption's doing, and historians who spend their time on the deliberations of ministers and the grievances of rioters are congratulating us on a story in which we are the protagonists. When I asked whether the political response to the failure was not also part of what made that year what it was, he repeated the sentence as though it had settled the matter. I don't think it settles anything, but I also can't say precisely what is wrong with it, because I can't say precisely what the sentence asserts.

What I want is a menu of the distinct claims that sentence could be making, ordered from the nearly trivial to the radical, with a verdict on each: which are platitudes nobody would deny, which are substantive and defensible, which are contentious but respectable, and which are simply false. Please organize the menu around the places where the sentence is ambiguous. What does "actor" mean here — bare causal efficacy, the kind of thing that makes a difference to how events turn out; action in the full sense, with intention and purpose; or the kind of thing that can be held answerable, praised or blamed? What is the force of "as much as" — that the volcano and the general are comparable in causal weight, that they belong in the same explanatory register, or that they are the same kind of thing? Is the claim about our explanations, that an adequate history must cite non-human causes, or about the world, that non-humans are agents? Does it range over this episode or over historical explanation generally? And where does it sit on the line between nature constraining what people can do and nature determining what they do?

Then the two things I care most about. First, which of these readings, if any, actually licenses the use he put the sentence to — the conclusion that questions about human deliberation and policy are beside the point for explaining what happened in 1816? I suspect only a fairly strong reading does, and that the strong reading is the false one, but I want that traced rather than asserted. Second, which readings slide into environmental determinism, and what exactly is the error in the slide — the inference from a cause's being necessary to human choices' being idle, from a cause's being large to its being decisive, or something else?

Please take a definite position rather than laying the options out and leaving the choice to me: say which reading you think is the most plausible reconstruction of what he meant, whether it survives scrutiny, and which readings you are prepared to call false. Where your answer depends on a contested view in the philosophy of history or the philosophy of action — about what agency requires, or about what a historical explanation has to do — say so, say which side you are on, and say why. No reading list needed.
