I am designing a ninety-minute session for a joint economics and philosophy doctoral workshop on the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem. The students have had a semester of game theory and are comfortable with dominant strategies and Nash equilibrium, but they have never seen social choice theory and none of them works on it. Two decisions about the session are already made, and both are slightly heterodox, so I want you to take a position on whether they work rather than quietly repairing them.

First, I want to try teaching the theorem backwards: open with the exits — restricting preferences to a single dimension, so that the outcome is the median reported peak; weakening the incentive requirement from dominant strategies to some equilibrium notion; admitting randomization; and cutting the alternatives to two — and let the theorem emerge as the statement of what remains once each exit is closed. Tell me whether that order can be made to work, and where in it the students will lose the thread.

Second, I want the session to turn on a distinction I have never managed to convey: between manipulability as a worst-case property of a rule and strategic voting as a feature of a situation. A rule can be manipulable in the formal sense even though no voter in a given situation ever gains by lying, and a strategy-proof rule can still produce outcomes that look like the product of manoeuvring. The theorem is a robustness result about rules, not a prediction about behaviour, and I want the students to leave with that.

Plan the session for me — do not explain the theorem to me. I need:

- the order in which the components must be introduced, with the dependence relations made explicit (what has to be in place before what, and why), including a verdict on the backwards opening;
- the single carrying example that should run through the whole session, what it must have in order to do its work, and why I should not use the obvious three-voter, three-alternative case if you think I should not;
- the misconceptions the students will arrive with or form, in the order they will surface, and the exact point in the sequence at which I should head each off. I expect at least: formal dictatorship read as political dictatorship; the theorem conflated with Arrow's; strategy-proofness read either as a requirement that people report honestly or as a prediction that they do; the belief that any of the exits above is free; and the conclusion that the theorem is a counsel of despair about democratic procedures;
- how much time to give to the hypotheses (at least three alternatives, unrestricted domain, and the range condition, that every alternative can win) and how to use the trivial counterexample of a rule that ignores the reports altogether;
- one closing question or task that will tell me whether the worst-case/actual-behaviour distinction landed, rather than whether they can state the theorem;
- what I should refuse to explain at this level, and what I should say to mark the refusal.

One substantive point I want the plan to take a position on: I take the equilibrium-based weakening of the incentive requirement to buy possibility only at the price of common knowledge of beliefs, so that it should appear in the session as a trade rather than a triumph. If that is right, say where in the sequence it belongs; if it is too crude, say so and replace it.

Give me the plan in prose with timings, as notes for myself. No readings and no citations; I will supply those.
