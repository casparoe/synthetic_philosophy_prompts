Until three weeks ago, Kavka's toxin puzzle was for me a parlour trick — something I could state in a sentence and shrug off. Then a relative made me an offer that turned out to be the puzzle in the flesh. She proposed to fund a long project of mine, and the condition she attached was not a promise, a contract, or a schedule but a state of mind: the money would be released if, and only if, I could, there and then, sincerely intend to carry the project through to completion. She was explicit that she could tell a real intention from a performed one and that a performance would buy me nothing; she was equally explicit that once the money was released, nothing further would be expected of me — no deliverable, no follow-up, no accounting. I spent an hour trying to summon the intention and could not. The fact that the intention itself was the price seemed to make it unbuyable, and I have not stopped turning the hour over since. I would like to know what to make of it.

I want your credence in a single proposition, and I want the reasoning decomposed rather than summarised:

P: The toxin puzzle, in its standard form — you are offered a large reward, paid immediately upon your forming the intention, if and only if you now intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow that will make you ill for a day but leave no lasting harm, with nothing further required of you once the reward is paid — has a rational solution. That is, there is a course of practical reasoning available to a normal, fully-informed, reflective agent by which forming that intention is either rationally required or at least rationally permissible, so that the agent can collect the reward without irrationality.

Please give me a number for P, then the decomposition: the sub-questions P depends on, a rough probability for each, and how they combine into your final figure. The sub-questions I can see are these, though you should add or split them as you think fit: (1) whether intentions are the sort of state that can be formed for reasons at all, or whether they resemble beliefs in being only indirectly controllable; (2) whether rationality attaches to actions, to intentions, or to extended plans and policies, so that the move of forming the intention and then deciding tomorrow can itself be assessed as rational or irrational; (3) whether the scenario is even coherent as stated — in particular, whether an intention the agent knows will not be acted upon can be the very thing the reward-giver is buying, or whether the puzzle trades on an equivocation between intending and merely predicting one's own future conduct; (4) whether the answer turns on how the reward-giver detects the intention — an infallible present-tense mind-reader, a probabilistic predictor of action, or an ordinary person relying on your word; and (5) whether there is a determinate fact of the matter here at all, or whether the case is under-described in a way that leaves P neither true nor false.

Then two further things. First, tell me which of those terms your number is most sensitive to, and what argument or piece of evidence would move it most. Second, tell me how much of your credence rides on the timing of the payment: if the reward were released only after the toxin had actually been drunk, so that no gap opens between intending and acting, would your number for P rise, fall, or hold, and by how much? Finally, say whether the hour I spent failing to form the intention is evidence for or against P, and how much weight it deserves.
