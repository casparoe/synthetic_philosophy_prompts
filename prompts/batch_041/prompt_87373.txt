I am a third-year philosophy student, and as part of a seminar on paradoxes and the epistemology of self-referential claims I have been asked to design and run a ninety-minute session for about sixteen of my peers in a standard seminar room, with a whiteboard, index cards, and a projector I will probably not use. My assigned topic is the surprise examination, and what I want the session to accomplish is that the group lives through the argument instead of hearing it summarized: I want them, at some point in the first half, to have talked themselves into the conclusion that the announced examination cannot possibly happen, and then to be confronted with the fact that it happens — and to feel that the collapse of their reasoning is not a sign that they were being stupid but something structural about knowledge, announcements, and time. Beyond that, I would like the activity to reach something the textbook version usually skips. The paradox appears to turn on what everyone knows about what everyone else knows, and on whether the announcer is genuinely choosing a day or merely leaving it to chance. So if you can arrange the mechanism so that the group itself discovers whether the backward induction needs the announcement to be common knowledge among them, and whether it survives an announcer who commits in advance to a genuinely random method of selection, that would be exactly the payoff I am after. I would much prefer that they arrive at these observations on their own; the names, the labels, and the literature can come afterwards, if at all.

Please give me a complete and runnable plan: the setting and materials; the rules and the exact sequence of rounds; the payoff scheme (no real money, but something the group will actually care about winning); any roles or secret instructions; the scripted announcements or sealed envelopes; a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes; and a debrief that maps each moment of what happened onto a part of the paradox — which move in the backward induction corresponds to which round, and where the group's confidence, the announcer's knowledge, and the common-knowledge condition each did their work. Be explicit about which version of the paradox you are staging and about the assumptions you are imposing on the announcer, because I suspect a good deal of the confusion in the literature comes from quietly sliding between a perfectly rational predictor, a chooser who actively wants to defeat the students' expectations, and a coin-flipper who has no such aim.

Finally, I need the honest parts. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: where the group will get stuck, which round will fall flat or overshoot, how to recover if the induction is triggered too early or not at all, and how to keep the whole thing from degenerating into a shouting match about whose reasoning was fallacious. And tell me frankly what the activity cannot reproduce — the feature of the paradox that no set of rules will make them feel, and that I will simply have to state myself.
