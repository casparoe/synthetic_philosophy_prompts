I run spiritual care for the palliative service at a mid-sized hospital, and two or three times a month a patient or a family member brings us a near-death experience and wants it treated as evidence about what happens after death: a woman who says she has seen the other side and now wants her do-not-resuscitate order reversed; a daughter who reports that her dying father sat up and described meeting his mother, and who takes this to settle the question; a man whose own cardiac arrest produced an experience he now cites when he asks us whether he should be afraid. Our team is not qualified to adjudicate anyone's metaphysics and will not pretend otherwise, but we cannot keep answering with a shrug either, because the claim is doing real work in the room — it steers treatment decisions, family conflict, and whether a patient can be comforted at all.

I want a decision procedure grounded in A. J. Ayer's treatment of this question. Ayer's criterion, in Language, Truth and Logic, was that a sentence carries factual meaning only if some possible experience could count for or against it; turned on survival of death, the criterion threatens to make the claim either a prediction whose verification conditions are obscure or not a factual claim at all. Ayer himself is the awkward illustration: in the late 1980s, after a near-death episode of his own, he reported the experience publicly, allowed that it had left him less certain than he had been that death is the end of him, and still treated it as possibly a hallucination of a distressed brain. A theorist with the strongest reasons to discount such reports, unable to fully discount his own — that is the shape of what my team actually faces, so the procedure has to be built for it rather than around it.

The procedure must sort what a patient or family member is asserting into the different things such reports can carry: a biographical fact about an experience that occurred, a prediction about what will happen to someone, a commitment that would make no experiential difference either way, and an expression of fear or hope that is not a claim at all. It should confront the strongest reply the family member has — that survival is verifiable after all, by the one who survives — and say whether Ayer's criterion absorbs that reply or explains it away. It should also say what the team is to do with the unexamined answer most families are relying on: what would count as the same person continuing, and whether memory, body, or character is doing the work. Then, in order: what to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops and hands the matter to a person (the patient's autonomy, a family's grief, a clinician's own conscience), and where it simply cannot help. It has to run in ten minutes at the bedside, be usable by a chaplain or a nurse with no philosophy training, and produce something that can be recorded in the chart.

Apply it to three cases: the patient who reverses her DNR after her experience; the daughter who cites her father's deathbed report as settling the question and presses the team toward continued aggressive treatment; and one case of your choosing in which the procedure returns an answer my team will not like. I expect that last to be a patient whose refusal of a treatment we believe would help rests on what he says he saw, and I want to see how the procedure handles a refusal it cannot argue with on the merits.

Include one step that turns the criterion back on the practitioner: before responding, the team member should ask whether the response they are about to give would change at all if the patient's claim about the afterlife were false. If it would not change, they are answering distress and not metaphysics, and should say so out loud. Close by naming what the procedure cannot do — it cannot tell anyone whether they survive death, and it should not try.
