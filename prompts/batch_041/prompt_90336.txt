I have come to accept a broadly Leibnizian position: that among the worlds a perfect creator could have actualized there is a unique best, that such a creator would actualize it, and that ours is therefore that world. I want to know what would count against it.

Let me be clear about what I am not asking for. I do not want the standard problem of evil, or the familiar replies to it. I already grant that the position can absorb any particular instance of suffering by holding that it is a necessary means to some greater good, or the least costly way of securing the whole's optimum — and I recognize that a reply with that shape can be fitted to any evidence whatsoever. That is precisely what worries me, and I want the answer to take the worry seriously rather than restate it.

So apply pressure where the position is less elastic. Consider, first, the suggestion that there may be no best possible world at all: that for any world one can describe a better, as for any number there is a greater, so that the definite description "the best possible world" has no referent and the claim that ours is it is not false but empty. Second, the thought that even if an optimum exists, worlds may not be ranked on a single scale of value, so that the creator's situation is one of maximality rather than of a maximum, and the word "best" is doing illegitimate work. Third, the suspicion that the position's real engine is not any claim about goodness but the principle of sufficient reason, so that it stands or falls with a principle that carries independent costs. Fourth, the possibility that the position leaves the creator no freedom worth the name: if only one world is worth actualizing, what could it mean to say the creator might have done otherwise, and does that tell against the position or only against a picture of divine freedom we should abandon anyway? I am also interested in a distinction I suspect the position needs and may not be able to sustain — between the best world that is possible, in the sense of being constructible from mutually compossible parts, and the best world we can conceive — since a defender may insist that "best" is measured by maximal variety together with maximal order rather than by aggregate happiness, and if so, the first two objections may be aimed at the wrong target.

Rank these objections by how decisive they would be for someone who starts where I do. Which would be fatal, which merely awkward, and which the Leibnizian ought to concede as counting against the position rather than explaining away? Tell me what the position predicts that its rivals do not, and what its defenders themselves would admit as evidence against it. Finally, give me a candid verdict: does the position as I have stated it risk anything at all, or has its capacity to absorb suffering made it immune to every world we could observe? If it is immune, say so plainly, and say whether that is a defect in a view of this kind or simply the price of a thesis about the actual world — and tell me which of the considerations above you would find most persuasive if you were in my position.
