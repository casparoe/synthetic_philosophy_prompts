I'm a philosophy of physics prof (measurement problem, mostly), and I've somehow agreed to do a "physics at the movies" night for our department open house, and I've picked Everything Everywhere All at Once — it's the one multiverse movie my students have all actually seen, and I think it gets the *shape* of branching right even where it gets everything else wrong. I want to use it to teach the Born rule problem, and I need a reading I can defend in front of colleagues, not the usual "ooh, quantum weirdness" stuff.

So take the role of a philosopher of physics who works on the measurement problem and has also thought seriously about how films make arguments, and think this through step by step. Before you commit to a reading, brainstorm on the page a bit — what is the film actually committed to about branching? Because "every life choice spawns a universe" is doing a lot of work in that script, and I'm not convinced it's the same premise Everett needs.

Threads I'd like you to pull on. First, verse-jumping: you can only hop by doing something bizarre and statistically wildly unlikely for you in your current branch, so probability shows up in the film as a resource, something a person spends in order to get somewhere. Meanwhile the whole fight in the Everett literature is whether you can get probabilities out of a deterministic Schrödinger evolution at all — the decision-theoretic derivations from rational betting, the typicality/self-locating-uncertainty moves, and the objection that every one of them quietly assumes a measure it was supposed to derive. So does the film presuppose a measure that the interpretation it's dramatizing can't supply? Second, the Everything Bagel: Jobu's nihilism looks like the nihilism of total branch-inclusion — every possibility equally real, therefore none of them weighing anything — and I want you to say whether that's a decent popular rendering of the measure problem or just existentialism with the physics as decoration. Third, the ending, where Evelyn stops jumping and stays in one life with one daughter: is that a cop-out, a film that needs a preferred branch in order to be able to end, or is it the film noticing something real that the many-worlds literature keeps having to stipulate?

But be a good critic in both directions. Don't flatten the film into an illustration of a physics debate, and don't bend the physics to make the film look smart. If your honest verdict is that the multiverse here is a narrative machine and the Born rule is simply not the film's business, say that, and then tell me what a class could still take away from watching it. Flag anywhere you think I'm over-reading a plot point, and if you're fuzzy on a detail of the film, say so rather than inventing it — I'd much rather have an honest gap than a confident fabrication.

Definately no bibliography, no reading list, no citations. Assume you can't look anything up and neither can I, so argue from the ideas themselves rather than from who said what. 700–900 words, and don't spend it summarizing the plot — I've seen it, and so have you.
