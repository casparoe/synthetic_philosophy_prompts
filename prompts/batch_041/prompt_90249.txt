Secessionist movements in liberal democracies tend to make their claim in a single shape: a majority within a territory may take that territory out of the larger state, and it may do so as an exercise of a general right of self-determination rather than as a remedy for any particular wrong. Scotland's nationalists, Quebec's sovereigntists, and Catalonia's independence movement have all pressed the claim in roughly this form. Take the claim at its strongest and see what it does when you run it downward.

In October 1995, Quebecers came within about 54,000 votes of voting to leave Canada. A few days before that vote, the Cree of northern Quebec held their own referendum on whether they should remain in Canada if Quebec left, and more than ninety-six per cent voted that they should; the Cree treated their own consent as a condition of any transfer of their territory from one sovereign to another. Had the province-wide vote gone the other way by a handful of ballots, would the northern two-thirds of Quebec's landmass have gone with it, or would Canada's border have stayed where it was? The question reappears one level down. If Quebec may leave Canada on a majority vote, why may the West Island of Montreal not leave Quebec on the same vote in order to stay in Canada? The satirical Tabarnia movement, which proposed as a reductio of Catalan nationalism that the coastal provinces around Barcelona secede from an independent Catalonia and remain in Spain, is funny precisely because nobody has a settled answer. Something similar can be asked of Scotland's northern islands, which voted against independence in larger proportions than the country as a whole.

The puzzle is not whether nations have rights; it is what shape the right has. It forces a choice among options that are usually left vague:

1. The right belongs to "peoples," and the regress stops wherever that word happens to fit.
2. The right belongs to any group capable of governing itself, so it runs all the way down until no territory is left to take.
3. The right is remedial only, and since Quebec, Scotland, and Catalonia are not oppressed in the relevant sense, none of them ever had it.
4. The right is primary but the exiting unit's borders are not — those borders were drawn by the very union being left, and so are open to partition and renegotiation.
5. There are no group rights at all, only the individual's right to emigrate; "secession" is simply many individuals leaving at once.
6. The right is primary but defeasible, and a dissenting sub-region is answered not by denying its claim but by granting it something short of statehood — internal self-government, special status, asymmetrical federalism — thereby protecting the same interest at lower cost.

Write an essay that defends one of these and explains why the others fail. Do the analytical work rather than gesturing at it. Which candidate principle is actually doing the work, and which are merely restating the conclusion? Is "people" doing any independent labour, or does every criterion for it come out arbitrary? Does the fact that a seceding region's own borders were fixed by the state it is leaving help the partition argument, or is that a trick that would dissolve every existing state, since all borders are historical artifacts? If you take the remedial view, what does that imply for the Scottish and Catalan claims, and is it too strong, given that it makes self-determination hostage to a judgement about injustice that the secessionists will always dispute? If you take the defeasible view, what exactly must the seceding region offer its dissenting sub-regions, and who decides whether the offer is adequate? State the strongest version of the position you reject and answer it rather than a caricature of it.

Then turn to the questions of design, since some principle has to be applied by someone: who drafts the question and sets the threshold, who votes (residents of the territory only, or all citizens of the state whose territory it is), and who negotiates the terms on the other side — borders, debt, citizenship, currency, defence? Canada's Clarity Act and the Supreme Court of Canada's 1998 opinion on Quebec's secession are one attempt to answer these; you may treat them as material, but the argument matters more than the literature, and you need not cite anything.

Reach a verdict. Don't close on the observation that reasonable people disagree; if your position is conditional, state the conditions exactly. Finally, tell me where the argument is likeliest to break: which premise, if false, would overturn your verdict, and which objection you found hardest to answer and why you were or were not able to set it aside.
