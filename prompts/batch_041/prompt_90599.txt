I'm a novelist, three books, one of them with a behavioral economist as the main character, which is how I've somehow ended up invited to give a talk at this reading group. Here's my argument and I need you to get me ready for the questions after.

My claim: revealed preference theory tells you what a person picks, not what a person wants, and the gap between those two is pretty much the entire business of fiction. I break it into three layers — what a character says they want, what they actually choose when it costs them something, and what the story betrays about them behind their own back. Stated, shown, betrayed. The economist, as far as I can tell, only ever gets his hands on the middle one, and I want to argue that a theory of utility that can't touch the third isn't exactly wrong, it's just answering a much smaller question than the word "utility" makes it sound like — and then quietly borrowing the prestige of the big word for the small answer.

The room is a reading group: mostly empirical and behavioral economists who use revealed preference as a workhorse and get a little impatient with philosophy, plus two proper decision theorists, plus one philosopher of economics who's a realist about preferences and definately thinks I'm going to be sloppy. None of them have read a novel since college and at least one will mention it.

What I need from you: the objections I'll actually get from these people, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way they'd actually phrase it, with a short reply I can give in the moment and a note on where that reply is soft. I especially want the ones coming from the two decision theorists, since those are the ones that'll really land. And tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks — I think I know what it is but I want to see if you find the same one. Prep sheet, not an essay.
