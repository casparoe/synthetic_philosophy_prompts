We have just finished the unit on causation by omission in my upper-level metaphysics seminar, and I have a problem going into the final exam. My students can all recite the same two moves — that an omission can count as a cause under a counterfactual analysis, and that my failure to water the plant caused its death — and then they stop, as if the interesting question had been settled. What I actually want to test is whether they can see that the hard question is not whether omissions are causes but which omissions are causes, and why. I have been pushing a specific line all term and would like the exam to force a reckoning with it: that so-called causation by omission is not a distinctive causal relation at all, but an artifact of causal selection, and that the selection is driven by norms and expectations rather than by the counterfactual facts, which are hopelessly abundant. Two cases have done the work in class. The first is a gardener who forgets to water a plant and the plant dies; we say the gardener killed it, but she also failed to install a sprinkler, failed to move it into the shade, failed to hire a service, and every other person in the city failed to water it too, and I want students to say what privileges her omission over all the rest. The second is a swimmer who drowns within sight of a lifeguard who is looking elsewhere and of a dozen sunbathers who notice nothing; the counterfactuals are symmetrical across all of them, while the norms plainly are not, and I want students to say which of these is a cause and on what basis. I would also like the question to force them onto the analogue, for omissions, of a preemption case — two people who each could have thrown a life ring and neither of whom does — so that they cannot get by with the standard machinery for positive causation.

Please write the single essay question for a ninety-minute final, pitched so that a student who has done the reading but who defaults to recitation cannot pass by summarizing the debate, and so that taking a side on my selection thesis is unavoidable. Then give me a model answer of roughly 700 words, written as a strong student would write it rather than as a textbook would, and a marking scheme organized around five must-hit points worth four marks each, twenty in total, where each point names a specific move a top answer has to make and is paired with a one-line note on the mistake that typically costs students those marks. Keep the whole thing self-contained: the students will have nothing in front of them, so the question must stand on its own, and I would rather it not turn on any particular author's name or title, since I do not want the answers graded on name-dropping.
