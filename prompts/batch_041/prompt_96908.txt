Six weeks ago I was appointed to my county's capital-improvement advisory committee, and I have since spent four Tuesday evenings doing something I did not expect to find so philosophically unnerving: scoring proposed projects against a rubric.

The rubric has five criteria — safety, ridership, cost per rider, equity, and readiness — each scored from one to five and combined with weights the committee fixed at the start of the cycle. In practice, every meeting turns into an argument about the numbers. Last week two projects finished in a dead heat at 3.72. One would shave eleven minutes off the commute of an already well-served corridor; the other would extend service, for the first time, to a neighborhood that has none. For ninety minutes we fought over a ridership forecast — the consultant's model, its error bars, whether the survey sample was representative — before it became clear to me that nobody's position would have moved if the forecast had been perfect. The quarrel was never about the forecast. It was about what the county is for.

I have been reading Carnap, and I drafted a short essay for myself. I would like your comments on it.

The argument I sketched is this. Carnap spent much of his career insisting that philosophy is not a form of knowing but a form of designing — that the philosopher resembles an architect more than a scientist, proposing frameworks rather than discovering facts — and he drew a hard line between theoretical questions, which have answers and are settled by evidence, and practical decisions, which are neither true nor false and are settled by an act of decision. Applied to my committee, the diagnosis is immediate: the fights over ridership forecasts are theoretical in form but practical in substance. Because the rubric stages a practical decision as a computation, it invites the committee to believe that more data will settle what only a decision can. The forecast argument was not a failed inquiry; it was a displaced decision.

But the diagnosis seems to me to prove too much. If practical decisions answer to nothing but the will, what rationality is left in the very idea of planning — the idea that a society can organize its affairs deliberately rather than drift? Carnap was a socialist, and the point of a planned society is that collective choices can be made rationally; yet voluntarism, taken at face value, appears to dissolve that rationality into preference. My escape is to say that voluntarism governs the choice of ends and frameworks, while reasoning about means given ends remains perfectly objective — so the committee should argue openly about its weights as a decision rather than defend them as measurements. But this leaves the ultimate weights unjustified, since one can always ask why those ends; and I cannot tell whether the escape is a real distinction or a polite circularity that quietly converts planning into preference-aggregation.

I would like comments of three kinds. First, on accuracy: is this reading of Carnap defensible, or is it a caricature — is voluntarism really as decisionistic as I have made it, or did Carnap have resources for saying that some practical decisions are more rational than others, and if so, what are they? Second, on the argument itself: is the application to my committee sound, or have I conflated "the rubric cannot settle this" with "no inquiry bears on it"? Third, on what you take to be the strongest objection to the whole line of thought, and what it would imply for how a body like mine should proceed — whether it should abandon the rubric, keep it but publish its weights as an avowedly political commitment, or do something else entirely. Please argue with me rather than summarize; I have no interest in being told that this is a large and contested topic.
