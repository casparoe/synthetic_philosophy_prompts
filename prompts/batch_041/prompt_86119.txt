Someone in my group chat — we were arguing about whether international law is anything more than great-power preference — dropped "might makes right" and then backed it up by saying, look, Pascal says it: justice and force have to go together, because justice on its own can't do anything and force on its own is just tyranny, so really it all comes down to who can enforce what. I'm fairly sure that's a mangled use of Pascal, but I can't articulate why, and I also suspect "might makes right" is doing at least three jobs at once and isn't even the same claim as the Pascal bit.

What I want is a menu of the precise theses "might makes right" could be asserting. Something like: descriptive ("as a matter of fact, whoever has power determines what gets called just"), conceptual ("there's no fact of the matter about justice apart from power"), normative ("one ought to obey whoever can enforce"), and debunking ("belief in objective justice is a downstream effect of power relations"), plus whatever else it could mean that I'm not seeing. For each one, say whether it's a platitude, substantive and defensible, actually false, or secretly trivially true, and which reading my friend would have needed for his conclusion to go through.

Then the Pascal part: which of those readings, if any, does he actually hold? I gather his fragments on this pull in different directions — he's writing an apology for Christianity, not a treatise on political legitimacy, and some of the bits about custom and law being grounded in nothing but received authority sound much more radical than the justice/force line. So I don't expect a clean verdict, but I do want to know whether he's making a debunking claim, a Hobbesian legitimacy claim, or something else entirely.

Please be self-critical here. If the framing is off — if "might makes right" is too vague to be worth precisifying, or if the Pascal connection is a modern retrofit and the fragments won't bear this much weight — say so plainly instead of giving me a tidy list anyway. And if you're unsure what he actually wrote (editions number the fragments differently, translations vary a lot), flag the uncertainty rather than asserting a version.
