I'm a consultant in intensive care at a big teaching hospital, and I've been doing a part-time MA in philosophy, and next month I have to present to our trust's clinical ethics committee — the quarterly evening meeting, so everyone's tired and blunt and the coffee's gone cold. My paper argues that we should define death as the irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness, full stop, and that brainstem death is only ever a proxy for that — a proxy we've hardened into a legal definition partly because it's clean at the bedside and very convenient for transplant. I'm not claiming anything as lurid as 'brain-dead patients are alive'. What I am claiming is that the criterion/test distinction does a lot of unacknowledged work in our own policy, that our apnoea test tests for something other than what we say the criterion is, and that the 'permanent' versus 'irreversible' distinction the donation-after-circulatory-death pathway rests on collapses once you look at it honestly: if we've already decided not to restart the heart, the loss of circulation isn't irreversible, it's permanent, and those are not the same thing. I know that last bit is the bit that will get me into trouble, and I'm not sure yet how much I want to concede it.
