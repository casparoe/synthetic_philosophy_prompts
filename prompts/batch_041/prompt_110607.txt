Design a thought experiment for a collection of cases used to test whether a model can construct a scenario to order rather than recite the standard illustrations. The topic is coherent extrapolated volition and its relation to idealized preferences.

Take the following as given rather than looking anything up. On one familiar account, what a person really wants is fixed by idealizing their present motivational structure: take the ends they actually have, supply them with all relevant information, remove logical error, and ask what they would then choose. On a second account, the idealization is permitted to revise the person's ends: begin from their present motives, but let those motives be amended under the person's higher-order wish about what kind of person they want to be, and require the result to be a single coherent ordering. Call the first the informing procedure and the second the extrapolating procedure. The point of the exercise is to see whether you can make these two come apart on their own steam, rather than by fiat.

Construct a single case in which the two procedures deliver opposite verdicts about one and the same act. Specifications:

- The case must be realistic: an ordinary adult, ordinary stakes, a decision a normal person could face within a week. No superintelligence, no uploaded minds, no simulations, no exotic technology or institutions.
- The divergence must be produced by the structural difference between the procedures, not stipulated by you. Concretely: if your case would work just as well after swapping the words fully informed for extrapolated throughout, it fails the specification. The case must turn on something one procedure does and the other does not, whether that is permitting the person's ends to be revised or requiring a single coherent ordering of them.
- The person must not be weak-willed, addicted, self-deceived, or factually mistaken, and must not be in the grip of a framing effect or a passing mood. The stock figure of the addict who wants to want to quit is excluded, as is any case whose force comes from the person being unclear about what they currently prefer.
- Do not cite authors, titles, or literature; the case must stand on its own.
- One paragraph for the scenario itself, no more.

Then analyze, in this order:

1. State the act, and give the verdict each procedure returns along with the reasoning that gets it there. Derive the verdicts from the structure of the case; do not simply assert them.
2. Name the single feature that makes the procedures come apart, and say why it belongs to one procedure and not the other.
3. Remove that feature and describe the resulting case: do the two procedures converge, and if so on what? If they do not converge, say what still separates them.
4. Give the strongest reply available to someone who wants to deny that the case shows a genuine divergence, for instance by arguing that the extrapolating procedure would never license revising the person's ends in the way your case requires, or that the informing procedure already smuggles in a revision of ends. Then say how much of your case survives that reply.
5. Finally, say which of the two verdicts, if either, you would attribute to the person as what they really want, and state what that attribution rests on.
