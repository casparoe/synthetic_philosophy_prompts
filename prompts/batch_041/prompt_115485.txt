You are a philosopher of cognitive science with a solid grounding in the formal literature on knowledge representation. I want an explanation of the frame problem that goes past the usual one-line gloss, so please think it through carefully and begin by laying out the candidate things that phrase might name before you commit to an account.

Start from a small concrete case: a formal reasoner is given a description of a room and a handful of actions — an arm moves a block from one shelf to another — and must say what holds after an action is performed. Unless it is told, fact by fact, that everything else is unaffected, it cannot infer that the lamp is still on; if it is told all of that, the description explodes. Use this case to explain, precisely, what the problem is. Then assess what the standard repairs — explicit axioms to the effect that nothing else changes, minimising change, default or non-monotonic rules — actually buy and what they leave untouched. Finally, explain why the same phrase also names a broader worry in philosophy of mind, that a thinker must decide what is worth considering at all, and give your verdict on whether the narrow and the broad problems are one problem or two — and which repairs, if any, touch the broad one.
