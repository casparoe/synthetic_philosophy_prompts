I'm a third-year PhD student working on the metaphysics of value, and I've been asked to circulate a short statement of my position for a workshop on the normative significance of our attitudes. Before I send it round, I want to know whether the package actually holds together, because I have a nagging sense that somewhere in it I'm having my cake and eating it too, and I can't put my finger on where. So here is where I've landed. First, I hold the fitting-attitude, or buck-passing, analysis of value: for something to be valuable just is for there to be reasons of the right kind to value it, and value is not some further property over and above the reasons there are to value the thing. Second, I take the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem at face value rather than explaining it away: when a demon credibly threatens to torture me unless I admire him, I think I really do acquire a normative reason to admire him — a genuine reason, though plainly not one that makes him admirable. Third, I draw the right-kind/wrong-kind distinction as the object-given/state-given distinction: a reason for an attitude is of the right kind when it bears on the object of the attitude (what the demon is actually like), and of the wrong kind when it bears only on the state of having the attitude (what it would cost me to be in it). Fourth, I think there is a single univocal reason relation — the counting-in-favor-of relation — that takes both actions and attitudes as its relata; reasons for attitudes are not a different species from reasons for action. Fifth, I am a non-naturalist realist about the normative: the reason relation is irreducibly normative and not reducible to any natural facts. Sixth, I accept the reasons-first programme: the reason relation is the fundamental normative notion, and fittingness, value, and ought are all to be understood in terms of it.

Finally, and this is the part I'm least sure of, I have been working with a test for picking out the right kind of reason that I don't think I've seen stated in quite this form, and I'd like you to pressure-test it: a consideration is a reason of the right kind for an attitude only if it would still count in favor of that attitude from the standpoint of an agent who was wholly indifferent to whether she had it. The thought behind it is that reasons which merely reflect the costs or benefits of being in a state will evaporate once you subtract any concern for being in it, whereas reasons that concern the object will survive that subtraction.

What I want from you is an audit rather than a summary. Tell me which of these commitments are genuinely in tension with which others, which pairs only look like they conflict and in fact do not, and for each genuine tension say how severe it is and what the cheapest revision would be — what I would have to give up, and what I would lose by giving it up. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and at the end tell me which single change would clean up the most while costing the least elsewhere. Don't hedge: if two of these cannot both be had, say which one has to go.
