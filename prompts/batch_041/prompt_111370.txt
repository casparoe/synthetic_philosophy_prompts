I'm writing a novel where a guy does a genuinely good thing for a genuinely bad reason — he bails out a neighbour's family after a fire, but only because his ex is standing right there and he wants her to watch him do it. If she hadn't been there he'd have kept walking. My editor says the deed then has no moral worth at all, and I half believe her, but I want to know what I'm arguing with.

So compare me two views. The first is the standard one: an action has moral worth only if you do it for the right reasons — the reasons that actually make it the right thing to do, not some ulterior motive that just happens to point the same way. The second is one I've been chewing on, that moral worth is really about credit: whether the act is attributable to the agent at all, whether it came out of him and his own responsiveness to reasons, rather than whether the reasons he was responding to were the good ones. On that second view my guy comes out looking better, since the generosity did come from somewhere in him and not from pure calculation about his ex.

Which of the two is right about moral worth, or are they answering different questions wearing the same word? Where exactly do they come apart — cases where one grants worth and the other witholds it — and where does my character land on each? And if you had to pick one to write by, which gives a novelist the truer handle on when a reader will forgive him?
