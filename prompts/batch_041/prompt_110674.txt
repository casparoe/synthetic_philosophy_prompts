I'm giving a colloquium paper next term at a joint session of my department's metaphysics group and its philosophy of physics group, and I need a Q&A prep sheet for the discussion.

My thesis: modal realism should be rebuilt on quantum branching rather than on spatiotemporal connectedness. The received picture, as I understand it, is that a possible world is a maximal spatiotemporal whole, that worlds are spatiotemporally and causally isolated from one another, and that de re modality is handled by a counterpart relation whose specification is a free semantic choice. I want to replace all three claims. On my account, a world is a maximal branch of the universal quantum state: a maximal set of histories that are mutually decohering. Worlds share a past and then diverge; there is no requirement of isolation; and spatiotemporal connectedness is neither necessary nor sufficient for worldhood. Actuality is indexical, and I mean that as a physical index rather than a semantic one: the actual world is the branch I am on. Finally, the counterpart relation is not a free choice but a fact about how structure is inherited along branching — two things are counterparts when their structural descriptions are continuous across a branching event.

I advertise three payoffs. First, the plurality of worlds stops being a brute posit and becomes a consequence of physics. Second, the awkwardness about disconnected spatiotemporal regions goes away, because whether such regions are one world or two becomes a question about branch membership rather than a stipulation. Third, counterpart theory stops being the embarrassing appendix of the package, because the counterpart relation is now read off the physics.

The audience is about twenty people in three groups. (a) Three committed Everettians, who think decoherence plus a decision-theoretic derivation of the Born rule solves the probability problem, and who will not tolerate anyone waving at branches without saying how branches are individuated. (b) Eight or nine ordinary analytic metaphysicians of modality: a couple of ersatzists (worlds are maximal consistent descriptions or representations, not concrete things), a couple of primitivists about modality, and several who think the indispensability argument is the only serious argument for concrete worlds. (c) Three Humeans about laws and chance, for whom a world is nothing but a distribution of local, particular, non-modal facts, with no irreducible laws and no necessary connections.

Give me the objections I will actually get from these people, ordered by how much damage they do, and say how likely each one is. Phrase each objection the way the person who raises it would phrase it — the Everettian's version of an objection and the ersatzer's version are different objections even when they share a target. For each: what I should concede up front rather than be forced into conceding, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and one line on where that reply is soft. Then give me the objection I am hoping nobody asks. Finally, if there is an opening ninety seconds that would defuse two of the top objections without sounding defensive, sketch it.

No references, no reading list, no as-someone-argues-in-somewhere: the audience knows the literature and I need the arguments themselves, in their own terms. Don't pad the list; if an objection is weak, say so and say why.
