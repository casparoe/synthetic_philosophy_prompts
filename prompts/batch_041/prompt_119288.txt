Right, so I'm deep in a paper on self-deception and I've argued myself into a view I honestly can't tell is original or just wrong, and I'd like you to have a go at it before I embarrass myself at a reading group.

Here's the view. Self-deception is possible, and the whole paradox — you'd have to believe p and believe not-p at the same time — is a trap that dissolves once you stop modelling it as two contradictory beliefs in one head. What actually goes on in a self-deceiver is a failure of evidence-uptake, not a failure of logic. The evidence is all there: the man whose wife is transparently having an affair can recite every one of the telltale facts; the researcher nursing a dead hypothesis can list the failed replications. It's not that they lack the evidence. It's that the evidence never gets to play its normal role in fixing what they believe — it's quarantined from the belief-fixing process. Call it quarantined evidence. So there's no contradiction to explain and no need to split the mind into two little homunculi. The person just runs a motivated policy of never letting certain inputs do their epistemic work.

Now tell me what would count against this. Not the boilerplate "is self-deception even possible" stuff — the real exposure. Which cases does it get obviously wrong? Does it collapse into something more familiar the moment you push on it — is quarantined evidence just a posh name for wishful thinking, or for weakness of will about belief, or for the deflationist line where the person doesn't believe the bad thing at all and merely half-suspects it? Does it even deserve the name self-deception, given that deception normally implies somebody is being misled about something they'd mind being misled about? And the thing I keep flip-flopping on: if the evidence really is intact and available, why the distress? Self-deceivers are anxious, they need the story to be true in a way plain wishful thinking doesn't seem to require. Can the account explain that, or does the anxiety blow a hole in "the evidence is intact"?

Rank these from fatal to merely awkward. Seperate the conceptual objections from the ones that are really empirical (I'd like to know what actual findings — confabulation, anosognosia, motivated reasoning in the lab, whatever — would bear on this, since people keep telling me all my cases are folk-psychological). Say which of these a defender would simply concede and which would sink it. And be honest about whether I've built something unfalsifiable, where every apparent counterexample gets redescribed as "ah, but there the uptake was working."

The kind of thing that would genuinely move me is a case where the person's evidence is intact, their belief is false and self-serving, and there's still no sense in which they're deceiving themselves — if you can find one of those, I want to hear it. Tell me how confident you are in each of your verdicts, and single out the one you're least sure about.
