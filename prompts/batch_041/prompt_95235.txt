Here's the question I set:

The debate over whether the statue and the lump of clay are one object or two is either a substantive disagreement about what exists or a verbal one. Explain the method of elimination for testing which it is, apply it to this case, and say what follows for the dispute if it turns out to be verbal.

And here's the answer, typed up as submitted:

A verbal dispute is one where the parties think they disagree about the world but are really only disagreeing about words. The method of elimination is the test for this: take the disputed term — here 'exists', or 'object' — and re-describe the situation without using it, in terms that both sides already accept. If the disagreement survives the paraphrase, the dispute is non-verbal, so the parties genuinely disagree about the facts. If the disagreement disappears, the dispute is merely verbal: the parties were never disagreeing about the world at all, they were just using the same word for two different concepts.

Applied to the statue and the clay: both sides agree on every ordinary fact. There is clay, it was shaped on Tuesday, it will survive being squashed, the statue won't, the museum can't sell the statue and burn the clay. So we re-describe the case in exactly those terms and see what's left. What's left is one side saying there are two objects here and the other saying there is one object under two descriptions. The disagreement survives the paraphrase, so on this test the dispute is not verbal.

But Hirsch's quantifier variance says it is verbal after all, because on his view 'there exists' has several equally good meanings and each side is using a different one. That means, for Hirsch, there is no fact of the matter about what exists independently of our language, which is a form of anti-realism, since it makes the furniture of the world depend on our concepts.

If a dispute does turn out to be verbal, Chalmers thinks we can stop there. Once you've shown that the parties are talking past each other, nothing further is at stake, because a verbal dispute is by definition not an important one — the thing being fought over is a word rather than a fact. So the practical upshot for the statue case is that both sides can be told they are right.
