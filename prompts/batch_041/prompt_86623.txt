I was commissioned as an officer in a reserve component a few months ago, and two of the rules I now live under will not sit still together in my head. The first is that I have no standing to refuse a deployment on the ground that I judge the war itself to be unlawful: declining to go is a punishable offense, and my opinion about the justice of the cause is no defense. The second is that I am held fully and personally answerable for what I do once I am there. Having been ordered to do something is no defense to an atrocity, and I am told I have an affirmative duty to disobey an order that is manifestly unlawful. So on whether to fight at all I am treated as someone whose judgment does not count, and on how I fight I am treated as a fully responsible moral agent.

The same split shows up outside the courtroom. The state honors and pensions everyone who serves, without asking whether the war was just; and when it has gone after the crime of starting an aggressive war, it has gone after the political leaders, leaving the soldiers who fought it untouched — while at the same time granting enemy soldiers immunity for killing our own, provided they kept the rules of war.

What view of the combatant does this whole arrangement presuppose? Does it require the claim that soldiers on both sides of a war are moral equals who kill permissibly whatever the cause — or is something weaker enough, such as a division of labor between the state's decision to fight and the soldier's conduct of the fight, or a concession that a soldier cannot reasonably be expected to know whether his war is just? Separate what the practice genuinely presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and note where it is underdetermined. Then tell me which rival views it rules out — the view that a soldier fighting an unjust war is simply a murderer and should be answerable as one, or the view that he has a duty to judge the justice of the cause and refuse. Notice, too, that the ceremonial half of the practice is symmetric while the responsibility half is not: say whether the two halves rest on a single view of the combatant or on two that cannot be held together. Finally, if the presupposed view were replaced, tell me what would have to change — whether the immunity of enemy soldiers, the honors paid to veterans, and the rule that a soldier must obey an order he thinks unwise would all survive. I care less about which view is correct than about how much of the law and the ceremony is load-bearing on the answer.
