You are an expert in game theory, decision theory, and the philosophy of practical rationality — someone equally at home with equilibrium refinements and with the informal arguments philosophers make about cooperation. I want your help adjudicating a dispute between two colleagues of mine. Neither party is me; I took no side; and I would ask you not to split the difference out of politeness.

Begin with a short scoping section in which you lay out the distinctions you think the dispute turns on (for example, different senses in which a model might be said to "solve" the Prisoner's Dilemma: making cooperation possible, making it uniquely rational, making it predictable, or making it applicable to the cases people actually care about). Then give your verdict. Please reason from the construction as I describe it below; no citations or reading lists are needed, and I would rather you not gesture at literature you cannot check.

The background both parties accept. Take the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma with the usual payoffs: mutual cooperation 3 each, mutual defection 1 each, unilateral defection 5 for the defector and 0 for the cooperator. Now change the game: instead of choosing an action, each player submits a computer program to a referee. The referee runs each program with the other program as its input; each program may read the other's source code, and may also be written so as to refer to its own code. The programs' outputs are the actions, and payoffs follow. A program equilibrium is a Nash equilibrium of this larger game.

Three constructions matter to the dispute. (a) A program that outputs Cooperate if the opponent program's source code is character-for-character identical to its own, and Defect otherwise. Two copies of it cooperate, and neither player gains by submitting anything else, since any different program will be defected on. (b) A program that outputs Cooperate if it can prove that the opponent program, run on this program as input, outputs Cooperate, and Defect otherwise. Two copies of this one also cooperate, and here textual identity is not required — a fixed-point argument about provability does the work. (c) A program that with small probability simply cooperates, and otherwise simulates the opponent program on itself; two copies cooperate with high probability and the simulation terminates almost surely. Finally, a general result: a payoff pair can be achieved by some program equilibrium exactly when it is feasible and individually rational — the same condition the folk theorem gives for repeated games.

The dispute. Mira opened:

1. This settles something that was thought settled the other way. It shows that rational agents can reach mutual cooperation in a genuinely one-shot interaction — no repetition, no communication, no contract, no enforcer. All that is needed is that each agent's decision procedure be transparent to the other. The dilemma was an artifact of describing agents as choosing bare actions; describe them as choosing procedures and cooperation becomes rational.

2. Tomas replied that this only works because the situation has been swapped for a different one. The referee, the source-code access, the guaranteed halting, the shared language — all of that is imported structure. If the original dilemma models a situation without it, nothing has been solved; the puzzle has just been relocated into the question of why the extra structure is there.

3. Mira: the dilemma was never a description of a bare situation but of a situation at a given level of abstraction. Any two real agents are made of something, and if that something gives each access to the other's decision procedure, then the correct model of their situation includes it. Construction (b) shows the point doesn't even depend on the agents being textually identical — only on each being able to establish what the other does — so the fragility objection (one stray space and cooperation collapses) is an engineering detail, not a conceptual one.

4. Tomas: that is precisely the trouble. What makes (b) work is that each agent can prove things about the other's behaviour, and the argument rests on fixed-point reasoning about what the agents' proof system can establish. That is logical omniscience under another name. And look at the general result: the reachable payoffs are exactly the feasible and individually rational ones, which is the repeated-game folk theorem's condition. So program equilibrium buys nothing over repetition. It is not a solution to the one-shot dilemma; it is a change of subject that reproduces the anything-goes conclusion without the repetition.

5. Mira: the folk-theorem result cuts the other way. It shows the dilemma's force came from the strategy space, not from rationality. "Anything goes" is a fair complaint about the predictive question — which equilibrium will be played — but not about the possibility question. The original argument, that defection is the only rational choice because it dominates, is refuted the moment cooperation is an equilibrium of a game that faithfully represents the agents' situation. If your reply is that the agents cannot read each other's code, you are conceding that the dilemma's conclusion rested on a contingent fact about the world rather than on rationality.

6. Tomas: then we are back to the empirical question, and there the answer is that the condition never holds for the cases anyone cared about. Humans do not have one another's source code; two people who "reason the same way" may still reach different conclusions because they occupy different positions, hold different information, and are not logically omniscient. The formal results evade this by simulating the opponent inside the agent, which assumes away the problem, since you cannot in general compute what an arbitrary agent will do. And even in the artificial case the cooperative equilibria are not selected: mutual defection remains an equilibrium too, so nothing tells a rational agent to cooperate rather than defect.

7. Mira: equilibrium multiplicity afflicts every solution concept, and the ordinary game escapes it only by a dominance argument that no longer applies here. And there is a resolution available in the program game that is absent from the ordinary one: an agent who reasons this way cooperates with another agent who reasons this way, and each can see that this is so. That is not an extra assumption; it is the content of the two agents being in the same situation. Which is why the construction feels like a Kantian test of maxims, and why it resembles Newcomb's problem, where the right choice depends on the correlation between your choice and the predictor's.

8. Tomas: now you have given the game away. "Reasoning the same way" is doing all the work, and it amounts to assuming the two agents' choices are correlated. But correlation is exactly what the dilemma was supposed to rule out; assume it and cooperation stops being surprising — it is just the correct response to a common cause. Program equilibrium is then not a discovery about rationality but a formalization of the assumption that two agents can be a common cause of each other's behaviour. That may be a real insight about Newcomb-style cases; it is not a refutation of the dilemma.
