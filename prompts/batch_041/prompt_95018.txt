So I've been sitting in this ML-safety reading group all term and we keep circling back to the shutdown/corrigibility thing, and before I have to present next week I want to write down where I've actually landed, because I have a suspicion I'm holding a package that doesn't hang together and I can't find the seam myself. Here's roughly my position. (1) I'm a consequentialist about agent design: the right design is whatever has the best expected consequences over the long run, full stop, no side constraints on the design space. (2) A well-designed agent should never resist being shut down or modified, and should actively help — if the opertor reaches for the off switch, cooperating with that is just part of what it is to be well-designed. (3) I think the cleanest route to (2) is to build agents whose preferences are entirely about the world and never about their own future behaviour, policy, or parameters — no self-referential preferences at all, so the question "will I still exist" just never enters into its deliberations. (4) I want the agent to be genuinely competent: a strong optimizer that makes long-range plans and carries them out well. A system that's bad at getting what it wants isn't safe, it's just useless. (5) It should defer to the operator about its own objective — if I tell it "your goal is now X," it takes that on, rather than treating its current goal as sacrosanct. (6) It should also be able to tell when the thing talking to it isn't actually the operator — a jailbreak, a confused subprocess, someone who's lifted the API key — and not treat any old instruction as the principal's word. A friend in the group keeps insisting the whole thing is a non-problem: just give the agent a single explicit term in its objective for how well its current policy matches the operator's latest correction, weighted high enough that everything else is noise, and it's corrigable by construction. I don't buy it, but when I try to say why, my objection sounds like it's just one of my own commitments turned around and pointed back at me, which is what makes me nervous.

Give me an audit, not a lecture. Exactly five numbered items, ordered by severity, each a sentence or two, no introduction and no wrap-up. Each item should name which of my commitments are actually pulling against each other (my numbered list or my friend's proposal, whichever is doing the work), say how bad the conflict really is, and then — this is the part I actually care about — take a definite stand on which one has to give and what the cheapest repair costs. Don't hedge at me with "it depends what you mean by X," and don't hand me a menu of options to pick from; I'd much rather you be wrong in a definite way than right in a vague one. If you think a pair only looks inconsistent, say so and say why the appearance is misleading, and put that one last since it's the least serious.
