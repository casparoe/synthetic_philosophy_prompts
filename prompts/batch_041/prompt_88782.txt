I run a small podcast that sits between econ and philosophy, and next week's guest is an economist who takes a pretty hard line: for her, utility just is a summary of what people choose. No hidden mental state, no true preference buried under the behavior, no feeling that the choice is supposed to track. When someone brings up framing effects or intransitive choices, her move is always that the choice set was described wrong -- widen the menu, let preferences depend on the menu, and the anomaly dissolves into a more careful description of the same choices. She's good at this, and I don't want to just hand her softballs or let her run the same rescue every time.

Write me a sequence of eight to ten questions that starts easy and gets progressively harder for her to deflect, with a follow-up for each one keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I need at least one question each on: (a) whether "re-describe the choice set" has any constraints at all -- what would make a re-description wrong rather than just a rescue; (b) how she does welfare economics, i.e. what it even means for someone to be better off if utility is nothing but choice; and (c) the wanting/liking split that comes out of the neuroscience -- dopamine seems to drive wanting or incentive salience, a separate hedonic system drives liking, and the two can come apart, with choice tracking the wanting -- so does her view have room for that, or does she just deny that liking is any part of utility. Also work in the case of choices that are stable but that the person would disavow on reflection (addiction, sour-grapes adaptation): press on whose judgment counts, the choosing self or the reflecting self, and why that doesn't smuggle a hidden true preference back in through the side door.

End on what would actually count against her view -- what she'd have to see, or what argument would have to land, to give it up -- and make that last question one she can't answer with another re-description.

Two things. Where a question leans on something that's settled among people who actually work on this, say so; where it's genuinely contested, say that instead, and don't dress up a contested view as the consensus. Same for your guesses at her likely answers: flag when you're guessing.

And start straight in with the questions, no intro.
