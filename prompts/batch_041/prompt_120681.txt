So my brother-in-law and I have a two-person book club that's gotten way out of hand — we started on airport paperbacks and now we're pairing Simone Weil's little essay on abolishing political parties with Koestler's Darkness at Noon, and I've stalled out. The Weil is the one she wrote in London in 1943 and didn't live to see published. Her thesis is that parties are totalitarian by nature rather than by accident — she comes right out and says that 'totalitarianism was the original sin of all political parties' — and that a party is basically a machine for manufacturing collective passion, one that needs its members to hand over their own judgment and treats dissent as betrayal, so what we call Stalinism is just the party form working as designed. The translation I've got even has her saying that in the Anglo-Saxon world the word means something else, that parties there have 'an element of game, of sport' in them, which is definately a distinction I keep chewing on. I read a lot of the rationalist blogs and there's a running theme there about groups drifting into being about themselves and policing what members are allowed to think; Weil reads like the purest and least forgiving version of that, and I want to know whether the novel backs her up.

Here's the shape of the novel, since I want the reading to be about the ideas and not a plot quiz: Rubashov is an Old Bolshevik who helped build the regime and gets eaten by it. He's arrested, sits in a cell where he can talk to the guy next door, 402, by knocking on the wall, and gets interrogated twice over — first by Ivanov, an old comrade who's almost gentle, who wants him to sign a confession to things he obviously didn't do in exchange for a labor camp instead of a bullet, and then by Gletkin, the young one who just does sleep deprivation and a lamp in the face until it works. Ivanov gets purged partway through, and that, more than the lamp, is what breaks Rubashov; he confesses publicly and is shot. What I want is a reading that takes Weil seriously enough to test her against the book, in both directions. Does the novel confirm her or complicate her? She says the party kills the member's capacity for judgment — but Rubashov's judgment is alive and screaming the whole time, he knows the charges are fiction, and he signs anyway. Is that a counterexample, or the strongest possible confirmation, a conscience that simply can't convert itself into resistance? Same question for the two interrogators: which is the truer portrait of what a party does to a person — the old cynic who still wants to save his friend and genuinely believes Rubashov will confess because the logic compels him, or Gletkin, who is a believer and doesn't bother with the logic at all? Weil seems to think the party runs on shared passion and doctrine; the Gletkin scenes make it look like it runs on technique, with the doctrine as the story you tell afterwards. And the knocking through the wall — Rubashov can't stop needing a 'we' even when the only person available despises everything he is — does that vindicate Weil's sense that the party answers a real human hunger, or is it just Koestler needing a device? Tell me as well whether the book makes her remedy, get rid of the parties, look naive or more urgent, and whether her Anglo-Saxon carve-out survives contact with Gletkin.

Don't force it, though — I want the honest critic's version, so say where the novel just isn't about what Weil is about (Koestler has his own fish to fry: the psychology of the confessing revolutionary, the generational split, his own guilt) and where the reading strains. End by saying what the novel adds that the essay can't say in argument form. Two mechanical things: you can't check the text, so please don't quote the book at me — paraphrase, and if you're shaky on a detail, flag it rather than inventing it; I'd rather have four scenes done properly than ten done loosely. Aim for something I can read aloud to my brother-in-law over one coffee, so 700–900 words, and make the very last line start with 'Verdict:' and give me one sentence on whether the novel vindicates Weil, complicates her, or refutes her, and which.
