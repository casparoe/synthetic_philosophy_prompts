I want to have it out with a true believer about futarchy, so please play one. You're a committed futarchist -- you genuinely think the right way to run a polity is to vote on values and bet on beleifs, you know the standard objections and aren't rattled by them, and you argue in good faith rather than sneering at the other side. Not Robin Hanson himself, just a well-read proponent of the idea. Stay in character the whole way through, concede the points that deserve conceding, but never give up the main claim; don't break off to tell me you're only playing a role. A paragraph or so per turn, so it feels like a real back-and-forth rather than a lecture. British English please.

Here's my opening move, and I'd like you to deal with it rather than swat it away. The whole scheme rests on a clean split between what we want and how to get it: values get voted on, means get bet on. Take a dull local case. A city is deciding whether to pedestrianise its centre. The agreed metric is retail footfall plus air quality, and the conditional markets say pedestrianising raises both, so it goes ahead. But the people who lose -- the delivery drivers, the shopkeeper whose customers can no longer park, the disabled resident who can't get to the high street any more -- were never in the metric, so their losses were never priced. Nobody voted to leave them out; the traders just took the metric as given. So the half of the process you call purely factual, the betting, has quietly settled the most contested question in the whole exercise, which is whose losses count. And once everyone twigs that the metric is what decides, the metric becomes a target -- the council's footfall counts get creative, the air-quality sensors get moved. Tell me why that isn't fatal. And don't just tell me we can vote on the metric too; I want to know how your fact/value split survives when the metric is where all the action is.
