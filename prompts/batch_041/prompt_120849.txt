I have spent some time trying to state, in a single principle, what I actually believe about accepting risks, and I have not succeeded. What follows is not a defence of any theory; it is a report of my verdicts on particular choices. I would like to know what rule, or what small set of rules, fits them, what that rule implies about cases I have not put to it, and which of my verdicts it obliges me to abandon.

1. A sure $40, or a coin flip paying $0 or $82. I take the $40, and I take it because it is certain. If you tell me that $82 is worth only slightly more to me than $40, so that the gamble falls short of the sure sum in utility, that is not my reason. The certainty is doing the work.

2. A sure $4,000, or a coin flip paying $0 or $8,200. The same verdict, for the same reason. I do not feel any more averse to the risk here than in the case of $40, and if you scale the whole thing down to forty cents and eighty-two cents my verdict does not change either.

3. A sure $95, or a one in ten thousand chance of $1,000,000. The gamble is worth $100 on average, and I take the gamble. The chance of the large prize is worth more to me than its face value, and not because $95 would mean little to me. What I am buying is the chance.

4. A sure $1,000,000, or a lottery with an 89 per cent chance of $1,000,000, a 10 per cent chance of $5,000,000 and a 1 per cent chance of nothing. I take the sure million. Now compare a lottery with a 10 per cent chance of $5,000,000 and a 90 per cent chance of nothing against one with an 11 per cent chance of $1,000,000 and an 89 per cent chance of nothing. I take the first. In the first pair what moves me is the 1 per cent chance of ending with nothing; in the second pair that 1 per cent has gone, and I am back to comparing the chances of the large prizes.

5. A 10 per cent chance of losing my house and a 90 per cent chance of gaining $100, or a 10 per cent chance of losing my house, a 45 per cent chance of gaining $50 and a 45 per cent chance of gaining $150. I am indifferent between these. Once the chance of the disaster is fixed and equal, all that concerns me is what I get on average in the event that I keep the house.

6. A single gamble that pays $0 or $201 on a coin flip, for a stake of $100: I refuse it. The same gamble played out a hundred times, so that the aggregate is very likely to land near $5,050: I accept it.

7. A 99.99 per cent chance of $10,000 with a 0.01 per cent chance of nothing, against a sure $9,900. I take the sure thing, and the residual sliver of chance weighs on me more than its size suggests. More generally, the step from no chance at all to a 0.1 per cent chance matters to me a great deal more than the step from 20 per cent to 20.1 per cent.

A friend who reads decision theory tells me that the structure I am circling has a name, risk-weighted expected utility, and that the repair is a single function of probability that reweights the chances, applied to the cumulative probability of doing at least as well as each outcome, so that the small chances at the bottom and at the top of a distribution are both treated differently from the chances in the middle. If I have the description right, that sounds as though it keeps 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 7.

What I want is the principle itself, stated precisely, with the case that motivates each of its clauses; an honest statement of which of my seven verdicts it cannot accommodate and why; and its predictions for two cases I have not put to it. First, a 20 per cent chance of losing $100 with an 80 per cent chance of gaining $200, against a 20 per cent chance of losing $100, a 40 per cent chance of breaking even and a 40 per cent chance of gaining $400: the chance of the bad outcome is the same in both, and so is the average gain when the bad outcome does not occur, so the reasoning I give in case 5 seems to pronounce them equally good, and I am not sure I believe it. Second, two urns, each containing fifty red and fifty black balls, with a prize for drawing a red ball; in the first urn red was designated the winning colour in advance, in the second the winning colour was fixed by a coin you did not see. The chances of winning are identical, and I would rather draw from the first. I want to know whether a principle about how chances are weighted has anything at all to say here, or whether it needs a second device, and if so what that device would have to be.
