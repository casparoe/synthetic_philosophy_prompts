ok so I've been lurking on rat-adjacent blogs and forums for like a decade and I keep getting into arguments about privacy with people who think it's basically either (a) a property right over information about you, or (b) a collective good like clean air that we all have to maintain together. I lean (b) but I've never had a historical text to point at, and I just hit this passage in Warren and Brandeis's "The Right to Privacy" (Harvard Law Review, 1890) that seems way more interesting than the "right to be let alone" line everyone quotes. Here it is:

"The intensity and complexity of life, attendant upon advancing civilization, have rendered necessary some retreat from the world, and man, under the refining influence of culture, has become more sensitive to publicity, so that solitude and privacy have become more essential to the individual; but modern enterprise and invention have, through invasions upon his privacy, subjected him to mental pain and distress, far greater than could be inflicted by mere bodily injury. Nor is the harm wrought by such invasions confined to the suffering of those who may be the subjects of journalistic or other enterprise. In this, as in other branches of commerce, the supply creates the demand. Each crop of unseemly gossip, thus harvested, becomes the seed of more, and, in direct proportion to its circulation, results in the lowering of social standards and of morality. Even gossip apparently harmless, when widely and persistently circulated, is potent for evil. It both belittles and perverts. It belittles by inverting the relative importance of things, thus dwarfing the thoughts and aspirations of a people."

What I can't figure out is what work the back half of that is doing, and whether the argument is even coherent. Three things I'm stuck on:

First, the piece is usually read as inventing an *individual* right, but this bit says the harm isn't confined to the people gossiped about -- it degrades the readers, lowers "social standards and of morality," and (my favorite line) belittles "by inverting the relative importance of things." That's a harm to third parties who never had their privacy invaded at all. Is that a real argument or just Victorian moralizing about tabloids?

Second, "the supply creates the demand." Applied to gossip, that looks like a causal claim about media shaping taste rather than just catering to it. Is it load-bearing, or could you delete it and keep everything else? And if you keep it, does it commit them to something like: the value of privacy is partly *aggregate*, because a society with weak privacy ends up with worse collective attention and worse public discourse -- so my friend who wants privacy as a personal property right is missing something structural?

Third, I think there's a tension. They say civilization and "refinement" make us *more* sensitive to publicity, so privacy becomes more essential over time -- privacy as a good whose value scales with social complexity. But then gossip is supposed to destroy "delicacy of feeling" and refinement. So refinement is both the thing that makes privacy valuable and the thing that gossip destroys. Do those pull in opposite directions, or am I misreading?
