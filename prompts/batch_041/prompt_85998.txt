I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on philosophy of mind — fifteen students, eighty minutes, no equipment beyond index cards, a cardboard screen, and a whiteboard. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of the split-brain cases for the unity of consciousness: the thought that one body can carry two streams of agency while a single narrating voice tells a seamless first-person story it has no authority over.

Build it around three roles rather than a demonstration. Two participants share one acting body, each steering it through a channel the other cannot see, while a third is charged with explaining, in real time and on the record, why the body is doing what it is doing. Arrange the rules so that the explainer sometimes works from the visible output alone, and require the group to treat the explainer's written account as the official record of what happened. The payoff should arrive when they find the record confident, coherent, and false, and then have to say whether the explaining voice and the acting hand were the same subject — ending with a recorded verdict and a statement of what evidence would have overturned it.

Give me the rules, materials, and timeline; a debrief that maps specific moments onto the competing ways of understanding unity (a single subject, co-conscious streams, unity as something the narrator constructs) without letting the labels do the work; an honest account of what predictably goes wrong the first time, including how to keep it from degenerating into a guessing or trust game; and a frank statement of what the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply.
