Right, so my seminar has spent three weeks on the dogmatism puzzle and Ive read one too many essays that just explain it back to me. So here's what I want from you instead: a short story. A page or two. An actual story — a scene, a character, some stakes — not a fable with a moral stapled onto the end.

Quick reminder of the puzzle in case you need it: suppose you know something, call it P. Since you know P, you know that any evidence against P is misleading — if it took you anywhere it would take you away from something true. And if you know the evidence is misleading, you're entitled to ignore it. So it looks like knowledge licenses you to dismiss all counter-evidence to what you know, which means a knower can rationally become a fanatic. Nobody likes this, and nobody agrees on which step to give up.

A few things I actually want to see:

1. Put the reasoning on the page. I want to hear the character make the inference themselves, out loud or in their head, and I want it to sound like good reasoning. Not stubbornness, not bad faith, not a character flaw. The creepiness should come from the logic, not the personality.

2. Make the reader believe the character knows. If we suspect they don't know and are just deluded, the puzzle evaporates and you've written a story about a crank. So pick a case where we're on their side at the start.

3. Then run the same reasoning somewhere else in the story, ideally in the mouth of a different character, where it licenses something we find appaling — so we see that it's a form of inference rather than a person.

4. Don't resolve it. No scene where the ignored evidence turns out to have been right, no redemption arc, no clever ending where the character was wrong all along and the reader gets let off the hook.

5. One constraint on the setup: don't let the character know that they know. Most of us think knowledge isn't luminous — you can know P without being in a position to tell that you know it — and the puzzle looks a lot scarier if you assume the character has that kind of access to their own epistemic state. (There's also a respectable line that the argument only bites on evidence that P is false, not on evidence that you never knew in the first place; play with that if you like, but don't lecture me about it inside the story.) Build the case so it stays genuinely unclear whether the character is entitled to the move they're making, and let that uncertainty just sit there.

Setting is up to you. Please not a scientist ignoring the data, that one's been done to death. I'll take a divorce, a translation, a diagnosis, a game of cards, whatever you like.
