Write a dialogue between two philosophers of mind arguing over sphexishness — the tendency of the digger wasp, when her routine is interrupted, to resume it from the start as though nothing had registered, hauling the cricket back to the burrow mouth and beginning again however many times the experimenter displaces it.

The first speaker, a rigidity realist, holds that the wasp exposes a real and consequential kind of mechanism: the routine runs to completion whether or not its steps remain relevant to the goal, and heaping on more complexity does not convert that sort of mechanism into the self-monitoring flexibility we take ourselves to possess. For her, sphexishness names a property, and the live question is how much of human deliberation, and with it how much of what we call free will, is sphexish underneath — a difference in degree rather than in kind.

The second speaker, a flexibility skeptic, holds that "sphexish" is a description we impose from outside, from a vantage point that already knows the displacement is irrelevant to the wasp's goal. She may register everything we register and simply weight it differently; calling her sphexish may say more about our expectations than about her, and "free will" may stand in the same relation to the systems we praise with it. For him, the very idea of a rigid routine presupposes a privileged account of what the routine is "for," and no such account is available from nowhere.

Give them the following shared, hypothetical material to fight over, and let it do the work rather than the well-worn cricket anecdote. Suppose a laboratory reports that a wasp interrupted in precisely the same way on twenty consecutive trips eventually stops relocating the cricket and carries it straight into the burrow — but only for displacements matching the exact manner of the interference, reverting to the full routine the moment the displacement differs even slightly. The rigidity realist reads this as a longer program with a hard-wired exception; the flexibility skeptic reads it as learning, and accuses the realist of calling it "hard-wired" only because the outcome is already known. Let the exchange then arrive at the disagreement that actually divides them: whether any principled criterion — for instance, whether a system's response to a perturbation tracks that perturbation's relevance to its goal rather than its surface features — can separate genuine flexibility from mere complexity, or whether every such criterion covertly assumes the standpoint of the system being judged, so that "rigid" and "flexible" are verdicts about us rather than about the wasp.

Requirements: neither philosopher should be a strawman; each must press the strongest version of their case and answer the other's actual arguments rather than talking past them. Roughly 1200–1800 words.
