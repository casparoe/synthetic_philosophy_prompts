For a graduate seminar on the perception–cognition boundary, I keep running into a dispute that will not resolve: whether perception is cognitively penetrable. Both camps accept the same psychophysics. What is at issue is how to describe cases in which what someone believes, knows, or expects seems to alter what they see — ambiguous figures; a briefly flashed stimulus seen as the shape one was primed to expect; effects that show up in adaptation and aftereffect measures rather than only in a report. One camp reads these as changes in the content of perceptual experience; the other insists they are attention, memory, or post-perceptual judgment in perceptual dress, and that early vision stays encapsulated. My hypothesis, and I want it treated as one, is that this is two disagreements fused: a verbal one about whether 'penetration' names a change in content or any causal influence at all, and a methodological one about what could ever rule out a low-level perceptual explanation when every behavioral measure is also compatible with a post-perceptual decision. Diagnose the impasse — is that the right diagnosis, or is a real empirical question being buried? — and then say what would count as progress: a sharper distinction, an experiment, or an argument that the two sides are answering different questions under one name and that no experiment will settle it.
