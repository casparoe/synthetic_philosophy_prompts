I have recently been asked to help judge a small community photography prize, and the experience has made a question feel urgent that I used to treat as merely academic: is beauty objective, or is it, as Hume seems to say, a matter of taste? A colleague who knows the history of philosophy better than I do summarized Hume's view for me as follows. Hume holds that beauty is a sentiment in the mind of the beholder rather than a quality of objects, so there is no objective fact of the matter about beauty. But he does not conclude that all tastes are equal. He thinks there is a standard of taste, namely the joint verdict of true judges -- critics who have delicacy of taste, practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice, and good sense. Their verdicts are the standard because they are the ones whose sentiments are most reliable. However, since the standard is ultimately grounded in sentiment, it is not a rule of reason; it is a rule of taste. So while some judgments are better than others, there is no standard independent of human feeling. And since the true judges are just people with better sentiments, their verdict is ultimately just a more refined preference.

Is this a fair summary? Please go through it claim by claim and tell me what Hume would accept, what is a common simplification, and what he would reject. I am inclined to think that some photographs really are better than others, and I would like to know whether that intuition can survive contact with Hume's actual position. In particular, does the conclusion that criticism is ultimately just persuasion follow from anything Hume holds, or is it an inference my colleague has added?
