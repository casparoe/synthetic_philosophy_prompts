After thirty-five years in experimental physics — the last twenty of them chasing small excesses in detector data — I retired and began reading philosophy in earnest. I have no training in it, so I ask you to be precise where I am vague. The problem that has occupied me is the one I associate with Hume: that testimony can never establish a miracle, because it is always more probable that the witness is deceived or deceiving than that the regular course of nature has been interrupted. I have never believed this as stated, yet my own reactions to testimony plainly follow some rule, and I want to know what it is. Here is how I actually respond to cases.

- A brother-in-law of mine, a careful man and a serious birder, tells me he saw a snowy owl in our county in July. I believe him without hesitation.
- A stranger on a birding forum reports the same sighting in the same county. I believe him too, though a little less firmly.
- Twelve people who do not know one another independently report that a man rose unsupported into the air above a stage at a revival meeting. I do not believe any of them saw a levitation, though I believe every one of them is sincere.
- A former colleague reports a five-sigma excess in a channel where we expected only a small background, in a region where a hypothetical particle has long been sought. I believe it, provisionally.
- The same colleague, whose competence with detectors I rate as highly as my own, reports a five-sigma signal of a psychic effect under a protocol I have read and cannot fault. I do not believe it.
- A man wins the state lottery and says he dreamed the winning numbers the night before. I believe he won; I do not believe he dreamed them.
- A woman I have known and trusted for decades tells me she was taken aboard a spacecraft. I believe she underwent the experience; I do not believe the abduction.
- A dying man confesses to a murder that fits the physical evidence and that no one had laid at his door. I believe the confession.
- A respected historian announces that a document that would overturn the early history of our town was found in a sealed attic, but declines to let anyone examine it. I do not believe it.

The pair that most troubles me is the fourth and fifth cases: the same man, the same statistic, the same care on my part, opposite verdicts. The third and eighth also sit badly together, since I reject twelve independent and disinterested witnesses and accept one interested and dying one.

My own attempt at a principle is this: testimony should move me only insofar as the claim it supports, if true, would leave the rest of my picture of how the world works roughly intact; a claim that would force me to give up a large, well-tested body of belief must clear a correspondingly higher bar, and no amount of sincere or independent testimony helps it do so, because sincerity and independence bear on how likely the testimony is given the claim, not on how likely the claim is to begin with.

Please tell me whether some principle of this shape — or a better one — actually yields my verdicts, and if the principle must be complicated to save them all, say so and tell me which verdict a simpler principle would force me to abandon. I would also like its prediction on two cases I have not listed: a hospital report, signed by three oncologists and accompanied by before-and-after scans, that a patient's metastatic cancer disappeared after a prayer vigil; and a widely repeated claim, supported by many witnesses of good character, that a deceased woman has been seen walking in her village.
