I am a postdoc in statistical mechanics, and last week I lost an argument to a friend of mine, a philosopher of science who will now be sitting in the front row of a session I have agreed to run. Her position, held with a serenity I could not dent, was this: the microscopic laws are time-reversal symmetric, so physics contains no arrow; the arrow we inhabit is a projection of our memory and our bookkeeping; and the usual move of positing a special low-entropy boundary condition on the early universe — the past hypothesis — does not explain the arrow of time but merely assumes it. I could not, on the spot, lay out the sequence of ideas that would have shown her where her argument actually turns, and I would prefer never to be in that position again.

The session is ninety minutes with a whiteboard, for a mixed reading group of about eight: mostly humanities graduate students, one retired engineer, no calculus, and nothing retained from thermodynamics beyond a hazy memory of the second law. Do not explain the direction of time to me. Plan the explanation I should give.

I need the order of ideas, and the dependency structure beneath it. I suspect that the question of why the early universe began in a low-entropy state is unintelligible to this audience until the second law has been demoted from a law of nature to a statistical statement, and that the past hypothesis cannot be heard as anything other than a cheat until the reversibility objection has been genuinely felt — but I want the ordering made explicit, including the places where my own sense of what depends on what is wrong.

I need the carrying example, and I need you to judge one candidate I have been turning over. Take a ring of N sites, each holding a ball coloured up or down. Some of the N edges between neighbouring sites are marked, fixed once and for all. At each tick every ball moves one site clockwise and flips its colour exactly if it crosses a marked edge. The rule is deterministic and perfectly reversible — run the clock backwards and the same rule, read anticlockwise, undoes it — yet if the marked edges are chosen at random with density p, the fraction of up balls relaxes, to a good approximation, geometrically toward one half, and after about N ticks the entire configuration returns exactly to where it began. A reversible, recurrent microdynamics yields a monotone-looking, irreversible macro-behaviour. Tell me whether this can carry the whole session or only part of it, what it must have and lack to do that work, and where it breaks down: it models relaxation rather than the initial condition, its recurrence is an artefact of finiteness, and it says nothing about cosmology.

I need the misconceptions, each with the moment in the sequence at which it should be headed off and a sentence I can use there. My list is: entropy is disorder; the second law says entropy must increase; reversible microdynamics entails reversible macrodynamics; the past hypothesis is vacuous; the thermodynamic arrow is simply the psychological arrow. Add whatever I have missed. The sequence should be built so that my friend's objection is answered by the shape of the session rather than by a rebuttal I improvise at the end.

Finally, give me one closing question that separates a group which has understood why the past hypothesis is a contingent boundary condition from one that has merely learned the vocabulary; and an account of what I should openly refuse to cover at this level — Liouville's theorem, the assumptions behind Boltzmann's H-theorem, fluctuation theorems, Boltzmann brains, cosmology — which of these the arc can survive without and which it cannot.
