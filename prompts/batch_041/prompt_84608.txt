I'm a first-year PhD student in environmental history, and I've spent the last month in a seminar on the Anthropocene that has unsettled the way I think about my own field. The episode I keep turning over is the Orbis hypothesis: the proposal, in a 2015 paper by Simon Lewis and Mark Maslin, that the Anthropocene could be dated to 1610 on the strength of a small but genuine dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide recorded in Antarctic ice cores — a dip they attribute to the reforestation of abandoned farmland across the Americas after Indigenous populations collapsed in the wake of European contact. Since you can't look any of this up, let me fix the facts: the CO2 decline is small but real in the ice-core record; the population loss is usually estimated at the overwhelming majority of the hemisphere's inhabitants; the proposed mechanism is that fields, terraces, and managed forests went unworked, regrew, and drew down carbon; Lewis and Maslin named 1610 as one of only two dates they thought satisfied the formal criteria for a new epoch; the Anthropocene Working Group ultimately proposed a mid-twentieth-century marker instead; and that proposal was voted down in 2024. I don't need a summary of any of that.

What I want is a case analysis run through a particular theoretical lens, and then run back the other way. The lens is the claim that non-human things can be historical actors — Latour's actants, or the looser and more common idea in environmental history that nature, disease, and climate act in human affairs. Map the Orbis case onto that claim with some precision. Who or what are the candidates for agency here: the smallpox virus, the regrowing forest, the CO2 molecule, the ice core that records it, the European sailor, the Indigenous farmer who stopped farming? Which of these can the theory describe as an actor, which can it only describe as a conduit or a medium, and where does the mapping strain or produce absurdity? Then turn it around: does this episode vindicate the non-human-agency framing, or does it expose the framing as a way of distributing responsibility away from the human decisions — conquest, enslavement, forced labor, dispossession — that set the collapse in motion? I have read criticisms that the framing flattens colonial violence into a natural process, and I want to know whether that criticism lands, and whether the charge of environmental determinism is even the right charge to bring against a view that was, in part, meant as an alternative to determinism.

Finally, the Anthropocene proposal's failure is usually told as a story about stratigraphy and dating conventions. Tell me whether it is also evidence about what the non-human-agency framing can and cannot do — whether the framing's inability to settle a dating question says something about its explanatory power, or whether that is a category mistake on my part. Be disciplined about the mapping and say plainly where the theory goes silent rather than forcing it to speak. I would rather have a short honest answer about the limits than a long one that makes the framework explain everything.
