Since I retired from the chemistry bench I have been reading my way into the philosophy of language, and last month I tried to explain the Twin Earth scenario to a reading group at the local institute. In the course of preparing, I built an argument of my own to see whether the externalist thesis really survives being pushed hard, and I have not been able to find where it breaks. I have written it out in numbered steps so that we can both see where it bends. (1) The meaning of a general term is fixed not by anything inside the speaker's head but by the speaker's environment -- more precisely, by the kind to which the term is causally anchored. (2) Two thinkers mean the same thing by a term only if their terms are anchored to the same kind, or to kinds that are the same in the relevant respects. (3) Yet no two thinkers are ever anchored to the world through the same causal trajectory: each of us perceives from a distinct position, with a distinct history of encounters, and no two of those histories are identical in every detail. (4) Therefore no two thinkers ever mean the same thing by their terms. (5) Therefore no two thinkers ever share a thought-content, and all apparent agreement and communication is a surface coincidence of sound. (6) That conclusion is absurd -- my colleague and I read the same paper last week and I am confident we understood it together -- so some step must be false, and I am inclined to blame premise (1), which my chemist's instincts already distrust, since even our own "water" is a mixture of isotopologues and dissolved ions rather than a single tidy kind, and a kind that is itself fuzzy seems a poor thing to hang meaning on. My colleague, who is a realist about mental content and rather fond of the Twin Earth story, insists the fault lies elsewhere.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Name the step that should be given up -- or, if none should be, say so and explain why the conclusion is not as absurd as it looks -- then explain why that step looked fine to me, and price the alternatives: for each candidate premise, what would I have to surrender in order to reject it, and how does that cost compare with the others? I should add that I have no interest in a survey of what various authors have said; I want your reasoning brought to bear on this specific chain, not a report of the field. Please be genuinely self-critical about your own answer: tell me which consideration would lead you to a different diagnosis, and where in your reasoning you think you are most likely to be going wrong. End with a short structured summary in the form of a table with the columns Step, Verdict (keep / reject / reject-only-under-a-particular-reading), Why it looked fine, and What rejecting it costs -- one row per step.
