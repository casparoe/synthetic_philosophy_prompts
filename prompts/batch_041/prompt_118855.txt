I build reinforcement-learning agents and run evolutionary simulations of populations of policies: an objective function that I write down, selection and mutation over generations, value estimates, regret measured against an optimal policy, exploration and exploitation, fitness landscapes. I know that formalism well; I have no philosophical training. I have come across a claim from the philosophy of biology, associated with Daniel Dennett and labelled by him a free-floating rationale, and I want to know what it looks like in my own vocabulary.

The claim, as I understand it, is this. A behavioural trait can have a reason behind it — a consideration in virtue of which the behaviour is appropriate — and that consideration can do real explanatory work in accounting for why the trait is there, even though no one, neither the organism nor any designer, ever has it in mind. Dennett's stock illustration is the broken-wing display of ground-nesting birds: the reason the display works is that it draws the predator away from the nest, and it is because it works that the behaviour persists, yet nothing in the bird represents the predator, the nest, or the drawing-away as a reason for anything. Selection, on this view, is an automatic reason-finder that is competent without comprehending. Dennett insists that this is no more ghostly than the fact that there were planets before anyone could count them, or that asteroids had centres of gravity before physicists calculated them.

Please restate this position as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use, element by element, and then give me the residue report: which parts survive the translation cleanly, which survive only with distortion — saying precisely what the distortion is — and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. I am especially interested in four things.

First, what plays the role of the rationale: the objective function, the actual selection gradient, the optimal policy, or something else — and what turns on the choice.

Second, whether the fact that in my world every objective function is authored by me, written down, exogenous, and fixed before the run begins, means that the free-floating element is precisely what my vocabulary cannot express, since I never have a reason that nobody wrote. Dennett's analogy with numbers and centres of gravity suggests that the rationale is supposed to be a fact predating anyone's representation of it; I want to know whether anything on my side is a fact of that kind, given that my reward signal is a design decision.

Third, whether the normative edge of the notion survives: whether saying that the bird ought to distract the predator, or that a plant failing to grow towards the light is making a mistake, is carried by anything I have — regret, suboptimality, misspecification — or whether optimality relative to a stipulated objective is a different kind of thing from a reason. Note that in the biological case there is no stipulated objective at all, only what actually propagates, so tell me whether the translation needs something like an expected or dispositional notion of fitness, and what that addition costs.

Fourth, the residue verdict: is my vocabulary missing something that is genuinely there, or is the philosophers' notion doing less work than its presentation suggests — for instance, is it merely a licence to use intentional idiom, which I could always translate back out?

Keep the translation honest. Do not quietly import assumptions of my framework — that the objective is given, that fitness is well defined and known to the modeller, that selection optimises — and present them as though they belonged to the original claim; where you must add something to make the translation go through, flag it as an addition. Write in British English.
