For the past three years I have lived in a country where I began by knowing no one, and the friendships that survived the move — together with the painting I took up to fill the evenings — have made a certain pattern impossible for me to ignore. I would like to know whether that pattern has a name, and if it does not, whether it can be given one that earns its keep.

The pattern shows itself first in friendship. A friendship that is going well seems to me to lose worth the moment it becomes useful. When a close friend and I started working together, and later when the friendship began producing introductions, favours and money, nothing about our actual meetings changed — the same conversation, the same affection — yet the relationship itself struck me as worth less than it had been, and I did not feel that I was being merely squeamish. The opposite also happens. A friendship that passed through a serious rupture and was repaired feels to me worth more than it would have been worth had the rupture never occurred, and more than the good moments on either side could account for if I simply added them up. Second, and independently, I notice the same structure in how I value pictures. A painting that is beautiful on its own seems worth more once someone looks at it with real understanding; and a fine passage set inside a mediocre painting can leave the whole worse than the passage alone would have been, while a clumsy passage can sometimes leave a good painting better. In each case the worth of the combination seems to depend on the arrangement, and on what each element is a part of, in a way I cannot recover from the worth of the elements taken one by one. Third — and I cannot tell whether this belongs with the others — I would not exchange this particular friendship for any number of friendships that were, by every measure I can name, at least as happy. If what I cared about were the happiness, equal amounts should leave me indifferent; they do not.

I have no training in philosophy, so I may be describing one thing, or two, or three. What I want is not a label but a carving. Which established ideas come nearest to each of these observations, and exactly where does each fall short? Do my examples belong to a single phenomenon, and if not, how many are there and how do they differ? If a single name exists, give it, but tell me precisely what it covers and what it excludes. If no single name exists, propose one, define it sharply enough that I could decide whether a new case falls under it, and say what it must not be confused with. I would rather be told that the pattern is three unrelated things than be handed one word that hides the joints.

Please structure your reply as follows. Bottom line: your verdict in two or three sentences, naming what you take the phenomenon to be and whether it has an established name. Closest established concepts: for each candidate, what it captures and where it fails, ranked by how well it fits — roughly 350 words. One phenomenon or several: sort my three observations, saying which cohere and which must be separated — roughly 250 words. A name and a definition: the established term if there is one, otherwise a coinage, with a definition precise enough to test and the contrasts it must be held apart from — roughly 200 words. Boundary cases: three or four cases near the edge, each with a verdict, so that I can see where the line falls — roughly 250 words.
