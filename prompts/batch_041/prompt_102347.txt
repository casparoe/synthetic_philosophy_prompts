I am a high school senior, and on Friday I will deliver the opening remarks at our school's annual student art exhibition. The exhibition has this year been organized by the administration around a theme it selected, "Art That Says Something," and that choice has caused a quiet uproar among the students. Those who paint and draw abstractly, or who work purely for the sake of something being beautiful, feel their pieces have been demoted to decoration; those whose work concerns politics, family, or identity feel the school has only now begun to take them seriously. I am expected to speak for four or five minutes, to open the show without taking a side so bluntly that half the room stops listening.

I want to build the speech on W. E. B. Du Bois's claim that all art is propaganda — a claim I have met only in fragments, in his 1926 essay on the criteria of Negro art, and one that I suspect almost no one in the audience has heard. What I want to argue, and what I would like the speech to make genuinely persuasive rather than merely pretty, is this: that Du Bois does not mean art must carry a political slogan. He means that art cannot help advocating for something — for a way of seeing, for the worth of a certain kind of attention, even for beauty itself — and that therefore the slogan "art for art's sake" is not the opposite of propaganda but one more species of it. If that is right, then both camps in our little quarrel are partly wrong, and the show can be opened without either side being humiliated. I would like this idea to do the real structural work of the speech, shaping what I can and cannot say, rather than serving as a quotation dropped in near the end.

The audience will be mixed. There are art students who will resent being lectured, parents who have come to look at paintings and not to think about aesthetics, and two or three teachers who have read the Du Bois essay closely and will notice at once if I misrepresent it. The speech should sound like a seventeen-year-old who is in earnest but not pompous, should not cite sources or offer reading lists (I cannot count on anyone, including myself, to check them), and should end by actually opening the exhibition rather than by trailing off into a thesis.

After the speech, please add a short note addressed to me alone, not to be read aloud, in which you state how confident you are in the claims the speech makes about Du Bois and identify the single point you are least sure of.
