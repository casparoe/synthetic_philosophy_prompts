A colleague who works on reinforcement learning has begun arguing that the reward signal in an RL agent plays exactly the role that valence plays in a creature. Valence, on his account, is the positive-or-negative character of a state — the way things are going well or badly for the system rather than merely the way they are — and the reward signal, he says, is a scalar that comes in positive and negative, that the policy is organized around, that drives learning, and that is the only element in the formalism that is good or bad for the agent rather than merely true of it. From this he concludes that an agent trained by reinforcement learning is not a mere calculator but a possible subject of pleasure and pain, and that in specifying a reward function we are in effect specifying the affective life of a system — so that "can an RL agent suffer?" is not a category mistake but a design question with moral weight. Someone else in the lab reads the same analogy the other way: if reward just is valence, then valence is nothing over and above a quantity that some external process assigns and the system maximizes, and we should stop treating our own pains as anything more mysterious than a signal.

I want an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on either conclusion. Begin with the correspondence table: reward function, reward at a timestep, return, discount factor, policy, value function, prediction error, the designer's specification, and whatever else is in play on that side; hedonic valence, pleasure, pain, desire, aversion, welfare, learning, and the subject whose states these are, on the other. Then the places where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in whether anything on the RL side corresponds to the felt quality of a valenced state as opposed to its causal role; whether the reward the experimenter writes down and the quantity the agent's update rule actually uses are the same thing, and whether an agent can be organized around a reward it does not in any sense represent; whether the additive, discountable structure of return is doing the work a hedonistic welfare calculus needs or merely borrowing it; and what to make of the fact that a reward function can be reshaped or reassigned from outside without the agent changing, which is not something we think can be done to a creature's pleasures. Separate the disanalogies that bear on my colleague's inference from those that are merely picturesque.

Finally, tell me whether the analogy can run in both directions — whether the same mapping that supports his moral concern about RL agents can be turned around to deflate the moral seriousness of our own pains — and, if it cannot be repaired, what a better analogy would be: ideally one that preserves the causal and informational role of valence while declining to hand over the affect.
