In a seminar I attend on the origins of inequality, a colleague habitually closes discussion with the claim that the agricultural revolution was the worst mistake in human history. He has used it twice now in ways I cannot reconcile: once to dismiss a question about whether early farmers were worse off than foragers, and once to explain contemporary inequality and our ecological predicament as its direct inheritance. I am fairly sure the sentence is doing more rhetorical work than argumentative work, but I would like to be able to say precisely what it asserts. Please lay out the distinct theses it could be expressing, as a menu of readings rather than a single preferred interpretation, and give each an explicit verdict: platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive but false, or not even well-formed.

I would like at least the following axes kept apart.

First, a claim about the typical person's welfare versus a claim about aggregate welfare. These can come apart: farming might well have made the average life worse while multiplying the number of lives lived, so that one reading of the slogan is true and another false at the same time. Say what each reading requires, and where the usual palaeopathological markers — more dental caries, more enamel hypoplasia and growth-arrest lines, shorter adult stature among early farmers — bear on each. Consider also whether describing foragers as affluent, in the older sense of having few wants that are easily satisfied, quietly changes what the comparison is measuring.

Second, a claim about distribution rather than welfare: that the mistake was not cultivation but the surplus, hierarchy and state that came with it. Ask whether that is a claim about agriculture at all, and whether it survives the existence of ranked and even monument-building sedentary foragers, such as the fisher peoples of the Northwest Coast, who had hereditary inequality without farming.

Third, a claim about trajectory rather than about a comparison between states: not that farming was worse than foraging, but that it was a one-way door, so that what was lost is option value rather than welfare. Say what this requires and whether the repeated independent invention of agriculture tells for or against it. Relatedly, work out the reading on which farming was chiefly a variance-reducing bet — a way of making bad years less catastrophic — rather than a mean-raising improvement, and say what that does to the word mistake.

Fourth, a claim about agency. Nobody chose agriculture, so determine whether mistake is being used as a metaphor for a bad unintended outcome, or whether it commits the speaker to a collective agent, and what turns on the difference.

Fifth, a claim whose comparison class is unspecified. Against what is farming being judged: no farming ever, a slower transition, or the same population under some different arrangement? Say whether the sentence is even well-formed without a counterfactual, and whether some readings presuppose a population axiology that nobody has supplied. Note in passing that the slogan's own vocabulary — revolution — presupposes a discrete event, whereas the transition spanned millennia and bundled together pastoralism, horticulture and mixed systems.

Sixth, the trivial reading, on which the sentence means only that farming brought grave costs as well as benefits, and is therefore unavailable for any of the argumentative work listed above.

Finally, for each of the uses I have actually witnessed — that the transition is to be regretted as such, that contemporary inequality and ecological crisis are its direct legacy, and that a return to foraging is the remedy — identify which reading the use requires, and say whether that reading is defensible on its own terms.

Set this out as a ranked menu, from the platitudinous to the radical, and do not supply a reading list or citations; what I want is the conceptual geography and the verdicts.
