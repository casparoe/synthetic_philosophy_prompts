Hey, I'm workshopping a short paper for a seminar on epistemic shiftiness and I'd love your comments on it. Here's the gist, which is basically the whole first half of the thing:

The setup: contextualists think 'knows' changes meaning with the attributor's context; pragmatic encroachers think knowledge itself depends on the subject's practical stakes. Everyone treats these as rival answers to the question of what makes knowledge ascriptions go shifty. I want to argue they're not rivals at all, because they're both instances of something more basic that I've been calling resolutionism.

Resolutionism: any knowledge ascription is really a claim at some level of grain. Saying someone knows the trail goes left is like saying the mountain is 4,213 feet high — it can be true at one grain and false at another without anyone being confused or dishonest. Two things set the grain. Stakes set how much error the conversation can tolerate (if a wrong turn costs you a night on a cliff you need a finer grain than if it costs you a twenty minute detour). Salience sets which possibilities are even in view, and therefore which contrasts the claim is distinguishing among. So contextualism and encroachment aren't competing theories, they're two knobs on the same dial.

Payoff one: I can explain the bank cases without giving up factivity. Nothing about the evidence changes, and the proposition known doesn't change either — what changes is the resolution at which we're asking whether the belief tracks.

Payoff two: it explains why knowledge talk is perfectly fine at coarse grain and starts to squeak at fine grain, which I think is a better story than either side tells on its own.

But here's the case that worries me. The bridge inspector. She's checked the bridge a hundred times, she's bored, she has no personal stake at all — worst case for her is paperwork. But the attributor is a commuter whose kids cross that bridge. On the encroachment picture it looks like her stakes are what matter, so she knows; on a salience picture the commuter's context is what matters, so maybe she doesn't. My resolutionism wants to say the grain is set by the question being asked, and the commuter is asking a finer question than the inspector is answering, so both ascriptions come out true at their own grain. That definately feels right to me, but I suspect it's cheap — I've made the two ascriptions index different questions and then acted like I discovered that they don't conflict. Is that a real result or just me relabeling the disagreement?

Also: does resolutionism just collapse into relevant alternatives theory with stakes picking the relevant alternatives? If so I've spent six pages reinventing a wheel and I'd rather know now.

So, comments please. Specifically: (1) is the unification doing any work, or is it a redescription? (2) the bridge inspector case — does it break the view, or is my two-questions move legit? (3) anywhere I'm just assuming what I want to prove. Don't be nice, I need this torn up before Thursday.
