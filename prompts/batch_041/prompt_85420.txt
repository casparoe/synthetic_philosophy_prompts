I'm building a unit on whether diachronic rationality is a real requirement or just rationality-at-a-time plus memory, and I need a case I can hand to the seminar. Build me a money pump in the broad sense for an agent whose preferences are complete and transitive at every single moment -- so nobody gets to accuse them of momentary incoherence -- but whose tastes shift over time in a cycle, so that a sequence of trades, each one strictly prefered by them at the moment of choosing, leaves them with a bundle they'd have ranked below the starting one ex ante and still rank below it at the end. Constraints: realistic (an ordinary adult, ordinary goods, sums of money or time a normal person actually faces), no exotic tech, no deception, no one with market power, and no fees or switching costs -- the loss has to come out of the trades themselves, so dont let a fee-collecting counterparty do the work; the other party need only have stable, boring preferences. The agent is fully informed and sophisticated: they see the whole cycle coming and would rather not be in it. Keep the case to a paragraph. Then walk me through it: which theory of practical rationality endorses every individual step, which one condemns the sequence as a whole, which single feature is actually doing the pumping, and what happens if you delete that feature -- does the case degenerate into an ordinary intransitivity pump, or into a plain weakness-of-will story? Finally, how would someone who thinks the agent's preference at the time of choice is authoritative attack the case? And be straight with me about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among the people who work on it -- dont hand me one camp's line as if it were the consensus.
