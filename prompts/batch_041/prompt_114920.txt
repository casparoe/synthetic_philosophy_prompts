For a graduate seminar on animal ethics I am assembling a teaching case, and I want the case to be a piece of satire rather than an argument, since the students are already fluent in the argument itself. The subject is fish and crustacean welfare in aquaculture, which I would like to keep off the familiar farmed-mammal ground. Please write the animal-welfare section of the annual sustainability report of a fictional vertically integrated shrimp producer — call it the Halcyon Aqua Group, and have the document describe itself as one of the largest farmed-shrimp producers in the world. Aim for roughly 650 words, in the register of a real ESG report: a materiality assessment, a statement from a named but invented head of aquatic animal welfare, tables described in prose, targets with dates attached, and the usual vocabulary of risk, assurance and stakeholder engagement. Everything — the company, the people, the figures — is invented, and nothing should appear in quotation marks except what the fictional company is putting into its own mouth.

The comedy has to come from getting the philosophy and the biology right, so that someone who works on invertebrate sentience finds the document uncomfortably accurate rather than merely cute. It should enact, without ever stating them as objections, the difficulties that make decapod welfare hard: that the evidence for pain is inferential, since nociception is not pain and the behavioural argument has to run through convergent evolution and the problem of other minds; that common slaughter practice — chilling, ice slurry, boiling — sits awkwardly beside any requirement that the animal be unconscious before it dies, and that an agreed stunning method is precisely what the field does not have; that a procedure such as eyestalk ablation, widely used in shrimp hatcheries to bring broodstock into spawning, is the sort of thing a welfare section would rather not discuss; and above all that the number of individual animals involved is so large that even a modest credence in their sentience yields an enormous expected-welfare obligation — an argument the company cannot answer and therefore manages. Let the corporate idiom carry the satire: the precautionary principle recruited as a selling point, welfare improvements that happen to improve texture and shelf life, and engagement with the science treated as a permanent condition rather than a step towards a conclusion. I want a reader to be able to work out what is being satirised — the conversion of a live moral question into a disclosure item — without the writer ever winking or leaving the register, and with no jokes at the animals' expense; the humour should be at the expense of the prose. It should be legible to someone who knows nothing about aquaculture, while a specialist should see that the author knows the terrain. No citations, no real organisations, no real people.
