"Question 2 (10 points). Consider the following case, which I'll call the Two Rooms case. In Room A, Priya is looking at a blue vase on a table and sees it. In Room B, Priya is having a hallucination of a blue vase on a table; her optic nerve is being stimulated in just the way it would be if a vase were actually there. By stipulation, Priya's experience in Room A and her experience in Room B are introspectively indistinguishable: she cannot tell, from the inside, which room she is in. (a) Explain how the traditional argument from illusion, extended to a case like Room B, is supposed to show that Priya's two experiences are experiences of the same fundamental kind, and say what that conclusion is supposed to imply about whether we ever directly perceive mind-independent objects. (b) Explain how the disjunctivist responds. (c) Does the disjunctivist's response commit her to the claim that Priya's evidence — what she is in a position to know — differs between Room A and Room B? Take a position and defend it."

And here's what the student wrote:

"(a) The argument from illusion starts from the fact that things can look other than they are — a straight stick half in water looks bent. Since what we are immediately aware of is the way things look, and the way things look can come apart from how things are, what we are immediately aware of must be something other than the mind-independent object: an appearance or sense-datum. The Room B case is really just a special version of that same argument. There the vase isn't even there, yet the experience is exactly like the Room A experience. If a veridical perception and a hallucination can be indistinguishable in this way, then they can't be two different kinds of thing; they must share a common core. That common core is what the sense-datum theorist calls the sense-datum and what a later theorist might call the representational content. The conclusion is that we never directly perceive mind-independent objects — at best we perceive inner proxies for them, and perception of the world is really an inference from those proxies.

(b) The disjunctivist rejects the step from 'indistinguishable' to 'same kind.' Even if Priya can't tell the two rooms apart, the Room A experience is a genuine relation to a mind-independent vase and the Room B experience is not; they are different in kind even though they are subjectively identical. So the disjunctivist says the Room B experience has no phenomenal character at all — there's nothing it's like to be hallucinating, because a hallucination isn't really an experience of anything. The disjunctivist can say all this without giving up the stipulation that Priya can't tell the difference, because the difference between the two states isn't a difference she has access to from the inside.

(c) Yes. If the Room A experience is a genuine perception of the vase and the Room B experience is not, then in Room A Priya is in a position to know there's a vase and in Room B she isn't. So her evidence differs between the rooms, and the disjunctivist has to say that a hallucinating subject and a perceiving subject with the same experience do not have the same evidence. This is the payoff of disjunctivism: it restores the idea that in the good case the subject can tell that she is perceiving, so perception gives us a kind of access to the world that the skeptic's 'common core' picture rules out."
