Act as a poet who's actually read the Hofstadter material on strange loops and self-symbols, not someone who just knows the vibe. Quick context: I'm a fourth-year PhD student in cog sci (phil of mind on the side) and I'm making a handout for the last session of our reading group on self-reference and the self. I want a poem for the closing page of it.

Before you write anything, think it through step by step and brainstorm a bit. I don't need the brainstorm in your answer, just don't skip it. What is a strange loop actually supposed to be: an abstract cycle in which you keep moving up through levels of abstraction and find yourself, to your shock, back where you started, a paradoxical level-crossing loop. Then why does Hofstadter think the 'I' is the symbol a system ends up using for the whole loop that's generating it, i.e. a symbol that stands for its own producer. And think about the awkward part, that the self-symbol is a fiction in one sense and a real pattern in another, and that it can't describe itself from outside because there's no outside to stand in.

Then write the poem, with these constraints:

- 14 to 20 lines, no fixed form, no rhyme needed, but it has to read as a poem and not as an essay with line breaks.
- The first line comes back verbatim as the last line. Nothing else may repeat verbatim. I want one loop, not a refrain, and reading the poem a second time should be part of finishing it.
- Somewhere inside there has to be nested quotation: a voice quoting a voice quoting a voice, with the innermost one turning out to be the one doing the outermost talking. Do the level-crossing with punctuation and line breaks; don't explain it in the poem.
- The speaker is the 'I' itself in Hofstadter's sense, the self-symbol. It should be exactly as substantial as he thinks it is: not a soul, not a ghost in a machine, but not a mere illusion either, since the pattern is real. It shouldn't be able to describe itself except by pointing at the loop it sits on top of.
- Don't use the words strange loop, tangled hierarchy, heterarchy, Gödel, Escher, Bach or Hofstadter anywhere in the poem, and no title or epigraph that gives the game away. If someone in the group knows the material they should recognize it within the first four lines; if they don't, it should still be a decent poem.
- Don't resolve it. The loop shouldn't close like a circle landing on exactly the same spot. I want the return to feel like a spiral, close enough to hurt but not identical, and since the last line is the first line you have to earn that.

Accuracy matters more to me than prettiness here. A phil of mind person who knows the book shouldn't wince, and a poet shouldn't either. If you're tempted to make the 'I' more mystical than Hofstadter would allow, definately dont.
