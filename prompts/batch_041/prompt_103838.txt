I'm taking a metaphysics module on vagueness this term and I've got to hand in an essay plan, but I've drafted the thing and I'm not sure it holds up. Would you read it and tell me where it's weak? Here's the draft:

Everyone explains the sorites with the tolerance principle: if one grain can't turn a non-heap into a heap, then no number of grains can, and yet somewhere it does. The usual suspects — epistemicism, supervaluationism, degree theory — all locate the trouble in the *meaning* of 'heap'. Either there's a sharp line we can't know, or there are truth-value gaps, or truth comes in degrees. I want to argue that this is a mistake about where the problem lives. The paradox isn't a fact about the word 'heap'; it's a fact about a *rule we use when reasoning*, and rules can be tolerant even when meanings are perfectly precise.

Here's the move. Take a predicate that is definitely precise, like 'is taller than 1.7m'. Sharp line, no vagueness at all. Now attach a *practical* rule: if two people differ in height by less than a millimetre, don't treat them differently for the purpose at hand. That rule is tolerant. Chain it and you get a contradiction — everyone is taller than 1.7m — even though the predicate was never vague. So tolerance is not a property of predicates; it's a property of the norms of discrimination we bolt onto them.

So I want to distinguish *semantic tolerance* (no sharp boundary in the extension) from *assertoric/practical tolerance* (a policy about which differences we treat as insignificant). The sorites only goes through if you read the tolerant premise as a claim about the extension. Read it as a policy instead, and the paradox dissolves: the policy is fine, it's just not a claim about where the line is.

I think this relocates the paradox onto us and our norms of reasoning rather than onto the world's joints. It also predicts something: you should get sorites for precise predicates whenever a tolerant policy is attached — queues, crowds, 'tall for a jockey'. Is that a point in favour, or am I just redescribing the same problem?

What I'm unsure about: (1) is the distinction real, or is it just the semantic/epistemic divide relabelled? (2) does 'practical tolerance' even make sense as a non-semantic notion, or does any policy already presuppose a semantics? (3) does the paradox actually dissolve, or have I just moved the bump under the carpet? I'd like comments on accuracy first, then on whether the central distinction does any work, then on whether the whole thing is interesting or just a definately-already-been-done reframing.
