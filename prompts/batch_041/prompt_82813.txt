I want a case analysis, run in both directions: the theory illuminating the episode, and the episode testing the theory. Not a survey, and no citations, reading lists, or bibliography — I am not testing your recall of a literature.

The episode is the Martian canal affair. What I take to be solid: after Giovanni Schiaparelli's charts of 1877 gave the planet a system of straight lines — his term for them, canali, means channels, and it entered English as canals, a word that suggests engineering — a considerable number of observers reported a globe-spanning network of such lines. Percival Lowell, who founded an observatory in Arizona and spent some fifteen years drawing Mars, went furthest: the network was, he argued, the work of an advanced civilization irrigating a drying world. The reports were made by eye; observers stared for hours, waiting for a moment of steady air, and drew what they had seen, and the network did not appear in photographs of the planet. Later observers reported lines where earlier ones had seen none, and some reported the lines as doubled, in parallel pairs. Lowell, no fool about instruments, preferred small telescopes to large ones on the grounds that they resolved fine planetary detail better. In 1909, at the eyepiece of one of the largest refractors in the world, Eugène Antoniadi — who had previously accepted the canals — saw none at all and concluded that they were an illusion. Spacecraft found no canals. Historians still disagree about what produced the reports; drawing convention, translation, optical and physiological artifacts, and expectation have all been proposed, and I do not want that dispute adjudicated from memory.

Read the episode through predictive processing and the free energy principle, in their standard shape: perception as inference under a generative model — the picture on which perceiving is a controlled hallucination, corrected only where prediction error is weighted heavily enough to matter; precision-weighting of sensory evidence; attention, action, and instrument choice as ways of changing the evidence rather than the model; expected free energy and the drive to seek information; and the dark room problem, which asks why an agent that minimizes prediction error does not simply settle into a dark, quiet room.

Then:

1. The mapping. What plays the role of the generative model, the sensory evidence, and the precision? What does the framework say these observers faced, and what does it say they should have done — including at what point one of them should have distrusted his own perception, and whether the framework can express that ought at all.

2. What it explains. Take the details a lazy telling skips: the network growing across successive oppositions, the doubling, trained observers reading structure that the plates did not contain, and the instrument chosen in a way that preserved the prior.

3. What it misses. Where does the framework need instrumental, institutional, or social history to do work it cannot do itself? Say precisely what the difference is between explaining why a percept formed and judging whether it was warranted. Two candidates for the gap: the translation of canali into canals, which is neither a perceptual nor an inferential event; and the fact that this was a community of observers, while the framework's home case is a single agent.

4. The theory under test. The flexibility is the worry: priors plus precision can be narrated as the story of accurate perception or as the story of the canals, in the same vocabulary. Is there anything internal to the framework that separates the two cases, or does precision function as a free parameter that absorbs whatever happened? Does the dark room problem get worse here — why did these observers keep observing, and does expected free energy predict the direction of their inquiry or merely redescribe it? Give the strongest reply available to a defender, then say whether it holds.

5. Verdict. Confirmation, anomaly, or a case the framework never anticipated? And would a rival do better — an older account in terms of theory-laden observation and the psychology of expectation, or a social-epistemological account of how a community calibrates its instruments and distributes trust? If a rival handles part of the case better, name the part.
