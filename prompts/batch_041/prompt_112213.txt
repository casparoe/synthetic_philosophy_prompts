So I spent last week at this two-day safety workshop — mostly lab people, a couple of academics, the usual — and I'm trying to turn my notes into a short piece for the ideas section, and I'd really like your comments before I file. Here's the gist of what I'm arguing. What struck me is how casually everyone slides between two projects that I think are actually pretty different. One is alignment: getting a model to genuinely care about the right things, so that when it does something nobody anticipated it's still on our side. The other is control: assuming the model might already be misaligned and asking, given that, how do we keep it from doing damage — sandboxing, monitoring, tripwires, that whole apparatus. Some researchers have started just calling the second thing "control" and the first thing "alignment," but in a lot of the conversation the two get used interchangably, as if a solid control story were a substitute for a solid alignment story.

My actual argument is that the substitution doesn't hold, and the reason is a cost I've been calling the containment tax. Every safeguard you build on the assumption that the model might be scheming against you eats into the capability you were trying to get in the first place — you restrict its tools, you route it through a monitor, you make it ask permission, and by the time you're done you've got something a lot less useful than the thing you were worried about. That's not necessarily a bad trade, but it is a trade, and I don't think people are pricing it honestly. There's also an asymmetry I want to build the piece around: alignment failures tend to be gradual and corrigible — the model's a bit off, you notice, you patch it — whereas control failures are lumpy, because control is a claim about the worst case, and the worst case either happens or it doesn't. So leaning hard on control trades a smooth risk curve for a spiky one, and I think that should change how much of the safety budget goes where.

What I'd really like from you is this: (1) is that alignment/control distinction as clean as I'm making it, or am I building a wall between two things that are really one thing with different marketing? (2) where am I getting the mechanics wrong — I'm a journalist, not a researcher, and I don't want to mischaracterize how monitoring or tripwires actually work in practice, especially the part where the thing doing the monitoring is itself a model; and (3) does the containment tax actually land as an argument, or is it just a fancy name for "safety costs capability," which everyone already knows and has already priced in? If it's the latter, tell me straight, I'd rather cut it than get dunked on.
