I'm putting together materials for an upper-level epistemology seminar on peer disagreement, and I need a joke for the handout — one that will actually land for readers who know the debate and fall flat for readers who don't. Please write it from scratch rather than reaching for a philosophy joke that already circulates.

Here is the ground I want the punchline to stand on, spelled out so that nothing turns on your recall of the literature. There are two poles:

Conciliationism (sometimes called the equal weight view): if you discover that someone you regard as a genuine epistemic peer — roughly as intelligent, as well informed, and as free of bias as you are, and who has considered the same evidence — disagrees with you about some question, then you must move your credence substantially toward theirs. You cannot stay put merely because you think your own reasoning is sound; your confidence that your reasoning is sound is precisely what the disagreement calls into question.

Steadfastness: on making the same discovery, you should re-examine your reasoning, but if on reflection you still find it sound, you may keep your view. The peer's disagreement is one further item of evidence to weigh, and on many occasions it need not move you much.

A middle position sits between them — that the disagreement is itself higher-order evidence, whose weight depends on how well each party actually reasoned — but the joke should be built on the two poles.

Two wrinkles I would like the joke to exploit; either one will do.

(a) Asymmetric surrender. The two views disagree about what to do about disagreement, so they can be aimed at the disagreement between themselves. A conciliationist who meets a steadfast peer is, by her own lights, required to move toward his position — including toward his position that she need not have moved — while he, by his lights, need not move at all. The theory that demands more revision is the one that loses the exchange.

(b) The regress about peerhood. Whether someone counts as a peer, or as a peer on this particular question, is itself something you can disagree about. If the conciliationist must conciliate about peerhood, then the class of people entitled to move her shifts, and the shifts can iterate.

What I want:

1. The joke itself — dialogue or short scenario, 150 to 300 words. It should turn on the actual content of these positions rather than on philosophers never agreeing or epistemologists being fussy. A reader who knows the debate should laugh at the punchline; a reader who does not should still be able to follow the setup and see why the ending is supposed to be funny.

2. A short note, 150 to 250 words, on what the joke commits itself to: which construal of the two positions the punchline requires, where it takes liberties (for instance, treating either position as more unified than it is, or quietly assuming a uniqueness thesis — that a given body of evidence permits only one rational credence — where the dispute may not need one), and which version of either position would make the punchline stop working. Treat this as the joke's substitute for saying what would change your mind: if the asymmetry I described turns out to be an artifact of how the two views are usually stated rather than something in the views themselves, then the joke is a cheap shot rather than a joke, and I want to be told so.

Write in American English.
