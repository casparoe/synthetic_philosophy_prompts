Give me your credence that the following claim is true: for every agent who satisfies some defensible theory of practical rationality — not necessarily a Savage-style expected-utility maximizer, but not irrational either — the option of learning something before acting is at least as good, by the agent's own credences and values, as the option of acting now without it. I take it as settled that this holds for agents who maximize expected utility, update by conditionalization, and face a decision problem in which the value of an act does not depend on which information they hold; my question is whether the conclusion survives outside those assumptions, or whether every apparent counterexample is really a case where a premise fails rather than a case where information is genuinely bad for a rational agent.

Decompose your credence into at least these terms, with a number and one line of justification each:

(a) the probability that suspense-type cases — where knowing the answer destroys the value of the experience, as when you read the ending of a novel you are enjoying, or when a surprise is spoiled — are genuine counterexamples rather than violations of the assumption that the value of an act is independent of which information the agent holds;

(b) the probability that cases where the information you receive is probabilistically correlated with the act you will later choose (predictor scenarios, blackmail scenarios in which receiving the message is itself evidence about your dispositions, and the like) are genuine counterexamples — this will depend on how much weight you give to causal versus evidential or functional decision theory;

(c) the probability that a non-expected-utility decision theory — imprecise or ambiguous credences, maximin or minimax-regret rules, risk aversion not capturable by any utility function — can make information expected to be harmful to an agent who is otherwise rational;

(d) the probability that something the framework cannot accommodate even by redescription — a preference for ignorance, the value of not knowing, the diachronic structure of preference, the value of retaining the option to remain ignorant — survives as a genuine residue;

(e) your probability that the dispute is largely verbal: that 'information,' 'expected utility,' and 'rational' can each be defined so that either answer comes out true, so that the real disagreement is only about which definitions we should adopt.

Then combine the terms into a single number, name the term you are least confident in, and say what argument or formal result would move your number most. If you think some of my terms double-count, say which and adjust rather than answering all five.
