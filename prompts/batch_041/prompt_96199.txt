I've been hanging around a comment thread about the two-envelope problem for what feels like years now (two envelopes, one holds twice the other, you pick one, you reason that the other is equally likely to be double or half so it's worth 1.25x what you're holding, you switch, and then the same reasoning says switch back, forever). It's a forum I read most days, full of the usual rationalist-adjacent crowd, and the argument has completely stalled. Nobody has changed their mind in ages. What I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a solution to the puzzle — I can get a solution anywhere, but I want to know what kind of disagreement this actually is.

Roughly the camps are:

1. Improper prior / undefined expectation. The switching argument needs the probability that the other envelope is double to be 1/2 whatever amount you happen to be holding. No real distribution over amounts has that property. Fix a genuine distribution and the expected gain from switching is exactly zero. So the paradox is an artefact of reasoning as if a prior existed when it doesn't.

2. Not just the prior. This camp says the finite-expectation version of the paradox still goes through, so "the expectation is infinite or undefined" can't be the whole diagnosis; the error has to be something about how the arguement uses the phrase "the other envelope" — treating a random quantity as though it were a fixed one, or conditioning on something you can't condition on, or sliding between two different questions. (I'm paraphrasing the camp, not endorsing it.)

3. Well-posedness. Expected value is only defined when the relevant expectations converge. On this view the paradox is a symptom of asking a question — what is the expected value of the other envelope — that has no answer, and the fix is to insist that decision problems be well posed before we ask what a rational agent should do.

4. The loud minority. Something is deeply wrong with expected utility theory, or at least with the assumption that an agent who prefers more money can't be a money pump. Usually stated with more confidence than argument.

Two things I've thrown into the pile myself, and I'd like them assessed:

(a) Someone in the thread who actually knows measure theory says that camp 1's move — you can't have a uniform distribution over an unbounded quantity — is a theorem about countably additive probability only. Go finitely additive and you can have a uniform distribution on the naturals, and then the switching argument seems to come back to life. If that's right, how much of this whole fight is about envelopes and how much is about which probability theory you're allowed to use?

(b) My own version, which I think is cute. Take N players. There are N pairs of envelopes, pair i holding amounts a_i and 2a_i, the a_i's fixed in advance and unknown to anyone. Each player is handed one envelope at random from their own pair. Each player runs the switching argument and concludes that swapping has positive expected gain. But if they all swap, the multiset of amounts people are holding is exactly the same as before, so the total is unchanged and the sum of the realised gains is zero. Expectations add, so the sum of their expected gains is zero, so they can't all have a positive expected gain — and since they're all in symmetric positions, either all of their reasonings are sound or all are broken. I think this shows the switching argument is invalid without having to locate the error, and I think it also cuts in favour of camp 3, but I can't tell whether it begs the question by assuming the expectations exist in the first place. Same thought, less formally: if you accept the switching argument you should be willing to pay a small fee to switch, and then again, and again, forever, which should ring alarm bells for anyone who's read the money pump stuff.

Is camp 4 raising anything the others need to answer?

No reading list please — I don't want titles to go and look up, and I can't search anyway. I want the diagnosis and the reasons for it.
