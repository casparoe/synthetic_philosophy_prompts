Alright, so I've been lurking on LW and the old SSC archives and basically every decision-theory comment thread for like a decade now, and I want to commission a bit of satire from you.

Give me a mock official driver's handbook — the deadpan, laminated kind of study guide a DMV hands out to learners, no winking at the reader — for a (obviously fictional) jurisdiction that has fully internalized the absent-minded driver problem and rebuilt its whole driver-training regime around it. You know the setup: you're driving down a road with two exits. Take the first and you get 0, take the second and you get 4, drive past both and you get 1. The catch is you can't tell the intersections apart — at either one you never know whether you're at the first or the second. So the handbook is a government agency earnestly trying to teach ordinary motorists to behave optimally on this road, written by people who take it completely seriously.

The comedy has to come from getting the decision theory *right*, not from inventing stuff. Specifically:

- the "wrong" method the handbook spends a whole chapter warning against should be the genuinely tempting one — deciding at each intersection as if you could just pick the best option *there*, right now, which is the naive per-node answer — and the handbook should be correct that it's wrong, for the correct reason (you can't condition on which intersection you're at, because by stipulation you don't know). If you present the naive per-node answer as the correct one I will close the tab in disgust.
- the "correct" policy the handbook endorses has to actually be optimal, so please do the math and dont fudge it. The joke only lands if a decision theorist reads it and nods instead of wincing.
- the driver's inability to tell the exits apart should be treated as a stipulation, not a memory failure to be repaired — and the handbook needs some deadpan bureaucratic reply to "why don't you just read the mile markers" and "why not put up signs", because that objection is definately going to occur to the reader and a real agency would have a canned answer ready.
- there should be at least a whiff of the pre-commitment / Newcomb-flavored thing, since that's what this problem is really about for the blog crowd — that you do better by binding yourself to a policy than by re-deciding at each node — but don't let it become a lecture on EDT vs CDT. Let it be the thing the handbook is secretly about.
- and let it gently rib the rationalist tendency to convert your entire life into a pre-commitment problem. That's me, I'm the guy with the written commitment contract for his own birthday.

Also make it readable for someone who's never heard of the problem — the jokes should mostly be about bureaucracy and self-binding, with the decision theory as the load-bearing thing underneath.

Format it as the handbook, roughly 700–900 words total, with these sections in this order and about these lengths:

- "Foreword from the Registrar" — ~70 words
- "Chapter One: You Do Not Know Where You Are" — ~130 words
- "Chapter Two: The Tempting Method, and Why It Fails" — ~150 words
- "Chapter Three: Your Policy" — ~150 words
- "Chapter Four: Objections You Will Raise, In Order" — ~180 words
- "Chapter Five: Questions from the Written Examination" — ~120 words, a handful of questions with the official answers printed underneath
- then a final section titled "Notes on the Joke" — ~150 words, in your own voice, telling me which bit of the real problem each part is keyed to, and where you worried the satire might be off or unfair

Deadpan all the way through, no narrator winking at me. Cheers.
