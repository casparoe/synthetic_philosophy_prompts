In my history seminar my teacher issues a verdict on how much credit to give each claim we meet about the distant past, and before our next unit I want to reverse-engineer the standard she is actually applying. So far she has accepted a grain-shipment figure preserved in a single administrative document, because a warehouse tally matches it; accepted the terms of a treaty recorded independently in two disinterested archives; rejected a miracle story that survives in one source written by a cleric with an obvious interest in promoting the shrine; rejected a boast attributed to a general when the only witness is the general's own admiring biographer, careful as that biographer otherwise is; and accepted the date of a distant eruption reported by a single annalist, since ash layers in the region agree with him. For next week she has set three claims: (a) a battle tactic described by a lone surviving source who had no stake in the outcome and whose account fits the terrain, but which no other source mentions and no physical trace confirms; (b) an atrocity reported by one writer who plainly hated the perpetrators, yet consistent with what we know of the campaign and corroborated by mass graves; and (c) a ruler's secret conversion reported by a single well-placed, disinterested courtier, contradicting everything else we know about that ruler's public religious policy and about the region's practices. Predict her verdict on each, state the rule she appears to be running, name at least two other rules that fit her earlier verdicts equally well, and say which of (a), (b), and (c) would best discriminate among those rules, and why.
