I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether lying is actually worse than just misleading someone, and I want to know if there's a principle behind how I feel about it. Can you take the role of a philosopher who works on deception, treat my gut reactions as data, and think it through carefully before you answer? Maybe start by sorting the cases into groups and saying what the grouping seems to track, then commit to a rule.

Here's where I've landed:

- Telling my boss I have a dentist appointment when I don't, to duck a call I don't want: a lie, and I feel a bit grubby about it, but it's minor.
- Same call, saying "I've got a prior commitment" when the commitment is watching the game: I feel fine about this one, even though he's just as fooled.
- Selling my old car and saying "it's been reliable for me" when I know the transmission is going: this feels like a lie to me, and worse than the dentist thing, even though every word is true.
- A company rep saying "our supplement is FDA-approved" when only one ingredient is: worse still, and I think it's worse *because* it hides behind the literal truth.
- The murderer at the door asking where my friend is. "He's not here" (a lie) or "I haven't seen him since this morning" (true, if it is): both fine.
- Telling my brother something false that he doesn't believe anyway, just to wind him up: technically a lie, but I don't think it's wrong at all.
- Telling my partner "I was out with Dave" when Dave was there and so was someone I shouldn't have been with: nearly as bad as lying straight out.

I've heard the word "paltering" for that true-but-misleading move — using a carefully chosen true statement to steer someone to the wrong conclusion — and it's the one I can't place.

The story I tell myself is that a lie puts the false thing into someone's head while paltering just lets them put it there themselves, so the second is more their own doing. But the car and the FDA cases make me think that's just me letting myself off the hook.

So: is there one rule that gets most of these, and what does it say about cases I haven't listed — lying to someone who already knows I'm lying, or just staying quiet and letting them assume? And which of my verdicts do I have to give up to keep the rule clean? Please don't just tell me "lying is worse" or "it depends how much harm it does" — I want the actual principle, stated so I could apply it, plus an honest note on where it breaks.
