I'm a backend engineer, no real philosophy training (one ethics elective in college, which I mostly remember for the guy who argued that cheating on problem sets is fine because of something about universalizability), but I've gotten into philosophy of science on the side lately, mostly podcasts and a couple of audiobooks on my commute. Anyway, there's a guy on my team, Ravi, who did a physics PhD before he bailed for software, and we've been going at it for months about whether we should believe our best scientific theories are actually true (or approximately true, or whatever the right word is) or just empirically adequate tools that happen to predict well. He's on the realist side. I've ended up anti-realist mostly because of the historical record argument — the one where you look at how many theories people accepted as successful turned out to posit things that just don't exist. Phlogiston, caloric, the ether, absolute space in Newtonian mechanics, that whole parade. I think the argument is called the pessimistic meta-induction or something like that.

The frustrating part is that after maybe six or seven rounds neither of us has moved, and it's not because either of us is being stupid about it, or at least I don't think so. His move is always: sure, the theories died, but the parts that were doing the predicting survived — the equations, the structural relations, the bits that carried the empirical load — so the moral isn't 'stop believing theories', it's 'be careful about which part of a theory you're believing in'. My move is always: that's a story you can only tell after the fact, and if you get to carve off the surviving bits once the dust has settled then the claim has no content, because nobody in 1880 could've told you which parts of the ether story were the load-bearing structural ones and which were just the discarded furniture. He says the fact that I can't tell in advance which parts will survive is exactly why my induction is a bad argument, since it needs me to claim that what got discarded is what people actually believed. I say that's precisely what they believed. And we loop.

What I want from you isn't a verdict on who's right about realism. I want a diagnosis of what this disagreement actually is, using the actual moves above. My hypothesis, and tell me if it's too cute: we're not really disagreeing about history at all, we're disagreeing about the unit the induction is supposed to range over. I'm counting theories as the units — a theory gets accepted, it's successful, it dies, that's one failure, tick. He's counting something finer-grained, posits or structures or equations, so his denominator is different and he computes a different frequency, and because neither of us will budge on the unit, the question 'how often do successful theories turn out to be true' doesn't even have a well-defined answer between us. If that's the shape of it, what kind of disagreement is that, exactly — a verbal dispute about how to individuate theories, a real dispute about the historical record, a disguised dispute about what counts as a theory being successful (novel prediction? unification? just not blowing up?), or a dispute about what believing a theory is even supposed to commit you to? And given your diagnosis, is there anything that would actually count as progress here — a distinction that dissolves the thing, some piece of history that would settle it, a formal result, or honestly just a change of subject — or is this one of those fights where no amount of work will end it because the two sides are answering different questions under one heading? One more thing, and this might be the real crux: does it matter whether the argument is about theories or about the reliability of the method that produced them? I had a thought that if you shift it to methods the induction gets worse, since theory-construction as a method is younger and its track record is shorter and messier than the theories themselves — and Ravi said that's cheating, because you'd be comparing a method against a set of outcomes the method partly produced. I couldn't tell if he was right. Be concrete and don't just give me a survey of positions.
