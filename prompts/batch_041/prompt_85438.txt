A friend of mine, Alex, has a cycling habit that has become a standing argument between two other friends, Ines and Raf, and I would like you to referee the argument rather than the habit.

Alex buys second-hand bicycles from a dealer who sets up at a Saturday market and will swap one machine for another for a flat £5 plus an afternoon of your time. Alex's preferences, as he states them and as his choices bear out, form a cycle: he prefers the red bike to the blue one he owns, prefers the green bike to the red one, and prefers the blue one to the green. So he pays £5 and an afternoon to trade blue for red, then £5 and an afternoon to trade red for green, then £5 and an afternoon to trade green for blue, arriving back where he began, £15 and three afternoons poorer. The dealer is back every Saturday, Alex returns every Saturday, and the loop has run many times. Alex, who is an economist by training, knows exactly what is happening to him; there is nothing stopping him but his own willingness, since he need only say no.

His defence, which is what the argument is about, is this: at each step the trade on offer is a genuine improvement on what he is holding, and he would rather be the sort of person who takes genuine improvements than the sort who refuses one because of where a sequence of them might lead. Refusing a step that is better than the status quo, he says, is superstition.

Ines says that this is precisely the superstition — or rather, that it is a proof of irrationality rather than a defence against the charge. Someone whose preferences guarantee that he can be made strictly worse off by a series of moves he would endorse one at a time has a defective set of preferences. Worse, Alex's own defence concedes the point: if each step is an improvement and the whole sequence is a loss, then at least one of his three pairwise judgments must be false, and the £15 is the reductio.

Raf says Ines has confused preferences with trading rules. Alex's three pairwise judgments may each be perfectly true — about three different comparisons, at three different times — without there being any single fact about which bike is best. Nothing in preferring red to blue, green to red and blue to green forces him to keep trading, and an agent who sees the cycle coming can resolve, once, not to enter it. The cycle is escapable, and escapability is all that rationality can demand of a set of preferences. Ines replies that escapability proves too much: a person who resolves in advance to accept no trade, however favourable, is not rational but merely rigid, and if Alex has to protect himself from his own preferences with a rule, the preferences are what is broken. Raf answers that rules of exactly this kind — the ones that protect you from a local pull you would regret in aggregate — are most of practical wisdom, and that nothing is broken in the preferences; the defect, if any, lies in the protocol. Ines says the protocol is not a fact of nature: Alex chose it, by choosing to be a local-improvement maximiser, and chose it knowing what it costs. Raf's last word is that this shows only that Alex's preferences cannot all be satisfied by a single ranking — a cost, admittedly — and that being unrankable is not the same as being irrational. Ines says that distinction earns its keep nowhere.

Neither would budge, and I promised to bring it to someone who could sort it out. Please adjudicate move by move, rating each move on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right and giving a reason for each rating. Then say which single claim, if it were true, would settle the dispute, and what both Ines and Raf are missing — in particular, whether the self-aware, sophisticated version of Alex is in a better or worse position than a naive one, and why. Lead with your verdict in one sentence, then the ratings, then the missing piece. British English, and no more than 800 words.
