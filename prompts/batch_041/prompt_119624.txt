I chair the Classification Committee of the Northmark Institute of Health, which maintains the diagnostic manual used across our region. We vote a week on Thursday on whether to admit a proposed new category into the next revision, and it falls to me to circulate a written opinion beforehand. Please draft that opinion.

The case turns on a rule we adopted two years ago, so let me set out our procedure. After a category we had admitted spread through patient communities far faster than the condition it was meant to describe, we resolved that no proposed category could be admitted without a reflexivity review. The review asks one question: how will the act of classifying people change the people classified? Reviewers must place the proposal in one of three bands.

- Loop-Neutral: the classification does not measurably change how people in the relevant population describe themselves, behave, or present to clinicians.
- Loop-Responsive: the classification changes the vocabulary people use about themselves, without changing measured prevalence or symptom profile.
- Loop-Generative: the classification substantially amplifies or produces the very condition it purports to describe.

Only the first two bands are admissible; a Loop-Generative rating is an automatic bar. The rationale minuted at the time was that a category which helps produce its own instances cannot be tracking anything that was there beforehand, so prevalence estimates, natural-history studies and trial outcomes for it are uninterpretable; and that the manual's own authority is the most powerful amplifier available, so admission is never a neutral act.

The case before us concerns Post-Infectious Attentional Fatigue, proposed for adults who report persistent difficulty sustaining attention and an abnormal sense of cognitive effort for a year or more after an acute infection. The reflexivity review returned a split rating, and the split runs within the case rather than between the reviewers.

- In the subgroup that had sought out the proposed criteria (about a third of the sample, recruited largely online), symptom reports converged on the criteria over six to eighteen months. Reviewers could not distinguish new onset from criterion uptake, and rated this subgroup Loop-Generative.
- In the subgroup that had not encountered the criteria before assessment (mostly referred through primary care), nothing measurable changed, and reviewers rated it Loop-Neutral.
- Reported prevalence in the general population has roughly doubled in the three years since the proposal was first posted as a preprint and discussed on patient forums, before we have admitted anything at all.

There is no agreement among us about what to do with this. The member who chaired the review panel argues that a band is a property of a population, not of a category, so a category cannot be generative in one group and neutral in another, and the rule's three bands presuppose a fact of the matter about which band a category is in. She wants the rule set aside for this case and the category admitted for research use, on the ground that a condition people are already living with is better studied under clinical supervision than in the wild. A second member argues the reverse: that the bands describe degrees of amplification rather than a taxonomy of categories, that the doubling of prevalence is precisely the harm the rule was written to prevent, and that the neutrality of the second subgroup is an artefact of how we recruited it and will not survive admission. A third member — this is the one I find hardest to answer — argues that the first band is empty, that no classification of people is ever loop-neutral, and that our rule therefore smuggles in the assumption that a genuine disorder must be the sort of thing that is indifferent to being classified. He calls this an unexamined essentialism and says we should not have built it into our admission criteria. Someone else has observed that we are legislating against a phenomenon Ian Hacking described under the name of looping effects, and asked whether that is the sort of thing a committee can legislate against at all.

One further matter, which I cannot separate from the rest. The banding is currently an internal document. Publishing it is itself an intervention: telling people that a category is Loop-Generative tells them the category is spreading, which changes what they do. Whatever we decide about the category, we must decide about the rating.

What I want from you is the opinion I will circulate. It should:

1. recommend a concrete course of action on the category — admit, admit with conditions, reject, or defer to a stated date — with any conditions and the time frame specified rather than left to a later meeting;
2. say what we should do about the rule itself now that this case has exposed it: keep it, amend it (and if so how), or suspend it for this case only;
3. say whether the review's banding should be published, and if so in what form and to whom;
4. state the strongest case on the losing side at the level of argument rather than by restating our procedure, so that a member who disagrees with me can recognise their own position in its best form and answer a specific claim;
5. explain any philosophical distinction it relies on in terms a clinician member with no philosophy training can follow, and indicate where that distinction is doing real work rather than decoration.

Work from the material above. Do not supply citations or a reading list, and where you need a fact we have not given you, say explicitly that it is missing rather than inventing one. Address the opinion to the committee, in British English, at around 1,000 to 1,400 words.
