I have no philosophical training, but I work as a software engineer, and the account of time travel I keep meeting — in science fiction, in popular physics writing and in arguments among colleagues — is essentially the git branching model: the timeline is a repository, a journey into the past forks a branch, the grandfather paradox evaporates because the killing happens on the new branch while the traveller's own history stays untouched, and so backwards time travel turns out to be unproblematic. I would like the analogy audited rather than the physics surveyed. Please set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the commit history, the branch point, HEAD, the merge and the remote — then say where the mapping breaks, paying particular attention to which features of version control are silently doing the work of dissolving the paradox, and to the rival view that the past is simply fixed and self-consistent rather than forked. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion that backwards time travel is unproblematic survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy has only renamed the difficulty. Separate the disanalogies that matter for that conclusion from the merely decorative ones, and if a better analogy exists, say what it is. Please answer in British English.
