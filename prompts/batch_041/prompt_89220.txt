I am a graduate student in philosophy of mind, and I have spent the past several months reading the literature on which animals are conscious. I would like to describe a pattern I keep running into and ask whether it has an established name and a literature of its own, because I can no longer tell whether I have identified a genuine methodological problem or am merely restating the ordinary difficulty of the subject.

The pattern is this. Someone argues that a species is likely to be conscious, and the evidence offered is behavioral: an animal with a damaged limb stops using it, and starts using it sooner when given an analgesic; an animal trades a reward against avoiding a noxious stimulus in a way that looks like more than a reflex; an animal behaves flexibly rather than in a fixed sequence. These behaviors are then presented as the marks of feeling. Someone else argues the contrary, and the objection is always that the same record could have been produced without any feeling at all — that nociception, or a learned avoidance, or a bare disposition to withdraw would have left the same traces. Pressed on what would count instead, the objector says: not more behavior, something else. But when I ask what that something else would be in the case of a human being, the answer comes back to behavior again — what the person says, how they wince, what they avoid. The only thing left over is that I know about one case from the inside, and that case is mine.

So the marks by which we sort species appear to be read off the cases we are already confident about, while the cases we are already confident about appear to be sorted by the marks. No one's list of criteria has ever surprised them. I notice this most sharply in the treatment of verbal report. What a human says is treated as the gold standard, although saying something is itself a piece of behavior, and although there are human cases in which report and capacity come apart — I am thinking of the phenomenon in which a person with certain kinds of damage insists that they cannot see, yet can guess correctly about what is in front of them — and in those cases we side with the report.

What I want to know is whether this exact situation has a name. I do not mean circular reasoning in the loose sense in which any bad argument can be called circular; I mean the specific structure in which there is a body of particular judgments about individual cases, a general rule or set of marks for classifying cases, and neither can be established without assuming the other, so that any defense of one must presuppose the other. If it has a name, I would like to know what the standard responses to it are, and which of those responses philosophers working on animal mind actually rely on, whether or not they say so.

If there is no single name, I would like the nearest concepts laid out and distinguished from one another: what each captures, where each fails to capture what I have described, and which fits best. Two things I have already been told, and which I suspect are not quite the same problem, are that I am simply flouting an old rule about not attributing a higher faculty where a lower one will do, and that I am asking for necessary and sufficient conditions for a concept that admits of none. If either of these does capture what I am describing, I would like to be told why I am wrong to think otherwise.

Finally, I want to know whether the loop is vicious in this case. In particular: does it matter that one of the particular judgments is not a sample judgment at all, but the one case I have from the inside, so that the two halves of the loop are not symmetric? Does that asymmetry improve the situation or worsen it, and does it change which of the available responses is actually open to us?
