I've been going back and forth with my brother about religion in politics, and I keep landing on verdicts that won't line up with each other. What I want from you is the principle I'm actually using — or the closest small family of principles — plus an honest account of which of my verdicts it forces me to drop. No citations, no reading list, just the principle and what it costs.

Here's what I think, case by case.

An abolitionist legislator in the 1850s stands up and says slavery is an offense against God and that no human law can make it right. I think thats the best thing anybody said in that chamber, and I don't think it would have been better if he'd left God out of it.

A different legislator in the same chamber says the state should outlaw divorce, because marriage is a sacrament. Out of bounds, I think, even though he's just as sincere.

A Baptist minister organizes for a higher minimum wage and says the reason is that every worker is made in God's image. Fine. The theology is doing real work there and I still think it's fine.

A city council member votes against a zoning permit for a mosque because her pastor says Islam is a false religion. Not fine.

A Sikh man wants to carry his kirpan into the courthouse. Fine, give him the exemption.

A baker won't make a cake for a gay wedding because of his faith. Not fine.

A bishop campaigns against the death penalty on the church's teaching about the sanctity of life. Fine. The same bishop, same kind of argument, campaigns against gay marriage. Not fine. I know that pair is supposed to embarass me.

And one I genuinely can't sort out: a small Muslim community argues the city should let the call to prayer go out over a loudspeaker, on religious grounds. A big church argues the city should put a nativity scene on the courthouse lawn, also on religious grounds. I feel fine about the first and slightly annoyed by the second, and I don't think "the first group is a minority" is a principle, it's just me rooting for the underdog.

The principle I keep reaching for is this: what matters isn't that the reason is religious, it's whether it's load-bearing. Strip the theology out and see what's still standing. If there's something left — cruelty is cruelty, a person is a person — then fine, argue away. If all that's left when you take the collar off is "my church says so," then it can't be the reason a law gets passed. But I can already feel that this doesn't sort the bishop cases cleanly, and maybe not the mosque/nativity pair either.

So: what's the principle, and which verdicts does it make me give up? Also, what does it say about a case I haven't put to myself yet — a legislator whose position is also supported by perfectly good secular reasons, but who'd hold the position anyway even if every one of those secular reasons were refuted, because her faith is what's really doing the work. Is that fine, or is that the thing I've been objecting to this whole time without saying so? And if two candidate principles both survive the cases I've listed, give me one more case that would pull them apart.
