Two people in a group chat I'm in just had a long fight about cooperating with aliens you can never talk to, and I honestly can't tell who came out ahead. The setup they both accept: the universe might be huge and full of civilizations, but they're so far apart there's no causal contact — nothing you do can reach them, nothing they do can reach you. Then it went like this.

A: 'If I'm the kind of agent that cooperates with strangers it can't reach, that's evidence that other agents in my situation also cooperate — same situation, same kind of decision procedure — so being that kind of agent makes it more likely others are too, and they might end up in a position to affect me after all, or else the sheer number of them makes it worth it. You cooperate evidentially: not because it reaches anyone, but because your choice is evidence about what agents running your algorithm do.'

B: 'That's wishing a correlation into existence. Their choices aren't correlated with yours unless something actually links you — a common cause or a shared procedure. In a really large world the civilizations are physically seperate and probably wildly different, so your choice is a drop of evidence about what a random distant civ does. And even if it were evidence, their cooperating only matters to you if it affects you, which by construction it can't. So you're just cooperating to feel good about your own self-image.'

A: 'Two replies. First, the acausal channel: if they reason like me and predict I'm a cooperator, they may cooperate back in a way that does affect me — you can't rule that out just by saying no causal contact, because the link is at the level of decision procedures, not ordinary physics. Second, even a tiny correlation is worth acting on if the world is big enough — the number of agents is astronomical, so a drop of evidence times astronomical stakes can dominate.'

B: 'The acausal channel is circular — you're assuming the correlation to get the payoff that's supposed to justify assuming the correlation. And "astronomical stakes" is doing all the work: you need the expected effect on you to be positive, and with no causal link it's exactly zero, so no number of agents helps.'

A: 'It's not circular, it's just evidential decision theory. You maximize expected value under the probabilities that condition on your action, and if your action is evidence about the world, it counts. Refusing to count it is the classic causal-decision-theory mistake.'

B: 'EDT only makes sense when the correlation is real. Here you stipulated no causal contact, so no common cause, so no correlation, so EDT and CDT agree and both say dont bother.'

Can you adjudicate this move by move? For each move, say whether it's clearly mistaken, defensible, or clearly right, and why — then tell me what both of them are missing that would actually settle it.
