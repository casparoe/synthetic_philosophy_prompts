I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or in mathematical logic, and I am looking for a careful explanation of a conceptual issue rather than a restatement of a well-known result.

I understand the mechanics of Russell's paradox well enough. Take the collection of all collections that are not members of themselves; assuming it is a member of itself and assuming it is not each lead to the other, so the naive principle that any condition determines a collection cannot stand as stated. What I cannot get clear on is what the paradox is actually telling us, and I would like that explained from the ground up.

Three things leave me unsure. First, the same diagonal shape appears in arguments that are not paradoxes at all: the proof that no collection can be put into one-to-one correspondence with the collection of all its subcollections, and the proof that no program can correctly predict the halting behaviour of every program, itself included. In those cases the conclusion is read as a discovery about a genuine limit rather than as evidence that the subject matter is incoherent, and I cannot tell how to decide which reading the set-theoretic case deserves. Second, there is a variant of the paradox that appears to use no negation at all: a sentence that says of itself that if it is true then some arbitrarily chosen claim is true, from which that claim, and indeed any claim, seems to follow. If that variant is sound, then the popular diagnosis, self-reference combined with negation, cannot be the whole story, and I would like to know what it leaves unexplained. Third, the standard repairs all look to me like different ways of forbidding the offending construction rather than of explaining what was wrong with it: restricting the principle so that a collection can only be carved out of a collection already given; arranging everything in a hierarchy so that nothing can be applied to itself or take itself as input; or letting the problematic collections exist while denying them membership in anything, including themselves. A related candidate I have seen mentioned is that the fault lies in specifying a collection by a condition that quantifies over the very collection being specified.

Please explain the conceptual issue here. For each of these diagnoses, say what the alleged error actually is and what the corresponding repair is committed to, including what it gives up. Then tell me how one distinguishes a principled restriction from a merely consistent stipulation: what would a principled fix predict that a rule chosen only to restore consistency would not? Finally, if the matter is not settled, I would like to know which part of it is genuinely open and which part is not. I am not after references or a reading list; I want the reasoning itself, pitched at someone who is comfortable with sets, subsets, bijections, and programs, but who has no logic beyond that.
