I have been reading rationalist and effective-altruism blogs for the better part of a decade, and the topic of wild animal welfare has been quietly bothering me for most of that time. I have never seen the argument below fully defused, and I would like either a refutation or an honest bill of costs. Here it is in the form I find hardest to escape.

(1) A typical wild animal's life is net negative: it dies young, usually by starvation, exposure, disease, or predation, and even the survivors spend much of their time in states of hunger, fear, and pain that we would unhesitatingly call suffering in any other animal.
(2) If a life is net negative, the world is better if that life is never lived.
(3) Habitat destruction — clearing, draining, development, the general conversion of wild land — reduces the number of wild animals that will ever live, and does so on an enormous scale; the aggregate is dominated by invertebrates, which makes the stakes larger still.
(4) Therefore habitat destruction prevents enormous numbers of net-negative lives.
(5) Preventing a net-negative life is a benefit.
(6) Therefore habitat destruction is good; other things equal, more of it is better than less.
(7) And by the same reasoning, the most effective animal charity would be one that eliminates wild animals as completely and as quickly as possible — not one that feeds, vaccinates, or rescues them.
(8) But (7) is a reductio. I am not willing to call the burning of the Amazon a mercy, or to make extermination the goal of animal advocacy. Something in (1)–(6) has to give.

I am not interested in the reply that (1) is false. I find the net-negative picture well supported, and if the argument genuinely turns on (1) I want to be told that plainly, but I suspect the trouble is downstream. I have also heard two stock replies and do not know how to assess them: that the animals killed are simply replaced by others, so nothing is gained; and that you cannot benefit a merely possible being, so (2) is confused. And I have read that shrinking habitat can crowd the survivors together and raise the suffering per animal, which I mention in case it matters.

So: name the step I should reject, explain why it looked innocent, and price the alternatives. In particular, tell me what I have to say instead about the case I most want to keep — that it is good to prevent an animal from being born into a life of factory-farm suffering — and, if the fix is a restriction on a premise, state the restriction precisely and tell me which other arguments it also blocks.

End your answer with a single final line of the form "Verdict: Step N — <a short label for the error>", and put nothing after it.
