I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy and I'm TAing the discussion section of a second-year course on rationality and choice — 18 students, 75 minutes, and next week is the week where the readings have the desert hitchhiker in them. You know the one: you're stranded, a driver says he'll only take you if you promise to pay him once you get to town, and everyone including him knows that the moment you're in town you'd have no reason to hand anything over. Every year I explain it, every year they nod and write something down, and I can tell nothing has landed. I want them to be in it, on both sides of the windscreen. What I want them to walk out feeling is that the driver's refusal isn't cynicism, it's just correct prediction, and that the hitchhiker's real problem is that he can't fix his character at the moment he needs it.

So design me the activity. Before you write anything, brainstorm three or four candidate mechanics, say what's wrong with the ones you drop, then build the best one out in full. You're a game designer and a philosopher of action at the same time, so the mechanic itself has to do the teaching — no warm-up lecture, and I don't want the vocabulary of commitment devices or credibility to show up until the debrief.

The shape I've been circling, which you should feel free to wreck: before any roles are handed out, while nobody knows whether they'll be a driver or a rider in any given round, players can take an irrevocable oath — for the rest of the session every promise they make gets kept, and I hold them to it. That's meant to hurt, so I'll seed a couple of ordinary rounds where reneging is plainly the profitable move (someone promises to split a pot, someone promises to do the boring task) so the oath bleeds points in situations that have nothing to do with deserts or rides. Then the desert rounds, where a lift is worth a lot and the driver can verify nothing about you except what you chose at the start. And I want one round flagged in advance as the last one, because that's the moment I actually care about: if the oath survives the last round, when there's no future left to protect, then its a character; if it folds, it was arithmetic all along. Use tokens, no real money, payoff table on one side of A4, and give me a minute-by-minute run sheet.

Here's the thing I keep tripping on and need you to solve. If I enforce the oath with a penalty, it's a bond with extra steps, and the drivers are trusting my clipboard rather than the person — I've just moved the problem into the rules. If I don't enforce it, it's cheap talk, and no driver with an ounce of sense should believe it, which means the activity can only ever stage the pessimist's half of the story. So either find me a mechanism where an unenforced choice can carry real weight for a self-interested driver (I'm not sure one exists), or tell me straight that it can't be done in a classroom game and show me how to run both versions and use the gap between them in the debrief. If it's any help, there's a cousin of this problem where you're offered a fortune for forming an intention you know perfectly well you won't act on — that structure might be worth stealing for one of the rounds.

Then the debrief: map the moments that actually happened onto the pieces of the idea — the promise that can't be believed, the refusal that's rational rather than mean, why a policy can be rational even when every act it prescribes isn't, and the difference between a bond that changes your incentives and a character that changes what you are. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm already braced for everyone taking the oath, or nobody taking it, or one burned rider killing the market in round two — and what to do in each case. And finish with an honest paragraph on what the activity can't reproduce, so I dont oversell it to them. Think it through carefully before you commit; the payoffs have to be tight enough that the rational move really is the one I'm claiming, or the whole thing falls apart in the room.
