I'm a journalist — science and ideas, mostly, which means I spend alot of my time turning other people's arguments into 1500 words and then apologising to them. Anyway, in October I've somehow agreed to run a 75-minute session at a weekend ideas festival, and I'd rather not stand at the front and talk at people for an hour. Fourteen or so in the room, adults, mixed bag: a couple of lawyers (which is either great or fatal, I can't tell yet), some students, several retirees who come to everything.

Here's the itch I want them to scratch. I've been reading around legal systems that contain the instructions for changing themselves, and the wierd hole you drop into when you ask who gets to change the rule about changing the rules. I want the room to fall into that hole for real, before I've said a word about it. The idea should live in what happens, not in my closing remarks.

So design me a game. The seed is a little rulebook, printed on one card, hand it to everyone. Roughly: any player can propose a new rule at any time; a proposal passes if more than half the room votes yes; the same vote can strike or rewrite any rule already on the books. Then the fun one — one of the rules is untouchable, it says the amendment procedure doesn't apply to it. I'll leave the exact wording to you, but keep it to six short rules max, and make sure at least one rule talks about rules (self-referential) and at least one makes the group stop and ask whether they're actually allowed to do that.

What I need from you:

- The exact seed card text, nothing longer than a card.
- A stake that isn't money — a trophy, the right to pick the topic of my next session, a chocolate bar, whatever — but enough that they care. Plus a win condition, or a reason there shouldn't be one.
- Roles or hidden agendas to hand out if you think the game needs them. I suspect it does, because otherwise everyone plays nice and nothing breaks.
- A timeline for the 75 minutes, including how long to let them flounder before I intervene.
- A planted provocation I can fire around the 25-minute mark if they haven't walked into the paradox themselves. Something like a motion to delete the untouchable rule, or to raise the voting threshold so a bare majority can never be undone by a later majority.

The debrief is the part I actually care about. Map what just happened onto the real problem: the rule that tells you how to change rules can't get its own authority from some further rule without a regress, so it has to rest on something that isn't a rule at all — a practice, a habit of obedience, a decision by whoever's holding the power. And then the specific snarl, where a majority tries to bind future majorities, and whether a clause saying it can never be changed can be changed away by the very procedure it forbids. If the standard moves from philosophy of law help here — the idea that a legal order rests on a social rule that officials simply use, or on a basic norm everyone presupposes, or on the fact that whoever decides is the one who's right — name them plainly and tell me what each would predict about our game.

And be straight with me about failure. My bet is someone says it's just a game and checks out, and someone else finds a loophole in ninety seconds and wins, and then there's nothing left for anyone to feel. What do I do about each of those? Then tell me honestly what the game can't reproduce — my hunch is that in a real legal order the paradox gets closed by power and by people believing the thing is legitimate, and a room of strangers with a chocolate bar has neither.

Don't pad it, and skip the reading list — assume I can't look anything up while I'm standing there.
