I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, supposed to be writing a chapter on personal identity and reproductive technology, but I keep getting sucked into the old origin essentialism fight. I've been going back and forth on this alot. You know the one: could you have come from a different sperm and egg? The essentialists say no, your actual origin is part of your essence, so any possible world with a different zygote contains a different person. The critics say that's a confusion -- maybe it's true of the human organism but not of the person, or maybe the intuition is just an artifact of how we imagine possible worlds, or maybe it's a fact about our naming practices rather than a metaphysical necessity. The weird thing is that after decades, neither side seems to move. Every argument is on the table and everyone just digs in. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right. Is this a substantive metaphysical disagreement, a verbal one about what the first-person pronoun picks out (organism vs person vs subject of experience), or a methodological disagreement about the evidential weight of intuitions and the right semantics for modal claims? Is there a hidden empirical question -- about twinning, mitochondrial replacement, or how early development individuates an organism -- that's actually doing the work? Or are the two sides answering different questions under one heading and never noticing?

Here's a case that made me think the dissagreement is more tangled than the standard sperm-and-egg framing. Suppose an IVF embryo splits into two viable embryos. One is implanted; the other is frozen for a decade. Later, the frozen embryo's nucleus is transferred into an enucleated donor egg from a different woman, and that embryo is brought to term. Is the resulting child the same person as the child who would have developed from the frozen embryo without the transfer? Is she the same person as her twin? If we say no in the first case but yes in the second, what exactly is essential -- the nuclear genome, the whole zygote, the cytoplasmic environment, the causal chain, or something about the subject's first-person perspective? I suspect the debate is stalled because the question about a different sperm and egg equivocates between the organism's material origin, the person's genetic ancestry, and the subject's causal history, and each side is answering a different one. But maybe that's too neat. Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here -- a distinction, a formal result in modal logic, an empirical finding about development, or a decision that the question is ill-posed? And if no progress should be expected, say why. I'd rather have a clear diagnosis with reasons than another survey of the positions.
