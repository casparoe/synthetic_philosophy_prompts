I run a one-session workshop on decision-making under uncertainty for early-career scientists: eighteen participants, one room, ninety minutes, no computers, and no real money at stake. I want them to run into the unilateralist's curse rather than hear about it, so design the whole session as an activity with rules rather than a lecture. Here is the structure I want them to feel. It does not need to be explained to them before the debrief, and I would prefer that it not be.

The structure. A single action that any one person can take alone, once, with consequences that fall on everyone in the room. Each person holds partial, private evidence about whether taking it is the right thing to do — enough, by itself, to make acting look reasonable, but not conclusive. Nobody may communicate before deciding, and nobody can see whether anyone else will act. Each person's rule for acting is naturally calibrated as if they were the only one who might act; it does not shift when they learn that seventeen others are weighing the same action. The group outcome does shift. With many people eligible, the action gets taken whenever any one of them has evidence that clears the bar, so it gets taken even in situations where it should not be, and with enough people it becomes close to certain that someone takes it, warranted or not. What is effectively deciding for the group is the most willing-to-act person, not the average one. Make the consequences asymmetric — a wrongly taken action costs the group more than a rightly taken one gains it, or the action cannot be undone — and the group can end up worse off the more people it has who are able to act. The individually sensible move and the collectively sensible move then come apart, and the collective one (raising your own bar because you know others are also weighing it, or standing down and letting them decide) is not something a person can arrive at by reasoning about their own evidence alone.

Requirements. At least two rounds, arranged so that the number of people eligible to act differs between them, so the results can be compared across rounds; the group size stays at eighteen, so vary eligibility with teams, turns, or per-round rules. Printed materials only — cards, slips, tally sheets — and a payoff scheme in points or tokens with a shared scoreboard, where losses wipe out accumulated credit and nobody wants to lose. A timeline with per-round timings. Rules precise enough that I can run the session from your write-up without improvising, including exactly who knows what and when. A debrief that reconstructs what actually happened using the session's own numbers, maps specific moments onto the parts of the structure above (the insensitivity of each private decision to the number of others, the compounding of error probabilities once any single person's mistake is sufficient, the asymmetry that makes the compounding harmful), and closes with the live question of whether the deference remedy is a principle or a license for free-riding. No reading list or citations — I need only the activity.

Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure: participants colluding out loud and destroying the independence the design depends on, everyone standing down so that nothing ever happens, a group too small or too uniform for the effect to appear, a round whose outcome is indistinguishable from noise, and one participant who spots the point in the first round and spoils it for the others. And be frank about the feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce, which I will have to supply myself in the debrief.
