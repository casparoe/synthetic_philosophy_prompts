You're a philosopher of time who's good at building diagnostic tools, and I need one for a grad reading group. Set aside the usual 'which theory of persistence do you accept?' questionnaire — everyone just picks the label they like and then answers the cases to match. I want a decision tree instead. Start from a commitment nobody in the group can deny (we all agree that at least some ordinary things — a person, a river, a chess club — are the same thing at two different times, whatever that amounts to), and let each branch node be a question that seperates the positions, phrased as a concrete case with two or three answers, so a reader can answer it without already knowing where it leads.

Use the classic cases (a poker that's hot and then cold; a ship rebuilt plank by plank; a person who divides into two), but the last batch is the real point of the exercise, because I want the tree to earn its keep on cases nobody has a ready-made answer for: a chess club that dissolves and is re-founded under the same name with no overlapping members; a river that dries up completely and then refills; a person in cryonic suspension; a piece of software saved to disk and reloaded — is the running instance the same thing?; and a song re-recorded note-for-note by a different band. Push on whether the answers to the non-standard cases line up with the answers to the classic ones. If they don't, that's a branch, not a footnote.

Leaves I want: endurantism (wholly present at each time), the temporal-parts/worm view, the stage view (what exists are momentary stages and persistence is a counterpart relation — I think it's called exdurantism), the 'properties are had only relative to a time' fix for temporary intrinsics (adverbialism, maybe?), the view that persistence is primitive and unanalyzable, conventionalism (sameness-over-time is something we stipulate rather than discover), and eliminativism about ordinary composite objects. Cross-cut with presentism/eternalism only where it actually changes a leaf, and say where it doesn't.

Two things I care about: (1) flag any pair of leaves your tree can't seperate rather than pretending a branch does the job, and (2) note which combinations of answers are unoccupied, and say whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Keep it to 6-8 leaves. Think it through before you write — brainstorm the space of answer-combinations first, then place the named positions in it, then write the tree. And be committed: where a case is ambiguous, pick where you think it most naturally falls and say why, don't answer 'it depends which theory you hold'.
