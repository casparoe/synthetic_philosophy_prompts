I'm a science-and-ideas reporter at a mid-sized daily, and for the last three years I've sat on the selection committee for the Wexler Prize, which our foundation gives annually for what the bylaws call 'an act of moral courage in science journalism.' We've got a nominee who has split the committee 4–3, and I have to circulate a written opinion before Friday's vote. The nominee, a reporter I'll call Dana, broke a story that a prominent nutrition lab had been quietly dropping outliers from its datasets before publishing. The story was solid: the lab retracted two papers, a funder changed its rules, and a bunch of other outlets followed up. But the backstory is messy. Dana started digging after the lab's director beat her out for a job she badly wanted, and she told a colleague she wanted to 'take him down.' She sat on a key spreadsheet for three months to time the story to her book proposal, and one source says she promised more anonymity than she actually delivered. One of her central sources was a postdoc the lab director had fired, and Dana knew that going in.

Half the committee says the prize is for the act, not the person: the retraction happened, the field is cleaner, and if we start policing motives we'll never award anyone, since motives are usually mixed. The other half says that's exactly the point of calling it moral courage — a useful act done for spite or career gain isn't moral courage, it's just aggression that happened to land well, and the award should mean something. Our bylaws don't define moral courage or say whether motive counts. I'm supposed to write the opinion that recommends a concrete course of action — award, no award, or award with a public statement — and gives reasons the losing side can actually respond to, not just a restatement of the bylaws. A friend who teaches ethics keeps telling me there's a difference between doing the right thing and doing it for the right reasons, and that the distinction is a mess, which is not helping.

Could you draft that memo? Please take a position on whether moral worth depends on acting for the right reasons, or whether the committee should care about that at all, and apply it to Dana's case. I'd like the bottom line up front and a time frame for the vote. Also, be honest about the strongest point on the losing side — don't strawman either the act-not-person view or the motive-matters view. And tell me what would change your mind: what fact about Dana, or about the prize's purpose, would flip your recommendation, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail? I'm not looking for a literature review or citations, just an opinion I can circulate. I should say I'm not on either faction's side yet; I keep going back and forth alot.
