My roommate dragged me to an AI safety reading group last week, and two of the regulars got in to it over the 'low impact' idea — the proposal that we can build agents that avoid side effects by penalizing how much they disturb the world, without first telling them what the world is supposed to look like. Roughly how it went:

Sam: The whole point of a measure like relative reachability is that it doesn't need a value function. You take the agent's default state, and for each action you ask: how many of the goals I might of pursued later are now unreachable that used to be reachable? Penalize that. Nothing in there says which goals are good. It just measures how much you've closed off, and closing off options is bad no matter what you want. So yeah, you can define 'avoid side effects' without a full spec of value.

Riley: That's the move I don't buy. To even count 'options' you need a state space, a set of actions, and a notion of reachability — all of which you chose, and choosing them is choosing what matters. A state space that tracks money and lives but not, say, the range of a species is already a value judgment. And 'keeping options open' is a value, not a neutral fact: an agent that keeps a vial of nerve agent reachable is 'preserving reachability' too. So the measure smuggles value in at the setup, then presents the output as neutral.

Sam: But any formal tool has to fix a state space. That doesn't make physics value-laden. The reachability measure is neutral in the sense that matters: hold the dynamics fixed and it treats all goals alike. It doesn't privilege any particular outcome.

Riley: Treating all goals alike is itself a substantive moral stance — it says shutting down a paperclip maximizer's options counts the same as shutting down a hospital's. And the neutrality is fake, because how you aggregate reachability across goals already decides whose options weigh more.

Sam: Now you're just saying it's hard to pick the parameters. That's an engineering problem, not a conceptual one. The concept — penalize foreclosing futures — is value-free.

Riley: And that's the whole disagreement. If the concept is value-free then it can't tell you which side effects to avoid, and the moment it can, it isn't value-free.

They turned to me and I had nothing. Can you referee this move by move — who's right at each step, what each of them is getting right that the other's missing, and whether that last exchange (engineering vs conceptual) is really the crux or a dodge?
