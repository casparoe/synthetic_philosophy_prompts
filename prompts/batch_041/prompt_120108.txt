My father died in March, and my brother has responded to it by becoming a four-dimensionalist. I do not mean that he has joined a sect; I mean that he has spent the past year reading about the philosophy of physics and has come to hold, with evident conviction, that the block universe is the true picture of reality — that past, present and future are equally real, that nothing ever passes away, and that the whole of history, including everything still to happen to me and to my children, is already there. He means it kindly. When I said that I could not bear that my father's life was finished, he told me that it is not finished, that it is there, as real as this afternoon, and that my grief rests on a picture of time that physics has already discarded.

I cannot tell whether this is a profound consolation or a piece of sophistry, and the question has begun to matter to me beyond the question of my father. I am also trying to decide whether to leave a career I have had for fifteen years, and my brother says that decision is already made — not, he insists, that deliberating is pointless, since my deliberating is itself a part of the world, but that the anxiety I feel about choosing between open alternatives is a confusion about what kind of thing a choice is.

The argument he rests all of this on he calls the Rietdijk–Putnam argument, and he states it as follows. First, special relativity tells us that simultaneity is frame-relative: for events separated widely enough in space there is no frame-independent fact about which happened first, and observers in relative motion disagree about which distant events are happening at the same time as this one. Second, then, what is present differs from observer to observer: my present is one slice through the world, my brother's is a differently tilted slice, and no frame has a better claim than any other to be the real present. Third, if every observer's present is as good as every other's, then the events in all of those presents are equally real — for otherwise something would have to decide which slice is the real one, and relativity has abolished the candidates for that job. Fourth, there is no principled place to stop the multiplication of slices, so the whole four-dimensional manifold is what exists, and the word now does no more work than the word here.

He takes the fourth step to be a result of physics, and he takes it to settle both that my father is not gone and that my career decision is already a fact. I suspect that the fourth step is not the same claim as the future's being fixed, and that neither of those is the same claim as my doing one thing rather than another making no difference — but I cannot defend the suspicion, and my brother says it is the last gasp of a pre-relativistic intuition.

Our friend Nadia, who is finishing a dissertation in the philosophy of physics, says the argument is a swindle, though she has never made the swindle clear to me. She holds that time genuinely passes and that the present is not merely a point of view, and she is not naive about relativity. My brother says she is defending a position that physics has refuted, and that she knows it.

What I would like from you is the dialogue between them that I am not competent to stage. Both should be as strong as their position allows: my brother should not be a man who thinks an equation by itself settles what exists, and Nadia should not be someone who refuses to follow the physics where it leads. Each should answer the other's actual argument, concede what is right in it, and press where it is weak, rather than restating a position at the other. I would like the dialogue to fix precisely which step of the argument Nadia rejects, and to say what that rejection costs her. I would like it to keep apart the four things my brother runs together — the frame-relativity of simultaneity; determinism; the claim that there is no objective present and that becoming is not a feature of the world; and the claim that what I do makes no difference to how things turn out — and to say which of these is supposed to entail which. It should also touch on whether the block picture really supports the consolation my brother offers me, or whether the consolation and the physics come apart. Neither of them should win a point by appealing to authority, to what most physicists are said to believe, or to the prestige of a name. The dialogue should be argumentative rather than consolatory, need not end in agreement, and should end with the two of them having narrowed their disagreement to a question they can both state plainly. Roughly 1,000 to 1,500 words.
