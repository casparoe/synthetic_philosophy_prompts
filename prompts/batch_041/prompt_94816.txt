I'm trying to think through the idea that moral compromise is basically a trade -- two sides with different values each give up something they care about less in order to get something they care about more, so the compromise is a Pareto improvement over deadlock, and that's the whole case for it. I want to test that against a real episode: the compromises over slavery at the Constitutional Convention in 1787. I know the broad strokes -- the three-fifths clause counting enslaved people as three fifths of a person for apportionment purposes, the clause that barred Congress from prohibiting the slave trade until 1808, the fugitive slave clause -- so take those as given and don't waste space hedging about the details.

What I want: run the trade model over the episode properly. Who are the parties, what exactly is being exchanged, what does each side give up and get, and in what sense (if any) the result is a Pareto improvement over the counterfactual where the small states and the Deep South walk out and the whole thing collapses. Then the part I actually care about -- where the model breaks. My suspicion is that gains-from-trade presupposes that what's on the table is each party's own value, but here a big chunk of what gets traded is the legal status of people who aren't parties to the trade at all. If that's right, say what it does to the theory: is it a fatal counterexample, or does the theory just need an extra clause about who counts as a party, and if the latter, is that clause quietly doing all the moral work the theory was supposed to be doing on its own?

What happens to the model when one side's 'value' is something the other side denies is a value at all (property in persons)? And then the reverse direction: tell me what the episode shows back about the theory. Does it confirm gains-from-trade as a decent descriptive model of what compromise is, or does it reveal that the theory was always smuggling in a constraint (only trade over your own goods, only among parties who count) that it can't justify from inside itself.
