For several months I have been drafting a short paper on Hofstadter's account of creativity, and I would like your comments on the draft before I send it to a colleague who is skeptical of the whole approach. I care most about whether the distinction the paper turns on is a real one or merely a restatement of Hofstadter's own view, and whether the argument I use to make it stick is sound. The draft follows.

Hofstadter's central image is that a concept is not a fixed category with a boundary but a family of variations governed by adjustable features. To possess the concept is to know which features are dials, how far each may be turned, and how the turns trade off against one another; an instance counts as belonging to the concept when its particular setting of the dials falls within a region the concept tolerates. On this account, creativity is the disciplined exploration of that space: you produce variations on a theme, and you exercise taste in judging which variations are still recognizably of the theme and which are merely adjacent to it. The analogy-making programs he and his students built were meant to show that this exploration, and this judging, can be mechanized.

I want to argue that the picture is half right, and that the half it leaves out is the interesting half. The picture explains generation; it does not explain selection. Worse, it takes the dials as given. It tells you how to move within a space of variations but not how a genuinely new space comes into being — and the acts we most want a theory of creativity to explain are exactly the ones that redraw the space.

Consider two things you can do with a recipe. You can turn the dials: more salt, less heat, a longer rest. This is real competence, and the model captures it well. Or you can decide that what you have been treating as a dish is really a sauce, at which point salt and heat stop being dials on the same instrument and a new set of dials appears — acidity, viscosity, how it sets when cold. The second move is not a large turn of the first move's dials; it is a change in what counts as a dial. I will call the first kind turning and the second kind re-carving, and my claim is that the model accounts for turning while silently presupposing re-carving.

The obvious reply is that re-carving is just turning at a higher level: there is a space of carvings, and the creative act selects one. But this reply either regenerates the problem or trivializes it. If the higher space has its own dials, we need an account of who carves those, and the regress is off. If instead the higher space is unstructured — any carving reachable from any other — then the model no longer explains why good re-carvers reliably land somewhere rather than nowhere. The variations-on-a-theme picture, I conclude, needs a second faculty, one that operates on the space rather than within it, and I do not think it has one. That picture is a theory of craft, not of creativity, and the two are being run together under one name.

What I would like from you: first, is the turning/re-carving distinction doing any work, or is it Hofstadter's own point restated in different words? Second, is the regress argument in the third paragraph valid, or does it prove too much — would it equally show that no account of anything can explain its own conditions? Third, and most usefully, can you name a case that would embarrass the distinction, either a creative act that looks like re-carving but is really turning or one that looks like turning but is really re-carving? Finally, tell me plainly whether the essay is interesting or merely tidy.
