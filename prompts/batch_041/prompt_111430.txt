I hold a final-year seminar on decision theory, and next week's session is devoted to Kelly betting and to what some of my younger colleagues insist on calling geometric rationality: the view that in repeated multiplicative settings one ought to maximise expected log wealth — equivalently, the long-run growth rate of one's capital — rather than expected wealth as such. I should like to open the session with a joke, and I have a rather fussy specification for it. It must be funny to people who actually know the position; the laugh has to be earned by a consequence of the theory rather than by the mere mention of gambling, and it ought still to make some sort of sense to a colleague who has never placed a bet in her life. The well-worn shapes are barred: no economist and the banknote on the pavement, no gambler's fallacy, no house-always-wins, and nothing that would work equally well as a joke about any theory whatsoever. Nor do I want a riddle or a bumper sticker; a shaggy-dog story, or a two-hander in which the second speaker's line does the work, would suit the room far better.

The following are common ground in the seminar, and any of them may be pressed into service. A growth-maximiser stakes a fixed fraction of his capital, proportional to his edge, and never the whole of it however favourable the terms. If he stakes a good deal more than that — on the usual continuous-time approximation, more than twice the growth-optimal fraction — his growth rate falls below zero and he is ruined almost surely, even though every single bet he makes has positive expected value: the arithmetic average of an ensemble of such bettors climbs handsomely, while the typical bettor is wiped out. He is willing to take either side of a bet whose odds match his credence, so that a market composed of such bettors prices propositions at probabilities. The maximum growth rate available to him is the information rate of what he knows, so that a private tip is worth precisely the information it carries, neither more nor less. And no finite gain will reconcile him to any chance, however small, of total ruin. If it assists, I have been toying with a setting, though you need not use it: a club whose members are obliged to play the same favourable game every evening for the rest of their lives, under a rulebook permitting any stake from nothing up to the whole of one's capital.

Write the joke in whatever form you think best — if a better shape than those I have sketched occurs to you, take it — and add afterwards a single sentence saying which feature of the position it turns on, so that I can judge whether it will land with a room that has done the reading.
