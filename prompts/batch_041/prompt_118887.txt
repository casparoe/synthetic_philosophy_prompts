I would like a parody: the glossy brochure and fine print of a life-insurance firm that writes policies for people who do not yet exist. The firm's product is, in effect, Harsanyi's aggregation theorem — the derivation of a utilitarian social welfare function from the premise that, behind the veil of ignorance, each person's chance of being anyone is equal — and the humour has to come from the theorem's own machinery rather than from invented absurdity. A social-choice theorist should laugh rather than wince, and a reader who has never heard of Harsanyi should still follow what is happening and feel the pressure of the question.

Invent the firm, its products, its regulator, its rival, and its complaints department; use no real company names and no real citations. About 700 words, mostly numbered clauses, riders, and marginal notes, in the deadpan voice of corporate reassurance. The parody must never state its objection outright: the reader should be able to see for herself which premise is carrying the whole structure.

Build the document so that the following are all present, in whatever order the fiction makes natural:

1. The underwriting rule, presented as ordinary actuarial prudence rather than as moral philosophy: because every applicant had an equal chance of being born as any of the firm's future policyholders, premiums and payouts are set to maximise the average of everyone's expected welfare.
2. A comparability clause: claims are settled on a single common scale of well-being, and an assessor's certificate is required. Say what happens when a policyholder insists that her scale cannot be commensurated with anyone else's.
3. An ex ante/ex post clause: the policy is written before the identity draw, and the firm declines claims of the form "now that I know who I am, I would not have bought this." The firm's language should be impeccable, and the reader should see what the clause costs.
4. A positional-claims exclusion, covering losses that consist only in ranking below other policyholders.
5. A form letter from the claims department to a policyholder who drew an unusually bad identity — courteous, complete, and quietly terrible.
6. A notice from a rival underwriter who refuses to gamble on identity and instead sells a maximin policy to the worst off, alleging that the first firm has smuggled in risk-neutrality and that nobody behind the veil would accept a lottery over who she turns out to be.
7. One short advertisement, in the same register, from a third firm that prices by headcount and boasts that the more policyholders are enrolled, the less each one pays.

Finish with a single final line beginning "Bottom line:" followed by one sentence, in plain prose, naming the premise on which the brochure quietly depends.
