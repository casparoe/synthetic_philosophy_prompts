For the last session of a reading group on the centipede game, write a poem of thirteen short lines in two alternating voices, one line per node, spoken as the game runs outward and the pot grows. The poem must be readable in both directions. Top to bottom it is the cooperative story: two players who keep passing, each line a reason for trusting the other one node further. Bottom to top it is the backward induction: each line, read in reverse, is its speaker's reason to take at the previous node rather than pass into the next, so that the poem's first line — the last step of the induction — is the first player taking the smallest pot. Let the middle line be the hinge, doing honest work in both directions, and let no line merely name the equilibrium: in the backward reading each must cite what the other player does one node further out, so that someone who knows the induction can check every step. The poem should carry the genuine difficulty rather than the textbook version: the induction asks each player to count on the other's rationality at nodes she reaches only if the other has just done something irrational, so the confidence the argument needs is undermined by the very fact that it is needed. Let the backward reading close on a certainty that the forward reading has already made unavailable. A game theorist should find nothing to wince at, and someone who has never seen the game should still feel the trap close.
