Until a few months ago I would have described myself as a consumer of this debate rather than a participant in it: I build probabilistic reasoning systems, and when the question arose of how to represent conditional judgments in one of them, I treated it as settled engineering and moved on. A colleague who works on natural-language inference pushed back hard, and I have since changed my own view twice, which suggests that I have been tracking the rhetoric around the issue rather than the argument. I would therefore like a number from you, and the reasoning behind it, rather than a survey.

Here is the claim I want a credence in, stated as tightly as I can manage it:

There exists a semantics for indicative conditionals that (a) assigns each conditional a content whose probability is well defined for every rational credence function; (b) entails that the probability of a conditional equals the conditional probability of its consequent given its antecedent, whenever that conditional probability is defined; and (c) is non-trivial in the following sense: conditionals embed in the ways ordinary language embeds them — they can be negated, conjoined, nested inside one another, and used as antecedents and consequents — without the semantics collapsing into the claim that a conditional says no more than the corresponding material implication, or into some other claim on which the conditional is evaluable as a truth function of its two component propositions.

I want your credence in that claim, decomposed rather than asserted. Name the sub-questions the number rests on; I would expect the list to include at least the following. How much of the pressure toward triviality comes from assumptions about embedding and composition rather than from the equation itself, and are those assumptions independently motivated or merely convenient? Can a defender retain the equation for a restricted class of credence functions while conceding that it fails in general, and would that count as satisfying (b) or as abandoning it? Is the equation even the right target, or is the operational content of a conditional judgment something like a disposition to revise one's credences in a particular way, which no probability assignment to a content captures? Is the probability of a conditional a single well-defined quantity, or several quantities that the dispute quietly conflates? And how much of the residual disagreement is verbal, turning on what a semantics is required to deliver rather than on any fact about conditionals? Assign a rough probability to each of these, give one line of justification for each, show how they combine into your final number, and then tell me which term the number is most sensitive to and what argument or example would move it most.

Two constraints on the answer. I do not need references — I need the reasoning — so please do not spend the response on names, titles, or who said what when. And if the claim as stated turns out not to be well formed, for instance if (a) and (b) cannot be assessed independently of each other, then repair it as little as possible, tell me exactly what you changed, and give me the number for the repaired version instead.
