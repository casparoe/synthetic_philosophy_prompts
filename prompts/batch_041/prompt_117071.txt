I teach a 90-minute session on the philosophy of technology to 16 undergraduates, and I want a runnable workshop that makes them feel, rather than hear, the claim that automation's effect on workers depends on who owns and controls the technology and not on the technology itself. Materials should be trivial (paper, tape, one cheap hand tool) and no real money is at stake. Structure it as rounds of a small production workshop: an opening round with hand methods, then a technical improvement introduced twice under different rules — in one run owned and its gains captured by a single participant, in the other held in common — so that the identical improvement is lived as immiseration in one round and as free time in the other. Nobody should be told the thesis in advance; they should blame the machine first. Arrange the payoffs so that a few participants come out ahead under the first arrangement, so the group cannot simply unite against a villain. Then give me a debrief that maps what happened onto the difference between machinery as a means of production and machinery as capital, onto the dispute over whether workers displaced by machines are re-absorbed elsewhere, and onto whether the technology is neutral in itself with purely distributive consequences. Include the rules, materials, payoff scheme, and timeline; say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle that; and be explicit about which part of the Marxian picture the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. Keep the whole answer under 800 words.
