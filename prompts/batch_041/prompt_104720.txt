Next month I'm presenting at our department's moral and political theory workshop, and I need a Q&A prep sheet. The paper is my attempt to defend a broadly aggregative (utilitarian-ish) view against the separateness-of-persons objection, and the room is going to be rough: the workshop is co-run by two Rawls-influenced liberals who think the objection is basically settled and can be invoked like a trump card, a Scanlonian who wants every moral claim framed in terms of what can be justified to each person, a Kantian who cares about persons as ends in themselves, and one social-choice economist who will keep dragging in interpersonal comparability and Arrow-style worries. So I need the objections I'll actually get from these specific people, in their vocabulary, ordered by how much damage each one does, each with a two-minute reply and a short note on where the reply is soft, and finally the one question I'm praying nobody asks. I've been arguing about this with my office mate for weeks so I've heard a few of these already, but I want the whole set laid out.

My move in the paper is that people run together two things under the heading of separateness. The first is the plain fact that persons are distinct loci of experience -- nobody shares a skull, harms to me aren't felt by you -- and I just grant that; it's true and it does no work. The second is a normative claim, that each person's good constitutes a seperate claim that can't be traded off against anyone else's. My argument is that the normative claim only ever gets its force from a picture in which a benefit has to be a benefit *for* someone in a demanding sense, i.e. the good has to show up as the good of some particular person inside the justification itself, and I try to show that this for-someone requirement is either trivial (every benefit is for someone, namely its recipient) or false (it would rule out things everyone accepts, like relieving five people's moderate chronic pain at the cost of leaving one person's mild pain unrelieved when the numbers clearly favor it). I also lean on an invented case I call Two Doors to make the point that there's a real difference between a justification being addressed to each person and each person's good appearing as a separate term in the justification.

So build the prep sheet to attack that distinction, because I suspect that's where I'm actually exposed, and tell me which of these people will find which seam. The Rawlsians will just keep repeating that this is the separateness of persons again to shut the conversation down -- I want a couple of prepared lines that make that move harder to use without me sounding like I'm dodging, and I want to know whether the economist's comparability point is a real threat to me or just a change of subject.
