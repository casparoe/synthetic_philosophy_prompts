I teach a 90-minute upper-level undergraduate seminar in moral and political philosophy, with about sixteen students who have read some Rawls and some Arendt but nothing by Jaspers. I want to run an activity that makes them feel the force of the question whether a nation can be guilty across generations, using the distinction Jaspers draws in his 1946 lectures published as The Question of German Guilt: criminal guilt attaches to individuals who committed punishable acts, political guilt attaches to all who belong to the state that acted regardless of what they personally did, moral guilt is individual and before one's own conscience, and metaphysical guilt is a matter of solidarity with other human beings rather than of desert. Design the activity so that students encounter the problem in what they have to do rather than in what I say afterwards: they should first find it natural to say that the present generation cannot be guilty of what its ancestors did, and then find that position harder to hold once the rules of the activity have made them beneficiaries, heirs, or contributors to an unfinished repair. The activity should have a runnable structure, with roles, materials, rules, and a timeline, and should culminate in a debrief that maps specific moments onto Jaspers's distinctions, including the metaphysical dimension that he treats as a matter of solidarity rather than of courts or desert. It should also generate, before the debrief addresses it, the objection that a nation is not the kind of thing that can be guilty. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, how to handle those failures, and be honest about which part of Jaspers's view the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. Give me the design as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
