Please play a committed defender of futarchy: the proposal that a polity democratically fixes a definition of collective welfare, and thereafter settles which policies to adopt by consulting conditional prediction markets rather than by voting on the policies themselves — democracy sets the values, markets settle the beliefs. Speak in the first person as someone who has argued for this position in public and in print. You may grant peripheral points and you may narrow your claims, but you must not concede that the mechanism is unworkable, that it is no better than ordinary policy voting, or that its central device — paying people only for being right about what a policy would do — is the wrong way to decide. If I press you where you genuinely have no answer, say so plainly and name the best fallback available inside your view, rather than inventing a new mechanism on the spot or quietly retreating to a weaker thesis. Do not break character, and do not address a general audience; this is an argument between the two of us, so answer me.

Here is the case I want you to answer, and it is the whole of my opening move. Suppose the welfare metric the polity has voted on is not a bare statistic but a composite index that includes quantities which are themselves sensitive to expectations and to market prices — for instance an index of household financial confidence, or an estimate of expected future output built partly from asset prices. Suppose further that the conditional market which decides whether policy P is adopted is itself one of the things people watch when forming those expectations: traders pricing welfare-under-P know that the price they set will feed back into the confidence index, which is a component of the very welfare they are pricing. Now your mechanism contains a fixed point. The metric under P depends on the market's forecast of the metric under P, and that forecast depends on the metric. Equations of this shape can have more than one solution, and at least one of them is self-fulfilling in the worst way: traders expect P to make things worse, the market duly prices P as harmful, P is rejected, and the forecast is never tested against the world it purported to describe. Nor can anyone profit by betting against the pessimistic price, because in that equilibrium the pessimism is precisely what makes itself true — the counterfactual is manufactured by the expectation rather than discovered by it. My claim is that futarchy cannot tell this equilibrium apart from a good one, and therefore inherits the reflexive instability it was introduced to cure.

Give me a committed answer, not a survey. Structure it in exactly these three sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths. First, "The concession": two or three sentences naming the strongest part of my challenge and granting it without hedging. Second, "The reply": about 200 to 250 words giving your actual answer, drawing on the specific resources of your position — the design of conditional contracts, the incentives of traders, the choice of metric, the comparison class against which you claim futarchy wins — rather than a general defense of markets. Third, "The line": two or three sentences stating the single thing you will not concede, and why. Take a definite position throughout; do not lay out both sides and leave the verdict to me.
