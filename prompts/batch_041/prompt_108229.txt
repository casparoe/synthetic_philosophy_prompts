Two friends of mine, an economist and a sociologist, fell into an argument at a dinner party over a case we all knew at second hand, and I would like a verdict that does more than pick a winner. The case is this. A mill town of about twelve thousand people loses its largest employer. Local unemployment rises from roughly five percent to roughly fifteen percent and stays there. A woman we will call Dana, who worked at the mill for eleven years, has applied for about forty jobs in the two years since and remains unemployed. Nothing about Dana changed across those two years: same skills, same age, same health, same willingness to relocate, same intensity of searching. Before the closure she had never gone more than a few months without work.

The argument went as follows.

(1) The economist: explaining Dana's unemployment requires only facts about Dana — her skills, her reservation wage, her search behavior, her beliefs about what is available. The local unemployment rate is a summary of many individuals' situations, not a further cause. Where it looks as though it is doing explanatory work, it is standing in for facts about individuals: in Dana's case, chiefly the fact that other job-seekers are competing for the same positions and that employers expect demand to remain weak.

(2) The sociologist: that cannot be right, because the individual facts are the same in the world in which Dana was employed. Hold everything about Dana fixed and change only the town, and her prospects change. The thing doing the causal work is therefore a property of the aggregate, and it is not a summary of Dana's facts, since her facts are being held constant. One cannot both hold the individual fixed and cite the individual as the explanation.

(3) The economist: of course the town changed, but the town changing just means that other individuals' facts changed — fewer firms hiring, more competitors per vacancy, employers revising their expectations downward. Write out the full individual-level description of every worker and every employer and the outcome is determined. The rate is a convenient index into that description. Reifying it adds nothing.

(4) The sociologist: then write it out. To describe the employers you must say what they believe — that demand is weak, that the mill is not coming back, that hiring now is a risk not worth taking. Those beliefs have contents that are about the aggregate condition of the local economy. So the individual-level description cannot be specified without already using the macro-level facts it was meant to replace, and the reduction is circular. And supervenience, even if granted, is not explanation; the question is which level carries the counterfactual dependence. Ask what would have become of Dana had the mill stayed open and nothing about her changed.

(5) The economist: beliefs about the economy are still beliefs, and still facts about individuals; you are confusing the order of specification with the order of explanation. And your argument proves too much. By your reasoning, the traffic jam, and not the drivers, made me late for my flight. But there is no jam over and above the drivers, only the pattern their separate decisions produce. In the same way there is no unemployment over and above the unemployed and the employers who pass them over.

(6) The sociologist: the jam is a good example, and it cuts my way. Every driver chose a route in order to avoid delay; the delay came from their choices taken together. No single driver's decision explains a two-hour standstill, and no driver could have brought it about alone; what explains it is the structure of the road network together with the fact that everyone was optimizing in the same direction. That is what a social fact is: an outcome no one intended and no one's biography contains. Dana's forty applications failed for the same reason the drivers crawled. What defeated her was a situation, not a set of people.

(7) The economist: then we disagree only about a word. I will grant that the local rate is a robust and projectible pattern with real predictive power. I deny that it is a cause over and above the individuals whose choices generate it, and I deny that any social explanation is complete until it says which individuals did what, and why. If an aggregate variable turns out to do work that no individual-level variable can do, that is evidence that we have failed to measure the right individual-level variables; the rate is a proxy for things about workers and firms we have not troubled to observe.

(8) The sociologist: that last move is the one I reject most firmly. As you have stated it, no evidence could count against it, since any success of the macro variable is reinterpreted as a proxy for unmeasured individual facts and you are never obliged to say which facts, or to produce them. Name them, measure them, and show that the rate stops predicting once they are in the model, and you win. Until then you are protecting the thesis from test rather than defending it.

I followed each of them for about two moves and then lost the thread. What I want is an adjudication that does not simply declare a winner: for each of the eight moves, say whether it is right, wrong, or right with a qualification, and why. Then tell me what distinction, if any, the two of them are running together, and whether the disagreement survives once that distinction is drawn. Neither position is mine and I have no stake in the outcome.
