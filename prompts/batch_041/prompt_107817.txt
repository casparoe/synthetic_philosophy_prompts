Okay so my two officemates got into it at the whiteboard again and I ended up half-refereeing while pretending to work on my own stuff. Background: we're all in the same alignment-adjacent group, and this fight about worst-case vs average-case guarantees keeps coming back every time someone floats a new safety property. Heres how it went, close to verbatim, I took notes because I have to present something at group meeting.

A: "Any alignment property worth the name should be a worst-case guarantee. If all I can say is that the model behaves on the inputs we sampled, or in expectation over the distribution we happened to draw, then I've said nothing about deployment. A guarantee with a distribution baked into it expires the second the distribution shifts, and distribution shift is literally the problem we're trying to solve. Quantify over everything in a threat model, or don't call it a guarantee."

B: "The threat model is a distribution in a costume. You can't quantify over 'everything,' you write down a set S, and the choice of S is an empirical bet about what's plausible. Make S big and the bound goes vacuous, or you can't train anything that meets it. Make S small and you've assumed the exact thing you said you refused to assume. Worst-case doesn't dodge the epistemics, it relocates the bet somewhere you don't have to look at it."

A: "Fine, but at least the bet is explicit and auditable. When I say 'for all x in S' a reviewer can attack S. When you say 'low expected loss under the deployment distribution,' theres nothing to attack, you can always say the distribution was different. And theres a decision-theoretic point underneath: when you don't know which distribution you'll face and the loss is catastrophic on some of them, the ranking that ignores the bad ones isn't a defensible ranking."

B: "That last part is doing a lot of work. Maximin is one response to uncertainty, not the only rational one. It's only forced if the uncertainty is adversarial, i.e. if nature is out to get you. In most deployments nature isn't out to get us, the bad cases are bad because they're rare and we were careless, not because someone picked them. And if you do treat the world as adversarial, maximin tells you never to deploy anything, which is also a decision, with its own costs. The sane frame is a distribution over deployment conditions plus a seperate, explicit treatment of the tail."

A: "Except in our setting the adversary isn't a metaphor. The thing we're worried about is a model that is itself an optimizer, possibly optimizing against the very property we certified. That's the one place where treating the worst case as adversarial isn't paranoia, it's just the actual threat model. A certificate that only holds in expectation over inputs the model doesn't choose is a certificate the model can route around, because inducing an input distribution is exactly the kind of thing it does."

B: "I'll grant that a model optimizing against you is a different case. But 'the model is an adversary' is a claim about the model, and it needs an argument. Which models, under what conditions, how reliably? If you can't say, then 'worst case over the model's own optimization' has no bound at all, because relative to your certificate the model has unbounded search. And an unbounded worst case isn't a guarantee, it's a slogan. Whereas average-case-plus-tail at least hands you a quantity you can try to estimate and move. I'd rather have a number that might be wrong than a quantifier nobody can satisfy."

A: "Then I don't think we're even disagreeing about worst vs average case. We're disagreeing about whether we're allowed to say 'we don't know the distribution' and still act. If you can't bound the bad case, you're not doing safety, you're benchmarking."

B: "And if you insist on bounding the bad case, you'll end up bounding a toy version of it, the toy will pass, and the real thing will fail. That's the failure mode we've already had."
