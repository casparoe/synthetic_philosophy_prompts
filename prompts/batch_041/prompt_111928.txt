I'm opening a seminar on weakness of will with a poem rather than a summary, because the discussion keeps splitting into two camps that talk past each other: the philosophers want to know how acting against one's own all-things-considered judgment is even possible, while the behavioral economists say the phenomenon is just preference reversal under hyperbolic discounting. Write me the poem, roughly 16 to 24 lines, no fixed meter or rhyme.

It has to get the discounting picture right: the discount applied to a delay is steep near the present and flattens as the interval recedes, so the implied rate falls with distance, and the same agent can prefer the larger, later reward while both are far off and the smaller, sooner one once it comes close — not indecision, but a reversal in the ranking itself. That is the sting, and I want the poem to carry both halves of it: the felt experience of being divided against oneself, and the deflationary reading on which there is no judgment left to betray, only a succession of selves each getting what it presently most wants.

Build the poem on a refrain that returns four or five times in identical wording, arranged so that the same words must endorse the later reward at the start and the sooner one at the end; the repetition, not the diction, should be what registers the reversal. You may include an attempt to bind one's future self in advance, but it shouldn't read as a solution. Leave the final repetition where both readings are still live — don't adjudicate, and don't name any philosophers.
