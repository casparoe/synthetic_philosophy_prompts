I'm in the second term of a moral philosophy seminar, and we have just spent three weeks on whether there can be genuine moral dilemmas — situations in which an agent is required to do each of two things she cannot both do, so that whatever she does she does wrong. I came in a sceptic, on the grounds that a requirement you cannot possibly meet is not a requirement at all, and I have left less sure. What I want from you is not a tour of the literature — I know the positions, and I don't need reading lists — but an attempt to find the principle behind a set of verdicts I have been keeping in a notebook, along with a candid account of which verdicts the best principle forces me to give up.

My verdicts are these. When a coast guard can reach only one of two swimmers caught in a rip on opposite sides of a headland, I do not think she faces a genuine dilemma: she must save one, she may save either, and the death of the one she cannot reach is a calamity rather than a wrong she commits. When a climber whose rope team is sliding toward a crevasse can cut the rope and drop the lowest climber, saving the rest, I do not think he faces a dilemma either; the dropped climber's death is a consequence of a move that is plainly permissible, not something done to him. But when a militia commander tells me to name one of my two innocent neighbours as a collaborator — the one I name will be shot, and if I refuse he shoots both — I do think I face a genuine dilemma, and that I come out of it having done something I should not have done whichever way I turn. Yet when a civil servant could avert a policy that will kill thousands only by planting evidence that sends an innocent colleague to prison, I do not think he faces a dilemma at all: framing an innocent person is simply off the table, so there is nothing to weigh. I can feel the militiaman and the civil servant pulling against each other, and I cannot say what separates them. Then there is the case I keep going back and forth on: I promised my dying mother that I would never let her estranged son back into the house, and he has now turned up homeless and ill with nowhere else to go, and I cannot tell whether that is a genuine dilemma or merely a hard choice.

The only principle I have managed to formulate is what I have been calling the debt test: an option is clean if I could take it and afterwards owe no one an apology, and a conflict is a genuine dilemma only when every available option is dirty in that sense. It seems to get the coast guard and the climber right, and the militiaman too. But it makes the civil servant's case a dilemma, which I resist; it makes the promise to my mother a dilemma, which I am not sure I want; and it suggests the coast guard's case is a dilemma after all, since I know I should feel I owed the drowned swimmer's family something. So I would like you to state as precisely as you can the principle, or small family of principles, that best fits these verdicts; to say what each candidate implies about cases I have not mentioned — whether a doctor who must give the last dose of an antidote to one of two dying patients faces a dilemma, say, or a person who could stop a bombing only by authorizing the torture of a man who may be innocent; and to be straight with me about which of my verdicts I have to surrender. I would genuinely rather have a simple principle that overturns two of my verdicts than a complicated one that saves them all.
