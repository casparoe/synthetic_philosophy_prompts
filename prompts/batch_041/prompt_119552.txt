I'm presenting a paper at a two-day workshop on self-reference across disciplines, and I need a Q&A prep sheet. The audience is split three ways: philosophers of biology who treat talk of a "genetic code" or "genetic information" as a metaphor that has done real damage; logicians who know the Gödel material cold and are allergic to loose analogies between formal systems and biology; and a handful of people who implement self-replicating programs in artificial-life systems. My paper takes up Hofstadter's Typogenetics — strands over A, C, G, T, enzymes built from amino acids that are specified by pairs of bases, each amino acid standing for a typographical operation on strands (cut, delete, move, copy, insert, search) — and argues that the standard reading, on which self-replication and self-reference arrive together, is wrong in both directions. Replication requires no self-reference: the enzyme that copies a strand never represents it, and the strange loop is in the eye of the interpreter. To test that, I extend the system with a single new amino acid whose operation writes the enzyme's own amino-acid sequence back into the strand as bases, so that the enzyme's output depends on its own code treated as data; and I argue that this addition buys no new replicative power. The moral I draw is that the DNA–Gödel isomorphism is a claim about what a formal system can be read as doing, not about what replication requires.

Draft the objections I will actually get, in the vocabulary each of those three groups would use, ordered by how much damage they would do, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front, and tell me the question I am hoping nobody asks. No citations or reading lists — I know the literature; I need to know what to say.
