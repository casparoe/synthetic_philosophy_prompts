I am in the third year of a philosophy degree and am currently taking a course on probability and induction. We spent the third week on the classical chord puzzle — the one in which three apparently natural ways of choosing a chord at random in a circle yield three different answers — and our written assignment for this term is to compose a short dialogue between two people who disagree about whether a principle of indifference can constrain rational credence at all. The single restriction is that we may not use the chord case itself; we are to construct a fresh case and build the dialogue around it. I have constructed one, and I would like you to write the dialogue.

The case is this. A point P lies somewhere in a disk of radius 1, and all you know about how it came to be there is that it lies in the disk. Consider two machines that might have placed it.

The first, which I will call Rain, drops points uniformly at random over the square that circumscribes the disk — two independent coordinates, each uniform on the interval from −1 to 1 — and we keep the first drop that lands inside the disk. Conditional on landing inside, such a point is uniform with respect to area.

The second, which I will call Dials, has two independent dials: one selects an angle uniformly between 0 and 2π, the other selects a distance from the centre uniformly between 0 and 1. The point is placed at that angle and at that distance.

Both machines treat every direction alike, and each is in a plain sense unbiased, but they disagree about the radius, and so they disagree about the question I want the dialogue to turn on: how confident should you be that P lies within half a unit of the centre? Rain says one quarter, since the inner disk contains a quarter of the area. Dials says one half. Nor is this an artefact of marking the second dial in distances: were it marked so as to select the squared distance uniformly, which seems just as natural a way of building an unbiased dial, the answer would be one quarter again, agreeing with Rain. The statement of the problem advertises no structure beyond the disk itself, and the rotations and reflections of the disk are respected by both machines equally. It is plainly a cousin of the chord puzzle, but I hope it differs enough that the dialogue cannot simply recite the standard three answers.

Please write the dialogue between two people, both competent and neither a foil. One holds that there is a fact of the matter about what someone who knows only that P is in the disk ought to believe, and that a principle of indifference, properly formulated, delivers it. The other holds that no such principle can do that work, and that rational credence must be anchored in something the problem actually supplies. I want the two machines to be what they argue about, and I want each of them to say what they take the answer to be, or why they think there is none.

Please make sure they engage with the following, since these are the points I expect my seminar to press hardest:

- whether the disk's rotational symmetry settles anything, given that it is satisfied by both machines;
- whether one of the two dials, the one marked in distances or the one marked in squared distances, can be shown to be the right one without already assuming the answer;
- whether indifference is even well defined over the points of a disk, given that there are uncountably many of them and each has zero area;
- whether the fact that Rain is uniform with respect to area while Dials is not is a reason to prefer Rain, or whether that simply presupposes what it was meant to establish.

They should respond to each other's actual points rather than deliver parallel monologues. They need not reach agreement — I would rather they did not, if the disagreement is real — but by the end I want to see exactly where the remaining difference lies. There is no need to cite or name any literature; I am interested only in the argument. About 1,500 words.
