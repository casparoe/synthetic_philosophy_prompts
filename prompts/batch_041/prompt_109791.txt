I have been collecting arguments that seem to have no name, and one entry keeps recurring. A long-standing arrangement is proposed for abolition — a professional licensing requirement, a firm's unwritten rule of precedence, a college's entrance ceremony. Its defender replies that the burden lies with the reformer: you do not pull down a fence until you can say why it was put up. The reformer then does say why: she gives an account of the function the arrangement once served and evidence that the function is now served elsewhere, better and more cheaply. At that point the defence changes shape. The arrangement, we are now told, was never a device with a function but a growth, whose worth lies in understandings that cannot be itemised in advance; the request to say what it is for cannot, in principle, be answered. Each reply, taken alone, is defensible. What holds my attention is that the standard of justification rises exactly when it is about to be met, and that both replies are offered as the position that presumes nothing, leaving the reformer permanently in the wrong.

I am after a dissection rather than a label. Chesterton's fence is the obvious candidate, and the conservative presumption in favour of settled practice is its obvious ancestor, but each seems to name only half of the thing: the fence rule is discharged once the reason is given, and a standing presumption is a doctrine about what may be assumed rather than a device for keeping a question open. Is there an established term for the composite — a burden of proof so specified that discharging it merely moves the goal — and if not, will you supply one, with its edges marked: how it differs from an honest conviction that some goods are inarticulate, from a fixed and openly avowed preference for the status quo, and from plain stonewalling?
