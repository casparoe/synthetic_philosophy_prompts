Two colleagues in a planning and knowledge-representation group spent most of a seminar arguing about the frame problem, and I have agreed to referee their exchange despite not being sure where I stand. I will reconstruct the argument as fairly as I can, move by move. Please adjudicate each move separately — rate it as clearly mistaken, defensible but overstated, or clearly right, with a brief justification — and then say who holds the stronger overall position and what both sides are overlooking. I am not asking for a survey of the standard positions; I want a verdict on the arguments as actually made.

The shared case is a household robot instructed to put a cereal box into a cupboard. Neither party disputes the elementary observation that, once the robot moves the box, almost nothing else in the world changes, and that a reasoner written in plain classical logic would have to be told this separately for every action and every fact the action leaves alone.

Move 1 (R). R holds that the frame problem is by now a solved technical matter. Allow nonmonotonic or causal-default representations — schemes that let the reasoner assume, absent specification to the contrary, that an action changes only what it is declared to change — and the proliferation of non-effects disappears. On his view the original puzzle was an artifact of insisting on a strictly monotonic logic, and to call it a deep open problem today is simply to be behind the literature.

Move 2 (S). S grants the mechanism but denies that it touches the problem. The reasoner no longer writes down every non-effect; it must instead decide which defaults to adopt, and that is where the difficulty lives. The color of the box, the city the kitchen is in, and the phase of the moon are all left unchanged by the action, but the robot has not handled the frame problem unless something in it determines that these are irrelevant rather than merely unmentioned. She adds that no such determination can be made once and for all, because what counts as relevant shifts with the task.

Move 3 (R). R replies that this proves too much. Every finite system must ignore the overwhelming majority of facts; that is a truism about bounded agents, not a defect peculiar to planning. If S means only that the robot cannot survey everything, no one ever claimed otherwise, and she has merely renamed the general problem of bounded rationality as the frame problem. If instead she means that the robot must first know which facts are relevant before it can act, then she is demanding that it know the relevance of everything in advance, which is incoherent.

Move 4 (S). S says R has forced a false choice. The interesting claim is not that the robot must know the relevance of everything, but that there is no representation of the agent's situation that fixes relevance independently of the task and of the agent's own earlier actions — and that this dependence is not an incidental engineering nuisance but the substance of the problem. She distinguishes two things that have been run together: a representational version, concerning how to write down non-effects compactly, which she agrees is solved; and an inferential version, concerning how the agent at any moment selects the small set of facts worth considering, which she says is not solved, and which she thinks the discussion should have been about from the start.

Move 5 (R). R accepts the distinction but says it cuts against S. If the inferential version amounts to efficiently deciding what to compute next, that is a research program in heuristic search and resource-bounded reasoning, with no special philosophical residue; progress comes from better heuristics, not from conceptual analysis. That we cannot state a general relevance function is no more mysterious, he says, than that we cannot state a general heuristic — and a problem that dissolves into engineering is not a philosophical problem.

I would like each of the five moves rated, a judgment on whether S's distinction in Move 4 genuinely separates a solved problem from an unsolved one or merely relabels the same difficulty, and an overall verdict on who is closer to right.
