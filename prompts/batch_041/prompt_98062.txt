I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I host a small interview podcast about ideas that sit between my field and other disciplines. Next week's guest is a researcher who defends a view he calls moral trade, and I want to interview him properly rather than let him give the standard pitch. His claim, as I understand it, is this: two people can disagree completely about which cause matters most and still make a trade that each of them, judged by his own moral rankings, regards as an improvement. I care most about cause A and almost nothing about cause B; you care most about B and almost nothing about A; so I give to B and you give to A, and we are both better off than if each of us had given to his own top cause. He also runs a small matching service that pairs donors on this basis, and he insists the matching should be designed so that nobody has an incentive to lie about how he ranks the causes.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions. I'd like at least one question each on the following, and for each question a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give:

- whether the trade really leaves both parties better off when one of them regards the other's cause as not merely low-priority but actively harmful;
- what "better off" can mean when the two parties' moral rankings may not be comparable at all, and whether the trade works only because it never asks either party to compare across the two scales;
- the integrity worry: that someone who donates to a cause she thinks is unimportant in order to get donations to her own may be betraying her own commitment, or treating her convictions as a bargaining chip;
- the mechanism design question: whether a matching rule can be strategy-proof in the relevant sense, and whether the usual impossibility results about truthful reporting in social-choice mechanisms apply here;
- a version of his proposal in which neither party is certain of his own ranking — say each thinks his own cause is more important with probability around seventy percent — and whether trading under that uncertainty is still trade or has quietly become ordinary expected-value reasoning;
- the broker's own position: can the matchmaker be morally neutral, or does running the service amount to taking a stand about which trades are acceptable;
- whether moral trade is merely permitted or sometimes required, and what he would say to someone who finds the whole practice repellent but cannot articulate why;
- whether trading replaces arguing, and whether a world of smooth moral trades would lose the pressure that disagreement puts on us to work out who is right;
- what evidence would make him abandon the practice, and where he draws the line between a trade and a bribe or a threat.

A few things about the format. Write the questions in my voice: plain, specific, and free of unexplained jargon, but not watered down — if a question needs a technical term, introduce it inside the question. For each question, don't just list a follow-up: commit to what you think he will actually say, then give the follow-up that makes that answer hardest to retreat from; where the argument is genuinely divided, say which way you would bet and why. Please don't give me citations, reading lists, or references to who has said what — I have no way to check them and I don't need them; if a question depends on an idea, spell the idea out instead of naming it.
