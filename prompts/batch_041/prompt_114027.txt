I am a doctoral student in the philosophy of mathematics, and I have been trying to reconstruct, in the sharpest form I can, the argument that the human mind cannot be a machine — the line usually associated with Lucas and with Penrose. I have ended up with the following chain, and I have grown uneasy about it, because I suspect it establishes considerably more than its authors intend. I would like you to diagnose it.

(1) Suppose, for reductio, that a given human mathematician's mathematical capacity is exactly captured by some consistent formal system F, rich enough for elementary arithmetic.
(2) By the first incompleteness theorem, there is a sentence G_F which is true but not provable in F; and the Gödel sentence can be chosen so that it is provably equivalent to a formal statement of F's consistency, Con(F).
(3) The mathematician, reflecting on F's axioms and rules, can recognise that F is consistent, and therefore can recognise that G_F is true.
(4) So the mathematician can prove a sentence — G_F — that F cannot prove.
(5) By (1), F captures the mathematician's capacity, so whatever she can prove, F can prove. Contradiction.
(6) Hence no consistent formal system captures the mathematician's capacity; the mind is not a machine.

My worry is this. Step (3) appears to be applied uniformly, to whichever system is under discussion. Within the reductio, the system under discussion is the one that is supposed to capture the mathematician herself — call it M. So the same step (3) entitles her to recognise the consistency of M, that is, to prove Con(M). But M, by hypothesis, is the system that captures her; so M proves Con(M). By the second incompleteness theorem that is impossible for any consistent M. The argument, if it goes through, therefore appears to deliver the result that the mind can prove its own consistency — a result I regard as either false or as showing the mind to be inconsistent, and I am prepared to accept neither. I take it that something has gone wrong around (2) and (3), but I cannot state the fault cleanly. Is it a scope confusion in the letter "F" — between systems we can survey from the outside and the system we ourselves are? Or is step (3) simply false, and if so, which feature of actual mathematical practice does it misdescribe? I want the diagnosis, and I want the price. If I abandon (3) as stated, what becomes of the anti-mechanist conclusion: is there a weaker version that survives, and if so what exactly does it now claim? Please write your answer in British English.
