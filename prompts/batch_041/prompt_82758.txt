I work on backend infrastructure, and the mind-uploading debate keeps getting settled for me in the vocabulary of my day job: the brain is the hardware, the mind is the software, and uploading is a port. On that picture, moving a person onto a new substrate is like moving a service onto a new cluster — the implementation changes, the thing that matters is carried over — and gradual replacement of neurons with silicon should be a non-event, since we hot-swap disks, NICs, and whole nodes underneath a running service all the time without anyone claiming the service died. That is the argument I keep hearing for being relaxed about both uploading and piecemeal replacement, and it is the argument I would like audited rather than endorsed or dismissed.

Specifically, I want:

- the correspondence table: what plays the role of the program, the running process, the persistent state, the interface the outside world depends on, the hardware, the operator;
- where the mapping breaks, and which of the breaks actually bear on the conclusion that the person survives, as opposed to being mere decoration;
- two features of my world that I suspect are the real pressure points. First, live migration: during a pre-copy the source and destination both exist and both hold the state, the source keeps dirtying memory faster than it can be copied, and only at the end is it throttled and stopped. If a person-upload worked that way, at what point in that sequence, if any, does the analogy license saying someone moved, rather than saying someone was copied and then killed? Second, in a distributed system no single node is the service; its identity is partly a convention about what the clients depend on. Does the mind have an analogue of that, or is it more like a single process, where there is a fact of the matter?

If the analogy cannot carry the conclusion, say what should replace it — whether the target is better modeled as a service than as a program, or whether no computational analogy does the work at all — and state plainly which of my day-job intuitions I ought to give up.
