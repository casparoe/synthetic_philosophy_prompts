For some years I have been trying to understand why the debate over the Frege-Geach problem for expressivism never seems to move, and I have come to suspect that the participants are not really disagreeing about what they say they are disagreeing about.

The problem, as I understand it, runs roughly as follows. Expressivism holds that a moral sentence — the claim that lying is wrong, say — does not state a fact but expresses a non-cognitive attitude, such as disapproval or the acceptance of a norm. The difficulty is that the same sentence occurs in unasserted positions: as the antecedent of a conditional, under negation, in a disjunction, as a premise. There it does not appear to express any attitude, and yet it plainly contributes its meaning to the whole. If the standalone occurrence expresses disapproval, then the conditional seems to equivocate, and an argument that moves from the wrongness of lying, through a conditional linking the wrongness of lying to the wrongness of getting one's brother to lie, to the wrongness of getting one's brother to lie, either fails to be valid or fails to be about disapproval. A generation of expressivists has replied with quasi-realist reconstructions of truth and validity, theories of normative commitment, deflationary accounts of truth, and hybrid views that admit belief-like states alongside attitudes; a generation of critics has replied to the replies; and here we still are.

I do not want a survey of those moves. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, and I have a hypothesis I would like tested rather than confirmed. My hypothesis is that the quarrel is no longer really about moral language but about the criteria of adequacy for a theory of meaning, and about whether a deflationary or minimalist conception of truth can bear the weight expressivists place on it. The critics appear to demand a compositional semantics that assigns the embedded occurrence a stable meaning and to treat its absence as decisive; the expressivists appear to offer reconstructions of the practice of asserting, inferring, and criticizing, and to treat those reconstructions as discharging the demand. If each side measures success by a standard the other rejects, then no further argument at the object level will help, because the disagreement is about what would count as a solution rather than about the phenomenon.

Two subsidiary questions follow. First, is the dispute partly verbal? The word meaning carries a great deal of weight on both sides, and if one party assumes that to give a sentence's meaning is to give its truth-conditions while the other assumes that meaning is fixed by use, then some of the apparent conflict may be a dispute about what a semantic theory is for. Second, are the two sides answering different questions under a single heading — the critic asking a semantic question about the embedded occurrence, the expressivist answering a psychological or pragmatic question about the state of mind that asserting the sentence expresses and the commitments it incurs?

Tell me how much of this holds up. Where my hypothesis is mistaken, say so and explain what is actually doing the work. If the disagreement is substantive, identify the question it turns on and what could settle it. If it is partly verbal, say which claims depend only on an ambiguity and which survive disambiguation. And then say what would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, a redefinition of the question — or argue that none should be expected, and why. I would rather have a diagnosis that takes a side on the structure of the dispute than a neutral inventory of positions.
