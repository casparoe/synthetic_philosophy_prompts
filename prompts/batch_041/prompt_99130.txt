I cover artificial intelligence and the philosophy of science, and I keep watching two camps in mechanistic interpretability talk past each other: internal-structure realists, who treat claims like "this direction encodes the concept X" as literal descriptions of a network's internal organization, and interpretive deflationists, who treat such claims as instruments that earn their keep by predicting and controlling behavior, with no further fact of the matter. I want the joint statement these two could actually sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

First, make them agree on what a representation claim asserts — naming its relata (a vehicle, a content, a downstream consumer) and the level at which it is made (a unit, a direction, a subspace, a circuit) — and on what would render such a claim well- or ill-formed, without appeal to citations. Then set out their residual disagreements, each phrased in wording its own side would accept, on at least three questions: whether underdetermination (many incompatible attributions fitting the same activations) tells against realism; whether causal use by downstream computation is sufficient for content or merely necessary; and whether the interpreter's choice of task and distribution is constitutive of the attribution or only evidence for it.

Then identify the cruxes, and for each give a test both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Include one test of your own construction: a swap test in which a decodable direction is transplanted into a context matched on nuisance variables, and downstream behavior is measured — say explicitly what each camp predicts when decoding succeeds but use does not transfer, and when use transfers without stable decoding. Format as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.
