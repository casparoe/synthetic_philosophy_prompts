I direct selection for a competitive fellowship program. Every cycle my committee reads several hundred applications and must rank candidates on qualities we cannot observe directly — persistence, judgment, and the capacity to finish hard things. We lean heavily on costly actions as proxies: an applicant who left a secure job to work full time on the proposed project, one who spent a year of personal savings on a data collection, one who moved to another country for the work, one who completed an expensive credential with no evident instrumental payoff. My committee treats these as proof of commitment. I suspect we are reading them wrong, sometimes in both directions, and I want a procedure rather than an essay.

Write a decision procedure my committee can run on any single costly action an applicant presents, grounded in what the theory of costly signaling and the handicap principle actually establishes rather than in its folk version. Assume no technical background on my side or my committee's: that a signal can be credible because it is costly is the whole of what we know, so the procedure itself must carry whatever distinctions matter, in plain language.

Deliver a short ordered sequence of questions. For each question, state what counts as a sufficient answer, what answer ends the inquiry with the signal discounted to near zero, and where the question cannot be settled by evidence and must be handed to a person. The procedure must handle explicitly, at minimum: a signal whose cost is roughly equal for strong and weak candidates; a signal so common among applicants that it no longer distinguishes anyone; a signal whose cost is not wasted but productive, so that it also yields something of independent value; a signal that is cheaper for wealthy applicants than for others; and an exceptionally strong candidate who presents no costly signal at all. Where the procedure will misfire, say so.

Then run it on three applications: the applicant who quit a secure job; the applicant who self-funded a large data collection; and one I expect you to find hardest — an applicant who has publicly staked a falsifiable claim about their own future work and offered to forfeit something if it fails. One of the three verdicts should be one my committee will not like; tell me which and why.

Close by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do: the questions it will never settle, and the decisions that remain ours even if we follow it perfectly. We have perhaps twenty minutes per file, the committee has no formal training in this material, and I have to be able to defend each verdict to a board that is skeptical of holistic criteria.
