I'm a third-year phil student and I've got a term paper coming up on pain. My seminar leader keeps using asymbolia cases—patients who say the pain is there, can point to it, sometimes even pull away, but say it doesn't bother them—to argue that the badness of pain is all in the affective punch and not in the sensory pointer. I've basically swallowed that line: I now think the sensory quality of pain is just information, and if you stripped the punch you'd strip everything that makes pain bad. She also floated a thought experiment: a drug that leaves the pointer intact but kills the punch, and another that leaves the punch but scrambles the pointer—would the second even be pain, and if not, does that show the pointer is doing more than carrying info?

What I want from you is the case against me. What would count against the view that the sensory side is evaluatively inert? Not just the obvious but it hurts stuff—I mean which observations, dissociations, or intuitions should actually move me, and which are just noise. Rank them from this would sink the view to annoying but survivable, and tell me which ones an asymbolia-based theorist would themselves have to concede count against them. I'm especially worried about cases where the sensory quality seems to carry its own demand—like the way a specific burning pain feels like damage, not just a signal—and about whether the view has quietly defined badness as what we mind, so that nothing could count against it. If that's what's happening, is that a problem? I'd rather keep the asymbolia lesson than give it up, but I want to know what I'm paying.
