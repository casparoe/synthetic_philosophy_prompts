I sit on the behavioral-insights review panel of a large public university, and we now have a proposal to decide. Our record so far is as follows. We approved a change to the retirement-enrollment portal that made participation the default and required a single click to decline, publicly announced as a default and reversible at any time. We approved a plain-language label on the dining-hall menus that reported calorie counts and nothing more. We approved a disclosed social-norms message that told students, accurately, how many of their peers had already completed a required survey, worded so that a reader could ignore it at no cost. We rejected a proposal to make students file a written, countersigned form in order to escape a defaulted meal plan. And we rejected a scheme that would have steered students toward a more expensive housing tier that mainly increased the university's revenue.

The new proposal is this. The same office wants to email students who have not yet paid a library fine and tell them that most students in their situation have already settled their accounts. The claim is not strictly false, but it is worded to suggest a higher rate of compliance than the actual figure. The email is not identified as a behavioral intervention, recipients can ignore it at no cost, and it reliably raises payment rates without harming anyone's interests.

Give me a recommendation that is consistent with our own precedents: what should we decide, which of our earlier decisions is the closest analog, and, if our record genuinely underdetermines the matter, which principle we would be adopting by approving and which by rejecting. Set out the competing rules our past decisions might be tracking, say what each of them predicts about this proposal, tell me which rule you think we are in fact running and why, and name the further case that would best tell those rules apart.
