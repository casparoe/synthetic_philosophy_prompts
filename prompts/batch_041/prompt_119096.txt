I am a retired experimental scientist — thirty-eight years in a national survey, most of it on sediment transport — who took up philosophy in earnest only after I stopped having a laboratory to go to. I have no formal training in it, which is why I am writing to you rather than to a colleague, and I would rather you correct my reading than defer to it.

The book that has unsettled me is Pascal's Pensées, and not the part everyone knows. What I did not expect to find there was a theory of institutional authority that reads like an audit of every committee I have ever sat on. As I understand him — and I am aware of flattening something subtle — Pascal holds that justice and force cannot in practice be separated: justice without force is helpless, force without justice is condemned, and since we cannot make what is just strong, we make what is strong just. The costume then does the work: the robes, the halls, the ceremony, the forms of proceeding exist because justice itself cannot be exhibited, and the appearance must substitute for it. He seems further to hold that the authority of custom rests on nothing beyond the fact that it has been received — that whoever traces a custom back to its first origin destroys it — and that there is a division between what the wise may quietly understand about the origins of authority and what the people must be permitted to believe. If that is right, it is a thesis about the foundation of every institution I have served, and it seems to me that the proper test of a thesis of this kind is not to argue with it but to build something out of it and see whether the thing stands.

So: write me a satirical document. I want an annex to the bylaws of an invented learned society — a scientific one, of the kind I spent my life in — titled something on the order of "Annex on the Resolution of Disputes and the Preservation of Authority," drafted in the flattest possible administrative prose by a registrar or general counsel who is entirely in earnest and never winks, in numbered articles, of roughly 800 words. The annex should adopt Pascal's account of authority outright, as the society's actual operating theory rather than as a subject of debate, and follow it wherever it leads. I do not want the document to argue for the view or to name its author; I want it to presuppose the view and get on with business.

Among the matters it should have to handle, deadpan, and without ever stating the joke:

- the ceremony of investiture and the function of regalia, and why the society maintains them even though, or precisely because, they establish nothing;
- the rule that a decision of the council is binding because it is backed by the council's power, together with the companion rule that it must also be seen to be just, and the procedure for supplying the appearance when the substance is unavailable;
- the treatment of a member who asks why a standing rule is just, and the separate treatment of a member who publishes a genealogy of it — tracing it back to its origin, and showing it to have been an accident of 1954;
- the appeals ladder, and the requirement that no appeal may reach a first principle;
- what the council may know and what the membership must be left to believe, and who is permitted to read which minutes;
- two or three of the cases we actually face: a priority dispute between a large laboratory and a solitary author; a demand for retraction from a well-funded group; a motion to rename a prize.

Every article should be something the theory actually licenses rather than something invented for a laugh: the comedy should come from following the logic past the point where the society would normally stop talking. Someone who has read Pascal should recognize the fidelity, and someone who has not should still find it funny and, by the end, a little cold.

Two constraints. First, do not quote Pascal and do not put any of his sentences in quotation marks; I would rather you work the ideas into the articles than guess at his wording, and a document of this kind would never cite him anyway. Second, keep the trappings modern: a reader should not be able to dismiss the annex as a piece of seventeenth-century costume, because the cases it handles — priority, retraction, funding, committees, prize names — are ours.

Finally, I would like a note to me, about 150 words and not part of the document, in which you commit to one reading of Pascal and defend it as the right one, rather than laying out the interpretive options and leaving the choice to me. Tell me which reading you have committed to, why you take it to be correct, and where the annex takes liberties with his position or leans on a point that specialists dispute. I would rather have a definite reading I can argue with than a balanced survey, and I will not be offended by the liberties so long as you tell me where they are.
