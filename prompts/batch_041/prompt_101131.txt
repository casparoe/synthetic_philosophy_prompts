OK so there's this move I keep running into and I want it properly stress-tested. The move is: brain is hardware, mind is software, so consciousness is substrate-independant -- run the same program on anything and the same mind comes along for the ride. A friend of mine takes this all the way out to the Einstein's brain dialogue in The Mind's I. His version: take that whole imagined conversation with Einstein's brain, write it out in a book, and make the book's physical structure mirror the brain's states closely enough that reading it page by page reproduces the state transitions. On the hardware/software view, he says, the book would be conscious -- it's just thinking really slowly, one page turn per thought. He insists the slowness is a red herring, that what matters is the causal organization and not the clock speed, and that anyone who finds book-consciousness absurd is just being sentimental about carbon.

How far does that analogy actually hold here? I want the mapping laid out -- what plays the role of the hardware, the software, the compiler, the running process, the I/O -- and then where it breaks. Be specific about which breaks actually matter for his conclusion and which are just incidental. In particular I'm suspicious that the software picture quietly imports something the book doesnt have: a running process rather than a static artifact, a distinction between a system that computes and a giant lookup table that merely encodes, maybe a notion of what counts as the same program when the substrate is that different. Which of those are real disanalogies and which am I overreading? And once they're all on the table, does the book-consciousness conclusion survive on its own, or was the analogy carrying the whole thing? If there's a better analogy for whatever the functionalist is really entitled to claim, tell me what it is and what it gives up.
