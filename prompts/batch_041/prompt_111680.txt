My old housemates Ravi and Dana were both in a decision theory / phil of econ orbit, and at a party last month they got into a thing about the one-shot prisoner's dilemma that went on so long I ended up sitting on the stairs with a beer waiting for it to end. I said I'd referee and get back to them, which was two months ago, so I'm outsourcing it. I'll try to give it to you straight, because I honestly couldnt tell who had the better of it.

(1) Ravi: in a one-shot PD between two people who know that each of them reasons as well as the other, cooperating is the rational move, and it's not about reputation or repetition or side payments. The deliberation that produces your choice is the same deliberation running in both heads. If the argument for defecting is good enough to move you, it's good enough to move them, and you both defect and both get the worse payoff. So taking your own reasoning seriously means acting as the person whose choice is one and the same as the other player's. He called this the superrationality thing Hofstadter came up with. (2) Dana: she said that's evidential decision theory wearing a hat, and it has the standard disease, which is confusing correlation with causation. What you actually get is a function of your move and their move, and their move doesn't change depending on what you do at the moment of choosing, so "if I'd defected, that would have shown me the world where they defect too" is a fantasy. She said it's the smoking lesion case: if a hidden lesion causes both the cancer and the urge to smoke, the evidence that smokers get cancer doesn't mean quitting would do anything for you. And she said in real life two people are never identical -- different priors, different moods, different noise -- so the honest version of Ravi's setup hands you some probability p that the other cooperates, and for any p short of 1 you can rig the payoffs so that defecting wins. She added that even if you gave both players a shared randomizing device and asked what joint plan they could be told to follow, "both cooperate" can't be it, because once each is told to cooperate each would still rather defect -- so the correlation isn't what's missing, the dominance is. (3) Ravi: he said she's smuggling in the wrong counterfactual, because you can't hold p fixed and vary your own action; in the case at issue p isn't a fact about them sitting over there independent of you, it's a fact about a shared deliberation. He said the question isn't "what if I defect while they stay the same" but "what happens if this way of deciding runs in both of us," and under that comparison cooperation obviously wins, and having to imagine it as a pair isn't cheating, it's the situation. (4) Dana: she said then it's near-tautological -- "cooperate when you're sure the other cooperates for the same reason you do" just unpacks to "cooperate when cooperating is better," and the identical-agents assumption is doing all the work. She said the principle also overgenerates if you take it as a general decision rule: if my twin and I both reason this way, does my deciding to move to Berlin mean my twin moves to Berlin too, and should I count his life as a consequence of my choices? And she said it says nothing about the cases anyone actually faces, which are ones where the other player is almost like you, or where the payoffs aren't symmetric, or where there are more than two players. (5) Ravi: he said yeah, it's an idealization and it doesn't cover asymmetric payoffs, but that's a limitation and not a refutation, and the twin case is disanalogous because in the PD there's a joint payoff being selected between, which is the whole point.
