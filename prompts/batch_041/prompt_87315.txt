My reading group has been working through Augustine on lying — we did the early treatise On Lying and then the later Against Lying, which I gather he wrote many years afterward against the Priscillianists, who thought it was fine to be cagey about doctrine with heretics — and one guy in the group wrote up a summary he's quite proud of. It goes roughly like this: Augustine is the great rigorist about lying, the Kant before Kant. Every lie is a sin, full stop, and he won't bend even for enormous stakes — you may not lie to save a life, to protect someone from being assaulted, or to trick a heretic into the true faith. He does rank lies by how bad they are, from the deadly ones down to the harmless jocose and officious kinds, with jokes sitting at the mild end. And for him a lie is simply a false statement: if you tell a dying man his son is still alive when you believe the son is dead, that's a lie whether or not it turns out the son really is alive, because what makes it a lie is the falsity, not your state of mind. The one place he seems to soften is the midwives in Exodus, who lied to Pharaoh to save the Hebrew babies and got blessed for it — so even Augustine has to admit that a lie that harms nobody and saves lives isn't really a sin, which is why people say his rigorism is more rhetorical than real.

I have a nagging feeling this is mostly right but wrong in a couple of places that matter, and I can't put my finger on where.
