I teach an upper-level philosophy of language seminar, about 18 students, one 50-minute session, and I want a game that makes the performative side of language feel like a fact about the world rather than a doctrine they can recite back. They can all say that some utterances don't describe anything, they do something, and then in discussion they slide straight back to treating every utterance as a report on the speaker's inner state -- he didn't really promise, he was just being polite; she didn't warn him, he didn't take it as a warning. I want them to bang into the following inside the rules of the game, before I name any of it, and I'll state it in my own words so you dont have to go look anything up. First, some utterances do their work alone: said by the right person in the right setting, the deed is done and stays done even if the speaker meant none of it, whereas others -- a bet, an offer, a dare, a deal -- just hang in the air and accomplish nothing at all unless someone else takes them up. Second, failure comes in two sizes and students conflate them: the botched procedure where nothing happened at all, versus the act that genuinely went through and was defective, since a promise made in bad faith is still a promise and finding out afterwards what it costs you doesn't unwrite it. Third, you can change the record just by talking as though something were already settled, and it stays settled unless someone objects in time, and objecting is expensive. Authority cuts across all of these: the same words from someone with the standing to say them are an act, from someone without it they're nothing, and nothing in the words themselves shows the difference. Design me the activity -- rules, materials, roles, a payoff scheme that needs no real money, a timeline for the 50 minutes, and a debrief that maps what actually happened onto those contrasts. The trap I want them to fall into is thinking their private intentions are what determine what they've done. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle that, and be straight about which part of this an activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to just tell them. I'm happy to play the registrar myself and enforce the rules mechanically. Write it as continuous prose, please -- no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables, I'll turn it into a handout myself.
