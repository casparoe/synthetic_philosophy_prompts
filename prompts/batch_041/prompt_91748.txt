Okay this is a slightly odd one. I TA a second-year metaphysics unit and next week I'm running the personal identity session — 18 students, 75 minutes, one room, no laptops. Normally I'd just talk about Locke and Parfit and call it a day, but my little brother has just put a deposit down on one of those brain-scan-and-restore services (you know, they map your brain and promise to "bring you back" later), and he keeps texting me asking whether the thing they bring back will actually be him. I don't have a good answer, and it's made Parfit's teletransporter feel a lot less like a cute puzzle.

So I want an activity that makes them feel the thing instead of hearing it: that "is it still me?" and "does it matter?" can pull apart, and that the first question might be doing less work than we assume. No lecture up front — I want the rules to corner them into it, and I don't want to say the word "identity" as the thing at stake until the debrief.

Rough shape I'm imagining (replace it if you've got something better): a staged teletransport where, before anyone learns the outcome, they have to commit something — write instructions, allocate a small pot of "care" tokens, place bets, whatever — and then the outcome is a case where the original survives *and* a psychologically continuous copy (or two copies) also exists, so all their careful is-it-me bookkeeping jams while their actual attachments don't. I want a payoff scheme in points, no real money, where the identity-fixated commitments lose and the continuity-tracking ones cash out, but not in a way that feels rigged or like I'm feeding them the answer.

Give me: materials, the rules, the payoff table, how to run the reveal, and a debrief that maps each beat of the game onto the pieces of Parfit's view — branching, why "me" can't pick between two equally continuous successors, why surviving might come in degrees even if being identical can't. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (I've run activities like this before and they always go sideways somewhere) and how to handle it, and be honest about what the game can't do — I suspect it can only show them that identity-talk gets stuck, not show them that there's no further fact of the matter at all, and if that's right I want to know so I can say it myself at the end.
