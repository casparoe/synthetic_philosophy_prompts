Two colleagues are arguing over what Harsanyi's aggregation theorem actually establishes. One holds that it is a genuine derivation of utilitarianism: grant that social evaluation respects unanimity among individuals and satisfies the expected-utility axioms over lotteries on welfare profiles, and a weighted sum of individual utilities is forced, so any non-utilitarian social rule must give up one of those premises. The other holds that the theorem is a representation result that begs the question -- applying the sure-thing principle to lotteries over whole profiles already builds in that social value is linear in individual utilities, and the veil-of-ignorance framing only disguises the assumption that everyone shares a single cardinal utility scale. What is your credence that the first reading is correct, that the theorem genuinely constrains a planner who accepts its premises rather than encoding the utilitarian conclusion in advance? Decompose the number: the probability that the sure-thing principle at the social level is independently defensible; the probability that the impartial-observer or veil-of-ignorance interpretation is the right reading of the premises; the probability that interpersonal comparisons of utility are determinate enough for the weighted sum to mean something; the probability that the result survives dropping the shared-cardinal-scale assumption; and any further term you think belongs. Give each a number and one line of justification, show how they combine, identify the term the final number is most sensitive to, and name the single argument or result that would move it most. Answer in American English.
