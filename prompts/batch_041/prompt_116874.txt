The question was:

"Explain Russell's account of definite descriptions, using (S) 'The chair of the Vantry Prize committee is a former physicist' as your example. In particular: (a) What does (S) mean on Russell's account, and does it commit us to there being a chair of the committee? (b) How does the account handle 'The chair of the Vantry Prize committee is not a former physicist'? (c) What does Russell's treatment of existence claims imply about 'the chair of the committee exists'? (d) In a sentence or two, contrast Russell's treatment with Strawson's and with Donnellan's."

The students answer:

"Russell's main idea is that a definite description like 'the chair of the committee' is not a name and doesn't stand for an object. A sentence of the form 'The F is G' is really three claims rolled into one: (i) there is at least one F, (ii) there is at most one F, and (iii) whatever is F is G. So (S) says that there is a chair of the Vantry Prize committee, that there is only one such chair, and that whoever it is, they are a former physicist. So yes — (S) does commit us to there being a chair; the existence claim is part of what the sentence asserts. Russell also held that existence is not a predicate: 'exists' doesn't pick out a property that the chair has, it just registers that the description is satisfied.

The theory was built to handle negation and negative existentials. Take 'The chair of the Vantry Prize committee is not a former physicist.' The existence and uniqueness claims are built into the description and are presupposed, so when there is no chair of the committee the sentence comes out true rather than leaving us with a truth-value gap. That's the payoff: we don't have to say the sentence is neither true nor false, and we don't have to posit some non-existent chair to make it false.

Strawson's objection is that the sentence does presuppose that there is a chair, so if there isn't one it fails to express anything true or false — Russell is wrong to call it false. Donnellan adds a further distinction between two uses of descriptions. In the referential use, the speaker uses the description to pick out whatever object happens to satisfy it; in the attributive use, the speaker already has a particular individual in mind and uses the description to say something about that person. Donnellan's point is that Russell's analysis really only captures the attributive use, so it can't be the whole story about how descriptions work.

Overall, Russell gets the truth-conditions of 'there is exactly one' sentences right, and his denial that existence is a predicate is an important advance, even if the account needs supplementing for the pragmatic cases Donnellan describes."
