I sit on the safety-review committee of a mid-sized hospital network, and I have spent two years trying to name a pattern that I keep encountering in post-incident reviews of automated and semi-automated systems. I am reasonably sure the general terrain is well mapped — people tell me I am describing a diffusion of responsibility, or the problem of many hands, and I do not doubt that these are nearby. But the specific configuration I want to describe is not quite either of them, and I would like to know whether it has an established name, and, if it does not, what it should be called.

Here is the configuration. A harm occurs. We convene a review, and the review is, by its own lights, a success: it yields a complete and satisfying causal account of how the harm came about. What it cannot yield is a person who was at fault. The clinician who countersigned the software's flag had four seconds per case, a caseload fixed years earlier by a staffing formula, and no way to tell which of the system's flags were the weak ones; the vendor validated on the population it could obtain access to; the regulator's guidance permitted precisely this division of labour; the purchasing committee bought the least expensive system that met the guidance. Each decision was defensible when it was made, and each person, asked what they should have done differently, can offer an answer that is both sincere and inert: that they would have needed to know then what we know now.

Three things about this seem to me to fall outside the usual descriptions.

First, the pattern is aggravated by being recognised. The first time we describe the structural character of such a failure out loud — in a review, in a memo, in a published finding — we do not repair it; we furnish everyone in the chain with a better excuse. The overseer now knows that the oversight was designed to be impossible, so their lapse becomes a documented property of the system rather than a failure of theirs. The vendor knows the buyer accepted the residual risk. The regulator knows the deployer is nominally in control. Each of them can now point at the acknowledged structure, and since the structure is not an agent, the pointing never lands. Recognition, which is supposed to be the first step toward accountability, has become an input to the excuse.

Second, the fault seems to belong to a series rather than to any of its members. No single incident is anyone's fault; the pattern of incidents plainly is someone's fault; and rather than these two facts being in tension, they appear to reinforce one another, so that the more incidents accumulate, the less there is to attach blame to.

Third, the whole thing is asymmetric in a way I did not expect: it is easy to demonstrate the absence of a culpable agent and very difficult to demonstrate the presence of a culpable pattern, even when the pattern is statistically obvious.

So what I am after is this. Is there an established concept for a structure of distributed agency such that (i) no particular failure is anyone's fault, (ii) the recurrence of such failures is unmistakably a fault, and (iii) making (i) common knowledge entrenches (i) rather than generating pressure to assign (ii)? If a concept exists, I want to know which parts of my description it fails to capture. If the nearest available concepts are, say, the problem of many hands, moral hazard, or ordinary diffusion of responsibility, I want to know precisely where each stops and mine begins — and in particular whether any of them treats the acknowledgement of the gap as something that widens it rather than something that closes it. If nothing fits, I would rather have a coined term with a definition, an account of what does not count as an instance, and at least one tempting case that turns out not to qualify, than a bare label.
