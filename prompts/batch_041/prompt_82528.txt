i'm putting together a little session on non-constructive existence arguements -- the ones that tell you a winning strategy has to exist without telling you what it is -- and i want one case where the standard trick gives the wrong answer. the trick i mean is strategy stealing, the hex/chomp family: the game is finite, symmetric, can't be a draw, and an extra move of your own can never hurt you; so if the second player had a winning strategy the first player could make some arbitrary opening move and then just pretend to be the second player, and any collision between the pretend-strategy and the real board only hands them a spare move. conclusion: first player wins (or at minimum never loses), and nobody can tell you how.

what i want is a concrete two-player, perfect-information game -- no chance, no hidden info, playable on paper -- where that argument looks airtight at every step and yet the verdict it gives is false. second player wins, or the game is a draw, either is fine, but the actual result has to be one you can spell out move by move, not "somewhere down the tree something goes wrong." keep it small enough that you can actually exhibit the winning or drawing strategy in full rather than gesturing at a search.

constraints on the game: (a) it has to be symmetric -- both players have the same moves available, the only asymmetry is who moves first, no first-player-only rules; (b) if you make it a second-player win, it can't also be a draw, and vice versa; (c) no chance, no infinite play, and don't bolt on a "you may pass" move just to break the argument; (d) the argument has to fail because one of its premises is actually false in an interesting way, not because i handed you a game where "symmetric" or "no draws" quietly doesn't hold.

then walk me through it: (1) the rules in a few lines; (2) the strategy-stealing argument as someone would actually write it for this game, step by step; (3) which step is the lie, plus the second player's (or the drawing player's) real strategy; (4) which single feature of the game is doing the work, and what happens if you delete that feature -- does the game flip to a first-player win, or does the argument just become inapplicable; (5) the best defence of the trick, i.e. how would someone who thinks strategy stealing is basically sound say i rigged this, and is that defence fair.

one more thing: i'd rather you didn't land on misere Nim or "whoever moves last loses" as the punchline, those are the ones everyone has seen. if your case turns out to be a parity or misere thing in disguise, say so up front and let me judge whether it still counts. and be honest at the end about where you're least sure -- if you had to bet on one step of your own analysis being wrong, which one would it be, and what would change your verdict (a different reading of the rules, a strategy you missed, or me having built in a draw without noticing).
