For years I have been trying to settle a disagreement with a friend about what, exactly, makes an echo chamber an echo chamber, and I have finally concluded that the thing needs to be staged as a dialogue rather than resolved by fiat. Her position is structural. On her account the defining feature is self-sealing trust: members of the community decide which sources are credible by consulting the very beliefs that are in dispute, so outside testimony is discounted in advance and no observation is permitted to count against the consensus. The circularity, she says, is the whole phenomenon, and it belongs to the community rather than to any individual member's inference. My position is deflationary. Strip away the sociology, I say, and what remains is ordinary Bayesian updating on a selected evidence stream. Testimony from sources that share a common origin is not independent evidence; an agent who treats it as such will drift; and nothing in that drift requires the self-sealing story. On my view the phrase names the selection rather than explaining anything further, and the supposedly distinctive pathology dissolves into a familiar failure to correct for correlated evidence.

Write the dialogue between us. Give each of us the strongest version of her own case, and have us respond to each other's actual moves rather than trade monologues. Let me press her with the hermit: a man alone with a single tendentious book, correcting for nothing, appears to display every epistemic vice she names, yet there is no community to bear the structural property she insists on. Let her press me on stickiness: my account predicts that an agent who encounters direct disconfirming evidence will move, and she will want to know why echo chambers so often fail to. Neither of us should be allowed to win by caricature, and neither should be made to concede a point that a competent defender of her view would not concede. Close by naming the crux on which the dispute actually turns, and say which of us has to give up more in order to avoid it.
