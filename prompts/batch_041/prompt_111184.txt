I'm in a comparitive federalism seminar and we spent a whole three-hour session on subsidiarity last week and I still can't sleep. Someone in section laid out this arguement and the TA said "interesting" and moved on, which is the worst thing that can happen to a person:

(1) Subsidiarity says a decision should be taken at the lowest level of authority that can take it competently.
(2) Applying it to any real question requires determining which level is competent.
(3) That determination is itself a decision about who has authority.
(4) So the principle can only be applied by someone who already has the authority to allocate authority.
(5) If that someone is the lower level, it can always declare itself competent and the principle does no work.
(6) If it's the higher level, the higher level gets the last word on what belongs to it -- which is the very thing subsidiarity was supposed to prevent.
(7) So the principle is either empty or a rubber stamp for whoever holds the pen.

I don't buy (7). Our prof keeps saying the word comes from Catholic social teaching, where subsidium means help, and that the point was that bigger bodies exist to help smaller ones rather than to swallow them -- so it's about the direction of assistance, not about which level decides. And real places seem to run on this: Germany's upper house is literally made up of the state governments, Swiss constitutional amendments need a majority of the cantons as well as of the people, and I think the EU gives national parliaments a short window to object to draft laws on subsidiarity grounds, after which the Commission has to go back and look again. So lower levels do seem to get real power over the boundary without being the ones who "decide" it.

But I can't find the break. And what really bothers me is that the same shape of argument looks like it would sink any rule that divides authority at all -- federalism, enumerated powers, the whole idea of limited government -- because in every case somebody has to draw the line, and if the line-drawer controls the line then the line isn't a constraint. That conclusion is obviously too strong, so something in the chain must be wrong, but every step looked fine when he said it.

I'd rather keep my denial of (7) than (5) or (6), though if I have to give one of those up I will. So: which step goes, why did it look fine, and what does giving it up cost me? And if it isn't a single step -- if the argument is valid and the real price is that subsidiarity is weaker than I want it to be -- say that instead, and tell me exactly what I lose.
