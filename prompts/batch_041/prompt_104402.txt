I am not a philosopher or a scientist — I repair bicycles for a living and read popular science in the evenings — but for some months now a few of us have been meeting on Thursdays in the back of a café to argue about whether the "higher levels" of the world (minds, organisms, economies, and the rest) are anything over and above the physics underneath them, and we keep running aground. The picture that comes up again and again, and that a friend of mine who administers servers states most forcefully, is that the sciences are stacked like a computer: physics at the bottom as the transistors, chemistry as the logic gates, biology as the machine code, psychology and economics as the high-level programs running on top of all of it. He sharpens the point with virtual machines. My mind, he says, is a virtual machine running on the brain's hardware; and just as a virtual machine can be migrated from one physical server to another, copied, snapshotted, or run nested inside another virtual machine, so a mind does not need any particular brain, so psychology need not wait on neuroscience, and so my deciding to lift my cup moves my arm in the same way that a program drives the machine it runs on. He thinks this settles the argument: nothing at the higher level is extra, but nothing at the higher level is a mere manner of speaking either, and the reductionist's project is one nobody is obliged to finish. I have the strong feeling that the hardware and the software are doing more work in this picture than he admits, and that some of it is being done by words like running and program, but I cannot pin down what.

Could you audit the analogy for me? Begin with a table of correspondences: for each element on the computer side — the transistors, the gates, the instruction set, the operating system, the compiler or interpreter, the program, the virtual machine, the migration between servers, and the engineer who laid down the interfaces (I am probably mangling the vocabulary; these are his words, and I have picked them up from him) — say what is supposed to play that role on the mind side, and flag any place where there is simply nothing to put in the corresponding slot. Then say where the mapping breaks, and be specific about the direction of each break: is it that the mind lacks something the computer has, or that the computer has something the mind could not have? I am particularly interested in what the picture quietly imports — design and purpose, interfaces fixed by us for our convenience rather than found in nature, the fact that running the same program on different hardware is something engineers arrange rather than something they discover, and the fact that a program's power over its machine is borrowed from the physics beneath it — and in which of those imports actually matter for his conclusions. Take the conclusions one at a time: (i) minds are not tied to brains, (ii) psychology is autonomous from neuroscience, (iii) thinking genuinely causes behaviour rather than being an idle by-product of the machinery. For each, tell me whether the computer picture supports it, leaves it untouched, or was the only thing carrying it. Finally, if the analogy cannot bear the weight he puts on it, tell me whether any comparison could, and what it would have to get right — or whether this is one of those pictures that is useful for teaching and useless for settling anything.
