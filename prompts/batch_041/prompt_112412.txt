Ok, odd request, bear with me.

I'm a philosophy professor — normative ethics, mostly consequentialism and the messes it gets itself into. For about fifteen years my main thing has been what people call cluelessness: the thought that our ignorance about the consequences of what we do isn't the ordinary kind you could in principle fix by gathering more information. It's that the causal ripples of anything we do run out in every direction, forever, and we can't even put meaningful probabilities on them. Some of us think that's fatal to consequentialism; some of us think it's an occupational hazard you learn to live with. I've definately written on both sides of that at different points.

Anyway. My old high school has asked me to give the commencement address. Two hundred kids, their parents, my old chemistry teacher, in a gym, in June. The town hasn't had a good decade — the plant closed, alot of these kids are staying, the rest are leaving and feel bad about it. I said yes before I thought it through.

Here's my problem. The commencement address is a machine for making exactly the claims I've spent my career saying you can't make. Take the harder path, it'll serve you. The friends you make now will carry you for decades. Study what you love and the rest works out. Every one of those is a confident prediction about consequences I have no license to make. And I don't want to be the guy who stands up in a gym and does a knowing little routine about how nobody can know anything — that's a seminar move and it would be cruel to that room. But I also don't want to just give the normal speech and hope nobody notices, because that's dishonest in precisely the way I've been arguing against.

So: write me the speech. Take the role of someone who knows this literature cold — normative ethics, decision theory under deep uncertainty, the actual arguments about whether cluelessness is fatal or survivable — and think carefully before you write. Start with a few lines of brainstorming, for yourself and for me: what does the genre require, what does cluelessness genuinely rule out, what does it only *look* like it rules out, and where's the overlap a real speech could live in. Then write it. If you think I've got the problem wrong — that the worry bites less hard than I'm assuming, or bites somewhere I haven't noticed — say so briefly up top, before the speech.

Stuff to work in:

- I don't think cluelessness shows that consequences don't matter. It shows I can't track them. The speech has to hold that line, because it's the trap the whole thing keeps falling into — if it slides into "just be kind and it'll be fine," I've conceded the argument by accident, and I'd rather not do that in front of my old teachers.

- The real question isn't whether cluelessness is true, it's what it does to the *form* of practical advice. That's what I want the speech to work out rather than gesture at.

- Two things from my own life, flesh them out as you like. When I was twenty-three I talked a close friend out of something, with arguments I still think were right, and it went badly in a way I could not have predicted in a hundred years. And a throwaway remark I made to a stranger at a conference turned out to matter enormously to them, and I didn't find out for fifteen years. I genuinely cannot tell which of those two was the good one. Neither can you. That's not a rhetorical flourish, it's the actual situation, and I'd like the speech to sit with it for a beat.

- There's one piece of consolation I actually believe and I want it in there: if I can't know my good acts will help, nobody can know their bad ones will harm. I don't know whether that's comfort or just symmetry. Let the speech decide.

- No citations, obviously. Also no philosopher name-dropping — if a parent in the third row can't follow a sentence, it isn't working. Plain language. Jokes are fine; that room will be grateful for a laugh and suspicious of sincerity.

- Six minutes or so, 850–950 words. And please don't end with "the future is in your hands." I'd rather it ended honest and a little unresolved. Actually I'd quite like it to end unresolved.
