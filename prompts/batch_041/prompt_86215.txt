I'm a graduate student in philosophy, and I've been assigned as the respondent to a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on law and computation. The paper's author, a law professor who spent years in formal methods before moving to legal theory, argues that Gödel's incompleteness theorems sink the ideal of the rule of law. His line is that a legal system is a set of rules doing the work of axioms, that decided cases are like theorems, and that any consistent rule system rich enough to be worth having will contain questions it can neither settle nor rule out; so judicial discretion, on his account, is not a political defect to be minimized but a logical necessity. He presents this as a result rather than an analogy, and a good part of the room nodded along. A classmate told me afterward that the whole thing is just the familiar jurisprudential point about open texture dressed up in proof theory, which may be right, but it doesn't tell me what to say. I'd like to respond on the merits rather than dismissively, and I don't need a bibliography, reading list, or citations, since I'll be working from whatever the response itself gives me. Please describe the most important implications of the incompleteness theorems for arguments of this kind: what the theorems actually establish, what conditions a system must satisfy before they apply to it at all, and what would and would not follow for the rule of law if one simply granted the professor's framing. I'm particularly interested in whether a weaker but still interesting version of the analogy survives, since I'd rather concede the interesting part and press on the rest than win by announcing a category mistake.

To be clear about the answer I'm after: I don't need the standard tour of extra-mathematical Gödelianism. Take for granted that I know the first theorem concerns consistent, effectively axiomatized theories that interpret enough arithmetic to encode their own syntax, and that the second says such a theory cannot prove its own consistency. What I'm stuck on is the middle ground. Why does it matter that the Gödel sentence is true in the standard model while being unprovable in the system, and does that distinction survive translation into a legal register? Does a theory's inability to certify its own consistency bear at all on the self-certifying ambitions of institutions, as opposed to formal theories? And does anything carry over once the legal system is permitted to be inconsistent or incomplete by design, or to contain its own rules for closing gaps, allocating burdens, and defaulting in the absence of proof? Throughout, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested: the mathematical content of the theorems is not up for grabs, but whether they have any legitimate philosophical upshot outside mathematics is a live disagreement among people who know the material well, and I don't want a contested reading of that upshot handed to me as though it were the consensus. If the honest verdict is that the analogy fails but for more interesting reasons than the usual ones, say so plainly, and tell me where your own confidence runs out.
