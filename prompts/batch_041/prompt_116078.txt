I'm writing the final for my upper-level phil of mind seminar and I need one essay question on cognitive penetration, plus a model answer and a marking scheme. Problem is every question I've drafted can be answered by reciting the usual encapsulation-vs-New-Look stuff, which half the class has definately memorized already. So I've made up a case and I want the question built around it. Don't worry that it's fictional; the point is the reasoning, not the example.

The case: Dana is a wine taster who's been through a flavour-identification course. Before the course she calls a certain Chardonnay clean and pleasant. After it she reports a distinct wet-cardboard band in the mid-palate of the same wine. Fine, she got better at tasting. But here's the twist — she also now reports wet-cardboard in a control sample the course instructors know is neutral, and she does it on about a third of trials. So she's produced a new true positive and a pile of new false positives.

I want the question to force students to ask what claim is actually on the table and what would seperate the candidates. Specifically it should make them: (1) say precisely which claim about Dana's experience the training is supposed to support, and distinguish that from the claim that her judgements changed; (2) lay out at least three rival explanations of the whole pattern — I want them to get to a criterion-shift / response-bias explanation, a perceptual-learning explanation, and an attention explanation, and say why none of those is obviously the same thing as penetration; (3) say what evidence would actually discriminate, and I want them to realise that a change in reports alone can't do it, and that even a genuine sensitivity change is still ambiguous between learning and penetration unless the concept is manipulated independently of exposure; (4) say how much of the dispute turns on how you define penetration in the first place, and commit to a definition.

The question itself should NOT spell out the rival explanations or the sensitivity-vs-bias point — students have to find those. It just needs to hand them the case and tell them what to do.

Deliverables: the exam question (students get 45 minutes, no notes), a model answer, and a marking scheme of five must-hit points worth 4 marks each out of 20, with a one-line note per point on how students typically blow it. Keep the whole thing under 850 words, the question itself no more than 80 words, and don't let the model answer depend on naming particular authors — I want it to stand on the argument.
