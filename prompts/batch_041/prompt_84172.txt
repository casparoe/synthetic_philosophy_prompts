Bit of an odd one. I took early retirement from a soil science job three years ago, and since then I've somehow ended up as a trustee of a small charity in my town that hands out one-off hardship grants — a few hundred quid, no more. There's five of us on the panel. I've been trying to work out what rule we're actually running, because I dont think it's the one we say we're running, and we've got a case on Thursday I can't call.

What we've done over the last two years or so:

- Said yes to a man in his sixties who gave up his job to nurse his mother through her last year. He could have paid for a care home if he'd sold her house. He didn't want to, so he lost his income.
- Said no to a bloke who put his rent money through an online betting account. He'd been out of work a long while, no one depending on him, and we all felt grim about it, but it was a no.
- Said yes to a woman in her fifties who'd had her benefits stopped for missing a run of appointments at the jobcentre. She'd worked at the same place for eleven years before that, had a teenage son at home, and had been signed off with depression. She'd also stopped taking her tablets, which I only found out afterwards.
- Said no to a man who'd been doing cash-in-hand building work and not declaring it. He was in serious arrears and I still think about him.
- Said yes to a family of refugees with no recourse to public funds. Nobody in the house was allowed to work; the father had been a pharmacist back home and was desperate to get back to it.

Now this one: woman in her early forties, two kids at the primary school, housing element stopped because she hadn't declared that her brother was paying her sixty quid a week to do his books. Undeclared, and she knew she was meant to declare it. But she was working, badly paid and off the books, and if we say no then the kids are the ones who end up in a hostel.

What are we going to say, if we're being consistent with ourselves? Which of the five is the closest fit? I've got a feeling that at least two different rules fit everything we've done so far and that they split on her, and I'd like you to tell me which ones they are, what each one predicts about Thursday, and which one you'd bet on. If you can think of a case we should have decided differently, or a case that's likely to come up and would settle it, that'd be worth more to me than the answer itself. I spent thirty years designing controlled trials and I can't stop asking what would have happened to these people if they'd done the other thing, but you can't run that experiment on a person.

Last thing, and it's the bit I actually care about: if we grant her, what principle have we quietly adopted? If we refuse, what have we adopted then? The trust deed is Victorian, I think, and says the money is for people in need who are of good character — nobody has ever taken that literally, but its still sitting there in the drawer.
