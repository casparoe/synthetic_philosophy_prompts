I am a high school student preparing an extended essay on Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life. My philosophy teacher distributed the following summary, and I am trying to decide how much of it I can rely on. The summary says:

Nietzsche argues that history is valuable only when it serves life—where life means the plastic power of a person or culture to grow, act, and create. He distinguishes three kinds of history. Monumental history studies great monuments and cultural treasures; it inspires us by showing that greatness was possible. Antiquarian history reverently preserves the particular past we inherit; it gives a people roots and contentment. Critical history is the mature, scientific form: it puts the past on trial, judges it by present standards, and frees us from its authority. Nietzsche treats these as a progression, with critical history as the highest, because only it can clear away the dead weight of tradition and let life flourish. He contrasts all three with the unhistorical and the suprahistorical, which are not further types of history but attitudes toward time: the unhistorical is the animal's happy absorption in the present, and the suprahistorical is the view that the world is complete and all events are of equal value. The essay's target is the historical culture of nineteenth-century Germany, which Nietzsche thinks has weakened life by turning people into spectators of the past. His conclusion is that we should become as unhistorical as possible, since forgetting is the condition of action.

In class, however, my teacher also said that each of the three kinds has both a healthy and a diseased form, and that Nietzsche's real contrast is between the historical and the unhistorical as forces within a person or culture. That seems to conflict with the handout's claim that the three form a progression with critical history as the highest. Could you audit the handout for me? I would like to know which sentences are accurate, which are common simplifications, and which are distortions. What would a careful reader of Nietzsche object to, and do the handout's conclusions—especially the claim that we should become as unhistorical as possible—follow from the parts that are accurate?
