My old college put me on the gift-acceptance committee (I'm an alum, they wanted a lawyer on it), and there's a bequest on the table that's turned into a real fight. An alum who died last year left about two million for a research fellowship on whether consciousness survives death. The will is specific: the fellow has to publish a public report on the evidence every year, has to hold at least one sitting a year with reputable mediums, and the college itself is barred from ever endorsing a conclusion either way. If we decline, the money defaults to a spiritualist institute that will spend it advertising that the dead talk to us. Half the board says taking it makes us a laughing stock and that we'd be laundering superstition with a college letterhead. The other half says refusing to investigate a question because the answer might embarrass us is exactly the dogmatism a college is supposed to oppose. I have to write the memo that goes to the full board Thursday, and I need it to land on an actual recommendation rather than a tour of the debate. This has also gotten personal for me: my mum died in March and she'd been a regular at a spiritualist church for twenty years, so I no longer get to treat any of this as an abstraction.

What keeps pulling me back is Sidgwick, the Victorian utilitarian who was the first and longest-serving president of the Society for Psychical Research. He got into psychical research because he wanted evidence that we survive death, sat through years of sittings, ended up accepting telepathy, and still refused to treat mediumistic phenomena as proof of survival — two very seperate claims that the will quietly runs together. And the society was set up so that it never held a corporate opinion; members beleived all sorts of incompatible things and the point was to keep the enquiry going without the institution ever putting its name behind a verdict.

So draft the memo. Recommend accept, accept with conditions, or decline — say it plainly, with reasons a trustee who disagrees could actually argue with. Make the conditions concrete enough that a lawyer could draft them, including what the fellow may and may not publish under our name, tell me which single condition is doing the real work and what collapses if the board strips it out, and give the strongest version of the side you're rejecting rather than a straw man. Don't give me a reading list, I won't have time before Thursday.
