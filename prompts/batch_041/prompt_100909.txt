I spent thirty-eight years as an experimental ecologist before retiring, and I have since been reading my way into game theory and the philosophy of cooperation. I have a question about where the conceptual work ought to go, and I would rather hear it argued than summarized.

Here is the idea I keep returning to, stated in my own terms because it lies some distance from my training. Imagine two or more parties facing a status quo arrangement: a treaty, a division of some shared resource, or two artificial agents that must split a task. We would like to identify modifications of that arrangement that leave every party better off. The obstacle is that we seldom know the parties' real preferences; we know only that their preferences fall somewhere within a set we regard as plausible. Call a proposed modification a safe improvement if it is a Pareto improvement over the status quo not merely under the preferences we happen to assume, but under every preference profile in that set, so that a misjudgment about what someone wants cannot turn the change into a harm. A second hazard is strategic: a party who anticipates the modification may misrepresent its preferences, or simply behave differently once the change is in place, in order to convert an apparent gain into an advantage. A fully safe improvement would have to survive both the uncertainty about preferences and the incentive to game the change.

I find this framing more promising than the older habit of asking which single bargaining solution is correct, because it does not force us to settle whose conception of fairness is right before we can say anything useful. But I am not confident that instinct is sound, and I am even less confident about which parts of the resulting research program are worth the effort.

So here is what I want from you. Suppose I can support two or three people for roughly two years on purely conceptual work in this area, with no experiments and no engineering, just theory and argument. Which open questions should they take up, and in what order? I care far less about a catalogue than about the reasoning: for each question, why it is upstream of the others, whether it is tractable for people with philosophical and mathematical training rather than a laboratory, how neglected it currently is, and what developments over the next year or two should move it up or down the list. Please also tell me which questions that look central to the framing are, on reflection, less important than they appear, and why. And if you think the framing itself is confused, that safety and improvement pull in opposite directions, or that the whole notion collapses into ordinary decision theory under uncertainty, say so and defend it rather than humoring me.
