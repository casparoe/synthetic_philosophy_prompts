I'd like a short story that makes this tension concrete, but not the illustration everyone uses. The setting I have in mind is a forty-household housing cooperative in a converted cannery on a river estuary, with the annual bonfire on the third Saturday of September. Bob, the founder's grandson, holds a duplicate. The only thing to settle is who, if anyone, reads the letter aloud, so there are three live outcomes: neither of them reads it (call this neither), Alice reads it and Bob does not (alice-alone), or Bob reads it and Alice does not (bob-alone). Alice ranks bob-alone first, alice-alone second, neither last: she thinks the letter should be heard, she would rather Bob, as family, be the one to read it, she will read it herself if he won't, and letting it stay sealed is the worst result. Write the story so that it dramatizes the conflict rather than explaining it, and make sure it contains three moments: (1) the three social verdicts that this group is committed to — one following from the Pareto principle, one from Alice's authority over her own reading, one from Bob's authority over his own — which together cannot all hold; (2) a scene in which a well-meaning mediator proposes a fix, such as a coin flip, a show of hands at the meeting, or a rule that each person decides only their own part, and the story shows why the fix relocates the problem instead of dissolving it; and (3) a moment showing what would change if their preferences stopped being interfering, that is, if each stopped caring which of them reads — show concretely how the conflict evaporates.
