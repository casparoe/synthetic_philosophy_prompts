I'm a third-year PhD student building an integrated-assessment-style model of a fishery, and I've run into a methodological question I can't settle on my own. One of my central assumptions is that harvesters have common knowledge of the full stochastic process governing the stock — not that they forecast well, but that they hold the true distribution and update on it perfectly. My referee calls the assumption absurd and asks me either to justify it or to drop it. A colleague says I should simply invoke Friedman's F-twist: Friedman showed that the more unrealistic a theory's assumptions, the more significant the theory, so the very unreality of my assumption is a point in its favor, and assumptions anyway are not the sort of thing that gets tested — that is what the as-if formulation means. This strikes me as too convenient, but I can't put my finger on where it goes wrong. Concretely: suppose I have two versions of the model, one in which harvesters hold slightly biased forecasts and one in which they have perfect common knowledge, and both fit the catch data equally well; does Friedman give me any reason to prefer the second? And am I right that I need not investigate whether the common-knowledge assumption holds — say, by eliciting harvesters' beliefs — since on Friedman's view the only relevant test is predictive? A further wrinkle: my colleague distinguishes distorting an assumption toward a limiting case (a frictionless plane) from omitting a factor altogether, and claims only the former is what Friedman is defending. Is that distinction doing any work here? I'd like the structure of the argument, not a reading list.
