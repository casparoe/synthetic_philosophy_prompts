One of my students holds the following credences about two mutually exclusive propositions A and B: 0.4 for A, 0.4 for B, and 0.6 for their disjunction. She is unmoved by the objection that this is incoherent, for two reasons. First, she says, her numbers report a non-additive measure of confidence and not betting odds, and she has no interest in betting in any case. Second, even if she did bet, her willingness to accept a wager depends on what she has already accepted, so no bookmaker could assemble the combination of wagers that would trap her; and she adds, correctly, that no pair of wagers against her yields a sure profit — the trap requires three. Write an essay that reaches and defends a verdict on what, if anything, the Dutch book argument shows about her: whether her state of opinion is irrational, and if so whether the defect is epistemic or merely prudential. Identify the premises the argument actually needs (determinate betting odds; evaluation of wagers by expected monetary value; the transmissibility of acceptance from individual wagers to their combination; the equation of sure loss with irrationality) and say which of them are load-bearing and which can be weakened or dropped. Consider one reconstruction of the argument on which her two replies are decisive and one on which they are beside the point, and end with a defended verdict rather than a survey.
