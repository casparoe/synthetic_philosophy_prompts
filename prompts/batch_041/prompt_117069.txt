Slightly odd request, this one. I took early retirement from a chemistry department a few years back and have been making up for lost time on the philosophy side ever since — I'm now the sort of person who reads the Stanford Encyclopedia over breakfast and buttonholes people at parties about Leibniz. Anyway, the local adult-education people have talked me into running one of their evening sessions, sixty minutes, mixed room of about twenty: two engineers, a retired GP, a couple of schoolteachers, my neighbour who does the crossword, and nobody with a philosophy degree. My topic is the identity of indiscernibles — specifically Max Black's old paper in Mind where he asks you to imagine a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres, identical in diameter, colour, temperature and so on, sitting a fixed distance apart, and then invites you to say whether that's two things or one. I can lay out the argument myself, that isn't the problem. What I want from you is the teaching plan for the hour, not the content: what has to be in place before the question even makes sense to them, and in what order, with a note on why each piece can't be moved earlier. The obvious candidate to carry the whole thing is Black's spheres, but I'm not sure it should be — my worry is that everyone's first move is going to be well obviously they're in different places, and I can't tell whether that's the audience spotting the crux or the audience walking straight past it. If you think a different case should carry the hour, tell me what it has to have and what it must not have.

Two things I've been mulling that you should factor in. First, I spent a week convinced this was all obviously true until I read that if you count being identical with sphere A as a property then the principle becomes trivially true or trivially false depending on how you read it, and I've been going back and forth on whether to let the room anywhere near that or whether it'll just collapse the session into semantics. Second, my own half-formed idea for a carrying example: I keep thinking about identical quantum particles, where there's arguably no fact of the matter about which one is which and swapping two of them isn't a different physical situation — that feels like a much more honest version of what Black was after than two billiard balls in an empty void, but I may be importing physics where it doesn't belong, and I'd like your honest read on whether that's a genuine teaching asset or a rabbit hole that will eat fifteen minutes. Also, and this may sound trivial: the word indiscernible sounds to me like it's about what we can tell apart, and I'm half expecting the whole room to hear it that way.

So: the running order with a one-line justification for each step, the example to build it around and why that one, the misconceptions you'd expect from exactly this crowd in the order they'll actually surface and the precise point in the sequence where I head each one off, one question I can put to the room near the end that would show me whether they've got the principle rather than just enjoyed the puzzle, and finally the bits I should openly announce I'm skipping at this level (I'm assuming haecceitism and the whole weak-versus-strong taxonomy, but tell me what else, and where saying so out loud will save me). I'd rather leave the hour with three ideas properly lodged than six half-understood, so be ruthless about what to cut. And please don't just hand me an outline of the argument — I've read the argument, I need the plan for how to loose them into it.
