I'm writing a seminar paper on truthlikeness and I keep circling a question I can't reduce to something I already know the answer to. The proposition I want a number on is this: it is possible to give a defensible formal definition of verisimilitude — a rule that assigns a verdict of closer-to-the-truth-than between any two theories, or between a theory and the truth — that satisfies all three of the following at once: (a) it delivers the verdicts pre-theoretic judgment demands in the standard cases, so that a theory which gets the broad structure right but a detail wrong comes out closer to the truth than one that gets the structure wrong, and a theory that says more true things without saying more false ones comes out closer than one that says less; (b) it is not trivial — it does not collapse into every false theory being exactly as close to the truth as every other; and (c) its verdicts are stable under changes to the language that don't change what the theories actually say, so that adding a predicate no theory uses, or re-parsing the same content in a different vocabulary, does not move the verdict. My own credence is about 0.35. A friend in the seminar, who thinks the whole program died with Popper's content-based account and the trivialization objection — roughly, that any false theory which adds true content also adds false content, so no false theory ever comes out ahead — sits near 0.1, and a third friend who works on the likeness or possible-worlds approach says 0.8. I want your own number, but I care far more about the derivation, so please decompose it: how likely is it that there is a single coherent pre-theoretic target here at all, as opposed to our intuitive closeness judgments being mutually inconsistent once you probe them; how likely is it that the content-based approach can be repaired after the trivialization objection; how likely is it that a likeness-based approach avoids trading one problem for another, say by needing arbitrary weights on respects of similarity or by depending on a privileged partition of logical space; and how likely is it that the question is well-posed to begin with, given that closeness may not be determinate without a fixed set of questions we care about. Give each sub-question a rough probability and one line of justification, then show how they combine into your number.

One further thing I'd like you to price in explicitly, since it's the part I'm least sure how to place. I've been toying with a repair I haven't seen stated in quite this form: instead of identifying a theory's content with its full classical consequence class, identify it only with those consequences that are expressible in the theory's own explanatory vocabulary, so that consequences inherited from the background language — logical truths, idle predicates, accidental generalizations the theory never leans on — drop out of the comparison. Tell me how much credence you'd put on a move of that kind rescuing the content-based program, and how much of your overall number rides on it rather than on the other terms. Then tell me which single sub-question your final figure is most sensitive to, and what argument or worked example would move you most in either direction — I'd rather see where your reasoning is likeliest to be wrong than a survey of positions. Please write in American English.
