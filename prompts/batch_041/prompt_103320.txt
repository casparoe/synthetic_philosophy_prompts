Okay so my two housemates Marcus and Priya had a big blow-up at the kitchen table last night about our friend Dev, and I was sitting right there and honestly still can't tell who won. Here's the whole thing.

Dev gave away a big chunk of his savings to a legal aid fund for asylum seekers. He's also totally open that one reason he did it is that he's running for council next year and it looks great, and that he enjoys being the guy who did it. He insists he'd have done it anyway. But last year the same fund asked him to give anonymously and he said he couldn't swing it, and this year he suddenly can. Also, last month he quietly helped an elderly neighbor he can't stand move furniture and told literally no one.

Marcus says the donation has no moral worth. His line: an act only has moral worth when it's done from duty, for its own sake, and not for any payoff. Dev's payoff is the reputation and the seat. Strip that away and the evidence says he doesn't give. So the vanity is the motive actually moving him, and the caring-about-refugees bit is window dressing. Nice outcome, zero moral worth.

Priya says that's way too harsh and actually confused. Moral worth is about the quality of your will — whether you're responsive to the right reasons. Dev is responsive; he does care, he gets why it matters. The presence of a selfish motive in the mix doesn't erase the good one. Basically nobody acts from one pure motive. Demand purity and the concept is useless, because no act ever qualifies.

Marcus: I'm not demanding purity, I'm asking which motive is doing the work. Having a good reason and acting for it are different things. If his concern were doing the work, removing the fame wouldn't change anything — but it does. So it isn't what he's acting for.

Priya: that whole remove-the-fame-and-see test is the problem. You can't subtract one motive from a person and see what the remainder does, like motives are seperate vectors. His ambition and his care are fused — part of what he wants is to be the kind of person who does this, and that wanting is itself shaped by how much he values the cause. And why should worth turn on which motive would've been sufficient counterfactually? What matters is whether what he actually did expressed a good will. It did.

Marcus: now you're the one dodging. How does his will get expressed in the act if it isn't what produced the act? You're smuggling in the outcome. If all you mean is the act was good and he doesn't disown it, you've just said good acts have moral worth — which is exactly what Kant was arguing against.

Priya: no, a good outcome with a cruel will has no worth, I agree with that. The worth comes from the will, and his will really does include a commitment to the cause — it shows up in more than this one act. You're cherry-picking one act as all the evidence, but a person's will is a standing thing, not a one-off.

Marcus: standing thing cuts both ways — a will that reliably shows up only when there's a reward is a will that answers to rewards. Look at the anonymous ask.

Priya: that shows his wallet wasn't ready, not his will. And notice you get to use one act as evidence when it helps you and I don't when it helps me.

Can you go through this move by move and say who's right about what — the counterfactual test, the fusion point, the standing-will point, the anonymous ask, all of it — and then who's more right overall? And is there something both of them are missing about what moral worth is even supposed to attach to — the act, the person, or the motive?
