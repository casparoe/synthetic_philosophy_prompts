I'm a graduate student in philosophy, and I've been trying to sort out my intuitions about rational choice in one-shot situations. I have a list of verdicts that each seem obvious to me when I consider them alone, but a friend in my decision theory reading group says they can't all be right. I'd like you to tell me what principle, or small set of principles, captures them, what that principle says about a case I haven't listed, and which of my verdicts I would have to give up if I want a consistent view.

Here are my verdicts. In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma where defection pays more than cooperation no matter what the other player does, but mutual cooperation is better than mutual defection, and where the other player is a perfect duplicate of me -- same brain state, same reasoning, so that whatever I choose they choose -- I think I should cooperate. In the same payoff matrix against a random stranger on the street, where I have no reason to think their choice is correlated with mine, I think I should defect. In a case where a predictor who has been reliable in the past presents two boxes, and I can either take both boxes or take only the opaque one, and the predictor has already put money in the opaque box based on their prediction of my choice, I think I should take only the opaque box. But in a case where a person has a gene that causes both a desire to smoke and lung cancer, and smoking does not cause cancer in them, I think they should smoke if they enjoy it, even though smoking is evidence that they have the gene. In a lottery where a prize is offered to everyone who enters, but the more people who enter, the smaller each entrant's chance, and if the number of entrants exceeds a threshold no one wins, I think I should not enter, and I think this is true even if I know everyone else is reasoning the same way. Finally, in a game with ten people who each independently decide whether to contribute one dollar to a public good, where if at least seven contribute everyone gets two dollars, and if fewer than seven contribute the contributors lose their dollar and the non-contributors get nothing, I think contributing is rational if I believe at least six others will contribute, but if I believe fewer than six will, I think not contributing is rational. Yet I also think that if everyone is rational and knows everyone is rational, then everyone should contribute, even though no single contribution changes whether the threshold is met.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and what does the resulting principle predict for a case I haven't considered? I'm particularly interested in whether the principle is a version of evidential decision theory, causal decision theory, or something else, and I'd like the principle stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, its verdicts on further cases, and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Please write your answer in American English.
