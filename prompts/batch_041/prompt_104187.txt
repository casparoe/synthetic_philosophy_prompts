For a long time I have been reading the rationalist blogs and the standard Bayesian literature they lean on, and the problem of old evidence is the one part of that story I have never been able to get comfortable with. The core of the puzzle, as I understand it, is this: if I formulated a hypothesis h while already in full possession of some evidence e, and e is now certain for me, then P(e) = 1 and so P(h | e) = P(h); conditionalizing on e cannot raise my credence in h at all, even though e is, by my own lights, precisely the reason I hold h. The proposed repairs are familiar to me in outline. One subtracts e from the background and recomputes; one denies that the agent was ever certain of e, treating it as merely highly probable; one asks what the agent would have believed had she not known e, or had not yet drawn out its logical consequences; one abandons the demand that confirmation consist in a rise in probability and adopts a measure that credits explanatory or coherence relations directly.

What I cannot settle is why the argument over these repairs has not converged, and I want a diagnosis rather than another survey. Is it a substantive disagreement about how a probabilistic account of belief ought to be extended to cases in which the agent's own history of learning is itself part of the phenomenon to be explained, a real gap in the formalism that nobody has closed? Is it a verbal dispute, in which one camp means by 'confirmation' the synchronic support that e lends h and the other means the diachronic change in the agent's credence, so that the two camps are answering different questions under one name? Is it a disguised disagreement about a neighboring and equally contested question, namely whether it matters that a theory was built to accommodate the data rather than to predict it, so that the old-evidence problem simply inherits the impasse of the accommodation-versus-prediction debate? Or is it a sign that the framework is being asked to do something it cannot, so that every repair purchases one intuition at the cost of another and none is strictly better than the rest? My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is the second: that the dispute is largely about what 'confirmation' is meant to be a theory of, and that the competing formal fixes are not rivals but answers to different questions. Diagnose it for me using what each side actually claims, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress, a theorem or a distinction or a change of subject, or else why no progress should be expected.
