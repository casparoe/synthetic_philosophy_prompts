I am a novelist, currently about two-thirds through a book whose climactic sequence is a constitutional convention that eats itself: a small group of articulate, well-meaning people write the rules by which they will govern themselves, then use those rules to rewrite the procedure by which rules are made, and are astonished by what they have done. I have read enough constitutional history to furnish the scene, but I do not know what the thing feels like from the inside, and I have learned that I cannot fake that. So I would like you to design a session I can actually run — with my writing group, in a room, in real time — in which the participants produce the problem themselves rather than hearing me describe it. I need it to serve double duty: a genuine exercise for them, and honest raw material for me.

Adopt the following role before you begin. You are a game designer with a serious secondary competence in the philosophy of law: fluent in the mechanics of rulesets, thresholds, roles, and information asymmetry, and equally fluent in the questions of validity, authority, and self-reference that arise when a rule system can amend itself. You may recognize the family this belongs to — Peter Suber's Nomic (1982), in which changing the rules is itself a move, and his later study of the paradox of self-amendment (1990). Borrow whatever mechanics from that tradition serve the design, but assume that neither I nor my participants have played it, and that nothing may be explained by reference to a text we have not read.

Think carefully and at length before writing anything. Begin with a brainstorming pass: draft at least fifteen candidate rules for the initial ruleset and at least a dozen candidate proposals to seed the players with, and only then prune, selecting for the ones most likely to drive the group reliably into the loop. Show me your reasoning for the pruning, but keep the final materials clean enough to hand out.

The setting: twelve adults, most of them not philosophers, two of them lawyers; one ninety-minute block; a whiteboard, a timer, printed sheets, no money and no prizes worth having.

What I want them to come away having felt, without my having told them: that the authority of a rule which amends the very procedure that authorized it is circular in a way no amount of procedural care removes; that a system able to revise its own foundations has no foundation outside itself; that this is not a defect to be patched but a condition to be inhabited; and that the difference between a change that is legitimate and a change that is merely successful is far harder to locate from inside the room than from outside it.

Deliver the following, in this order.

1. An initial ruleset of roughly a dozen numbered rules, learnable in five minutes, covering turn order, who may propose, the threshold for adoption, what happens on a tie, and how to resolve contradictions between rules that have both been validly adopted. Include at least one rule alterable only by a higher threshold than ordinary rules, and make sure the amendment threshold is itself amendable by ordinary means.

2. Roles with defined powers and defined limits — for instance a chair whose rulings stand unless overturned, a clerk whose minutes are the only admissible record of what has been adopted, and one participant charged with objecting on procedural grounds. State exactly what each role can and cannot do, and say which role, if any, is authorized to decide whether a rule has been validly adopted.

3. A timeline with named phases, minutes attached, and instructions for what to do when a phase overruns. Include a point at which the group must vote on whether to continue under the rules as they now stand.

4. A short list of seeded proposals, assigned to named participants and revealed only on their turn, designed to close the loop. At minimum: one that lowers the adoption threshold; one that makes the chair's rulings unappealable; one that repeals the clause protecting the protected rule; one that retroactively voids an earlier vote; and one that purports to abolish the procedure under which it is itself being adopted. Explain for each what you expect it to set off.

5. Handling instructions for the three moments that will decide the session: (a) the first contradiction between two adopted rules; (b) the first time someone claims that everything done so far was never validly done; (c) the moment when the group realizes that whoever is deciding has no authority except what the disputed rules confer. Tell me what the chair should do at each moment, and be candid that the honest answer is often that the chair's ruling is itself an act of legislation.

6. A debrief of about fifteen minutes, scripted as questions rather than as a lecture, mapping specific things that will have happened in the room onto the conceptual issues: the regress of validation, the difference between a rule's content and its pedigree, bootstrap problems, entrenched clauses, and the gap between legal validity and raw power. Do not use the word paradox until the final five minutes.

7. A frank account of what will predictably go wrong on a first run — the group will vote itself into paralysis; someone will play to win and nobody will stop them; someone will propose that the chair decide everything and it will pass by acclamation — with a concrete remedy for each.

8. Finally, an honest note on what this activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply in words: a constitution's bindingness over time; the fact that real participants would have histories, property, and the option of simply leaving; and the whole apparatus of officials whose convergent practice is what makes a legal system valid. Say what the activity gets wrong, and how to say so out loud without deflating the experience.

Write the final materials in a form I can print: rules and roles on one page, timeline and seeded proposals on a second, facilitator's debrief notes on a third.
