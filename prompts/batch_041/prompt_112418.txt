A friend and I spent most of a long drive arguing about whether an ant colony can have a mind, and it took us an embarrassingly long time to notice that we were disagreeing about the science when the real disagreement was philosophical. What I want to work out here is the mismatch I could not get past. In one register, the literature on social insects and the popular writing around it describe colonies in unmistakably mental terms: a colony chooses a new nest site, weighs the merits of competing cavities, allocates foragers to richer patches, remembers a route, recovers from a disturbance. A whole vocabulary of colony-level cognition and swarm intelligence has grown up around that habit. In another register, nearly everyone who uses that vocabulary will insist, if pressed, that the colony is not conscious, has no experiences, and feels nothing, that there is no one home. And then there is Hofstadter's Aunt Hillary, the fictional ant colony in the dialogues of Gödel, Escher, Bach, who is not merely a system that behaves as though it were deciding but a character with moods, opinions, and something like a personality.

What I want from you is an account of what the real practice presupposes, rather than what its practitioners say when asked. Take the practice to be the ordinary scientific and popular habit of predicating cognitive and intentional verbs of the colony as a unit, the colony decides, assesses, remembers, prefers, together with the standard disclaimer that the colony is not conscious. Reconstruct the theory of mind and agency that this practice only makes sense under, if it makes sense under any single one. Does it require functionalism about mental states, or is it equally at home with a view on which mentality is tied to a particular kind of physical substrate? Does it require that intentional states can be realized by a system with no central controller and no representation of its own states, or does the attribution of deciding smuggle in a commitment to inner representations that the individual ants plainly do not have? Does the combination of mental verbs with a denial of experience presuppose a sharp distinction between cognition and consciousness, and if so, is that distinction doing real work or papering over a contradiction?

Then separate two things I suspect keep getting conflated: what the practice strictly presupposes, in the sense that it could not be carried on without it, and what it merely fits comfortably with, in the sense that several rival theories would license exactly the same language. I want to know where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, because my suspicion is that it is underdetermined in more places than either side of our argument assumed, and that much of the apparent disagreement is about which theory is nicer to have rather than which one the practice forces on us.

Finally, treat Aunt Hillary as a test case rather than a decoration. Hofstadter's dialogue is a thought experiment about whether the colony is the mind and the ants are the neurons, and I want to know whether that inversion is a genuine possibility the science leaves open or a vivid picture that the real practice implicitly rules out. If the practice does rule it out, if the mental verbs are licensed only as shorthand for the aggregate behavior of individuals, then say so plainly and tell me what would have to change in the science's self-description if we stopped speaking that way, and what would be lost.

Please answer in American English. And because I have already been wrong once in this argument, be explicit about your own confidence and about what would change your mind: which empirical finding, or which argument in the philosophy of mind, would move you from your verdict to a different one, and where in your reasoning you think you are most likely to be wrong.
