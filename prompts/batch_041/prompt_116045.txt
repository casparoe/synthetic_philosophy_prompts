I run a small upper-level seminar on irrationality and want a decision tree that takes a student from "self-deception looks impossible" to a named position. Please build it, using the paradox rather than a list of labels as the spine.

The root is the apparent inconsistency: to deceive someone is to get them to believe what you take to be false; where deceiver and deceived are one person, the self-deceived subject seems both to take the proposition to be false and to hold it. The first nodes should be the ways of giving up exactly one ingredient of that argument — that deception must be intended, that the deceiver must disbelieve what he induces, that one subject is doing both jobs, that there is deception here at all — and each node must be phrased so a student can answer it without knowing which leaf it leads to. Include, among the branch points, a node on whether the division of mind an account needs, where it needs one, is between two quasi-agents or between sub-personal systems that differ only in what they can access.

Then place the leaves. I want the tree to be able to reach at least these, by whatever route is honest: (a) intentional self-deception requiring a genuinely divided mind; (b) an account that keeps the motivational story but drops the intention; (c) an account on which the subject never believes the unwelcome proposition but only avows, imagines, or pretends to; (d) an account on which the subject holds two conflicting beliefs; (e) a deflationary account that re-describes the cases as wishful thinking, fear, or ordinary weakness; (f) an account on which there is no such phenomenon to explain.

Use concrete cases at the nodes: the candidate who has not revised and cannot name a reason for confidence; the spouse who does not register what everyone else has noticed; the parent who will not accept a diagnosis; the hypochondriac; the jealous partner who finds confirmation everywhere; and at least one case where the subject believes something worse than the evidence supports. Some leaves will founder on that last case; say which, and why.

Eight to twelve nodes, no more than eight leaves. For every leaf, one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Note any combination of answers that no one occupies and diagnose whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate. Label leaves by the position rather than by philosophers; give a name only where you are confident of the attribution. Begin with the instrument itself, with no preface or commentary on what you are about to do.
