For the better part of a year I have been collecting a sentence. I write about science and ideas, and the sentence is this one: the passage of time is an illusion. It arrives in softer dress as well — time is just another dimension; relativity has shown that the present moment is nothing special — and it is almost always deployed not as a description of a formalism but as a conversation-ender. The instance that finally pushed me to write the piece came at a panel on time and human agency, where a cosmologist answered a philosopher's question about whether deliberation presupposes an open future by saying that physics had settled the matter: the future is already as real as the past, the sense of choosing among genuinely open possibilities is a residue of folk psychology, and his own acceptance of quantum indeterminacy changes nothing about that. He was not, I think, being careless. He was relying on a chain of reasoning whose links I want to be able to name.

What I need from you is a menu, not a verdict on the man. Set out the distinct precise claims that "the passage of time is an illusion" and its relatives could be making, ordered from the nearly vacuous to the genuinely radical, and give a verdict on each: which are trivial restatements of well-established physics, which are substantive and defensible, which are live and contested, which are false, and which are not claims at all but shifts in vocabulary. I expect the list to include, though it should not stop at: the claim that the laws are Lorentz-invariant and single out no privileged foliation of spacetime; the claim that simultaneity is frame-relative, so that "now" is indexical in something like the way "here" is; the claim, made by the argument that runs from frame-relative simultaneity to the conclusion that all events exist on a par, that past, present and future are ontologically on a level; eternalism as a metaphysical thesis in its own right, and how it differs from presentism and from the growing-block view; the claim that temporal passage is not a feature of fundamental reality; the claim that the experience of passage is a systematic illusion of some specific, statable kind; and the claim that the whole dispute is verbal, or at least that "real" is being used in two senses inside a single paragraph. For each reading, say what would have to be true for it to hold and what it does and does not entail — in particular, whether it entails determinism, and whether it is compatible with an indeterministic world.

Then the part I actually need for the piece. Which of these readings, if any, licenses the cosmologist's conclusion that deliberation and choice are beside the point once the future is "already real"? Trace the inference from a claim about the geometry of spacetime to a claim about the efficacy of action and say where, if anywhere, it breaks — and say plainly whether the argument he is drawing on delivers determinism or only the equal reality of times, since I gather the original presentations of it were not careful about that distinction. Finally, tell me which reading he was most likely relying on, given what he said, and whether he can consistently hold it while insisting that quantum indeterminacy leaves it untouched.

Format it as a working document I can take into a follow-up interview: a numbered list of readings, each with a one-line verdict, then a short section on the inference. I don't need a reading list or citations; I need the distinctions and where each one lands.
