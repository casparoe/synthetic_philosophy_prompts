I write about politics and social science for a general-reader magazine, and after years of covering how little voters actually know, I've pretty much talked myself into epistocracy — the idea that political competence, and not just personhood, should decide who gets a say, or how much of one. I know the standard hits: who watches the watchmen, the demographic objection, the slide into technocracy. What I want from you is the honest ledger of what would count against the view. What would have to be true, empirically or in principle, for epistocracy to be a worse bet then plain universal suffrage? Push hard on whether political competence is even measurable, whether the miracle of aggregation really rescues an ignorant electorate, whether the competence gap survives on the issues that actually matter, and whether it matters which flavor we're talking about — a restricted franchise, weighted votes, or a randomly picked citizens' body with real teeth. And I'd like the real-world track record of unelected expert bodies — central banks, constitutional courts, juries, regulatory panels — counted in, for or against. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, tell me which ones an honest epistocrat would concede themselves, and say how confident you are overall and which single point you're least sure about.
