I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind working on emotion and fiction, and I'm trying to force the standard solutions to the paradox of fiction into an actual contest rather than another survey. The stock example—grief for a character we know to be invented—is too easy to accommodate, so I've assembled a battery of cases, several of which I had to construct because the literature's usual examples don't put enough pressure on the candidate views. I want the solutions ranked against that battery rather than against the textbook statement of the problem.

The battery:

1. The stock case: a reader weeps at a character's death, knows the character never existed, and reports the weeping as grief rather than as play-acting.
2. The unengaged reader: skimming on a train, not visualizing, not entering into anything, no imaginative act she could name—and still her eyes sting at the same passage.
3. The long tail: days after finishing, she still carries a low-grade grief for the character, cannot talk herself out of it, and would call the state irrational if pressed.
4. The misclassified text: the same manuscript moves her identically when she believes it is memoir and after she learns it is invented; the intensity and character of the feeling don't shift with the belief.
5. The re-reader: knows every beat, feels no suspense whatsoever, and still weeps at the ending.
6. The pantomime case: a child shrieks at a costumed villain she has been told is a man in a suit, and five minutes later asks cheerfully to meet him.
7. Aesthetic shame: a viewer is moved by an obviously manipulative scene and is embarrassed and angry at her own response—a second-order attitude toward an emotion she does not disown as unreal.
8. The asymmetry: someone who cannot cry at a real bereavement but cries reliably at fictional ones, and finds this disturbing about herself.
9. Export: grief over a fictional death changes what she does in the real world the next morning, and she would say the grief caused it, not the pretending.

Please do the following. First, list the distinct families of solution you can genuinely distinguish, merging any that turn out to be one view under two labels and not padding the list to look comprehensive. Second, sort them into tiers by how much of the battery they handle while preserving the face-value datum that these are real emotions—grief, fear, shame—rather than disguised or pretend versions of them. For each family, give one sentence on what it explains and one on the case that costs it most, naming that case explicitly. Third, re-tier the same list under a second and deliberately stingy criterion: coverage of the battery per unit of mental machinery posited, with no special-purpose attitudes and nothing introduced solely to save the phenomenon. Fourth, tell me where the two orderings disagree, and whether each disagreement reflects a real trade-off between the two criteria or an artifact of my battery. Fifth, identify which single case does the most separating work and which case is inert—one that no view can use to discriminate among the others—and flag any case that begs the question against one family rather than testing it. Sixth, add one solution of your own that falls outside the standard families and place it honestly in both orderings, including if it lands low.

No citations, no reading list, no names and dates; I know the literature and want the reasoning. Where you suspect two positions don't really differ, say so and rank the merged version.
