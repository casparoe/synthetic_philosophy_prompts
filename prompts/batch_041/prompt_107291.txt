I'm in a grad seminar on philosophy of science and for the final I have to write something creative instead of a normal paper, and I really want it to land with the people in the room rather than being the usual "haha science is just opinion, man." Could you write me an op-ed, maybe 600-700 words, in the voice of the provost of a mid-sized university (invent a name and a school) who has just sat through a faculty lecture on the underdetermination of theory by evidence, came away genuinely convinced by it, and has drawn the worst possible conclusion from it. The deadpan premise is that since any finite pile of data is compatible with infinitely many mutually incompatible theories — and since even the same data can be re-described with gerrymandered predicates if you're willing to be annoying about it — he announces a sweeping "Theory Equity" policy: every empirically adequate theory gets equal resources, so the physics department and the astrology program get the same budget, grading in the sciences is replaced by a lottery, and nobody is allowed to call one explanation "better" than another.

The comedy has to come from him getting the actual philosophy right and *then* making the non-sequitur, so that the specialists laugh instead of wincing — he should correctly lay out empirical equivalence, the point that a hypothesis is never tested in isolation (you always bring auxiliary assumptions along, so a failed prediction doesn't even tell you which part was wrong), and the gap between finitely many observations and a universally quantified claim, and then slide into "therefore all theories are equal" as if that followed. I don't want a strawman who never understood the argument; I want someone who understands it and misuses it, which is honestly the more damning and funnier version. And here's the hard part: by the end, even while he's being ridiculous, the reader should feel the real sting of the problem — that no amount of data literally *forces* one theory, and that something beyond the evidence (simplicity, values, funding, fashion) is always already doing work inside supposedly "objective" science. So the piece shouldn't let the reader off with a smug "well obviously he's wrong"; it should leave them a little queasy about what actually does decide between theories. Keep it deadpan, no winking at the reader, no "of course this is absurd" asides, and don't name-drop or cite any real philosophers — a fake in-universe quote from the lecture he attended is fine as long as it's clearly invented. It can have a coupla typos if that makes the voice feel more real, he's a busy administrator.
