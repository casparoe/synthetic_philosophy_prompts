I'm halfway through a unit on Indian epistemology for a phil course and I keep bumping into the same wall. The way it's presented, there are four seperate basic routes to knowing something — perceiving it directly, inferring one thing from another, learning what a term applies to by being told and then matching it up with the thing itself, and just taking a trustworthy person's word for something — and the whole selling point is that these four are irreducibly different, none of them a disguised version of the others. But when I run my own gut verdicts over cases, I can't get them to fall out of any single rule, and I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, my verdicts actually encode, and which verdict I'd have to abandon to get a clean principle.

Here's what I actually think, case by case. Seeing smoke on the hill and concluding there's fire: that's knowing, and it's inference, not perception — the seeing and the knowing are two different things. Seeing a coil of rope in dim light and taking it for a snake: not knowing at all, vivid as it is. A friend who just came from the station tells me the 6:40 was cancelled and I believe her, and it really was cancelled: I know it, and this feels like a genuinely different route than either seeing or inferring — but if she's a known liar I don't know it, true or not. Someone tells me a gavaya is the animal that looks like a cow, and months later in the forest I see a cow-like animal and know it's a gavaya: I've ended up knowing something I didn't, but it feels like the telling and the seeing stacked, not a third route — pull out either and I've got nothing. Remembering that there was a fire last night: I know it, I'd bet on it, but I don't think remembering is a way of finding anything out, it's just re-reading something I already had. And Devadatta is fat and never eats by day, so he must eat at night: that hands me a fact I definately didn't have and I'd call it knowledge, but it feels like nothing but inference wearing a hat, so I don't want to give it its own category.

So the shape of my problem is this: I want the testimony case to be its own route to knowing, but the gavaya case not to be, even though both hand me knowledge I didn't have; and I want the Devadatta case to be knowledge without being a route of its own, while memory is knowledge that isn't a route either. Is there a principle that saves all of that, or do I have to give one of my verdicts up, and which one? And whatever principle you land on, what does it say about a case I haven't listed — working out that my flatmate is annoyed from the fact that he's being unusually polite? Lay it out as a numbered list of exactly five points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up.
