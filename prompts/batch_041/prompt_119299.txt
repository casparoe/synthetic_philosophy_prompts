I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on self-locating belief and anthropic reasoning. The seminar has already spent two weeks on the Sleeping Beauty problem and the Doomsday argument, and next week we turn to the dispute between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption. I want the handout to be a decision tree rather than a survey: a student should begin from a small number of commitments about what indexical uncertainty is and how to model it, and end at a named position. Please build that tree. It should have at least eight branch nodes and no more than twelve, each phrased as a question a student can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. The leaves should include the self-sampling assumption with the reference-class choice made explicit, the strong self-sampling assumption that replaces observers with observer-moments, the self-indication assumption, a full non-indexical conditioning view, a view that rejects the need for any special anthropic principle, and at least one hybrid or unstable position. I want the presumptuous philosopher case to appear as a diagnostic node rather than a leaf: the question should be whether a philosopher who has not looked out the window can be rationally required to believe the universe is large or infinite on a priori grounds, and the answers should route students differently depending on whether they treat that as a reductio or as a surprising but acceptable consequence. Please also include a node on the Doomsday argument: whether the argument is sound, unsound but instructive, or a reductio of the principle that generates it.

To keep the handout from becoming a glossary, I have invented a new case for the seminar and would like the tree to use it at least once. The case is called the Archivist's Coin. An archivist flips a fair coin. On heads, she creates exactly one simulated observer, with a full set of memories of having been a philosopher who has just been told the setup. On tails, she creates a million such observers, all with identical memories, and none of them can see or communicate with the others. You are one of the observers and you know all of this. The archivist then asks your credence that the coin landed heads. Please make the tree ask what a student thinks the right answer is, and make the branches distinguish between answers that depend on a reference class, answers that depend on a principle of indifference over possible observers, and answers that deny there is a fact of the matter. Also include a variant in which the archivist tells you that on tails she will flip a second coin and, if it lands tails again, create a further million observers; the question is whether your credence in the first coin's landing heads should change when you learn this second-stage rule.

Finally, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and for each unoccupied combination of answers, say whether you think it is unoccupied because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it is merely unexplored. The handout should fit on two sides of a page, so keep each node and leaf to a sentence or two. Do not include a reading list or citations; I want the tree to stand on its own.
