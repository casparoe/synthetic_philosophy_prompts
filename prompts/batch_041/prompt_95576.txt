I'm a novelist, and for the past four years I've also been reading submissions and voting on what goes in a tiny literary magazine that my friend Nell runs out of the back of a bookshop. Nell has the last word. I need you to tell me what she's going to do with the piece we vote on Tuesday, because I'm about to spend alot of goodwill on this and I'd like to know if the fight is even winnable.

Her track record, roughly in order. Ignore her stated reasons, they're useless — she says whatever sounds right in the moment and they contradict each other (she killed one piece saying "its a permanent record, it'll be there when their kid googles them in fifteen years," then ran something worse two months later and never mentioned it again). Just the rulings:

1. Killed a first-person nonfiction piece by a local guy about how he tracked his ex-girlfriend for a year after she left him. Named her, small town, she'd already moved twice, and she went quiet online before we'd even decided.
2. Ran a long, vicious essay arguing that a well-known local spiritual teacher is a fraud who fleeced his students. One of our two advertisers pulled out and Nell ran it anyway, with a note in the margin: "he can write a reply."
3. Killed a reported piece saying a specific named shopkeeper was laundering money for a trafficking ring. The reporting turned out to be garbage, but she killed it before anyone checked.
4. Ran a nine-page story written from inside the head of a man abusing his daughter. A volunteer quit over it. Her margin note: "it's a story, nobody in it exists."
5. Ran a piece, with documents, arguing that the town's beloved late philanthropist was a bully and a fraud. Half the town still isn't speaking to us.
6. Killed a piece that named a local teenager as the driver in a fatal crash, before any charges were filed.

Notice what's missing from all six: nothing that goes after a whole category of people for what they are. Which is why Tuesday is a problem.

Tuesday's piece is a 4,000-word comic essay by a writer I know from a residency. It's genuinely funny — I laughed twice and then felt sick. It's one long riff on how disgusting fat people are: how they move, how they smell, the noises they make, the clothes. No argument, no statistics, no slur, no names, nobody identifiable, no health claims, no call for anything. It just holds a whole category of people up as the joke. The writer offered to cut the last two pages, but the rest is the same. Nobody who reads us is going to go do anything to anybody over it. Nell hasn't seen it yet.

So: does she run it, run it cut, or kill it? What rule is she actually running — my friend who teaches philosophy says it's just Mill's harm principle, but he says it like that settles it, and I dont think it does. If more than one rule fits her six rulings, tell me what each one predicts about Tuesday, which you'd bet on, and why. And tell me what we'd have to see to know for sure which rule she's on — some case that would push the rules apart in the other direction. Also tell me whether any of her six rulings is actually the same shape as this one, because I can't find one and I've been looking.
