I recently read a short popular book on Adam Smith — the sort written by a journalist rather than a specialist — and I would like to know how much of its account of his moral philosophy I can trust. I have no background in the area, so I cannot tell where the seams are. Here is the gist of its central chapter, in my own paraphrase:

"Smith is remembered as the father of capitalism, the man who explained that we are all driven by self-interest. His moral philosophy is the flip side of that: precisely because we are self-interested, he has to explain where morality comes from, and his answer is sympathy. Sympathy, for Smith, is our capacity to feel pity when we see someone suffering — we catch the other person's emotion the way a yawn is contagious. Everything in his ethics is built on this one mechanism. The central device is the impartial spectator: the internalized voice of the community, the conscience you acquire by growing up among other people, which tells you whether your conduct would be approved of. To be moral, on this account, is to conform to the standards of your society, and the 'impartial' part is simply the requirement that you judge yourself by the same standards everyone else applies to you. Smith is therefore a sentimentalist in the strict sense: for him moral judgment is a matter of feeling, and reason has no role in it at all. This is also why his two famous books fit together — the self-interest of The Wealth of Nations is tamed by the sympathy of The Theory of Moral Sentiments."

My instinct is that the self-interest part is obviously right and that the sympathy material is a warm addition meant to soften it. But a friend who took a seminar on Smith laughed at the whole passage and then declined to explain why, which is what sent me here.

Please audit the summary claim by claim. For each claim, tell me what a specialist in eighteenth-century moral philosophy would accept as accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what is a genuine misrepresentation — and in the last case, be explicit about which of Smith's own distinctions the summary is blurring or collapsing. I would especially like to understand two things. First, whether the picture of morality as conformity to the standards of one's community is something Smith actually held, or something his account is designed to resist; if it is the latter, explain what in his theory does the resisting, and how it bears on the case of someone whose community's standards are cruel or unjust. Second, whether calling him a sentimentalist, in anything like the summary's sense, commits him to the view that reason plays no role in ethics. Finally, suppose the individual errors are corrected: does the summary's overall conclusion — that Smith's ethics comes down to social conformity plus a contagious pity — survive the corrections, or does it depend on the misreadings?
