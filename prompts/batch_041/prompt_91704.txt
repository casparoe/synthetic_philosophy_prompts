You are a monetary economist and historian of economic thought with close knowledge of George Berkeley's The Querist (first published 1735) and of the modern literature on complementary currencies, currency boards, and the monetization of debt. Think carefully before answering: in particular, consider more than one way of translating Berkeley's account of money into operational tests, and choose the one that survives contact with a real institution rather than the one that sounds best in a seminar. Begin your answer with its bottom line in a single sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my question.

I work in the research unit of a small central bank. Our board keeps receiving proposals for monetary reform — usually from outside economists, sometimes from politicians, occasionally from a well-funded enthusiast. They arrive as one-page pitches: back the currency fully with gold; replace notes with a purely digital token; let a depressed province issue its own local currency; index the unit to a basket of commodities; create a state credit bank that lends at zero. We have roughly two weeks per proposal and no authority to change the law. Our job is to tell the board whether a proposal deserves serious study, and to say which parts of it are technical questions and which are political ones. I want a procedure, not a verdict on any single proposal.

Berkeley's position, as I read him, runs roughly like this. Wealth is not bullion but the produce of land and labour. Money is useful only insofar as it stirs up industry and lets people share in one another's labour. The true idea of money is that of a ticket or counter, so its metallic content is incidental, and the denominations could in principle be retained even if the bullion were gone. What matters is circulation, so a small quantity of money moving quickly can do the work of a larger quantity moving slowly. And the creation of new wants, the framing of fashion and taste, and the extension of credit all belong to the same system as coin. Treat this as a working doctrine, not as a slogan.

Please produce the following.

1. A decision procedure we can run on any proposal, in ordered steps: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which answers stop the procedure with a no, and which questions must be handed to the board as matters of value rather than of fact. It should be runnable in two weeks by two people with graduate training in economics but no specialism in monetary history. It should state what evidence each step requires and how much of that evidence we could realistically obtain.

2. An explicit account of which parts of Berkeley's doctrine you had to drop, bend, or simplify to make the procedure usable, and why — including any point at which the procedure will misfire or can be gamed by a clever proposer.

3. Two worked applications. First, a proposal to back the currency fully with gold. Second, a proposal to let a depressed province issue a local complementary currency redeemable in national currency at a fixed rate. At least one of the two must come out in a way I will not like; say plainly which one and why.

4. A short list of the questions the procedure cannot settle, with a note on what kind of person or body should settle them.

Do not cite literature or give me a reading list; I need the procedure itself, written so that a colleague who has never read Berkeley could apply it.
