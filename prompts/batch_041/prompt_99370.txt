I am a doctoral candidate in comparative politics, and I am finalizing a cross-national survey module on corruption before we pilot it. My advisor and I are deadlocked over how to order the items, and I would like an outside judgment to break the tie.

The module shows respondents a short scenario and asks whether the behavior described is corruption. The scenarios were drafted by a team working from the familiar gloss that corruption is the abuse of entrusted power for private gain, but several of them sit exactly on the seam where that gloss and a more culturally situated reading of obligation, gift, and patronage pull apart. I need a defensible ordering before we field anything.

Please rank the eight scenarios below by how strongly each supports the verdict that the behavior is corruption, from strongest to weakest. For each, give a two- or three-sentence justification. Mark any scenario whose placement would flip if one adopted the other defensible reading of corruption, and say briefly what the flip depends on. Where you think a behavior is better classified as a distinct phenomenon, such as clientelism or gift exchange, rather than as corruption at all, say so and say why the distinction matters for coding.

1. A mid-level official accepts a large cash payment from a contractor in exchange for steering a tender to that contractor.
2. A civil servant hires her nephew for a junior post. He meets the qualifications but is not the strongest applicant.
3. A customs officer accepts a small payment to release a shipment on the normal schedule rather than holding it for a spurious inspection.
4. A village head directs development funds toward families of his own lineage, consistent with a local expectation that leaders distribute resources to kin.
5. A bureaucrat directs a contract to a firm owned by his wife's relatives, discloses the relationship, recuses himself from the scoring committee, but remains the signatory on the award.
6. A diplomat accepts valuable gifts from a foreign government; protocol treats such gifts as state property, but he keeps them for personal use.
7. A party official channels state scholarships to members of the party's youth wing as a reward for campaign work.
8. A regulator accepts nothing of value, but holds open the prospect of a lucrative private-sector position with a firm he oversees, in exchange for favorable treatment later.

After the ranking, give me a single number between 0 and 1: your estimate of the fraction of these eight scenarios that would receive the same verdict from a panel of experienced civil servants drawn from, say, Denmark, Nigeria, and China. Then tell me which single scenario is doing the most work in the disagreement between my advisor and me, and why.
