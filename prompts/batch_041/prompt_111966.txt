I keep getting two views tangled up in my head and I'd like you to untangle them, then tell me which one I should actually believe.

First: epistemic contextualism. 'Knows' is context-sensitive — the standards a speaker is operating with shift with the conversation, so the same true, well-justified belief counts as knowledge in a low-stakes chat and not in a high-stakes one, and the shift lives in the word 'knows' as used by whoever's doing the attributing. Second: pragmatic encroachment (the subject-sensitive kind). The practical stakes of the person holding the belief are part of what determines whether they know at all — the shift lives in the knowledge relation itself, not in the semantics of the verb. I can see why people conflate them: both are stakes-sensitive, both handle the bank-style cases without collapsing into skepticism, both leave the classical core of knowledge (truth, belief, some justification-ish thing) alone. But they're obviously not the same view and I want the real fault lines, not a table of slogans.

So:

1. Are they ever distinguishable by their predictions rather than by their packaging? Give me at least one concrete case where they come apart — same facts, different verdicts — and not just two different explanations of the same verdict. If you think they never come apart in verdicts, say so and defend it.

2. Here's the case that's been bugging me. A doctor is checking dosages with two nurses, standing side by side in one conversation. Nurse A is checking a dose for a healthy adult; Nurse B is checking the same dose for a toddler with a known allergy. Both have exactly the same evidence — same chart, same scale, same everything — and each knows about the other's patient. The doctor asks, of each one, 'does she know that dose is right?' As I understand contextualism, there's one standard in play in that conversation, so the doctor has to give the same answer about both nurses, which sounds flat wrong. Encroachment gives different answers for the two, which sounds right, but then it seems to make how much someone knows turn on something their evidence is identical over, and to make the two nurses epistemically unequal even though they were equally careful with identical evidence. Which view handles this better? Does either need a patch, and what's the patch?

3. Is part of the disagreement about whether stakes can be a reason for the belief — an epistemic reason in the same sense the evidence is — as opposed to just a factor that raises or lowers how good the belief has to be? I suspect that's where the real difference is buried, but I can't dig it out. Say whether that's the right way to cut it, and what follows either way.

4. Could you hold both at once, or do they genuinely exclude each other? And is one of them just the other with the shifting part relocated (word-level versus relation-level), such that we should be indifferent between them?

Then give me your verdict on which is more plausible, with an argument, not a survey. I've definately never found the 'the word quietly changes meaning' story convincing when the same person's standards seem to shift mid-thought, but its hard to square that with the two-nurse case either. No reading list please — I'm not looking for who said what, I want the comparison and a defended verdict.
