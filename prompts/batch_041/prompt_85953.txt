My mate Dan is an economist and we had a proper row in the pub on Friday about whether precaution is just cowardice with a lab coat on, so I want to get this straight.

Heres what kicked it off. I work in chemical risk assessment. What we actually do is take the highest dose at which nothing bad happened in the animal study and divide it by an uncertainty factor -- ten for the jump from rats to people, ten again for the fact that some people are more sensitive than others, so usually a hundred, and more when the data are thin. That number isn't a probability of anything, its just a shove in the safe direction. My brother does structural engineering and says it's the same on his side: the number the code hands you isn't a probability of the bridge falling down, it's a factor, and you size the thing to carry several times the load you expect. But the same regulator that gives us the uncertainty factor will then do a cost-benefit analysis of the rule with expected values, monetised statistical lives and discount rates -- full Bayesian kit, no factors anywhere.

So what does that split presuppose? Don't just tell me it's inconsistent, it might not be. I want the implicit theory of rationality under uncertainty that the uncertainty-factor half only makes sense under, and whether anyone would actually defend it. Does it need the view that when you can't assign probabilities you should reason differently -- pick the option whose worst case is least bad, say, or take the worst case across a set of plausible distributions? Or is it just expected utility with an enormous risk aversion bolted on? Does it presuppose that the Ellsberg preference, the business of ducking the urn you can't count, is rational rather than a quirk of psychology? Dan says Ellsberg's urn shows we're simply bad at probability, same family as the Allais paradox, and we should put numbers on things and get on with it. If he's right, does the uncertainty factor lose its justification? And if we replaced it with an explicit probability-of-harm target, what would really change -- and would anything be lost that the crude factor was quietly capturing? I'd also like to know which rival views the practice rules out and which it leaves open, and where it's underdetermined: what it strictly needs versus what it merely fits with.

British English please, and dont send me citations or a reading list, I can't get at any of that anyway.
