I teach a 300-level seminar (war and morality, cross-listed with poli sci) — 18 students, one 90-minute block, and I want them playing a game for most of it, not listening to me. Here's the thing I keep failing to land on the whiteboard: a threat you'd never actually carry out is worth nothing, and the way you make it worth something is to hand part of the decision to something outside your own control — a dice roll, a subordinate with standing orders, a deadline that runs out on its own. The nasty part is that this works, so both sides do it, and then neither can dial it back without losing, and the danger ends up manufactured jointly by two parties who would each prefer less of it.

I want a game where they discover, mid-play, that they'd rather not be in control of their own decision. Then I name the thing afterward.

What I need from you:

Rules and materials for something two teams can play in about 20 minutes and then replay faster with a twist in it, plus a full timeline for the 90 minutes (setup, play, replay, debrief). Keep it to arithmetic a first-year can do in their head.

The top of the escalation ladder has to be genuinely dumb to choose on its own, in plain expected-value terms, so that "we'll go all the way" reads as an obvious bluff. Do the arithmetic for me and put the numbers in the rules — if pushing the button isn't EV-negative at the last rung, the whole exercise is fake and I'd rather know that now.

Some private information that only leaks through behavior — what each side actually loses if it goes bad, how much pain they can eat — so resolve is something you infer and bluff about rather than read off a scoreboard.

At least one mechanic where the strong move is to give up control: a tripwire, a delegated trigger, an automatic-retaliation rule, a fuse you can't put out. It should be visible to everyone when it's played, and playing it should make things more dangerous for both sides while improving the position of whoever played it. That's the moment I'm after. If there's a way to make them feel it twice — once as the one who plays it and gains, once as the one who has to live with somebody else's — do that.

No real money. Candy, or the losers write up the reading next week, something small but real enough that they care.

Then the debrief: map specific moments from the run onto the ideas — where the credibility gap showed up, where somebody chose to lose control on purpose, where the shared risk got manufactured, why neither side could unilaterally turn the dial back down even when both wanted to. If this whole cluster has a standard name, tell me what to call it so I can say it out loud at the end and point them at it.

Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it. My guess is they race to the top, someone gets blown up, and it's over in five minutes with nobody having learned alot, or they treat the danger as background noise and ignore it. What do I do when that happens?

And be straight with me about what the game can't do. I already suspect that representing the catastrophe as a big negative score is a cheat — in the real case there's no afterward in which the score matters — so dont paper over that. Same for anything else it flattens.

Two more small things. First, if there's a cheap way to tack on a second phase showing that the standoff at the top is exactly what makes small fights lower down the ladder feel safe to start, I want it — that connection is the part my students never see coming. Second, last year a student told me my chicken game taught that the correct play is to be the craziest person in the room. If this one teaches that, say so.

Write it in American English, and write it so I can hand it to a TA who's never taught this before.
