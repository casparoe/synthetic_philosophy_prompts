A friend at work and I keep getting into this over lunch, and we've now got an arguement I'm pretty sure proves too much, but neither of us can find the bad step. Here it is.

(1) Something is a real category only if its members share some feature in common, over and above the fact that we've decided to lump them together.
(2) In a socially constructed category, members are members only because we treat them as members — there's no shared underlying feature doing the work.
(3) So socially constructed categories aren't real.
(4) Race and gender are socially constructed.
(5) So race and gender aren't real.
(6) We were both fine with that for about ten minutes, until we noticed money is socially constructed too: a piece of polymer is a tenner only because we all treat it as one.
(7) So by the same reasoning money isn't real either.
(8) But money clearly is real. My rent is real, the bank's insistence is real, and if my whole street agreed tomorrow that leaves were money, the bank still wouldn't take leaves for the mortgage.
(9) So something in (1)–(7) is broken, and I'd like to know what before I go around telling people race isn't real.

Personally I'd rather keep (8) than (1), but I want to know what I'm paying for that. Which step do I give up, why did it look so obviously fine, and what do I have to say instead about what it means for something like race or gender to be real? Also, be straight with me about which bits of this are actually settled among people who work on social ontology and which are genuinely contested — don't sell me one camp's position as the consensus. British English, please.
