I'm taking a seminar on the philosophy of logic and the instructor handed out a two-page thing she wrote herself (not published anywhere, just for us) that walks through Carroll's Achilles-and-the-Tortoise dialogue and draws some morals from it. I think I follow the setup fine -- Achilles gets the tortoise to grant that things equal to the same thing are equal to each other, and that the two sides of this triangle are equal to the same thing, and then the tortoise cheerfully grants all of that and still won't grant that the two sides are equal to each other, and when Achilles writes down as one more premise that if those two are true then the conclusion is true, the tortoise grants that too and just asks for the next one, and it goes on forever. She calls this "the premise trap," which I think is her own coinage.

It's the morals she draws that I'm not sure about. She says (a) the regress proves that the demand for a justification of deduction can never be satisfied -- there's no non-circular way to justify any inference rule, so anyone who asks why modus ponens works is asking for something provably impossible; (b) the tortoise wins, and Carroll, who was a working logician and knew exactly what he was doing, let it win on purpose; (c) since finite beings obviously can't write down infinitely many premises, what we actually do is stop somewhere and take the final step on faith, so deduction ultimately rests on a psychological habit rather than on anything rational; and (d) this is basically the same phenomenon as the incompleteness results -- a system can't prove its own rules -- and also what Kripke's Wittgenstein is getting at about rules and meaning. She says the tortoise's only real "error" is wanting a reason where there isn't one, and that this is what Ryle was on about with the difference between a conditional and an inference ticket.

Some of that sounds right and some of it sounds like she's arguing with a position nobody holds, but she's the expert and I've only read the dialogue once, years ago. Could you go through her claims one by one and tell me which ones a philosopher of logic would sign off on, which are misreadings (and what the actual diagnosis is), and which are definately just false? I'm especially suspicious of the "faith" part -- it feels like it's smuggling in a claim about what it is to accept a rule rather than a proposition, but i can't put my finger on it. And if the regress doesn't show that deduction can't be justified, what does it show?
