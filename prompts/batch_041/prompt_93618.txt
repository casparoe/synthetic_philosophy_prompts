So I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy working on the ethics of long-horizon climate policy, and I run a tiny podcast on the side. Next week's guest is an economist-turned-philosopher who's become known for a heterodox take on social discounting, and I want the episode to actually dig into her view instead of rehashing the Stern-vs-Nordhaus fight for the hundredth time.

Her position, roughly: she'll happily grant zero pure time preference, but she thinks the whole battle over the pure rate is a battle about the wrong object. A single social discount rate, she says, only makes sense for goods that are substitutable across time — consumption bundles, basically. For losses that are irreversible or non-substitutable (a species, a stable climate, a civilisation), trading them off against anything at a "rate" is a category error, so no rate should apply to them at all, not even a zero one. And she thinks the usual "we're uncertain about the future" argument for a positive rate quietly smuggles in a discount on future people's moral status rather than on the value of the goods, which she thinks is indefensible.

Can you draft me 8–10 interview questions? I want them to press on the actual content of her view, not let her just restate it, and for each one I want a follow-up that's keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. In particular I need:

- a question that tries to collapse her substitution claim into the ordinary point that losing a species is just a very large consumption-equivalent, plus the follow-up for when she resists that;
- a question about whether her view ends up lexicographic (irreversible losses always trump), and the standard objection that any lexicographic ranking lets a tiny probability of a huge irreversible loss swamp everything — with the follow-up;
- a question on the uncertainty argument and her value-vs-moral-status distinction: if uncertainty just lowers the expected value of future states, where does the extra "moral status" discount actually come in?
- one on the intergenerational vs intragenerational asymmetry — does she think her framework treats them differently, and is that asymmetry doing any honest work?
- one on declining / time-varying discount rates, and whether that's a patch or a confession that a single rate was the wrong tool;
- one on whether her view needs actual future people (person-affecting) or merely possible ones, and what that does to her zero-pure-time-preference commitment;
- and finally one about what evidence or argument would actually change her mind.

No "what got you into philosophy" fluff, and definately no questions that just ask her to explain what the Ramsey equation is — she'll do that anyway. Keep it conversational, the way I'd actually ask them on mic.
