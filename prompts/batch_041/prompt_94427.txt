So I teach a seminar — cross-listed with the MFA program, which is always an adventure — on the ethics of representing real people in fiction, and every year we get stuck on the same wall: the dead can't be consulted. We spend a couple of weeks on the usual puzzle, that if someone no longer exists it's hard to see how anything can be bad *for* them, and yet almost everyone has the intuition that you can wrong the dead — that a libelous biography wrongs its subject and not merely the surviving relatives. Then we get to the part my students actually care about, which is fiction: the novelist or the screenwriter isn't reporting, she's making, and the making requires putting words in a mouth that never said them. We've been calling it, half-jokingly, the proxy problem. When you dramatize someone who's dead, you appoint yourself their proxy — you speak for them — and the uncomfortable bit is that no proxy can be authorized by the principal, because the principal is gone. So every act of historical fiction is, in a strange sense, unauthorized representation. One student put the stakes nicely: for a person, which is worse — being forgotten, or being remembered as something you weren't? Call it the second death.

Anyway, I want to use a story in class to get at this, and I'd rather it be yours than another exerpt from something everyone's already read. So: write me a short story, 1500-2000 words, about a woman — let's say a playwright in her fifties, in a mid-sized city, present day — who has spent three years writing a stage play about a real local figure who died about a decade ago. An organizer, a doctor, a schoolteacher, whatever you like: someone whose life survives in a small archive, and who has a living daughter who has been cooperating with the playwright and has now read the draft. The daughter objects to a scene, not because it's factually false exactly, but because it's true and unflattering, and it turns her mother into a person she doesn't recognize and can't defend. Somewhere in there the play needs to turn on one concrete thing from the archive — a letter, a ledger, a photograph with someone cropped out — something that makes the ethical picture messier rather than cleaner, so that both women are right about something and neither is simply the villain. I don't want a tidy resolution, and I especially don't want the narrator adjudicating. What I want is for the reader to feel the pull of both positions: the playwright's conviction that a person is not owed flattery, that to write a saint is to write a lie, and a lie is a second death too; and the daughter's conviction that her mother is not a character, that the play will outlive every correction anyone could ever make to it, and that the dead cant sue. Let the abstract stuff surface in dialogue if it wants to, but keep it a story — scenes, weather, a kitchen table, somebody being petty about tea — not a philosophy seminar with a plot stapled on. Also: invent all the names, don't use any real person or real play, and if you're tempted to end with somebody learning a Lesson, please resist.
