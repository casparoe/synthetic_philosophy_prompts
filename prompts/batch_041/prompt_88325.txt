Bit of an odd request. I did 35 years as a crystallographer, retired, and then let a philosophy department adopt me, so I'm now sitting on the second-year "Action and Agency" paper and have to hand in one exam question. The topic I drew is Buridan's ass — donkey, two identical bales, starves.

What I want the question to go after is this. The usual telling slides two claims together that arent the same: (i) the ass has no more reason for either bale, and (ii) the ass has no reason at all to move. I don't see how you get from (i) to (ii) without smuggling in the premise that reasons are always comparative — that a reason has to pick out a winner. I also want them to seperate two cases the donkey flattens into one: options that are *equal* (as good as each other in every respect the ass cares about) and options that are *unrankable* (no common scale to weigh them on — a kid choosing between music and medicine with nothing to measure them against). And my professional hobby horse: as a crystallographer I know exact symmetry is an idealisation. Any real donkey has some tiny asymmetry, one bale a millimetre nearer, and then the tie is broken — but by a cause, not a reason. So I want the question to put a case where the choice gets settled without being decided alongside a case where the options are genuinely unrankable, and ask whether an arbitrary pick (coin flip, standing rule "when tied, take the left one") is doing justificatory work or only causal work.

Then make them say what picking is. I half-remember someone making a fuss about picking needing no reason where choosing does, and I'd like them to say whether picking counts as action at all, or whether the ass is just a badly built machine we've been dressing up as an agent.

Deliverable: the essay question itself, answerable in 50 minutes with no notes; a model answer of about 600 words; and a marking scheme out of 20 — the specific moves that separate an A from a B, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how students usually get each one wrong. They're second-years, they've had a bit of Aristotle and Hume and nothing else, so don't let the model answer lean on anything they couldn't have read.
