For thirty years I ran numerical models of a system I could never forecast more than a fortnight ahead, and I retired without ever settling whether that fact belongs to the world or only to me. I have since spent three years reading philosophy of science, and I would like to commission a poem — for a reading group that has been humouring me — that treats determinism and predictability as the separate questions they are. Not another butterfly-effect set piece; the group has already sat through two.

The form I want is a canon. Take a fixed text of six or seven short lines and give it to three voices entering at staggered intervals, so that what the reader actually receives is the interleaving: each successive line of the poem is a different voice's pass through the same words. Set it out as that interleaved sequence, with a marginal letter marking which voice owns each line, and keep the lines short enough that the overlaps are audible. The constraint is the argument, and I would like the poem to know it — nothing any voice says is chosen at the moment it is heard, and yet the sense that emerges from the collisions belongs to no voice and could not have been read off any single one of them. If the canon cannot carry that without turning into a puzzle, say so and give me the nearest form that can.

Accuracy matters to me more than beauty here, though I would like both. I would like the poem to hold, without claiming more than is true: that a deterministic system may be unpredictable in practice, and that this is not merely our ignorance of initial conditions; that indeterminacy at the small scale does not by itself deliver unpredictability at the large scale, nor freedom either — I keep meeting the contrary claim and remain unconvinced; and, as the turn the poem resolves on, that a trajectory computed from a chaotic system need not be a trajectory of that system at all, while a genuine trajectory can shadow it for a long stretch, so that the computation may be false in every particular and dependable in its statistics. That last idea is the one I have never seen in verse, and the one I care about; if it is too fine-grained to bear the weight of a closing turn, tell me rather than fudging it.

I would rather be corrected than accommodated. If I have smuggled a conflation of determinism with predictability into the commission itself, or if the canon is simply the wrong instrument for the point, tell me so in your own voice before writing anything. And when you do write, close with a short prose note on where the poem takes liberties with the physics or leans on a contested reading — I want to know which parts you had to fudge.
