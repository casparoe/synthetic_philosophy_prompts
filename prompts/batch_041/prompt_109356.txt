Some friends and I have a running argument about the paradox of voting and we never get anywhere with it. The bit everyone agrees on is the setup: your single vote only changes the outcome in the case where it breaks or makes a tie, the chance of that in any real election is astronomicaly small, so the expected payoff of voting sits below the cost of bothering, and a self-interestedly rational voter should stay home — except turnout clearly isn't zero, so either the voters are irrational or the model is broken. That's where we stall. One of us says fine, voting must be expressive — people vote to affirm who they are rather than to move the result — and that's the paradox dissolved. Another says that's a cop-out, the expressive move just relabels the behaviour it was supposed to explain. A third says the whole expected-payoff calculus is itself the reductio: a theory of rationality that implies nobody should ever vote is a theory of rationality with a problem, not voting with a problem. We've been round this five or six times and nobody budges. (For what it's worth I'm the expressive-voting sympathiser of the group, so take my framing with a pinch of salt.)

My hypothesis, and I want you to tell me whether it's the right one, is that a lot of this isn't a disagreement about voting at all but about what it is for an action to make a difference. A friend who works on causation pointed out that the pivotality calculus quietly assumes your vote matters only in the but-for sense — the outcome would have gone the other way had you not voted, which happens only in the tie. But on the other "INUS" picture, an effect has a whole cluster of conditions each of which is an insufficient but necessary part of a sufficent set, and your vote looks like one of those even when it isn't pivotal: pull it out and the actual sufficient set falls apart, even though some other set could have done the same job. If that's right, the calculus camp and the expressive camp may be running on two different theories of causal relevance and talking straight past each other, and "should I vote" may really be two questions — one about expected marginal impact, one about being a member of a set that gets the thing done — wearing one hat.

So diagnose it for me. Is this a verbal dispute about the word 'rational', a substantive dispute about what actually motivates voters (an empirical question in disguise), two different questions sharing one name, or a case where the question is just ill-posed from the start? Ground it in what each side actually says rather than giving me a survey of the literature. And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, some empirical work — or whether I should give up expecting the argument to ever resolve.
