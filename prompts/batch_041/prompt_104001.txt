I recently became my father’s caregiver as his memory began to fail, and the problem of logical omniscience has stopped feeling like a technical embarrassment for epistemic logic and started feeling like a description of something I watch happen daily. I would like you to read Christopher Nolan’s Memento through that problem, but not as a simple illustration. The film gives us Leonard Shelby, who cannot form new long-term memories and who therefore outsources his beliefs to Polaroids, notes, and tattoos. Standard epistemic logic treats an agent’s beliefs as closed under logical consequence: if you believe A and A entails B, you believe B. Real agents are not like that, and Leonard is a limit case. But I want to press a distinction that I think the usual discussion runs together: implicit closure versus access. A formal belief set can contain every consequence; a mind must be able to retrieve a consequence when it needs it, and must be able to tell which of its inscriptions are premises, which are conclusions, and which have been tampered with. Read the film as a study in that distinction. Where does Leonard’s externalized belief store genuinely model the difference between implicit and explicit knowledge, and where does the film cheat by giving him a stable world of objects and a reliable enough system of notation? Does the reverse chronology make the viewer undergo something like consequence closure, or does it merely withhold information? And does the film’s ending, where Leonard deliberately falsifies his own records, show that the problem of logical omniscience was never only about deduction but also about the will to know? I want a reading that is faithful to both the film and the logic: do not reduce the film to an example, and do not distort the problem to fit the plot. End by saying what would change your mind—which scene, which formal consideration, or which alternative interpretation would lead you to revise your verdict—and where your reading is most likely to be wrong.
