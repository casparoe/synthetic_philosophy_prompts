I spent thirty-five years as a geophysicist, most of it on seismic hazard assessment — trying to attach defensible numbers to events no living person has witnessed — and I retired four years ago into what has become a rather serious amateur study of philosophy. Through a chain of circumstances I will not bore you with, I now sit as the least qualified member of a small foundation's advisory board. The foundation can support roughly eight researchers for three years on the philosophy and theory of direct democracy — referendums, ballot initiatives, citizens' assemblies, and that family of institutions — and it can support nothing else. My task is to bring the board a reasoned agenda rather than a list of topics, and I would like your help constructing it. Which open questions in this area most deserve that money, in what order, and on what grounds? The ordering itself is the least useful part of what I am asking for; the reasoning is the product. For each item I want to know why it is upstream of the others — which further questions would become easier, or simply moot, if it were settled — how tractable it is for people who work with arguments and cases rather than with data, and how much the answer would actually change how referendums are designed, defended, or challenged. Please state the criteria you are using and say where they conflict; in my own field the important and the answerable routinely pulled in opposite directions, and the honest move was to name which one you were sacrificing.

To give you something to push against, here are the puzzles that have been occupying me, though I have no confidence they are the right ones. First, a referendum is almost always a choice between a proposed text and the status quo, with no amendment and no compromise permitted, whereas a legislature can split differences, trade across issues, and phase reforms in over time; does that structural difference make the referendum systematically worse at registering what people actually want, or is it merely a different instrument with its own logic? Second, is a referendum better understood as a jury or as a principal — as a device for reaching the correct answer by aggregating many independent judgments, or as a device for conferring authority on an answer whether or not it is correct — and does anything of practical consequence turn on which picture is right? Third, when one referendum overturns an earlier one, or binds an electorate that has not yet voted, whose will is being expressed, and does the notion of a popular will even have determinate content across time? Fourth, I cannot see what converts a narrow majority's expression of preference into an authorization to do something that nearly half the country rejects, and I suspect the answer, if there is one, has to do with the baseline: a referendum's result is measured against the status quo rather than against what the representative body would otherwise have done, so the identical vote may mean opposite things depending on a counterfactual that no one voted on. Some of these strike me as live and at least one as a pseudo-problem; tell me which is which. I would also like to know what evidence over the next two or three years would move an item up or down your ranking — a case, a constitutional court ruling, an experiment in deliberative design, a change in how a real referendum is worded. If the most important question here is one I have not mentioned, argue for it and displace something on my list; and if my whole framing is off — if the interesting questions live somewhere other than the philosophy of referendums — I would rather be told that plainly than handed a tidy catalogue.
