My flatmate's a neuroscientist and thinks panpsychism is woo, so I've spent the last few weeks trying to build a version of it that can actually be argued with. What I've ended up with is what I've been calling inheritance panpsychism, and it has two parts. The first is the standard Russellian move: the intrinsic natures of fundamental physical properties just are phenomenal — the "what it's like" isn't something the physics leaves out, it's what the physics is the structure of. The second part is my own and I'm much less sure of it: when micro-subjects get arranged into a macro-subject, the macro-subject inherits their phenomenal qualities — its experience is genuinely a blend, so if the parts were reddish and buzzy then the whole is reddish-buzzy in a way that's continuous with them — but it does not inherit their subjectivity. There's no subject-summing. The micro-subjects don't merge into the big one and they don't survive inside it either; they're used up in the production of the new one. So one subject per combination, always, and the combining relation itself isn't experiential.

What I want from you is a proper map of this thing's exposure. What would count against inheritance panpsychism? I want the observations, arguements, intuitions and conceivable results sorted by how decisive they'd be — not a generic list of the usual objections to panpsychism, but the ones that bite specifically on this variant. Which of them would a defender of my view have to conceed, and which could they bat away as begging the question? What does it actually predict that its rivals don't — plain physicalist emergence, illusionism, cosmopsychism, and the ordinary constitutive panpsychism in which the micro-subjects do survive into the whole? And be honest about whether the "used up" move is doing real work or just insulating me: if there's no fact of the matter that could distinguish inheritance panpsychism from its rivals, say so, and say whether that's a fatal problem for a view of this kind or just the price of doing metaphysics. I'd be most convinced by a case my view simply can't describe, or by an empirical result that would embarrass it — for instance, if anaesthesia turned out to abolish all reportable experience whilst leaving the micro-level processes I'm pointing at completely untouched, or if split-brain patients turned out to have exactly one stream of experience after all, which would be awkward since I'd have predicted two macro-subjects and, on my story, some rather odd accounting for the used-up micro-ones. Tell me where I'm exposed.
