I am a professor of philosophy who works on logic and the foundations of computation, and I would like your help resolving an argument I recently overheard and could not settle on my own. I will set out the exchange as a neutral bystander; I have no stake in which of the two participants turns out to be right.

At a small departmental workshop, two colleagues fell into a dispute about the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis. Their moves, as faithfully as I can reconstruct them, were these.

The first colleague (A) maintained that the thesis is not the kind of claim that can be confirmed or refuted at all, because it is a definition in disguise. Terms such as "effective procedure" and "computable" are informal; the thesis, on her reading, is the proposal to identify the informal notion with the precise one supplied by Turing machines. Once that identification is made the thesis holds by stipulation, and asking for evidence in its favor is like asking for evidence that a bachelor is unmarried. The long period in which no counterexample surfaced is therefore no evidence whatsoever, since there is no well-defined population of procedures for us to survey.

The second colleague (B) replied that this cannot be right, because the thesis is used, and must be used, as a substantive bridge. When we argue that a function we can specify only informally is computable, or — in the undecidability results — that no effective procedure could decide a given problem, we rely on the claim that our informal notion of effectiveness has been captured rather than merely renamed. A stipulation about our words could not license those conclusions. The thesis also has testable consequences: it implies that a device built from ordinary discrete parts, or a room full of people following mechanical rules, cannot compute a function that no Turing machine computes. Were we ever to meet such a device we should have to revise the thesis. So it is a hypothesis, well supported but not certain, and the absence of counterexamples is genuine if defeasible evidence.

A then objected that this testability is hollow. If someone produced a "device" that appeared to compute a non-Turing-computable function, she said, every one of us would conclude that it was not really executing an effective procedure — that some hidden finitary trick was at work, or that we had misdescribed the device — rather than abandon the thesis. A claim that we would shield from every conceivable observation by redescribing the observation is not an empirical hypothesis but a norm or a rule of description, and B has mistaken the thesis's centrality for its empirical character.

B answered that reluctance to surrender a claim is not the same as unfalsifiability, and that the parallel with definitions breaks down: we are equally reluctant to give up our most central physical beliefs, yet those are plainly empirical. A, he added, must also explain how the thesis could do its work if it were a mere definition, since a stipulation about our vocabulary cannot tell us what any physical or human procedure can and cannot do; at most she has shown the thesis to be deeply entrenched, not analytic.

I found both of them more persuasive than I had expected and could not adjudicate. Please help me.

Act as an expert in the philosophy of logic and computation, at home both in the theory of computability and in debates over the analytic and the empirical. Think this through carefully; before you commit to a verdict, brainstorm the strongest version of each position and the strongest objection to each.

Then give me a point-by-point adjudication: for each of the four moves, say who has the better of it and why, separating the parts of a move that succeed from those that fail. Tell me who is more nearly right overall, and identify what both A and B appear to be missing — in particular, whether this is really one dispute or several conflated under a single name. Open with a one- or two-sentence statement of your verdict, then give the detail.

Close by stating how confident you are in your adjudication and naming the single point about which you are least confident.

Keep the whole reply to at most 700 words.
