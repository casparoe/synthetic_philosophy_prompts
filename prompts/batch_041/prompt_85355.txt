I am a novelist. I work in close third person, the camera pressed to the back of one skull, and my native language is the language of craft: scene and summary; the beat; the turn; the legibility of a motive that is never announced; the shifting distance between narration and the mind it narrates; the difference between what a character wants and what the story needs her to want; the reader's reconstruction of intention out of behaviour. That vocabulary I can argue in. Moral philosophy I read as an amateur and remember as an amateur, which is why I am asking for help rather than for a summary.

I know Williams's attack on utilitarianism mostly through its cases: the chemist who would rather not put his skills at the service of weapons, the traveller handed the power of life and death in a village square, and the man who saves his wife from drowning and then, in Williams's telling, adds the thought that he is permitted to save her — a thought that arrives one too many. I have the cases; I do not trust my grip on what is supposed to follow from them, and I suspect the argument is doing something my craft vocabulary has no slot for.

What I want is a translation. Restate the integrity objection, and the point about the thought that comes one too many, in the vocabulary I actually use: motive and scene, what a beat can carry without statement, what a reader will infer rather than be told, what a character can be shown to be thinking when the narration refuses to enter her head, and what it means for a person to be constituted by commitments such that losing them is not a loss among losses but a kind of dissolution. Take the pieces one at a time and say, for each, whether it survives the crossing intact, arrives only with distortion, or fails to arrive at all.

Then the residue report, which is the part I care about. Which elements of Williams's position have no counterpart in craft terms? Is the remainder a defect of my vocabulary — something fiction is constitutively unable to say — or evidence that the philosophical claim was less determinate than it sounded? And does anything run the other way: something craft can render precisely that his argument cannot state? I would rather have an honest account of the gaps than a tidy correspondence, so please do not smuggle craft assumptions in as though they were his.

One concrete test, since I am mid-draft. In my novel a woman authorises a dangerous operation for her father while two other patients wait on the same surgeon, and as written the scene gives us her thought that she is allowed to choose him. By my instinct the line should be cut and the choice left to stand in the action. Does the objection predict that the line will read as cold, and is my instinct to cut it a translation of Williams's point or merely a different point that sounds like it?
