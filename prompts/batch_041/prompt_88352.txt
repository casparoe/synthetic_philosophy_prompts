I run a graduate seminar on Dennett's real-patterns framework and the three stances, and I've hit a pedagogical snag I'd like your help with. The framework, as I teach it, has two moving parts that my students keep collapsing into one: the claim that a pattern is real to the extent that it earns its keep by compressing or predicting the underlying data rather than merely re-describing it in fresh vocabulary, and the claim that we can describe the same system from the physical, design, or intentional stance, where the intentional stance licenses predictions we could never extract from the physical one. The students keep turning both into a single blunt question -- "is X real?" -- and I want an exercise that forces them to hold the two apart. So I've drawn up a list, and I'd like you to rate it.

Please sort the following items into tiers -- call them S through D, or give a 0-1 score if you prefer -- by how robustly each deserves to be called a real pattern in the compression-and-prediction sense, and for each item name the stance that is doing the explanatory work and give me a sentence or two on why it lands where it does. For each, say additionally whether the pattern would survive being described purely from the physical stance, or whether it exists only relative to the design or intentional stance; and flag any item you suspect is an interpretive artifact -- something that looks like a pattern in the sample we happened to describe but that you would expect to dissolve, or to stop compressing, out of sample. The list: (1) the trajectory of a hurricane; (2) a thermostat's "belief" that the room is too cold; (3) a chess engine's "threat assessment" of a position; (4) a bacterium's "pursuit" of a chemical gradient; (5) a corporation's "intention" to raise prices; (6) a gene's "interest" in being copied; (7) a market's "expectation" of an interest-rate cut; (8) a linear probe's claim that a language model "represents" grammaticality; (9) an immune system's "recognition" of a virus it has met before; (10) the "average household" of a macroeconomic model. Then give me your single overall ranking, and tell me which item is the hardest case and why -- I expect the seminar to fight about that one. I don't need references or reading suggestions; I want your reasoning.
