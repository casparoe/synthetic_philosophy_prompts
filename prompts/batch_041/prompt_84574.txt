I write a science-and-ideas column for a mid-sized daily — mostly "here's why everyone is suddenly talking about X" pieces with a philosopher or an economist on the phone — and next month I'm finally doing the cliodynamics one: Peter Turchin's bid to turn history into a predictive science, secular cycles, structural-demographic theory, elite overproduction, popular immiseration, state fiscal distress, the Ibn Khaldun asabiyya business, the whole kit. I've got Turchin's book with Nefedov on secular cycles and his Ages of Discord on my desk, so I'm not going in cold, but I'm a journalist, not a modeller, and I keep losing the thread of what's actually being claimed versus what's being plotted on a graph after the fact. My editor keeps needling me that the whole thing is just Asimov's Foundation, and the more I look at it the less I can tell whether he's being lazy or making the sharpest point in the office. So I'd like you to read the original Foundation books through cliodynamics as it's actually practised, not the romanticized version. Seldon's psychohistory works only on populations in the quadrillions and explicitly cannot predict individuals; the early crises at Terminus resolve on schedule with no heroes, because the players get cornered into doing the rational thing whether they like it or not; then the Mule turns up and breaks the Plan outright, which gets patched by a hidden Second Foundation that has apparently been steering all along, and by the later sequels the Plan has been retrofitted into something Seldon himself didn't know about. I don't want a plot summary or a "sci-fi predicted the future" puff piece — I want a reading that takes both the novels and the theory seriously.

Here's what I'm actually after. Where does the novel get the real assumptions of macrohistory right — the aggregation, the statistical laws, the irrelevance of individual agency, the felt need for some correction mechanism when the model drifts? Where does it cheat, in the way fiction always cheats, so that the "crises" are legible only because an author arranged them, which is suspiciously like the way a secular cycle looks obvious once you've drawn it through the data you picked? And the bit I most want to think about is reflexivity: in real life, publishing a forecast of instability can be self-fulfilling or self-undermining, and there's a cousin of this in the Lucas critique — you can't forecast a system whose rules change once people hear your forecast. Asimov seems to half-see this, since the Plan only holds because its contents are secret and because somebody is quietly enforcing the corrections. So is the novel's genuine insight that predictive macrohistory comes bundled with an unacknowledged politics of secrecy and enforcement — that the science is inseparable from who gets to know it and who gets to correct whom? And is the Mule a legitimate objection to cliodynamics (exogenous shock, black swan, no model could have seen him) or an illegitimate one (great-man history, which macrohistory claims to have already priced in)? Be faithful in both directions: don't turn the trilogy into an illustration of Turchin, and don't water down Turchin to make the books look prophetic.

Don't hedge and don't give me a both-sides tour of the options — take a definite position, commit to it, and if you think the reading distorts either the novels or the theory, say so and tell me which one you're bending to make the argument work. Skip the citations and reading list, my fact-checker will chase those down for me; argument and scenes are what I need.
