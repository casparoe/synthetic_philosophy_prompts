Right, so I'm teaching the value/normativity seminar this term and I promised the grad students a one-page where-I-stand handout for week two so they can tear it apart, and puting it together I've started to suspect the thing doesn't actually hold together. Here's the list as I wrote it out. (1) Value gets analysed in terms of fittingness: for something to be good is just for it to be fitting to value it — I'm happy to put that in the buck-passing idiom. (2) The wrong kind of reasons problem is a real objection to that analysis and not a bit of trickery, and the object-given/state-given distinction is the fix: only object-given reasons bear on whether an attitude is fitting, so the fact that a demon will burn my family unless I admire him is a reason of the wrong kind and never touches the fittingness of the admiration. (3) Reasons are univocal. There's one relation, counting in favour of, and it takes attitudes as its objects, beliefs included; I don't believe in a separate epistemic species of reason that plays by different rules. (4) Fittingness is primitive. If you ask me to say what it is for an attitude to be fitting, I'll start giving you reasons for the attitude, and I think that's answering the wrong question in the wrong direction. (5) I'm a reasons-firster about the normative generally: you understand what's good, what's required, what's fitting, by understanding the reasons there are; reasons are the atoms and the rest is built out of them. (6) And on the case itself — a demon credibly threatens my family unless I believe there's an even number of stars — I've got a reason to believe it, a state-given one, but the belief isn't fitting and it isn't epistemically rational, and I don't want to soften that by saying it's rational but not justified, or some other hedge.

A colleague read it and said it was a house of cards, which is the kind of comment that costs nothing. What I actually want: go through the list and tell me which items genuinely pull against each other, which only look like they conflict, and for the real ones how severe the tension is and what the cheapest repair is — then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and what it costs me. Rank them by severity rather than handing me six bullets saying they're all in some tension.

I'm not after a reading list or a tour of the literature, I've read it, and please don't tell me it's all just terminological, because I've had that conversation twice this month and it wasn't true either time.
