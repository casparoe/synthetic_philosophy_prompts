Exam question: Parfit's hitchhiker. You are stranded in a desert. A driver offers to take you to safety if you promise to pay $1,000 on arrival. You cannot pay now. Once you are safe, paying would leave you worse off. The driver knows that you are a self-interest theorist and that you never do what will be worse for you. He does not believe your promise and drives on. Later you are rescued by chance. In an essay, explain how this case is supposed to bear on the rationality of keeping a promise that you know will not benefit you. Address (a) whether the case shows that the act of paying is irrational or that the disposition to pay is irrational; (b) whether the driver's refusal is best modeled as a prediction about your future choice or as a constraint on your present options; (c) how the case differs from a one-off precommitment device and from Kavka's toxin puzzle; and (d) what, if anything, the case shows about the distinction between act-rationality and rule- or disposition-rationality. Take a definite position on what Parfit's example actually establishes, and on whether it succeeds.

Student answer: Parfit's hitchhiker is a classic illustration of the self-defeating nature of self-interest theory. The driver will only help you if he believes you will pay. But if you are a self-interest theorist, you know that once you are safe, paying the $1,000 will not maximize your self-interest. So you will not pay. The driver knows this, so he refuses. The example shows that self-interest theory is directly self-defeating: by following it, you end up stranded. It also shows that the act of paying is irrational. After all, if you pay, you are deliberately making yourself worse off for no reason. Parfit's point is that morality can sometimes be rational because it requires you to keep promises even when it is not in your self-interest. This is why the hitchhiker should pay: keeping the promise is the rational thing to do because it makes you trustworthy. If you had a precommitment device, you could bind yourself to pay, and then the driver would take you. The case is exactly like Kavka's toxin puzzle: in both, you must intend to do something later that will not be in your self-interest, and the intention itself is what gets you the reward. So the hitchhiker shows that act-rationality and rule-rationality come apart, and that rule-rationality wins. The driver's refusal is a prediction about your future behavior, not a constraint on your present options. Parfit concludes that self-interest theory is irrational because it cannot account for the rationality of keeping promises.
