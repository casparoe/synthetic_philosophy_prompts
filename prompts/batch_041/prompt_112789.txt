There is a slogan that circulates in political-theory conversations and is usually traced to Rawls: that in a liberal democracy one must justify political positions by reasons everyone can accept, and therefore religious arguments are out of place in politics. I have now run into two uses of it. A colleague deployed it to argue that a religiously motivated voter is being undemocratic unless she brackets her faith when she casts her ballot. A second friend used the same line to argue that any law whose only support is a religious rationale is illegitimate — even where the religious citizens backing it could have offered non-religious reasons as well, and even where the issue is one most people would classify as a matter of basic justice.

Please treat the slogan as a piece of language rather than a settled doctrine. Lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making, running from the near-truism to the sweeping thesis, and for each say whether it is trivial, a defensible substantive position, or false or at least unsupported — and then say which reading each of my two friends is most plausibly relying on. The menu of readings should separate at least the following axes:

- what exactly is constrained: the justification a law needs in order to be legitimate, the reasons an official may cite in deciding, the considerations a private citizen may act on when voting, or the reasons anyone may voice aloud in public;
- who is bound: public officials and candidates, ordinary citizens, or everyone — and whether the constraint is a legal requirement or only a moral one;
- which subject matter falls under it: all legislation, or only constitutional essentials and questions of basic justice;
- which venues it governs: the formal public political forum, or also civil society, churches, universities, and private discussion;
- what the excluded class of reasons really is: religious reasons specifically, or any comprehensive doctrine — secular philosophical and moral views included — over which reasonable people disagree;
- whether the requirement is unconditional, or whether it permits a religious argument to enter provided that something further is supplied later, and if so, what is supplied and by when;
- whether "reasons everyone can accept" is even the correct gloss on the standard, as opposed to a weaker or more idealized criterion.

For each of the two uses I described, state which reading it depends on, whether that reading is one a careful defender of the position would actually endorse, and whether the conclusion my friends drew follows from it. Where a reading turns out to be a common misreading, say what the underlying view holds in its place. I am not asking for a literature survey or for further reading; I want the disambiguation with verdicts, and a clear statement of which readings would genuinely do the work the slogan was being made to do.
