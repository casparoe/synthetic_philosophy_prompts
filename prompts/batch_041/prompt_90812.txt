I'm in my second year of a comparative philosophy course on death and the self, and we've just spent three weeks on the Zhuangzi. I'm writing a paper on a problem I can't resolve, and I keep going in circles. Our instructor framed Zhuangzi as an ancient cousin of Epicurus: death is nothing to fear because whatever dissolves at death is precisely the thing that could have been harmed, so there is no subject left over to be harmed. That reading explains a great deal, including the composure with which Zhuangzi meets his own approaching end in the dialogues with his friends. But it sits badly with the passages where death is described not as an ending but as one more phase of an unending process of transformation — the earth that bears me in bodily form, wears me out with life, eases me with old age and rests me in death; the friends who treat life and death as a single body; the account Zhuangzi gives after his wife dies, where he says he stopped wailing once he had traced her back through the stages before she had a body or a breath and saw her death as another turn of the seasons. If death ends the subject, there is nothing left to be transformed; if something is transformed, the Epicurean consolation looks beside the point. So which is it?

And then there is the skull. Zhuangzi finds a skull by the roadside, questions it about the life it must have led, pillows his head on it, and dreams that the skull tells him death has no ruler above and no subject below, no seasonal labors, and that its ease surpasses even the joy of a king; when Zhuangzi offers to have the man restored to life, the skull recoils and asks how he could possibly trade that ease for the toil of being human again. My study partner says this proves Zhuangzi thinks we survive death and are better off for it. I think that can't be right, since in the story Zhuangzi himself doesn't believe the skull and offers to bring him back, and since elsewhere he asks how we could possibly know that the dead do not regret having died, and compares our fear of death to a woman who wept when she was taken from her village and only later understood how much better her new life was. I suspect I'm importing a distinction the text does not make — that between surviving death and being annihilated by it — and that my whole question may be malformed. But I can't work out how to state the issue without it, or what Zhuangzi is actually claiming about death if it is neither of those things. Could you sort out what the text is doing here, and tell me where my framing goes wrong?
