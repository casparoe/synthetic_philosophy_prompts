I hold a view about diachronic rationality, and I want to know what would count against it. I want the map of its exposure, not a survey of the literature.

The view is this. The money-pump argument is sound, and it establishes a genuinely diachronic requirement of practical rationality rather than a curiosity about cyclical preferences. If an agent's preferences, together with her dispositions to trade, make available a sequence of transactions that she would accept one at a time — each because it strictly improves her position by her own lights at the moment of choosing — and if completing the sequence leaves her worse off by her own lights than she began, then something is defective in her preference structure. The defect is not in her information and not merely in her circumstances; and it cannot be located in any single choice, since each choice is optimal given the preferences she has then. So any theory that certifies every step and then shrugs at the whole sequence is incomplete. Rationality constrains preferences across time, and each step was optimal is no defence.

I am not interested in the shallow objection that a pump requires a counterparty. I am interested in three pressures, which I have not been able to settle.

First, the too-much problem. Consider an agent with no cycle at any time: in odd periods she strictly prefers A to B, in even periods B to A. Each momentary ranking is complete and transitive; she is never intransitive. A trader offers, every period, to move her to her currently preferred option for a small fee, and she accepts. At the end of each two-period cycle she holds exactly the option she began the cycle with, having paid twice; the fees accumulate without bound, and no single trade was a mistake. Her only loss is the accumulated fee, and at no moment is she worse off than she was a moment before. Is she irrational? If she is, then the pump condemns every change of taste, and with it learning and any life in which values develop. If she is not, then I want to know what the defender says instead — presumably that her preferences are perfectly coherent and the trouble lies in her situation, whereas the cyclical agent's preferences are incoherent, representing no ranking at all. But if that is the answer, the work is being done by synchronic coherence, and the pump is an illustration rather than an argument. Put that dilemma to me as sharply as you can, and tell me whether the specifically diachronic requirement — exploitability across time as a defect in the preferences themselves — survives the concession.

Second, the zero-fee version. Give an agent with A over B, B over C, and C over A the three pairwise swaps at no cost, in sequence. She accepts each and ends exactly where she started, with nothing lost. So the loss comes entirely from the fee, and the fee can be made arbitrarily small: the pump begins to look like a Sorites of individually negligible losses. Does the argument inherit the standard difficulties of Sorites reasoning? Can it be run without assuming that the agent cannot foresee the sequence or cannot commit to refusing it? If it cannot, the pump may refute only the conjunction of her preferences with her trading policy, not the preferences themselves. I want to know whether a defender can concede that and still claim a diachronic requirement.

Third, exploitability against irrationality. Some dispositions that make an agent exploitable are not defects: a disposition to trust, to keep a promise, to be moved by a request. If the pump criterion condemns these, it proves too much; if it does not, what restricts it to preference structures? And is rationality hostage to the existence of a clever counterparty? A pump requires an exploiter; incoherent credences are condemned by a Dutch book that requires no counterparty and yields a loss in every state of the world. Say whether the case for diachronic requirements is stronger or weaker when made by the Dutch-book route rather than the money-pump route.

For each pressure, say what a defender of my view would concede and what she would deny, and whether the denials are principled or ad hoc. Then rank what you have offered from would make me abandon the view to merely awkward, say what the view predicts that a purely synchronic rival does not, and say whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability — in particular, whether every refusal to trade can be redescribed as a commitment policy that quietly presupposes the diachronic requirement, and if so, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. Finally, tell me which consideration should be most persuasive to someone who wants the view to be false; and if the honest verdict is that the view survives only in a weakened form — as a claim about defective preference structures rather than a verdict on agents — say that too.
