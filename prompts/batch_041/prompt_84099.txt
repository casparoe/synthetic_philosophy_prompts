so my friend and I burned like two hours last night arguing about a court case we've both been following and have read literally the same coverage of, and at the end of it he shrugs and goes "look, we just have different priors," which I find infuriating as an explanation but I also couldn't articulate why it isn't one. anyway it made me try to write down where I actually stand on this stuff, and now I'm worried the list doesn't hang together. here it is:

1. The agreeing-to-disagree result is right. If two people share the same prior and each of them is certain what credence the other assigns to some event — and that's common knowledge, they both know it, they both know they both know it, and so on — then their credences in that event have to be equal. You cannot have common knowledge that you disagree.

2. Nothing about being rational forces you to have a common prior. The probability axioms don't require it, conditioning doesn't require it. Coherence is the whole requirement on priors, and any prior you like can be coherent.

3. So I think the common prior is a substantive empirical claim about how people's minds are actually related, not a norm of rationality. And in plenty of real cases I'd bet it's just false — different upbringings, different cultures, different species, whatever.

4. But I assume a common prior in basically every model I build, because if I don't, the model stops having any empirical content: any pattern of disagreement can be explained by positing different priors, so nothing is ever falsifiable. The assumption is what makes the thing do any work. The "nobody bets against you unless they know something you don't" results, correlated equilibrium, all of that leans on it.

5. When two people who are both smart and have both read the same material still disagree about something, I treat that as evidence that at least one of them is fooling themselves. Which is basically why my friend's "different priors" line annoys me — I think it lets him off the hook.

6. And I dont think "they've just seen different things" is any kind of counterexample to the common prior picture. Two people conditioning on different signals will obviously end up with different credences. That's not a violation of anything, that's the framework working.

My friend says some of these can't all be true, and I'm pretty sure he's right, but he wouldn't tell me which ones. So: which of these are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and how bad is each one? Order them by severity and for each one tell me the cheapest thing to give up and what that costs me. If there's a single revision that would clean up the most, say which.
