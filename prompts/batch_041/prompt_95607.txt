I am a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been arguing with a friend — a labor economist, as it happens — about whether the case for trade between people with different tastes carries over to people with different moral outlooks. The ordinary story is familiar enough: if you like apples and I like oranges, we can swap and both be better off, and the gain does not require us to agree on which fruit is better. My friend thinks the same structure applies to values. If she cares a great deal about animal welfare and I care a great deal about free expression, we can each do things the other cares about and both come out ahead, even though we disagree about what ultimately matters. I would like a credence on whether this is true in general, stated as precisely as I can manage. The claim: for any two agents A and B whose value systems are each internally consistent and are not identical, there exists at least one outcome — reached by each agent performing some action, possibly one that the agent regards as a concession — that is strictly preferred to the status quo by A's own value system and strictly preferred to the status quo by B's own value system. I will call the size of the improvement over the status quo the compromise surplus. Treat the claim as resolved true if a counterexample-free argument establishes the general case, and resolved false if there is a clean counterexample class; partial or edge-case answers count as neither.

Please give me your credence in that claim, and decompose it rather than asserting a number. At a minimum I want your probability for each of the following, with one line of justification apiece, plus any term I have missed that you think belongs: (a) that a value system can be represented as a ranking of outcomes at all, given incomparability and vagueness; (b) that agents' value systems are other-inclusive, meaning they rank outcomes partly by what the other agent does, so that my concession is something you can actually value rather than merely tolerate; (c) that a concession does not trigger a deontic or integrity constraint that overrides the agent's own ranking, so that being better on balance and being permissible to choose come apart; (d) that preferences stay fixed across the trade rather than being reshaped by the trading itself; (e) that the relevant actions are divisible enough that some small, mutually acceptable exchange always exists; and (f) whatever you think the analogy to ordinary trade smuggles in that I have not noticed. Then combine the terms into a final number, tell me which single sub-question that number is most sensitive to, and say what evidence or argument would move it most.

For calibration: my own credence is about 0.7 and my friend's is about 0.3. More useful to me than your own number is your view of where our disagreement actually lives — which of the sub-questions above is doing the work in that gap, and whether there is a version of the claim, more carefully restricted than the one I wrote, on which we would both turn out to be right.
