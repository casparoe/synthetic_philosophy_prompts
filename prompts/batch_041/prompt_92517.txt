I keep encountering a pattern in discussions of deterministic chaos and I cannot tell whether it has a name of its own. The standard story runs: the system is deterministic, its exact future state is unpredictable because minute differences grow, and better measurement only postpones the failure. But in the very same system a different sort of question seems answerable indefinitely and to arbitrary precision — not "where will it be at such-and-such a moment" but "what fraction of its time will it spend in this region," "how often will it return near this point," "what is the long-run average of this quantity" — and answering it seems indifferent to how crudely the initial condition is known. The familiar account of chaos appears to be a claim about the first kind of question alone, as though unpredictability were a property of the system; yet here it looks like a property of the question, and the boundary between the answerable and the unanswerable does not seem to be drawn by precision or computing power. So I want to know: is there an established concept for this dependence of predictability on the kind of question asked, and for the class of questions that stay answerable? Is that class principled — is there a genuine characterization of why the frequency and averaging questions survive while the trajectory questions do not — or is it merely a loose family? And how does the distinction relate to, and differ from, the butterfly-effect story and from the bare remark that the statistics are predictable? I would rather have the distinction drawn with its edges than a single label.
