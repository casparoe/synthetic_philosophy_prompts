Last night a friend and I argued for several hours about whether an I can be spread across more than one brain, and we ended further apart than we began. I would like you to settle the disagreement by comparing the two positions on their merits. I do not want a survey of who has said what; I want an argument about which one is right.

The position I defended is Hofstadter's. The self, on his account, is not a thing but a pattern — a self-referential loop of activity in a brain — and a pattern can be instantiated more than once, and in fragments. It follows, or so I claimed, that a person's I can be present in reduced degree in the heads of those who knew her; that surviving in others is a partial but literal form of persistence; and that selfhood and death come in degrees rather than being all-or-nothing. The position my friend defended is that what crosses between minds is content and process: memories, habits, styles of reasoning, even routines that two people run jointly. The subject of those processes, he insisted, remains single and unshared. He added that the familiar idea that cognition can spill out of the skull into notebooks and instruments gives no comfort at all to the view that subjects multiply — extending the vehicles of thought is not multiplying the thinkers. On his account, saying that a dead friend lives on in me reports an influence, not a location.

Please compare the two. Where exactly do they come apart, and is the disagreement real or merely verbal about what deserves to be called an I? Does my friend's view simply presuppose the very thing Hofstadter denies, namely a subject that is unshareable by definition? Conversely, can Hofstadter's view say anything beyond the uncontroversial fact that we are shaped by one another? I am particularly interested in the multiplicity problem: if selves come in degrees, then two brains might each host half of me — is that one self or two, and do split-brain cases, in which a single brain appears to support two centers of experience, tell for or against him? End by saying which position better explains the experience that started the argument: catching oneself, months after a death, not merely recalling how someone thought but thinking that way oneself.
