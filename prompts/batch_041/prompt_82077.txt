My colleague has been issuing verdicts on a run of small cases, and I am trying to determine whether she is applying a principle or deciding case by case. Each case concerns disclosing a person's private information without that person's consent. Her verdicts so far: a doctor may tell a patient's long-term partner that the patient carries a serious transmissible condition; a doctor may not tell the patient's employer the same thing; a therapist may tell the police that a client has made a specific credible threat against a named person; a therapist may not tell a client's spouse about the client's childhood trauma, which puts no one at risk; and a researcher may tell a participant's sibling that the participant carries a gene for a serious but treatable condition. Next week she takes up a case in which a patient has explicitly refused to have anyone in her family told of her diagnosis, and her doctor is considering telling her adult daughter anyway, since the daughter could keep the patient on a treatment that will otherwise fail. Predict her verdict. Set out the two or three rules that fit her earlier verdicts equally well, what each predicts here, which rule you would bet on and why, and which further case would best separate them. Say also whether her record is better explained by a particularist refusal to generalize, on which the same feature can count for and against depending on the case, and what would have to be true of the new case for that reading to win.
