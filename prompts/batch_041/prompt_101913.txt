I'm giving a workshop paper arguing that the boundary problem is not a puzzle awaiting the correct criterion but a recurring constitutional moment, and that the most we can demand of any demos is what I call a revisability constraint: a boundary is legitimate only if a decision to place someone outside it remains open to challenge by those it excludes, through a standing procedure that can redraw the boundary over time. The argument turns on a case I construct for the paper — a coastal city that shares its sewage system and its labor market with an unincorporated settlement it refuses to enfranchise — which I use to show that both the all-affected and the all-subjected principles misfire on their own and that revisability is what each was reaching for.

The audience is roughly fifteen people: a majority of deliberative democrats who treat legitimacy as the product of inclusive deliberation; two agonists who will hear revisability as an attempt to tame politics into procedure; one historian of political thought fluent in the constituent-power tradition and its Rousseau-to-Sieyès lineage; and one immigration theorist who will keep steering everything back to people who are governed but have no route to membership at all.
