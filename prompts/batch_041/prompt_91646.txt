In a first-year seminar on early human societies, a classmate keeps reaching for the line that the agricultural revolution was the worst mistake in the history of the human race — first to argue that we would have been better off never having settled down at all, and later, in a different discussion, to argue that social inequality is a recent and contingent invention we could in principle do without. I can't tell whether these are two claims or one, or whether either follows from the slogan. Please lay out, as a menu rather than a single reading, the distinct theses that sentence could be standing for: that agriculture was bad for health; that average welfare fell; that total welfare fell; that inequality or hierarchy was born with it; that foragers had more leisure, autonomy, or variety than farmers; that "mistake" is an ex ante verdict about a choice rational people should have refused versus an ex post verdict about how things turned out; and whether a mistake has to have been avoidable or reversible to count as one at all. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, a contested empirical claim, or simply false, and flag any that turn on whose welfare is being summed. Then tell me which of the readings would actually license each of my classmate's two uses — that foragers were better off, and that inequality is contingent — and which reading the people who repeat the line probably have in mind.
