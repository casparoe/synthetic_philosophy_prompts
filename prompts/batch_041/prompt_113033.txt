I've been trying to work out what I actually think about moral trade — the whole business of swapping moral actions with someone who cares about different things than you do — and my gut keeps giving me answers I can't square with each other. I'm hoping you can tell me what principle, if any, is generating them.

Take the role of a philosopher who works on this stuff (consequentialism, moral uncertainty, the ethics of cooperation and exchange) and think carefully before answering. It might help to brainstorm the candidate principles first, lay them out, and then go through my cases one by one rather than pattern-matching to the first tidy theory.

Here's what I think:

- My friend cares mostly about animal welfare, I care mostly about global poverty. We agree that I'll give $500 to an animal charity and she'll give $500 to an anti-malaria charity. Both of us think the other's cause matters, just less than our own. This seems completly fine to me, maybe even good.

- Same friend, same deal, except the payment is that I have to post publicly that animal suffering is the most urgent problem facing humanity, and she has to post that global poverty is. I dont believe that about her cause — I think it's a real problem but nowhere near the top — and she doesn't believe it about mine. This feels bad to me in a way the money version doesn't. It's not the deception (nobody's fooled), it's something about having to say it.

- On a neighborhood committee, I trade my vote on her parking proposal for her vote on my bike lane. Fine. Everyone does this, and honestly it's how the thing functions.

- At a general election, someone offers me $200 to vote for a candidate I was already going to vote for. I think that's wrong, and I'd think it was wrong even though my vote is unchanged and no one is harmed. This one really bugs me, because by any straightforward accounting the trade costs nothing and buys $200.

- Someone offers me $2000 to go vegetarian for five years. Weirdly, this seems fine to me, or at least not wrong. Which sits oddly next to the vote thing — both are just cash for behavior.

- A company does something damaging and then buys a bunch of offsets so the books balance. That feels worse to me than the two friends' swap, even when the numbers come out the same. Something about it being a company, or about the harm being the very thing it's paying to keep doing.

- I'm asked to help out a campaign for a policy I think is actively bad, in exchange for their help on a policy I think is very good, and I judge the expected total to come out positive on my own values. I'd probably do it, but I'd feel dirty.

- Somebody wants to trade me: they'll make a large donation to my cause if I lie to my partner about something small. I won't do this. It's not the size of the lie, it's that I'd be doing a wrong thing to buy a right thing.

- Last one: I agree to eat meat at a dinner I could have skipped (I'm vegetarian and think eating meat is mildly wrong) in exchange for a big donation to famine relief. I'd do this in a heartbeat and feel fine about it. Which seems to conflict with the lie case, since both are do-a-small-wrong-to-buy-a-big-good.

My own guess at a principle was: a trade is OK if both sides understand it, nobody's made worse off, and the expected moral value goes up by my lights. But that obviously doesn't get the vote case or the public-statement case or the company case, and it seems to allow the lie.

So: what principle actually fits these, or which verdicts do I have to give up? State it as precisely as you can, tell me which of my cases is doing the work for each part of it, and then use it to say what I should think about a few things I haven't listed — say, a trade where the action I'm buying from the other person is one I think they have no right to do at all, and a trade where I'm buying someone's omission rather than their action. And be honest about which of my verdicts your principle can't save.
