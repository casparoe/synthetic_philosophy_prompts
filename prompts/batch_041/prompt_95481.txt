I run research ethics review for a mid-sized hospital-affiliated research institute -- about a dozen new studies a month, mostly surveys, biobank stuff, a few behavioral experiments, one or two that touch on mental health. Our legal checklist is fine and I can do it half asleep. The problem is the forms that pass the checklist and still aren't real consent: everything disclosed, signature on the line, and I know perfectly well the person didn't actually agree to anything. I want a decision procedure for that gap. I have to be able to run it in about thirty minutes per study, my coordinator has to be able to run it too, and it has to end in one of approve / send back with specific fixes / escalate, where escalate means the compliance director decides, and she signs off and also has no philosophy background.

Ground it in what the philosophy of consent actually says, but I've been reading around and there's a few distinctions I want the procedure to keep, because I think they're the ones doing the work: the difference between consent as a state of mind and consent as a signed act (a form can be perfect and the agreement absent); the difference between consent and mere compliance, where someone goes along because saying no is awkward or costly, not because they agreed; consent that's valid but fragile, meaning it survives everything we told them but would collapse if they learned one more fact; and the idea that how much someone needs to understand is set by what's at stake in that particular decision, not by a fixed bar of competence that you either clear or don't. I also want the threat/offer distinction kept separate -- coercion and an offer so good it distorts judgment feel like different failures to me and I don't want them crushed into one box called undue influence.

What I need from you: the questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it stops with a yes, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is about values and not facts. My guess is that how much risk it's fair to ask someone to accept for free is one of those, and whether broad consent to unspecified future research is consent at all is another, but tell me if I'm wrong about either. Flag which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure's clothing, and say what to do when I cant get a clean answer on a step -- I need to know whether a missing answer should count against the study or be marked unknown and passed up.

Then run it on three, showing your work at each step:

1. A biobank asking for broad consent to future research nobody has designed yet, form says we may share de-identified samples with academic and commercial partners, no end date.
2. A wellness study inside a company where the person recruiting participants is their direct manager, and participants get $500, which for some of them is a weeks groceries.
3. A behavioral study that tells people it's about memory and mood when it's actually about stereotype threat, with a debrief at the end.

At least one of those should come out in a way I won't like. I'd rather you be right than agreeable about it. And close by telling me what this procedure can't do and what I should stop expecting it to do, because I suspect I'm asking consent review to carry weight it can't carry.
