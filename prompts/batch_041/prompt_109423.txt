For the past several months I have been responsible for the anomaly-detection pipeline in a small interpretability group, and our recurring argument has quietly stopped being about detectors and become about a prior question. We flag a model's output as anomalous when the computation that produced it looks unlike anything in our reference set, and the flags are meant to be more than curiosities: they are how we hope to notice a model doing something on the inside that it does not do in training. The trouble is that our records are organised around a claim we have never defined, namely that two flags implicate the same mechanism. We assign stable tags — M-114 and the like — and we expect a tag to survive a fine-tune, to carry over to a distilled sibling, and to mean something to a reader who has not seen the case before.

Our lead wants us to adopt, as the official criterion, what he calls an interventionist standard: two episodes involve the same mechanism just in case the same family of interventions — ablation, activation patching, steering, whatever the case affords — yields the same pattern of downstream effects, with nothing about the mechanism fixed by how it is described, only by how it responds. Two of us prefer a descriptive standard, sameness of computational structure up to an agreed granularity, and one prefers a purely functional standard, sameness of input–output profile on a probe suite. The interventionist proposal is a half-remembered import from interventionist accounts of causation in the philosophy of science, and I suspect it is doing more work than we realise.

Please set out the most important implications of adopting the interventionist standard as the criterion that governs our tags. I am interested in what follows for how we could validate a detector at all, given that the standard seems to make detection and validation lean on one another; for whether a tag can honestly be said to transfer across models, fine-tunes and random seeds; for how we should build, merge and prune a registry of named mechanisms; and for what we could responsibly tell an outside reader or a regulator who wants to know whether our system recognises the same mechanism that they have in mind. Where the standard forces us to abandon a claim we would like to keep, say so plainly, and where it makes something cheaper or more tractable than we expected, say that too.
