I do a little interview podcast — small, like forty listeners, but we get actual philosophers sometimes — and next month's guest is someone I've wanted on for ages: he defends the idea that lying and merely misleading someone are morally different in kind, not just in degree, and he has a specific line on why. Roughly: a lie asks you to take the speaker's word for it, so it recruits your trust and makes him a party to your belief, while a misleading truth makes you do the work of getting it wrong yourself, so it recruits your own inference and he stays off the hook. He's been on other shows and he's good, so if I just ask him "isn't it all deception really?" he'll give me the polished version I've already heard twice. What I want is questions that go at the joints of that specific position rather than at deception in general.

Here's why this stopped being abstract for me. Six weeks ago I moved from a research-adjacent job into writing customer communications for a company that, let's say, administers benefits, and the whole craft of the job — I was trained in it, there's a style guide — is writing sentences that are literally true and that a normal person will read as meaning something else. The line in the style guide is "we do not make statements that are false; we make statements that are complete." We send a notice saying your coverage "may be reviewed" when it's already been flagged for termination. Nothing in the sentence is false. My friend is a contracts lawyer and she says her entire field rests on the fact that fraud basically needs a false statement of fact, so as long as nobody types a false sentence, everybody sleeps fine. I keep wanting to say that's a loophole and I keep being unable to say exactly what's wrong with it, which is seperate from but related to why I want to do the episode.

So: give me the questions. Nine to eleven of them, in the order I should ask them, and for each one the follow-up I should have ready for the answer he's most likely to give — he's a nice guy who answers in roughly the shape you'd expect, so the follow-up is the thing that has to make the answer cost him something. Three things I specifically want hit. (1) Whether the difference he's drawing is moral or only legal/conventional: if the law's line and the moral line happen to coincide, why should I think that's anything other than the law borrowing a convenient seam, and what would count as evidence for the moral version? (2) My letters, or something like them — this is the case where I suspect misleading is *worse* than a lie would be, because a lie at least puts the truth in play, whereas my sentences leave the reader holding a wrong belief she'd defend if you challenged her. I want him to have to say why the trust-recruitment story doesn't just get this backwards. (3) What I've been calling the hearer's share: the idea that anyone dealing with an insurer, a salesperson or a politician has some duty to read defensively, so misleading only wrongs you when the speaker has reason to think your guard is down. On that one I want to know whether it makes the wrongness of misleading hostage to how sophisticated the audience is — because if it does, the same sentence is fine for me and wrong for my grandmother — and I want him to either own that consequence or explain why it doesn't follow. Keep everything in plain English; he's a philosopher but the audience isn't, and neither am I, really.
