I have recently been appointed to a citizens' assembly in a mid-sized city whose population has declined for two decades, and the experience has made the question of whether a larger population is better feel urgent and personal. The council has drafted a Population and Flourishing Charter, and I have been asked to help the assembly see the full range of positions rather than the usual slogans. Please write a decision tree that sorts the positions one might take on whether a larger population is better.

The audience is lay assembly members, so every branch node must be phrased as a question they could answer without already knowing the philosophical labels. Begin with the most basic disagreement about whether population size is a good at all, and let each branch narrow the space. The tree should have eight to ten branch nodes. Each node should turn on a concrete case or claim, not on which name one prefers. Each leaf should carry the name of a position and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where a combination of answers corresponds to no position anyone actually holds, note it and say whether you think it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate.

The charter contains three provisions that must be classifiable by the tree. First, a demographic vitality index that weights births, longevity, and immigration equally in measuring municipal success. Second, a kin-credit that gives tax relief for third and later children but not for first or second children. Third, a carbon-birth levy that charges a fee for each additional child above replacement level and uses the revenue for elder care. Positions may accept or reject each provision for very different reasons, and the tree should be able to distinguish those reasons.

As you build the tree, make sure it separates positions along at least these axes: whether a larger population is intrinsically good, merely instrumentally good, or not a good at all; whether the relevant unit is the household, the city, the nation, or the species; whether adding lives is to be evaluated by total welfare, average welfare, a critical level of welfare, or non-welfarist goods such as cultural continuity, security, or ecological integrity; whether procreation is a right, a duty, a privilege, or a morally fraught act; and whether a position accepts that adding a life can be good even when it lowers average welfare. Also make sure the tree distinguishes positions that accept the three charter provisions for pronatalist reasons from those that accept them for liberal, ecological, or fiscal reasons, and from those that reject them for antinatalist, libertarian, or religious reasons.
