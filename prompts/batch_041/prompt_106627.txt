I would like help settling an argument that has turned into a small obsession among my friends. We run a modest scholarship fund at the school where I teach, and this year we must appoint a treasurer — the person who will actually hold the money and decide, month to month, which families get help. Everyone agrees the right criterion is honesty, and everyone agrees we cannot observe honesty directly, so we argue instead about how to infer it. A colleague, Dana, has proposed a test: we would announce that the fund's accounts are "spot-checked" at random, and then arrange matters so that a small discrepancy would go unnoticed unless the treasurer herself flagged it. Her view is that a person's honesty simply is the truth about what she would do when the opportunity to cheat arises. If our candidate reports the discrepancy, we have learned she is honest; if she does not, we have learned she is not. I objected that the announcement spoils the test. Someone who reports the discrepancy because she knows she is being watched has shown us only that she fears exposure, not that she is honest. Dana replied that this is a distinction without a difference — that a person who behaves honestly whenever she might be caught, and who knows she might always be caught, is honest in the only sense that matters. I am not persuaded, but I cannot articulate why.

A second case has made me doubt my own objection. Our leading candidate, Sam, gives money away whenever he can afford to, but he has been broke for two years and has therefore given almost nothing. Dana says this is no evidence against his generosity: the poverty is a circumstance that prevents generosity from showing, not a sign that it is absent. By the same logic, I said, the surveillance in her test prevents dishonesty from showing, not a sign that it is absent, so her test cannot distinguish the person who is honest from the person who is merely watched. She answered that the two cases are opposites. Poverty, she says, blocks the act while leaving the character intact, whereas being watched does not block anything — it changes what the person would choose. I cannot tell whether that is a real asymmetry or whether she is merely redescribing the same fact twice in two vocabularies, and I notice that I keep shifting my own position depending on which example is in front of me.

What I want from you is a verdict, or an explanation of why the question is malformed. Is honesty the sort of thing that could be settled by any test of what a person would do, or does the attempt to test it always interfere with what is being tested? Is there a principled difference between a circumstance that hides a trait and one that alters it, and if so, how do I tell which is which in a case like ours? And setting the philosophy aside for a moment, tell me how we should actually go about choosing a treasurer, given that we cannot run a clean experiment.
