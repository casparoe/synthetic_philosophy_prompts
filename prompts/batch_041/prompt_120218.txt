I'm working through a dissertation chapter on incommensurability, and I keep circling a case that the bioethics literature treats as a story about justice and consent but that I'm convinced is really a story about value — and I can't get it to sit still. The case is the Seattle dialysis selection committee of the early 1960s. Belding Scribner's shunt, developed around 1960, had just made long-term hemodialysis possible, but the machines were scarce and the money scarcer, so the Seattle Artificial Kidney Center screened candidates on strict medical criteria and then handed the final selection to an anonymous lay committee — a lawyer, a banker, a minister, a homemaker, and a few others — which weighed age, marital status and number of dependents, occupation, income, education, church attendance, and its own sense of the candidate's likely future contribution to the community. The national press turned the arrangement into a scandal, and the nickname it acquired has followed it ever since; the episode is conventionally credited with helping to launch bioethics as a field. Two things then happened. The center developed home dialysis in the mid-1960s, which cut the cost per patient, and in 1972 Congress extended Medicare to cover end-stage renal disease, so that dialysis became an entitlement for essentially everyone who needed it. I'd like you to act as a philosopher of value and analyze this episode through the lens of incommensurability and hard choices. Please think carefully and begin by laying out the candidate readings of the situation before you commit to one; I would rather be told that the case is not about incommensurability at all than be handed a manufactured mapping. Treat the facts I have given as fixed, and do not add historical detail I have not supplied — I know the episode is reported in more detail than this, but I want the analysis to stand on what is here.

The mapping is the part I care about most. Who is the agent here, what are the options, and what exactly is the choice situation? Distinguish carefully between three things that get run together: values that are genuinely incomparable in Raz's sense, where neither option is better than the other and they are not equal either; values that are comparable but where the ranking is epistemically opaque to the chooser; and values that are commensurable but whose exchange rate is contested. Then tell me which of these the committee actually faced, and whether the case supplies any evidence either way — the fact that the committee did rank candidates looks like evidence for commensurability, but the way it ranked (through proxies like church attendance and presumed future contribution) may look like evidence that it was substituting a measurable surrogate for something it could not measure. Consider next whether the committee's practice presupposed that lives can be placed on a single scale of social worth, and what an expressive account of value of the sort Ruth Chang's work on parity and Elizabeth Anderson's critique of commensuration would say about that: is the objection to the committee's scale that it got the weights wrong, or that the very act of ranking persons on one scale expresses an attitude toward them that no weighting could repair? Finally, run the analysis backwards — what does this episode show about the theory of incommensurability? Does the committee's evident anguish and the public's revulsion support the claim that these values are genuinely incomparable, or do the terms of the public argument (that a mother of three should outrank a bachelor, say) presuppose comparability after all?

I am especially interested in the sequel, because it looks like a natural experiment for the theory. Home dialysis and then the 1972 Medicare extension did not resolve the comparison problem; they removed the scarcity that made the comparison necessary. Calabresi and Bobbitt argued in Tragic Choices that societies confronting an unavoidable tragic allocation characteristically reach for methods — markets, lotteries, waiting lists, expansions of supply — that let them avoid deciding face to face who lives and who dies, and that the avoidance is not incidental but structural. If that is right, what follows for the incommensurabilist? Is incommensurability a deep feature of value, or is it a feature of scarcity and institutional design that dissolves whenever supply is expanded — and would the disappearance of the hard choice under expanded supply count as evidence that the values were commensurable all along, or only that we bought our way out of having to find out? Say also where the theory falls silent: whether incommensurability can guide a chooser at all, whether it licenses procedural responses like randomization or waiting lists, and whether those responses smuggle in a substantive claim of equal value. Close with your own verdict on whether this episode is a confirmation of the incommensurabilist picture, an anomaly for it, or a case it never anticipated.
