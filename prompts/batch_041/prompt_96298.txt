I write the Ideas column for a general-interest science magazine, and next month I will conduct an onstage interview with a philosopher of mind whose recent work defends a striking claim about the old ant-colony thought experiment that philosophers have nicknamed Aunt Hillary. Her position, in her own terms, is that an ant colony is literally a cognitive system — it carries, stores, and revises information about its environment — while there is nothing it is like to be the colony; she calls it a cognizer without a point of view. She grounds the claim in a three-part test for colony-level representation: the signal must be decoupled from the stimulus that triggers it, must be revisable when the colony encounters contrary evidence, and must be re-identifiable as the same representation across turnover in the ants that carry it. I would like eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, that press on the actual structure of this position rather than letting her restate it. At least one question each should address the following: why the fact that no individual ant understands anything does not equally show that a brain knows nothing, given that no individual neuron does; what her three-part test implies for hard cases at the boundary, such as a slime mold that solves a maze, an immune system that retains a memory of a pathogen, or a colony that can be split into two viable halves and whose membership turns over entirely every few years; whether a parasite that mimics colony signals and hijacks workers shows that the colony can misrepresent its environment, or instead shows that the signals are more like hormones than like beliefs; what makes an ascription such as the colony believes there is food to the north true when there is no one to whom anything appears; and what, if anything, follows for how we should treat colonies if they are cognizers with no point of view. Please also include a question on whether the engineering success of swarm-intelligence algorithms, which run on silicon and appear to satisfy her three criteria, commits her to calling those systems cognizers, and a closing question on what evidence or argument would change her mind, given that her thesis sounds conceptual rather than empirical. Do not include questions that ask her to recommend books or summarize the literature; keep the focus on the argument, and write in American English.
