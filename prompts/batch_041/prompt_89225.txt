I am assembling a seminar on teleosemantics and the naturalization of content, and I want to use Ridley Scott's Blade Runner as the extended case study rather than as a parable about personhood. Nearly all the philosophical writing on the film asks whether the replicants are persons and whether Deckard is one of them; I want a reading that keeps those questions in the background and puts this one in front: what fixes the content of a mental state in a being whose history is a history of manufacture rather than of selection?

Use the standard instruments of the debate as the reading's tools, and be explicit about which one is doing the work at each point: biosemantics of the Millikan kind, on which a state's content is fixed by the proper function conferred on it by the intertwined histories of the mechanisms that produce states of that kind and the mechanisms that consume them; indicator or informational semantics of the Dretske kind, on which content derives from the causal-informational link a state bears to what it indicates; and the asymmetric-dependence strategy associated with Fodor, on which a state means what it does because its connection to that thing is what sustains its other connections. Treat the capacity for misrepresentation as the test case it is usually taken to be, and say whether the extension of biosemantics to artifacts is available here at all — whether the function of a made thing is derived from the purposes of its makers, or whether it must be conferred by a history of use that nothing in the film supplies.

The feature I most want pressed is that the replicants' states appear to be first-generation. They have design histories and no selection histories: no lineage of earlier tokenings of the same state, no established practice of consumers responding to it in a particular way. Rachael's installed memories are the sharpest case — states that answer to no event in the world and have no consumer except the engineers who wrote them, yet which seem to have determinate content and to be capable of being false in a way that matters to her. Roy's demand for more life, and his grief on the roof, raise the same question for conative states: are those states about his life, or about what his designers needed him to do? A secondary puzzle: the Voight-Kampff questions are themselves artifacts, built to elicit a response rather than to describe anything, while the film treats a disposition to respond — empathy — as the mark of a mind. Is that criterion simply orthogonal to a theory of content, and does the film know it?

Test a suspicion of mine as you go: that teleosemantics makes the replicants' inner lives easier to naturalize than the film's humanism can tolerate, and that the film's real subject is less whether they have minds than whether anything their states are about is of the right kind to fix content at all. If the suspicion holds, say what the film loses and whether it loses it honestly; if it fails, say exactly where the derivation of a function from a maker's intention stops, and whether the film dramatizes that stopping point.

Point me to the scenes that carry the weight — the interviews, the photographs, the four-year lifespan as a designed expiration, the daydream and the folded-paper figures in the versions that include them, the roof — and say where the film resists the reading, so that I can teach it without flattening either the film or the theory. I know the primary literature and want no bibliography and no quotations; what I want at the end is what the film adds to the debate that the arguments alone do not.
