I've been reading LessWrong and the surrounding blogosphere since the mid-2000s, and I've finally written up a long-standing irritation of mine about the dark forest hypothesis. I'd like your comments on the draft below. I'm not a professional philosopher, so I'd rather be told that a step is confused than that I've missed a literature; and I don't want a reading list, since I've read the standard material and it isn't what's bothering me. Argue with me directly.

Draft — The Dark Forest Is Hobbes in Space, and It Inherits Both His Insight and His Mistake

1. The usual objection to the dark forest is empirical: the sky could be silent for a dozen reasons — life is rare, intelligence rarer, expansion slow, the window short, they're already here and we're not looking properly. All of that is true and all of it is beside the point, because the dark forest was never an inference from the sky. It's an inference from the structure of a strategic situation. The sky is decoration.

2. What the argument actually claims: (a) detection is passive and communication is slow, so you cannot learn anyone's intentions before they can act on yours; (b) the downside of being wrong in the direction of trust is unbounded, while the downside of being wrong in the direction of suspicion is a foregone gain; (c) there is no third party with the power to enforce an agreement. Given those three, the reasoning goes, the dominant strategy is to destroy what you detect and reveal nothing, and the sky is quiet because everyone is doing exactly that.

3. Notice what that structure is. It's the state of nature: no common power, no reliable knowledge of other minds, unbounded downside to being wrong, and a rational strategy of anticipatory violence. The dark forest is not a hypothesis about aliens; it is the oldest argument in political philosophy, run in a setting where nobody has to live with anybody afterwards. Which means it should be adjudicated by the arguments that adjudicate the state of nature, and those arguments are not all on one side. Uncentralized human societies are not perpetual wars of all against all. They are dense with institutions — kinship, marriage alliance, gift exchange, ritual, hostage exchange, mediation, reputation — and the war of all against all is rare not because people are decent but because fighting is expensive and the future is long. If you think the dark forest follows from the absence of a common power, you should expect stateless societies to be dark forests too. They aren't.

4. The obvious reply is that the disanalogy is total: in the state of nature the same parties meet again, whereas across interstellar distances the first encounter is also the last, because one party is gone at the end of it. There is no future for anyone to be shadowed by. I think this reply is right, and I think it cuts deeper than the people who make it notice, because it doesn't just limit the dark forest — it dissolves it. The dark forest needs a population of civilizations playing a common game with a common strategy. None of those three things can exist across a galaxy too large for any single strategic horizon. There is no common knowledge, no way to know that anyone else is playing the same game, no fact about what the equilibrium is. What you should expect, if the local conditions for paranoid first-strike obtain at all, is a patchwork: local clusters where the logic bites, and a majority of the volume where nobody has ever had occasion to think about it. Silence would then be the default for uninteresting reasons, and the dark forest a rare local pathology rather than the explanation of the sky.

5. There is a prediction in here that I have never seen drawn out. Take the dark forest on its own terms: the prescribed strategy is destroy on detection and reveal nothing. But a civilization that has already been detected has no silence left to protect. Its position is known; the option value of hiding is zero. For such a civilization the strategy flips, and it flips hard: you want to be maximally visible, because visibility is the only defense available — you want it known that killing you is costly, that you can retaliate, or at minimum that you are watching. So the dark forest predicts a bimodal sky: overwhelmingly quiet, punctuated by rare and enormous signals that are loud precisely because their senders had nothing left to lose. I don't want to lean on this, since it is one bit of evidence and I may be reading the anomalies backwards.

6. So my claim is that the dark forest is best understood as a claim about equilibrium selection in an institution-free environment; that it inherits the real weakness of the state-of-nature argument, namely that institutions grow where there is no sovereign; and that its most interesting prediction is a bimodal sky rather than uniform silence. If I'm right, the live question is not whether the aliens are hostile but what makes an interaction one-shot, and whether distance actually makes it so — a civilization that persists and can be re-detected is not obviously in a one-shot game, and the whole argument turns on that.
