You are a philosopher working in the tradition of early analytic philosophy, with a specialist's knowledge of Moritz Schlick's ethics and of the Vienna Circle's ambivalent relationship to questions about the meaning of life. You are also an unusually careful reader of literature — the kind who can hold a philosophical theory and a work of fiction together without flattening either. I want a critical reading of Antoine de Saint-Exupéry's The Little Prince through the lens of Schlick's 1927 essay "Vom Sinn des Lebens" (On the Meaning of Life).

Before you write, brainstorm: list the scenes, characters, and structural features of the book that seem most relevant to Schlick's argument, and list the points at which the book seems to pull against him. Then write the reading.

Some context on Schlick's essay, so that the reading is anchored in his actual position rather than a generic existentialism. Schlick opens by dividing people into those who have the soul of the child that does not yet ask after a meaning, those who have unlearned to ask, and the seekers in between, among whom he places himself. He then takes up Schopenhauer's diagnosis — that willing brings pain, satisfaction brings boredom, and life oscillates between them — and considers Nietzsche's attempts to escape it through art and knowledge, before locating the real error in the domination of life by purposes. His thought experiment is the hurried street of the great city: stop the passersby and ask why they rush, then ask the purpose of that purpose, and you arrive quickly at earning a living and preserving life; ask why life should be preserved, and no intelligible answer is forthcoming. Mere existence, he says, has no value in itself; it must have a content, and only in that content can the meaning of life lie. His positive proposal is that the meaning of life is not to be found in work, where work means goal-directed activity, but in play — in activity that is its own end — and in the spirit of youth; he invokes something like a Schillerian dream of a humanity freed from tormenting purposes and serenely given to the moment. He is a logical empiricist, suspicious of metaphysical consolation, and yet the essay is a piece of normative reflection on how to live, not a piece of logical analysis.

Now the reading. Address at least the following, though do not treat this as a checklist to be ticked off mechanically.

1. The narrator's two childhood drawings and the grown-ups' advice to set drawing aside for geography, history, and arithmetic. What does Schlick's distinction between the child's soul and the purpose-driven adult world make of this scene, and what does the scene make of Schlick?

2. The gallery of grown-ups: the king, the conceited man, the tippler, the businessman, the lamplighter, the geographer. Which of these are Schopenhauerian figures, which are figures of the purpose-of-purpose circle, and which resist both descriptions? The tippler who drinks to forget that he drinks, and the businessman who counts stars in order to own them in order to be rich in order to buy more stars, deserve particular attention. Is the lamplighter, whom the prince loves, a slave of purposes or something else? Be honest about the difficulty.

3. The fox and the theme of taming. Is the creation of ties a purpose, an obligation, or a form of play? The fox's teaching about ritual, about the wheat field, and about responsibility seems to complicate Schlick's opposition between purpose and play; say how, and whether the complication damages Schlick or enriches him.

4. The rose, the well, and the ending. The prince's care for his single rose, the water that is sweet because of the walk to the well, and the final departure by the snake: does the book vindicate Schlick's claim that meaning lies in the content of existence rather than in its bare continuation, or does it finally locate meaning somewhere Schlick's naturalism cannot follow — in the invisible, in the stars, in a kind of resurrection?

5. Where the book resists the reading. This is important. Schlick's essay is serene, almost cheerful, about the possibility of recovering the spirit of youth in adult life, and it treats the loss of the child's world as a mistake to be corrected. The Little Prince is elegiac; it may hold that the child's world is irrecoverable and that the price of adulthood is grief. If the book is right, what does Schlick miss? Does the book dramatize a cost that his argument cannot register — the mourning that accompanies the loss of the child's relation to the world? Say so plainly if you think so.

6. What the book adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not. Perhaps it is the phenomenology of the transition, or the social dimension of the purpose-driven world, or the role of love and grief. Be specific.

Write as a philosopher and critic, not as a summary. Do not retell the plot except where a scene needs to be described to make a point. Do not attribute quotations to the book or the essay; I want paraphrase and argument, since you cannot consult the texts. Do not turn the reading into an illustration of Schlick in which the book merely confirms him; a good reading is faithful in both directions. Where the book exceeds or corrects the essay, say so. Where the essay illuminates the book, say that too. Aim for about 1,200 words, in polished prose, with clear paragraphs rather than a numbered list, though you may use brief signposting. Begin with a short paragraph stating the central claim of your reading, and end with a paragraph on what the pairing reveals that neither the essay nor the book reveals alone.
