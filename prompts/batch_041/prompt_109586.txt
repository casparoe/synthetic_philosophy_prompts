I run a small forecasting shop — four analysts, we put probabilities on stuff for clients (will this supplier default, will this drug pass phase 3, will the bill get through committee). The thing that keeps eating my week is two of them disagreeing: one says 15%, the other says 40%, and then we burn an hour in a meeting that ends with someone saying lets just split the difference, or well, agree to disagree, and nobody's actually smarter at the end of it. Aumann's agreement theorem keeps coming up, mostly because one of my guys, Dev, has read about it and now uses it like a crowbar. His line is: if two rational people with the same information updated correctly, they'd already agree; so if he and Priya disagree, one of them is being irrational, and he's pretty sure its not him. I don't think that's what the theorem actually says, but I can never articulate the difference fast enough in a meeting to shut it down.

So what I want from you is a decision procedure I can actually run, in order, when two of my people disagree on a number. Not a lecture about the theorem — a procedure. The questions to ask and in what order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops and says fine, one of you just has different priors and that's allowed, move on, and where it says no, one of you has to move, here's who and roughly how much. It has to handle the obvious hard cases: they've got genuinely different information; they've got identical information and read it differently; they've got different priors and neither is nuts; and the weird one where they agree on the number but for incompatible reasons. Be honest about which steps are real epistemic constraints and which are just me managing personalities, and mark the spots where the procedure can't get me an answer and I just have to decide as the boss.

Constraints: I need to run it in about ten minutes, alone, before the meeting — so no steps that require both analysts in the room, and no asking them to go gather more data, that's not on the table. The final number goes to the client under my name, so ultimately I sign off. And it has to survive Dev: at least one step should tell me what to say when someone invokes the theorem to justify never moving at all. Also, somewhere in there, explain why just average the two numbers is not what the theorem licenses — I keep getting told to do that and it smells wrong to me.

Then run it on three cases:

1. Priya at 15% vs Dev at 40% on a supplier defaulting, where Dev has actually seen the internal cash-flow sheet and Priya hasn't.
2. Same two, same spread, on a drug passing phase 3, where they've looked at the identical trial data and just weight the subgroup analysis differently.
3. They agree it's 20%, but Priya got there from base rates for this class of supplier and Dev got there because the CEO sounded nervous on the call. I want to know if the procedure says anything useful here at all, or just shrugs.

Include at least one case where the procedure tells me something I won't like — e.g. that I should move my own estimate, or that the disagreement is legitimate and I've been wrong to force a single number out of them.

Format: write the procedure out in prose first, then give me the steps as a table with these columns — step, question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, if the answer is no or unavailable then, and one more column: fact question or judgement call. After the worked cases, give me a JSON object per case with fields: case_id, my_estimate, their_estimates, same_information, share_priors, common_knowledge_reached, procedure_outcome, estimate_we_go_with, does_disagreement_survive, who_decides, procedure_limitation.
