For most of my professional life I was a marine biologist working on shell-bearing mollusks; since retiring I have been reading my way into philosophy of science on my own, and I am very much a beginner. Next month I am to lead a fifty-minute session at our local philosophy-of-science reading group, an audience of intelligent non-specialists with no formal logic and only a distant memory of high-school probability. The subject is Hempel's raven paradox. Do not explain the paradox to me; I understand its shape well enough. What I want is a plan for teaching it, concrete enough that I could follow it at a whiteboard. Please give me the following.

1. Order. Give me the sequence in which the ideas must be introduced, and for each step say what had to be in place before that step could be understood. I am especially unsure where the bare notion of evidential support has to be pinned down, as opposed to the principle that a generalization is supported by its positive instances, and I want the plan to make that dependency explicit rather than leave it to intuition.

2. Carrying example. I do not want the ravens, and I do not want the white shoe; those are the textbook props and my audience will recognize them, which means the example will be doing theater rather than work. I had considered using one from my own corner of biology: the claim that every species in a particular genus of snail is left-coiled (sinistral), where the plentiful, boring non-instances would be the countless right-coiled shells of other genera that one handles in the course of ordinary sampling. Tell me whether that will carry the lesson or whether something else should, and in either case tell me what the carrying example must have and what it must lack for the paradox to emerge honestly rather than by sleight of hand.

3. Misconceptions. Map the misconceptions my audience will arrive with or form, in the order they will form them, and tell me exactly where in the sequence to head each one off. I can offer two of my own that I suspect are really one: I keep wanting to say that a non-black non-raven supports the hypothesis a little, and also that it supports it not at all, and I suspect I am running together two different senses of support. Tell me whether that diagnosis is right, where each temptation comes from, and how to defuse it.

4. The pivot I want the lesson to turn on. I would like the plan to rest on the difference between evidence that selectively supports a hypothesis and evidence that merely fails to count against it, and to say where the related worry belongs in the sequence — namely, whether a hypothesis and some irrelevant additional claim are supported by the very same observations — and whether that worry should be raised at all with this audience.

5. Check for understanding. Give me one question to put at the end that will show whether the paradox has landed as a puzzle about the logic of support, rather than as a piece of trivia or a reason to distrust science.

6. What to leave out. Tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level, and say so plainly.

Please write this as a sequence of teaching moves rather than as an essay on confirmation theory, and please do not give me a reading list; I will not be assigning anything.
