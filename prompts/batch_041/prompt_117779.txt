You're a philosopher of language and logic — someone who's spent alot of time in the weeds on vagueness — and I'd like you to act as the neutral scribe for something. Before you write anything, brainstorm a bit and then think carefully about what's actually at stake in each claim; don't just hand me the textbook lines.

Background: I'm a retired analytical chemist who got into philosophy after I stopped running a lab. A friend of mine, a logician, and I have been argueing for about two years about whether vague words like tall, heap and bald have sharp boundaries. He's an epistemicist: there's a fact of the matter, a unique extension, and we simply can't know where the line falls. I'm drawn to the supervaluationist picture: a borderline case isn't true or false, it comes out true on some admissible ways of making the predicate precise and false on others, and a claim counts as true when it's true on all of them. We keep talking past each other and I'm tired of it.

So draft the joint statement we could both sign. Number the agreed claims first — what we both accept about vague predicates and their borderline cases, about the sorites, about whether vagueness lives in the world or in language, about whether classical logic and bivalence are up for negotiation, and about what a competent speaker does or doesn't know. Then a table of the real disagreements, with each side's position phrased the way that side would want it phrased (I should be able to read my column without feeling set up). Then the cruxes — the sub-questions where settling one would actually move one of us — and for each crux a case or argument we'd both agree bears on it, with his prediction and mine written down in advance.

Three things to treat as cruxes or test cases, since they're what made me start this:

1. Higher-order vagueness: what either of us says about the boundary of definitely-tall, and whether my view escapes the regress or just pushes it up a level.
2. A case from my old lab. We had a spectrometer with a hard cutoff at a fixed reading, and a sample either tripped it or it didn't, even though the underlying property was plainly graded. Does the existence of a device with a clean threshold tell us anything about whether the property itself has a clean threshold? I say no, he says the analogy cuts his way. Write the case up so we can each see what we're predicting about it.
3. A case where an authority stipulates a line — a regulator declaring that a residue counts as unsafe above 0.05, or a club rule that you're tall at six foot. Ask both of us what's true one hair below the stipulated line, and whether such a stipulation is a precisification in the technical sense or something else.

Last thing: tell me which parts of our dispute are verbal and which are real. I suspect some of it is the two of us using the phrase fact of the matter differently, but I'd rather you tell me than assume. Keep the document tight, no literature review and no citations, and don't pad the agreed section with things nobody would deny.
