I've been roped into giving the after-dinner speech at our county historical society's annual dinner — they're handing me some plaque for a novel I wrote about a woman who really did live here in the 1840s, and her great-great-grandaughter is going to be sitting at the table right in front of me, and about half the room are amateur historians who keep a mental spreadsheet of every date I fudged. What I actually want to talk about is the thing I've never once said out loud at one of these: that I invented most of it, and that the invented version is now the version people around here know. Here's the idea I want the whole speech built around, and I want it taken seriously, not just name-checked — I've come to believe a life isnt over when the person dies; it keeps going as long as somebody's still telling it, which means a writer can reach back and change what a life meant, for better or worse, and the dead have no way to say not that, not me. So the speech has to actually answer, out loud, to both halves of that room at once, what I owe her — and whether getting the facts right is even the kind of thing you can owe somebody who no longer exists to be wronged by it. Dont let me off with a soft speech about imagination and empathy. Six or seven minutes, warm enough for the family, honest enough for the historians, and it needs to land on an actual position rather than a shrug.
