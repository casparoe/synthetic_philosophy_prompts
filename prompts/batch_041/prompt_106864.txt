Im a second-year PhD student working on Islamic philosophy and philosophy of religion, and I've got a reconstruction of al-Ghazālī's argument about causation that I built for a seminar and now find suspicious, because the conclusion is obviously absurd. The original is the long stretch in the Tahāfut where he's attacking the philosophers' claim that causes necessitate their effects; I've compressed it and added a couple of steps of my own. It goes like this:

(1) Whenever we say fire burns the cotton or bread nourishes, all we ever observe is one thing following another. We never observe the one making the other happen.

(2) Nothing in the essence of fire, taken on its own, contains burning. So there's no contradiction at all in conceiving fire touching cotton with no burning.

(3) If the connection between them were genuinely necessary, we'd have to know that necessity either by pure reason or by observation. Reason gives us no contradiction in the denial, and observation only ever gives us the succession.

(4) So the connection is not necessary.

(5) If it isn't necessary, then whatever regularity there is has to be produced by something that decides to produce it — God, whose habit (ʿāda) it is to make fire burn.

(6) But a habit can be broken at will, and that's what a miracle is.

(7) And if it can be broken at will, then at any moment anything could follow anything.

(8) So no inference from 'it's always gone this way' to 'it'll go this way next time' is actually warranted, not even a probable one.

(9) So strictly speaking I don't know that the fire will burn the cotton, or that the bread will nourish me.

(10) And if I don't know that, I also don't know that any particular event is a miracle rather than just the next item in a very long run of coincidences — which means 'miracle' stops meaning anything, since on this picture every event is equally uncaused.

I built the thing as a defence of the occasionalist line, because I want (5) and (6): I want miracles to be possible without making God a prisoner of his own creation. But (9) and (10) seem to just fall out of it, and (10) in particular looks like it eats the very thing I was trying to save. A friend also said that if I keep (4) I lose the right to say that my own actions are caused by me, which is awkward for a man who wrote a mountain of law — though he also said there's a patch for that, the kasb thing where God creates the act and we 'acquire' it, and I can't tell if that's a real fix or just a relabeling.

Where does this go wrong? Which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? If it's a real choice I'd rather keep (5) and (6) than (8). I'm also half-worried the whole thing is just a symptom of some general slide from 'not necessary' to 'not warranted', and that anyone who grounds induction in something non-necessary — Humean custom, reliabilism, divine habit — pays the same price. Tell me if thats the real issue or if I'm flattering myself.
