I was arguing with some friends after a combinatorics reading group about strategy-stealing arguments -- you know the trick where you suppose the second player has a winning strategy, then show the first player could've just played some throwaway opening move and then adopted that same strategy, so the second player never really had one. I have a pile of verdicts about when this actually works and I'm worried I'm just being inconsistent, so I want to know if there's one principle behind them.

Here's where I land. Hex on a big board: I'm completely convinced the first player wins, even though nobody can draw me the strategy. Chomp: same, first player wins and the stealing argument settles it. Tic-tac-toe: I think the very same argument goes through fine, but here it only buys me that the second player can't force a win -- it plainly doesn't give me a first-player win, since the game is a draw, and I don't feel the argument has *failed*, it just proves less. Chess: I don't think you can run it at all, even though chess is finite and perfect-information, because of zugzwang -- there are positions where being the one who has to move is a liability, so I can't assume my throwaway first move left me no worse off. And a board that's almost symmetric except one square is already pre-marked for one player: I say stealing is dead there too. What's bugging me is that I'm happy with the argument in tic-tac-toe but refuse it in chess, and my rough story for both is just "the first player can't be worse off" -- which I suspect is either two different conditions mashed together or else doing all the work in a way I haven't noticed.

So: is there a single principle that tells me (a) when a strategy-stealing argument is *sound*, and separately (b) when it delivers a win rather than a mere non-loss? State it precisely enough that I can apply it cold to games I haven't listed, and give me a verdict on the case I keep flip-flopping on -- an infinite game where play can run forever with no winner, where I have a hunch the argument still says something but I can't tell what. Also tell me which of my verdicts you'd have to overturn to get a clean principle rather than a pile of epicycles, and be honest about where your own reasoning is most likely to break: which one premise, if it turned out to be carrying more weight than I think, would flip your verdict.
