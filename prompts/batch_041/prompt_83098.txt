Ok so I've been thinking about the old rhyme about the kingdom getting lost for want of a horseshoe nail, and I want you to make an argument for a position I'm half-convinced by but can't tell if it's actually any good.

Here's the setup. Take some outcome O at a later time and some small, arbitrary event e at an earlier time (a nail, a fog bank, a messenger who sleeps in). Two tests:

Rewind test: change e, hold the rest of the world fixed, see whether O changes.
Re-roll test: leave e as it was, but re-roll all the chancy stuff in between — weather, illness, who loses a letter, who gets promoted, who gets a cold — and see whether O changes.

My half-position: an outcome is only genuinely historically contingent if it fails the re-roll test. Failing the rewind test proves nothing on it's own — it only shows that the particular path we happened to get was fragile, not that the outcome was. The nail decided the date, not the fate.

Please argue for that. Stuff I want you to deal with:

- At least one concrete case, real or invented, where the two tests come apart in each direction: rewind-fragile but re-roll-robust, and re-roll-fragile.
- The obvious objection, which is that the rewind test as I've stated it is incoherent, since you can't hold everything after e fixed if e changes — a lot of those facts are downstream of e. Either patch the test or explain why the patch doesn't rescue the argument.
- Whether the re-roll test even makes sense. What exactly is the space of chancy stuff you're re-rolling over? If it depends on how you individuate events, or on what grain you describe O at, does the whole distinction collapse?
- And what follows for the rhyme: is the nail story just bad history, or is there a reading on which it survives?

Write it as tightly as you can, don't worry about citing anyone, and feel free to pull in analogies from math or physics if they help.
