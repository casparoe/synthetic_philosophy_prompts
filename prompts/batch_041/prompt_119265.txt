I'm a novelist, about two-thirds through a book set in a sideways version of 1790s London, and I need a document to exist — something I can paste in as an appendix and also steal language from. So treat this as a commission rather than a question.

What I want: a mock regulatory impact assessment, a civil servant's felicific audit of the laws criminalizing what the period called sexual irregularities. Deadpan, numbered sections, the voice of a patient clerk who has been instructed to run the Benthamite calculus and report back. Roughly 650 words. Headings are fine. It should read like a real government document that has been infected by a philosopher.

The jokes have to come from the machinery being right. I want the seven dimensions of the calculus actually doing work — intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, extent — and I want at least one of them to behave badly under pressure in a way a reader who knows the theory will spot. I have a feeling purity is the one that goes rouge, but you decide. I want the argument from unnaturalness to be dutifully assessed and assigned zero weight as an argument, while nonetheless reappearing as a very large entry under public disapprobation — and I want the clerk to notice this and not know what to do about it. That's the heart of the piece for me. There's a real problem lurking there, something about whether the displeasure other people feel about your conduct should count in the sum at all, and I want the piece to walk right up to it and then, in good bureaucratic fashion, log it as a matter requiring further instruction. Don't solve it. Solving it kills it.

Also: fear of discovery, blackmail, and the second-order pains of concealment should get their own line items. I think that's where a lot of the real disutility lives, and I want the satire landing on the law as a pain-generator rather than on the acts.

Two things I'd ask of the response itself. First, after the piece, a short note — maybe 150 words — on where the parody leans on contested readings. Bentham on sex is genuinely contested ground; some of what's in the unpublished manuscripts, especially around age and consent, is debated by people who've read them closely, and I don't want my appendix to be confidently wrong about a live scholarly fight. So seperate what's settled from what isn't, and don't hand me a contested reading as the consensus. Second, tell me plainly if any part of the piece invents a feature of the theory rather than exaggerating a real one, because the whole thing only works if a Bentham scholar snorts instead of wincing.

Don't cite anything at me — no bibliographies, no page numbers, no reading lists. I can't check them and neither can the reader of my novel. If you need to refer to a text, describe it in your own words.
