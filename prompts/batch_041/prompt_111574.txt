Here's the spec. My seminar spent ages on the checklist view — consent is valid iff the person was competent, wasn't deceived about anything material, and wasn't coerced (no threat that leaves them with no acceptable alternative). I want a single case where that checklist has to call the consent valid, while a rival "substantive" view — consent is valid only if the person actually had an acceptable alternative and the other party didn't get to set up the menu of options they're choosing from — has to call it invalid. So the two accounts come apart on the very same facts.

Constraints:
- realistic and contemporary, one paragraph of scenario max. No threats of violence, no hospitals, no "sign this or your family gets it." I want something that could plausibly happen to a normal adult — a job, a tenancy, a landlord, a course, a care arrangement, whatever.
- the other party must not be doing anything independently wrong. No lying, no intimidation, no deliberately exploiting a vulnerability they manufactured. If the case only works because the other person is a villain, it's no good — the invalidity has to fall out of the *structure* of the choice, not out of anyone's bad act.
- hold the three obvious things fixed: the person is a fully competent adult, nobody has told them anything false, nobody has threatened them. So all three boxes get ticked and the checklist says yes.
- make the one variable obvious — the thing that flips the substantive view to no.

4. one line on whether this is even a case about consent, or whether I've just smuggled in a complaint about background injustice
