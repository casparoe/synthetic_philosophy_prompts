Ok so I'm putting together an application for a small internal grant in my department — it buys me one semester of course release, so realistically about four months of actual work — and I need a short proposal, two pages max, they're strict about it. It has to be philosophy of mind / self-related.

Here's my half-formed idea and I'm honestly not sure yet if it's any good: when people say the self is a center of narrative gravity, they're leaning on the physicist's center of gravity — something real but not an extra part of the world, just a fact about how the parts are arranged. I think the analogy breaks in a way nobody's really pinned down. A center of gravity is fully determined by the mass distribution and doesn't depend on anyones purposes. A narrative is picked out and shaped by whoever's telling it, for whatever reason they have. So if the self is a narrative posit, it's real in the way a character in a story is real, or maybe the way a border or a price is real — an institutional or social kind of reality — not the way a center of gravity is real. Those are really different kinds of "real but not a thing," and I think alot of the disagreement in the literature is people sliding between them without noticing.

What I want the project to do is take that three-way split seriously — (a) physical abstraction, like a center of gravity; (b) social/institutional posit, like a border or a price; (c) fictional entity, like a character — and ask which one the narrative self actually is, and whether the answer shifts depending on which role we mean: the self as the protagonist of the story, the self as the one doing the telling, or the self as whatever the word "I" picks out. My suspicion is the account is stable for the first role and quietly borrows authority from the second, and I want to know whether it survives being pulled apart.
