I'm on the membership committee of a small housing co-op, and we are redesigning our intake process. The current process is a weekend of hard communal labor—clearing a lot, scrubbing common areas, cooking for everyone—that everyone agrees is grueling and, in a narrow economic sense, pointless. The founder wants to keep it, arguing that only people who really want to live here will endure it. A newer member wants to replace it with a $400 deposit, refundable after a year, arguing that money is a cleaner proof of seriousness and that the labor requirement just selects for people with free weekends. A third proposal would let applicants choose either the labor or a nonrefundable fee, with the fee going into a hardship fund for members who fall behind on dues. A fourth would have a current member sponsor the applicant and forfeit a $200 credit if the applicant leaves within a year, on the theory that this avoids pointless suffering and makes someone stake their own reputation on the newcomer. Assume we cannot screen directly on income or free time, and that applicants know all the rules. I worry that the fee option turns a genuine test into a price, while the labor requirement may exclude excellent members who cannot give up a weekend. Which design should we adopt if our goal is to predict who will actually stay and contribute, and how should we think about the fact that the labor is deliberately wasteful? I am not looking for general advice about inclusivity or co-op culture; I want to understand what each arrangement does as a test of commitment, and whether the pointlessness of the labor is a bug or a feature.
