Ok, novelist here — I'm about two-thirds through a book about a billionare who bankrolls a population-ethics institute, and I've got two characters who are basically the two camps in this fight, and I keep writing them like one of them is obviously right, which is cheating and my editor will smell it.

So here's what I actually need from you. Take a totalist — the view that we should just maximize total welfare, more happy people is more good, that whole picture — and take the other side, someone who thinks there's a critical level, or a person-affecting constraint, so that adding lives that are only barely worth living isn't automatically a gain. Write me the joint statement those two could actually sign at the end of a real good-faith adversarial collaboration. Not a dialogue, not a debate transcript — an actual document with both their names on it.

What I want in it:

A numbered list of claims both can sign — about what's even being aggregated, about whether 'better than' comes out transitive once you start adding people, about the mere addition paradox, about the non-identity problem, whatever is genuinely shared. And no weasel claims, nothing that's true by definition — if it's vacuous I can't use it.

Then the disagreements, each one phrased the way that side would want it phrased for themselves — the totalist's version of the totalist claim, the other's version of theirs, in framing the other can live with.

Then the cruxes. I have a hunch one of them is whether a life that's barely worth living counts as a good or merely as not-a-bad, and another is whether the sheer number of people can matter in itself. But you tell me — and for each crux give me a concrete case both sides agree would actually bear on it, plus what each one predicts about that case, written down in advance. Put the cruxes and predictions in a table if that's cleaner. Use this case if it's any help: a choice between a small population of very happy people and an enormous population where everyone is mildly content and no one is ever miserable — then a variant where those extra lives last a single day. Does duration change the verdict for either of them, or is that a red herring?

And if you think part of their fight is just words — that they mean different things by 'better', or by 'welfare', or that one of them is answering a different question — say which part and why.

One more thing, and it matters for the book: keep the settled stuff and the contested stuff clearly separate. Don't tell me the repugnant conclusion is refuted, don't tell me totalism is the consensus or that it's been abandoned — tell me what people who actually work on this agree on, and where it's still genuinely open. If some part of this is a live dispute among the people who know it best, I want it flagged as such rather than smoothed into a tidy answer. I'd rather the document admit uncertainty than paper over it, because I will definitely get letters.
