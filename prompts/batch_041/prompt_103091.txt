Van Fraassen's reflection principle is usually introduced to me through an analogy, and I want to know how much of the principle the analogy can actually carry. The picture runs like this. My future self is like an expert. An expert knows more than I do about some question, so I defer to them: whatever credence they end up having, that is the credence I take on. The reflection principle, on this telling, says the same about my own future — my present credence in a proposition ought to equal my expectation of my future credence in it — because my future self stands to my present self as the expert stands to the layperson. The moral usually drawn is that reflection is nothing more than the ordinary demand that I not treat my better-informed future self worse than I would treat a well-informed stranger, and that refusing to defer to my own future credences is simply incoherent. (I have also seen the principle glossed as the requirement that my credence behave like a fair bet under my own expected updating; if that framing is doing the same work, hold it to the same audit.)

I suspect the analogy is being asked to do more than it can, but I do not yet see which parts of it are load-bearing. Give me an audit, not a survey.

Set out the correspondence first: what plays the role of the expert, of the expertise, of the deference, and of the single question both parties are answering; and state, in your own terms, what the principle asks and what it is conditioned on.

Then locate where the mapping breaks. I am most interested in these points: whether deference to an expert is earned by a track record that my future self need not possess; what becomes of the analogy when I know my future self will be drunk, distracted, bribed, or otherwise corrupted, or will simply have forgotten evidence I now hold; whether the expert picture quietly assumes that the future credence is reliably responsive to evidence, when reflection appears to demand deference whether or not it is; whether the analogy has anything to say when the question is not what the world is like but who or where I am; and the reflexive worry that our grounds for deferring to experts are themselves something like reflection, so that the analogy cannot ground the principle without circularity.

Then sort these failures. Which of them genuinely threaten the principle, and which are mere friction that a better framing would dissolve?

Then reach a verdict and defend it. Is reflection a genuine requirement of ideal rationality, a defeasible heuristic that other considerations can override, or a norm worth abandoning? Do not end on a non-committal note; commit to a position, and say what would have to be true of a future self for the principle to bind with respect to it.

Finally, if the expert is the wrong picture, offer a better one, and say precisely what it captures that the expert analogy misses.
