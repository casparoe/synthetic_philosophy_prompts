I'm a science journalist at work on a long feature about the theology of Isaac Newton — the private manuscripts, the anti-Trinitarian convictions, the chronology of empires — and I've run into a passage in the General Scholium, the theological coda he attached to the second edition of the Principia, that I can paraphrase but not really explain. In the 1729 Motte translation the key sentences run like this:

"This Being governs all things, not as the soul of the world, but as Lord over all: And on account of his dominion he is wont to be called Lord God, or Universal Ruler. For God is a relative word, and has a respect to servants; and Deity is the dominion of God, not over his own body, as those imagine who fancy God to be the soul of the world, but over servants."

A little further on he adds:

"For we say, my God, your God, the God of Israel, the God of Gods, and Lord of Lords; but we do not say, my Eternal, your Eternal, the Eternal of Israel, the Eternal of Gods; we do not say, my Infinite, or my Perfect: These are titles which have no respect to servants. The word God usually signifies Lord; but every lord is not a God. It is the dominion of a spiritual being which constitutes a God; a true, supreme, or imaginary dominion makes a true, supreme, or imaginary God."

And then, in the middle of the discussion of God's duration and presence: "He is not Eternity and Infinity, but Eternal and Infinite; he is not Duration and Space, but he endures and is present."

I can see that something is being done with the word "God," and my rough working gloss is that Newton is denying that "God" names a nature or a substance and treating it instead as a title a being holds by virtue of ruling — which would let him call the Father God and the Son God without conceding that they share one essence. A friend who works on early modern philosophy tells me this is the load-bearing move of Newton's anti-Trinitarianism, and that it anticipates the contemporary idea that "God" behaves more like a definite description than like a proper name. I don't need a reading list, and I'd rather not be handed a summary of what "the literature" says; what I want is the argument rebuilt from the text in front of me, step by step, with the joints tested. Specifically: what work is the grammatical test with "my God, your God" against "my Eternal, your Eternal" supposed to do, and does it do it? Is "God is a relative word" a claim about language, about metaphysics, or about how a title is conferred — and does the argument survive under each of those readings, or only one? If dominion is what constitutes a deity, doesn't God's being God come to depend on there being creatures to govern, and how is that squared with the necessity and self-sufficiency he is claiming two sentences later, and with the odd formula about not being Eternity and Infinity but being Eternal and Infinite? Where exactly does the text stop and my friend's gloss begin? I'd like the weak points identified and the stakes of each made clear, so that when I write about this passage I know which claims are Newton's and which are mine.
