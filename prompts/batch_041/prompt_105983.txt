I have a bunch of gut reactions to how people should handle being wronged, and I want to know if there's actually a principle behind them or if I'm just making it up case by case. Here they are.

Someone talks over me in every meeting; I flatly call it out each time until it stops — I admire that, no notes. Some guy at a bar insults my dad, who died last year; I deck him — not okay, I get the impulse but it's wrong. A friend makes me the butt of a joke in front of six people; I laugh it off and never mention it — fine, maybe even a little admirable. My old boss underpaid me for three years; I just quit and never said why — this feels weak to me, I don't like it, even though nobody was harmed by my silence. A stranger makes a crack about my accent that stings for a week; I later demand a written apology — wildly out of proportion, that's worse than the original slight. My kid gets bullied; I march to the school and insist the kid be punished in front of the class — I think this is wrong but I honestly can't say why. A colleague takes credit for my work in front of the whole division; I forgive him privately, never ask him to correct the record, never tell anyone — I want to call this admirable and I can't quite. I was harassed at work; I went through the formal complaint process knowing full well it'd cost me socially — I think that's clearly right, and the fact that it cost me is part of why.

So: is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? State it precisely, tell me which case forces each part of it, and say what it predicts for cases I haven't listed. I especially want to know how it handles the pair I can't square — the punch and the harassment complaint both impose a real cost on someone who wronged me, so why does one seem right and the other not? And the pair where quitting silently feels weak but forgiving the colleague silently feels admirable, when both are just... not saying anything.

The three broad styles I'm circling are: defend your standing on the spot, refuse to let other people's opinions of you matter at all, and insist the wrong be publicly named and acknowledged. Does any single principle actually vindicate calling one of those three superior, or does each one look good or bad depending on the case, and the whole "which culture is better" question is malformed?

The one I keep flip-flopping on: a mentor tears apart my work unfairly at a conference in front of people I respect; I say nothing there, then six months later send them a careful private letter explaining why it was unfair and asking them to acknowledge it. Is that dignified, weak, or petty? I genuinely can't tell, and if your principle can't decide it either, just say so.
