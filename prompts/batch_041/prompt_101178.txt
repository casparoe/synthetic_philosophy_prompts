I'm putting together a position paper on secession and self-determination for a political philosophy seminar, and before I commit to a line I want to check whether the verdicts I actually hold can be captured by any single principle. Here is where my intuitions land on particular cases, stated as bluntly as I can manage:

1. Scotland leaving the United Kingdom after a referendum agreed with Westminster and carried by a clear majority: legitimate.
2. Quebec leaving Canada after a unilateral referendum carried by a clear majority, with Ottawa refusing to negotiate terms: legitimate — though this is the case I keep going back and forth on.
3. Catalonia declaring independence on the strength of a referendum held over Madrid's objection, on a turnout that most of the region's unionists boycotted and with the result disputed: not legitimate.
4. Kosovo's declaration of independence from Serbia without Belgrade's consent: legitimate.
5. Crimea's absorption by Russia after a snap referendum held under military occupation: not legitimate.
6. South Sudan's secession after decades of civil war, via a negotiated referendum: legitimate.
7. A wealthy, contented region with no serious grievance holding a unilateral referendum and voting 52% to leave: not legitimate.
8. The same region, had its residents spoken a distinct language and been denied any lawful route to greater autonomy for a generation, voting 75% to leave: legitimate.
9. A sub-region attempting to secede from a state that itself seceded legitimately, even if a local majority within the sub-region wants it: not legitimate.
10. A region whose boundaries were drawn by the departing leadership itself, precisely so that the departing group would form a majority inside them: not legitimate.

I suspect these add up to something, but I cannot see what. Please tell me which principle, or small set of principles, comes closest to getting all ten, and be honest about which verdicts any such principle has to overturn. I want the tensions among my verdicts ordered by how severe they are rather than listed as though they were all equally bad, and I want to know which single revision would resolve the most.

Finally, I would like whatever principle you land on to say something about a case I have not listed: a region split almost evenly, where one half belongs to a distinct national group concentrated in the region's cities and the other half are settlers from the parent state who moved there under state encouragement.

Give me the whole thing as a JSON object with the following fields:

- "candidate_principles": a list of objects, each with "name", "statement" (the principle in one or two sentences), "cases_it_gets_right" (the case numbers), "cases_it_must_overturn" (the case numbers), "strongest_support" (the case that most motivates it), and "worst_case" (the case it handles least plausibly).
- "recommended_principle": an object with "name", "statement" (your repaired principle, stated precisely), and "clauses": a list of objects, each with "clause" (one clause of the principle) and "case_that_forces_it" (the case number that makes the clause necessary).
- "case_audit": a list with one entry per case, each with "case_number", "my_verdict", "recommended_principle_verdict", "agrees" (true or false), and "if_not_why" (empty if it agrees).
- "tensions_by_severity": a list of objects ordered from most to least severe, each with "tension" (the two or more verdicts pulling against each other), "cases_involved", "severity" ("high", "medium", or "low"), "cheapest_repair", and "what_the_repair_costs".
- "unlisted_case": an object with "case" (a short restatement of the split-region case), "predicted_verdict", and "reason".
- "closest_single_revision": a string naming the one change to my verdicts that would do the most to make the whole set hang together.
