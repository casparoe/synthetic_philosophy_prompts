I've been reading Turing's 1950 Mind paper again, specifically that weird section near the end where he brings up extra-sensory perception, and I want a dialogue that takes it seriously rather than treating it as a goofy footnote. Setup: two people, both of whom have read the paper and neither of whom is a rube. One of them thinks Turing's ESP move is actually a devastating methodological point that everyone skips over. The other thinks it's a rare moment where Turing's epistemic standards slipped, and that the whole thing collapses once you look at it closely. Neither should be a strawman and they should actually engage with each other's specific arguments, not just trade monologues.

Some stuff I'd like them to fight about, and please have them push back and forth rather than each getting one big speech:

First, Turing seems to think the evidence for telepathy is strong enough that a reasonable person can't just dismiss it, and then he says the imitation game should be run in a room that screens it out. Is that a principled experimental control, or is it Turing quietly admitting his test only measures the thing he wants if you first stipulate away a channel that real minds might use? If humans are telepathic and machines could be too, is excluding it arbitrary?

Second, there's a funny asymmetry: if the interrogator can read the machine's mind, or the machine can read the interrogator's, who does that help? Turing seemed to think it favored the machine in some setups, which seems backwards. Have them argue about whether telepathy would make the game easier or harder to pass, and whether that even makes sense as a question.

Third, the deeper one: if ESP is real, does the whole behaviorist-flavored logic of the test fall apart, since you'd be testing a capacity that isn't reliably channeled through the text interface at all? And if ESP isn't real, what was Turing doing conceding it? Was he being generous to a fringe view for rhetorical reasons, or did he genuinely believe the stats?

Fourth, have one of them argue that this section tells us something about Turing's actual metaphysics of mind, and the other argue that it tells us nothing and we're all overreading a paragraph.

Keep it conversational and in-the-weeds, like two people who've argued about this before. And please, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no numbered lists, just continuous prose the way you'd write a long email to a friend who shares the obsession.
