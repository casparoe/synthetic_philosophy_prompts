I have been trying to work out, case by case, when one is entitled to flatly say something, and I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to. Please treat what follows as my considered intuitions rather than as any settled doctrine, and do not assume I have a theory already in mind — I may just be inconsistent.

My cases, with my verdicts:

1. I have checked the timetable and know the 8:14 exists; I tell a stranger at the platform, "the 8:14 exists." Permissible.
2. I have not checked and am merely fairly confident; I say, "the 8:14 exists." It does exist. Impermissible — I had no business stating it, and I do not think its turning out true rescues me.
3. I have strong evidence, I believe it, and it is false; I state it. Impermissible, though I feel I am less to blame than a deliberate liar.
4. I state something true, but I believe it only because I half-remember a source I cannot place and that happened to be right. Impermissible — it is a guess wearing the clothes of a statement.
5. I know my colleague missed the deadline; I say, "he did not submit by the deadline," knowing this is true, intending my listener to conclude he is careless. The statement is true and I know it, yet I feel I have done something wrong.
6. A friend rushing for a plane asks which gate; I answer "gate 12" on a hunch, with no knowledge at all. He will double-check the board anyway. I lean permissible, which sits badly beside my verdict on case 2.
7. While rehearsing a play I say, "the bridge is out." No one takes me to be telling them anything about the world. I do not think the question of entitlement even arises here.

The tentative principle I keep reaching for is this: one may flatly say that P only if one knows that P — where "knows" is meant in the demanding sense, so that a true belief arrived at by luck or by a source one cannot vouch for does not count. I suspect it captures cases 1 through 4, but I cannot see how it accommodates my unease in case 5, where I do know, or my leniency in case 6, where I plainly do not. Perhaps I am conflating two things — whether I have the standing to be relied on, and whether what I convey is fair to my hearer — and I want you to tell me whether that distinction is doing real work or is just an excuse.

What I would like from you:

- State precisely the principle or small set of candidate principles that best fits my verdicts, giving for each clause the case that motivates it.
- Tell me honestly which of my seven verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate, and, if you must overturn one, which it should be and why. I would rather have a simple principle that costs me a verdict than an elaborate one that saves them all by fiat.
- Say what your favored principle predicts for a case I have not ruled on: I have just "seen" that the answer to a puzzle is 47 — not inferred it, not remembered learning it, it simply arrives with full conviction and, as it happens, is correct. Does that count as the kind of knowing that licenses me to say it, or as the kind of guessing that does not?
- Where your principle turns on a distinction I have not drawn, name the distinction and show me where my cases force it.

End with a final line of the form \answer{...} enclosing the short name you would give the norm that best fits my verdicts, or "no single norm" if you conclude that none does.
