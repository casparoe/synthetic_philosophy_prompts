I'm a retired field ecologist — spent most of my working life at a small research station counting plants and trapping small mammals — and since retiring I've been reading philosophy, mostly ethics, and I've got quite taken with the debate about wild animal suffering, which as a working scientist I never once thought about. I'm hoping you can help me with a person I can't read.

My old colleague at the station, Ruth — she's a vet, retired now, and has very firm views about when we should and shouldn't step in. Over the years I've collected these:

- She was all in favour of treating a skin disease across a whole population of native marsupials, once the disease had arrived with livestock.
- She was dead against culling a herd to stop a disease that had turned up on its own, even though it kills slowly.
- She's against feeding animals through a bad winter — says it just concentrates them, spreads disease, and props up animals that would of died anyway.
- She's completely fine with an individual animal that's been hit by a car or tangled in netting being picked up and treated.

Now the station has a proposal in front of it. A parasite that's been in the area as long as anyone can remember — nobody brought it in — causes a slow, painful death in a lot of the native mammals. There's a cheap, safe treatment, no side effects on anything else, and you can dose the whole population with bait. They asked me for a view and I said I'd ask someone who thinks about this properly.

So: will Ruth be for it or against it? And what rule is she actually running? I can see at least two rules that fit everything she's said so far, and I don't think they give the same answer here — so tell me what each one predicts, which you'd bet on and why, and what case would settle it, since this proposal maybe isn't the one that does. I care less about getting the right answer than about whether I've got her right.
