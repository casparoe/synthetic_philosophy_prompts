Write a sestina in which the six end-words are the recurring episodes out of which one person's self-narrative is woven — a departure, a betrayal, a place, a smell, a name, and a death — so that the form's permutation of those end-words enacts the way the same handful of memories is recombined every time the person tells the story of who they are. The poem should dramatize the deflationary view that the self is a center of narrative gravity: an abstraction posited to organize the episodes, real enough to be the thing the telling orbits, but not a further entity over and above the episodes and the telling of them. Let the envoi be the moment the speaker notices that the remembering first-person voice is itself a product of the memory-work rather than its author. Do not let the poem settle whether this is a deflationary discovery (there is no self, only the story) or a realist one (the story genuinely constitutes the self) — keep both readings live at the end. After the poem, add a short prose note stating where the poem takes liberties with the idea or leans on a contested reading of it, how confident you are that it captures the position accurately, and which single line you are least sure of.
