This June I'm giving the commencement address at the small liberal arts college I graduated from, and I've been stuck on it for weeks. It's an ordinary enough occasion — four hundred graduates, their families, a few trustees, and a president who in her invitation suggested I remind them that no generation has ever mattered more — but it has become personal, because my own daughter is in the class, and because I've spent the past few months reading about an argument I can't get out of my head, the one people call the hinge of history hypothesis: the claim that because the future could contain an enormous amount of whatever it is we value, and because what we do now may shape that future in ways later generations won't, we might be living at the most consequential moment there has ever been. I teach nineteenth-century history, and my instinct is to be skeptical, since every period I study was certain it stood at a turning point. But I also can't shake the thought that if the argument is even roughly right, then the platitudes I'm expected to deliver — follow your passions, be kind to the people near you — are either the most important sentences anyone could say to these graduates or a pleasant distraction from the only thing that matters.

What I want is a speech that takes the idea seriously enough to be honest about it, and that doesn't flinch from the hard part: what the hypothesis implies for an ordinary good life, for the graduate heading to nursing school, for the one moving back to a town of nine hundred people, for my daughter, who has already told me she is afraid of wasting her life. I don't want a lecture in decision theory, and I don't want to hand four hundred people in their early twenties a weight they cannot carry; I also don't want to smuggle in the usual commencement flattery and call it philosophy. So please write the address, roughly fifteen hundred words, in a register that will hold the grandparents in the back rows as well as the two philosophy majors in the front, engaging honestly with the strongest objection you can find to the hinge idea — including the thought that we cannot know the long-run consequences of anything we do, so that telling these graduates their choices matter cosmically may amount to telling them nothing at all. Please don't pad it with quotations from famous people; I want the argument itself to do the work.
