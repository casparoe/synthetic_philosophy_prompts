I'm writing a paper on money pumps and what they do and do not establish about the transitivity of preference in diachronic choice, and I need a small set of cases built to order rather than recalled.

The setup, shared by every case. There is one agent and one counterparty, and a fixed stock of ordinary goods: money, plus at most two ordinary consumables. The counterparty knows the agent's preferences and credences exactly, may make any pairwise offer those goods allow, may present offers one at a time in any order, may choose each offer in the light of the agent's replies, and may continue for any finite number of rounds. Nothing is hidden or misdescribed: the agent knows all of this and knows every offer that is coming, and she may refuse any offer at no cost. Her decision rule is the same in every case and is deliberately local: she accepts an offer if and only if the position the offer would leave her in is strictly better, by her preferences and credences at that moment, than her current position; ties are refused. No commitment devices, no side payments, no change of rule partway through. Call a case extractable if the counterparty has a finite sequence of offers, each of which the agent accepts, that ends with her holding strictly less money and no more of any good than she began with.

Fill one box each: (1) preferences transitive, extractable; (2) preferences transitive, not extractable; (3) preferences intransitive, extractable; (4) preferences intransitive, not extractable. For boxes 1 and 2, the agent's preferences must be transitive and complete and admit a utility representation at each moment, with no cyclic strict preference at that moment. For boxes 3 and 4, supply a witness triple: positions x, y, z with x at least as good as y, y at least as good as z, and z strictly better than x. Do not let the contrast between the extractable and the immune boxes come from the offers on the table: the agents in boxes 2 and 4 get an environment at least as rich as those in 1 and 3. And do not let immunity consist in refusing to trade — the agents in 2 and 4 should accept offers readily, over many rounds. Keep the four cases to a few sentences each, with the numbers small enough to check by hand, and put the weight on the analysis that follows.

(a) State, as a general criterion, the single condition that sorts the four cases into extractable and immune, and check it against each of them. The criterion may not be transitivity; if it is not, say precisely why transitivity is neither necessary nor sufficient for it, and prove the half of it that is easy to prove.

(b) For each immune case, prove that no sequence exists, and name the feature of the preferences that carries the proof.

(c) For each extractable case, walk the sequence, showing at each step that the offer is one the agent accepts under the stated rule.

(d) Replace the local rule with a look-ahead rule: accept an offer only if no continuation of the game from that point ends, judged by her own preferences at that moment, worse for her than her current position. Say which cases change and which do not, and what the pattern shows about how much of the pump rests on the local rule rather than on the shape of the preferences.

(e) Now take an agent whose credences are incoherent — say they sum to more than one — while her preferences over positions remain transitive. Under the same local rule, can she be led to a sure loss? Answer, and if the answer is no, identify the step of the usual sure-loss construction that fails, and say what this shows about the relation between a money pump and a Dutch book.

(f) Finally, say what the four cases together show about the money-pump argument for transitivity: what such an argument, stated at its best, actually establishes, and what its defender must concede in order to keep it.

Flag the case doing the most work, the one that is weakest, and the strongest objection a critic could raise against box 4.
