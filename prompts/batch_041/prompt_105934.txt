I got into a long argument over the holidays with my brother-in-law about whether we should be pulling down statues of Jefferson, or renaming things that honour Churchill, or — his favourite target — getting worked up about Columbus at all, and he kept coming back to the man-of-his-time defence, and he had this analogy he was really proud of. His line was: judging Jefferson for owning slaves, or Churchill for his views on empire and race, or Columbus for the whole business, is like getting angry at a doctor in 1700 for bleeding a patient, or like blaming Newton for not having discovered relativity. The knowledge just wasn't in the air yet, you can only be held to the standards that were actually available to you, and if you'd been born then you'd have thought the same as everyone else. It's a clean analogy and he used it to end the argument — he said if it holds, then the entire genre of retroactive condemnation is a category error, and the right measure is how someone compared to their own contemporaries, not to us.

I want to know how far it actually holds, because I suspect it's carrying more weight than it's entitled to. So give me the correspondence table first — what maps onto what here (the state of medical/scientific knowledge onto the state of moral opinion? the individual's epistemic limits? the consensus of the experts? the cost of dissenting?) — and then the places the mapping breaks. And I want you to take a definite side rather than laying out the options for me to pick from: does the analogy survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and does the "it's a category error to judge them" conclusion survive along with it? Three things I want you to actually engage with instead of waving at: (1) the difference between a factual discovery that genuinely nobody could have made yet and a moral question people were actively fighting about at the time — the abolitionists and the critics of empire existed, so was the relevant knowledge really "unavailable" in the way germ theory was, or is the defender quietly treating the majority view as the only view? (2) whether "the standards of the time" is even a single thing you can be held to, or whether the phrase just smuggles in "whatever most people happened to think"; and (3) the gap between not knowing something was wrong and knowing perfectly well but not caring, or profiting from it regardless. If the analogy collapses, tell me what the man-of-his-time defender should retreat to instead, and if there's a better analogy for whatever they're really getting at, hand it to me. Be opinionated — if you think my brother-in-law is basically right and I'm the one being smug and anachronistic, say so and say why.
