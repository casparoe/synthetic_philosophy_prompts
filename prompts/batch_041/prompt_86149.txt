I never went to uni. I work in insurance claims, which means I spend all day deciding whether something was done "intentionally", because that's basically the difference between paying out and telling someone to get lost. And the longer I do it the more I think the word isn't a description of anything in anyones head at all — it's a verdict. You work out whether you're paying, then you work out whether it was intentional. Same broken window, same dopey bloke, different word.

Then I found out there's a whole research thing about this, the Knobe effect, where a CEO brings in a new process that'll make more profit but will also harm the environment, and everyone says he harmed it intentionally, but when the exact same process helps the environment instead, nobody says he helped it intentionally. I wrote a blog post saying it's the same thing I do all day, it got shared way more than it deserved, and now the philosophy society at the uni here has asked me to come and give a talk on it.

The room will be: two people from the psych department who run experiments like that themselves and get a bit prickly when the method gets questioned, a couple of proper academic philosophers who I'm fairly sure think the whole research programme is just people being muddled about a word, a criminal solicitor who keeps saying juries do exactly this, and a pile of undergrads who did it in first year and think it's all settled.

What I want to argue is that it's got nothing to do with morality and nothing to do with what was in his head — it's about surprise. Bad side effects are surprising, good ones aren't, and "intentionally" is the word we grab when we're startled. Two predictions I'm going to stick my neck out on: make the good side effect the surprising one (say the lake only gets cleaned up because he cut a corner that happened to help) and people should start saying he did it intentionally too; and make the bad side effect harm nothing at all (he ruins a view nobody ever looks at) and the word should fall out of their mouths.

Can you get me ready for the questions? The ones this specific lot will actually ask, in their words, worst first, what I say back in about two minutes each, and where my answer is thin. Plus the one I'm dreading.
