I have just moved from our Paris office to run the Tokyo office, and at next month's regional management meeting I have to make the case for a position I have only recently come to hold: that chronic overwork in knowledge work is best explained as a positional competition in which long hours serve as legible sacrifice — the one proof of commitment everyone can read — and that this is why a statutory cap such as the 35-hour week treats the symptom, relocating the competition into after-hours email, business travel and so-called voluntary overtime rather than ending it. What I want instead is to change what promotion decisions are permitted to reward, on the view that the signal works only because our inherited Protestant reading of work still treats visible labor as evidence of character, and that karoshi is what the competition looks like when it has no floor.

The room will be about a dozen people: nine or ten Japanese managers who came up under the long-hours system and are loyal to it, two European colleagues who are partisans of the 35-hour week and will hear my proposal as a betrayal of a hard-won right, and one labor lawyer who cares only about what is enforceable. Prepare me for the question period: exactly five numbered items, no introduction and no conclusion, each at most three sentences, giving the objection in the voice of the person who would raise it, the reply I would actually deliver in a couple of minutes, and the point where that reply is softest. Put the first four in the order they will really come; make the fifth the question I am most hoping nobody asks. Where a matter is genuinely contested among people who know the field — whether hour caps create jobs, whether my positional-competition diagnosis is the right one or a rationalization of my own preference for a different working style — say so rather than presenting it as settled, and do not give me reading or citations, since I will be answering from the podium.
