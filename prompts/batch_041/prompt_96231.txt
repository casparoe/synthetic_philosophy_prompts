I am applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship and need a two-page research proposal. My half-formed idea concerns the Judy Benjamin problem and the broader question of how an agent ought to update her credences when the new information is not a proposition but a constraint on a conditional probability. The standard setup: an agent has a uniform prior over a four-cell partition—say, Red versus Blue territory crossed with Headquarters Company versus A Company—and then receives information that fixes the value of one conditional probability, such as the probability of being in Red given that she is in Headquarters Company. The probability axioms plus the constraint do not determine a unique posterior; several distributions satisfy them. The most discussed candidate is the distribution that minimizes relative entropy with respect to the prior, but this rule is not forced by any obvious epistemic principle, and it has features that many find troubling: it can be sensitive to the choice of partition, it can be order-dependent when several conditional constraints arrive in sequence, and it treats the constraint as a piece of evidence whose evidential import is unclear.

My suspicion is that the Judy Benjamin problem is really a cluster of distinct problems that have been run together. In particular, I want to distinguish three things that a piece of conditional information might be: first, information that a certain conditional credence of mine should now equal a certain value; second, information that a certain conditional proposition is true; and third, information that a certain conditional is assertible or that a certain conditional probability is objective. These are not the same, and the correct update rule may differ for each. If I am right, the apparent need for a single rule like minimum relative entropy is an artifact of conflating them, and the standard puzzle cases can be sorted into the cases where a unique update is determined and the cases where the underdetermination is genuine and should be accepted.

Turn this into a proposal. I need: a precise statement of the question, so that it has a determinate answer; an explanation of why the existing positions leave it open (I will add citations myself, so please refer to positions and arguments rather than to specific papers); a description of the method—what I would actually do, month by month, over twelve months—and why that method rather than the obvious alternatives; a statement of what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and an honest assessment of what could make the project too big for a one-year fellowship. The proposal should be written for a philosophy audience, not a technical statistics audience, but it should not water down the formal issues.
