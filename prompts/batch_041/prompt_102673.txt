For a while now I have been bothered by the way the simulation argument gets sold in my corner of the internet as a cure for fanaticism about the far future, and I would like a careful audit of the particular version of that claim I keep running into.

The version I have in mind is a post by a blogger I used to follow, and it goes roughly like this. Bostrom's simulation argument establishes that at least one of three things must be true: civilizations tend to destroy themselves before reaching a posthuman stage; posthuman civilizations have no interest in running ancestor simulations; or we are almost certainly living in a simulation. The blogger reads the trilemma as carving the probability space into three roughly equal pieces, so that the argument effectively tells us there is a substantial, call it one-in-three, chance that we are the simulated ones. From there he draws the payoff for longtermism. The far future that longtermists are so willing to sacrifice present welfare for would, in that scenario, be a simulated future rather than a real one, a mere shadow of the genuine future of the base reality; and once the stakes are discounted in that way, the astronomical figures that give fanaticism its grip lose much of their force, so the expected-value case for gambling on the far future weakens accordingly. Finally, since we cannot know which of the three horns we are on, he concludes that the rational posture is to hedge: the simulation argument should make us less willing to take large bets on the far future, and in that sense it dampens fanaticism.

Something about this feels off, but I cannot put my finger on where. Please audit it sentence by sentence. Which parts would someone who actually works on the simulation argument accept as fair, which are the kind of simplification they would wince at, and which are simply wrong? I am especially interested in whether the move from "we might be simulated" to "the far future is discounted" is licensed by anything in the argument, whether the conclusion that the argument dampens far-future fanaticism survives once the errors are corrected or whether the correction may cut the other way, and what a cleaned-up version of the blogger's claim would actually amount to. Since I have been persuaded by loose versions of this argument before, I would also like you to close by telling me which single consideration would most change your verdict — the argument or piece of reasoning that, were it to go the other way, would lead you to call the summary fair after all — and where in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to be wrong.
