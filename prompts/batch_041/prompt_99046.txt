I'm a graduate student in philosophy and I'm convening the scepticism reading group this term; we have a session on Moore coming up, and I'm tired of the usual hands-and-vats routine. What I want from you is a case built to order rather than a survey of the literature, and it needs to earn its keep: the point of the exercise is to show that a proof can satisfy everything Moore demands of a proof and still be dialectically useless against a doubter who is not being unreasonable, so a case that merely stipulates its own conclusion, or that smuggles in an irrational or dogmatic doubter, will not do.

The specifications are these. The scenario must be ordinary and non-philosophical — no brains in vats, no dreams, no evil demons, no simulation hypothesis — and it must involve two people: a prover and a doubter who genuinely doubts some particular proposition. The prover offers the doubter a proof that satisfies the conditions Moore lays down for a good proof, and the scenario must actually satisfy them rather than merely assert that it does: the premises must be known to the prover, the conclusion must follow from the premises, and the premises must be different from the conclusion. On top of that, each premise must be more certain to the prover than the conclusion is. The interesting requirement is this: the proof must be dialectically idle against the doubter — it must not be reasonable for the doubter to be moved by it — and yet the idleness must not arise from any failing on the doubter's side. The doubter may not be dogmatic, may not hold a false belief, and may not simply find different things certain; the failure has to fall out of the structure of the proof itself, so that even an ideally reasonable doubter who concedes everything the prover is entitled to concede would still not thereby acquire warrant for the conclusion. Keep the whole scenario to a single paragraph.

After the scenario, work through the following in order. State the premises, the inference and the conclusion, and show premise by premise why the prover's certainty exceeds the certainty of the conclusion. Then explain why warrant does not travel from the premises to the conclusion, and name the single structural feature that is doing the work. Say what happens to the case if that feature is removed — does it collapse back into an ordinary, dialectically effective proof? Then tell me what a Moorean ought to say to resist the diagnosis, and finish by saying whether the same construction can be run against Moore's own two-hands proof, or whether something about that case blocks it.
