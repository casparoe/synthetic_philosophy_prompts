I'm a novelist, and I'm assembling the documentary apparatus for a book in progress: the story is told partly through the papers of a forecasting outfit, and I want one of those papers written properly rather than sketched. What I need from you is a comic piece I can drop into the appendix — sustained deadpan, no winking, nothing that breaks the fiction, nothing that reads as though the author knows he is being funny.

The document is an internal risk memorandum from the research division of a fictional institute devoted to cliodynamics — the project of treating history as a dynamical system, with long secular cycles of roughly two to three centuries and a faster two-generation rhythm running inside them, driven by the standard structural-demographic variables: elite overproduction (the ratio of aspirants to available elite positions), immiseration of the commoner population, state fiscal distress, and a solidarity term (asabiya, a word the program has revived from a medieval source) that everyone agrees matters and nobody agrees how to measure. The memo is addressed to the institute's board and concerns positioning for the coming decade. Around 800 words, in numbered sections, with at least one footnote or appendix.

Everything in it must be invented: the institute, its authors, its numbers, its forecasts. Do not name any real researcher, and do not attribute the framework to anyone, so that a reader cannot check whether my institute's claims are real — only whether they are coherent. No citations, no reading lists, no bibliography.

The comedy has to come from the method rather than from buffoonery, so let the memo be calm, competent, and a little proud. The jokes should be the ones a working analyst would actually make: a forecast reported to the nearest decade; a confidence interval that widens every time the model is refitted; a threshold chosen after the fact and then treated as natural; a variable that is a metaphor wearing a number; a paragraph observing that the last three times the index reached this level the resolution was war, pestilence, or revolution, followed without a beat by a policy menu proportionate to that observation. Include a minority opinion from inside the institute — a marginal note or a dissent appended without comment — and make it the objection my specialist readers will be waiting for, but keep it in-house rather than turning the memo into a debate.

By the end, a reader who has never heard of secular cycles should know what the theory claims and feel the shape of the worry: that a model can be rigorous, illuminating, and still unable to say what happens next, and that accepting it makes certain things thinkable which one would rather not think. Be fair to the enterprise — I want the real thing with its temperature turned up, not a strawman — and please do not resolve it for me. The last line should land as a fact, not a verdict.
