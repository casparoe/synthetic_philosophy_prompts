I'm a journalist — science and ideas, mostly, the kind of stuff where you end up reading more footnotes then you'd like — and I've somehow talked myself into making an eight-part narrative podcast about a single week in 1848, when a crowd of women in a fishing village on the Nova Scotia coast boarded a schooner and carried off its cargo of flour during a food riot. The pitch that got it funded was history from below: the story of the people nobody bothered to write about. The problem is that I'm not a historian, I've got one research assistant and about five months, and the archive is almost entirely the paperwork of the people on top — a magistrate's deposition, the ship owners insurance claim, one line in a burial register for a woman who may or may not have been the ringleader. The women themselves left nothing I can find, and my editor wants to see the method before we record anything.

Two sets of people are already yelling at me. One says I'm romanticizing — where's the state, the market, the empire that actually set the price of flour? The other says that if the only records about poor people are records that institutions made for their own purposes, then every poor person in my story is going to come out looking like a criminal or a deviant, because those are the only poor people anybody wrote down.

So what I want is a procedure I can actually run — a checklist, in order — for deciding when a small case is telling me something real about a bigger structure and when I'm just telling one good story. What to ask of every source; what counts as enough to say this was typical rather than this was one person; how to read a document written in order to convict someone as evidence about how they lived; how to tell the difference between a case that's odd in a way that reveals the norm and a case that's just odd; when I have to zoom out to the state or the market even though it kills the intimacy; and where the method runs out and it's a judgment call I have to make and say so on air. Then run it on my flour riot and on a second case I'm inventing to stress-test it: a farmer's wife's household account book from the 1790s, which is the opposite problem — pages and pages in her own hand, and no way to know whether she's ordinary or a one-off. And tell me where this thing will fail me.
