I am preparing the lecture notes for the philosophy of language segment of an advanced undergraduate course, and I have been sent a short passage from a popular introduction — the sort of book a keen student reads the summer before the course — with the request that I say whether I can let it stand. Before I decide whether to correct it line by line or simply set it aside, I would like an audit rather than a verdict. The passage reads:

'Kripke's central achievement was to show that some truths are necessary yet knowable only a posteriori. His example is the identity of Hesperus and Phosphorus. Because "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" are proper names, and a proper name is a rigid designator — it tags the very same object in every possible world, much as coordinates fixed at the factory keep pointing to one place no matter how you travel — the identity holds in all worlds and is therefore necessary. Since we could not have known it without looking, it is a posteriori; and with this, Kant's assumption that necessity and a priority always travel together is refuted. The deeper lesson is that the necessity here is a fact about our words rather than about the heavens: in learning that Hesperus is Phosphorus we learn what the name was picking out all along, not some new feature of the planet. And because a rigid term is fastened to its object independently of any description, names carry no descriptive content whatsoever — which is exactly why we can sensibly say that Aristotle might never have taught Alexander. Rigidity, moreover, comes in degrees: "water" is more rigid than a definite description but less rigid than a name like "Hesperus," which is why the natural-kind cases remain contested. The upshot is that a posteriori necessity is confined to identity statements and the identifications of science; those are the only places where a necessary truth can await empirical discovery.'

Please go through it sentence by sentence and tell me, for each claim, whether a careful reader of Kripke would endorse it, whether it is a standard pedagogical simplification that does no real harm, or whether it is genuinely wrong — and for each error, what the correction is and whether the surrounding argument survives it. I am particularly interested in four things, and I would rather be told I am mistaken about any of them than be humoured: (a) the line that the necessity is "a fact about our words rather than about the heavens"; (b) the claim that names carry "no descriptive content whatsoever," together with the use it makes of Aristotle and Alexander; (c) the suggestion that rigidity comes in degrees; and (d) the closing claim about the scope of the a posteriori necessary. On that last point, if the scope is drawn too narrowly, I would like a better statement of where the boundary actually falls. The passage need not be salvaged; what I want to know is what to keep, what to soften, and what to strike before it goes in front of students.
