I sit on the patient-safety committee of a mid-sized teaching hospital, and we have a recurring quarrel that I have come to suspect is philosophical rather than clinical. After every serious adverse event our template obliges us to name a single "causal factor" — the thing that, in the committee's judgment, made the difference — and the committee splits cleanly in two. One faction reasons by asking what would have happened had the factor been absent: the drug not given, the nurse not pulled to another ward, the alarm not silenced. The other faction reasons by asking what we could actually have intervened on beforehand, and treats anything we could not have touched as background condition rather than cause; a resident has lately pushed this further, proposing that we count as a cause only factors that were abnormal relative to the ordinary run of events, on the theory that the uneventful is never the thing we mean to blame. On a case from last month the two methods came apart in a way I could not adjudicate on clinical grounds: a chronic staffing shortfall that no one on shift could have fixed, paired with a moment of inattention that produced harm only because of the shortfall. The but-for reasoning pointed at the inattention; the interventionist reasoning, applied at the level where we can actually act, pointed at the staffing policy.

A colleague in the philosophy department tells me this maps onto a long-standing split between counterfactual or difference-making accounts of causation and interventionist accounts, and that each has consequences I have not thought through. Please describe the most important implications of that split for how our committee should conduct these reviews. I want to understand where the two accounts will systematically disagree and why — not merely that they sometimes do — what each gets right that the other misses, how the contrastive character of causal questions (cause of this rather than what?) bears on our single-cause template, and, since we cannot suspend root-cause analyses until the metaphysicians settle matters, what a defensible practice would look like given that the two frameworks diverge. Assume I am comfortable with the standard but-for test and its familiar counterexamples; skip the textbook tour and tell me what follows for us.
