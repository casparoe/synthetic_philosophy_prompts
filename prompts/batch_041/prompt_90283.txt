For the past two terms I have been a regular at a small population-ethics reading group in my department, and one of the other regulars — a colleague I shall call Nadia, who works on intergenerational justice — cannot help pronouncing on every pair of outcomes we put up for discussion. I am now drafting a paper in which I want to describe her position accurately, and I would rather not mischaracterise her, so I should like to know what her settled verdicts already commit her to before I put my own case to her.

The scale we use in the group runs from 0, the point at which a life is exactly worth living, to 100, about as good as a life can go. Her verdicts, all delivered with confidence and, so far as I know, never revised: (i) an outcome of ten billion people all at 100 is better than one in which those ten billion are joined by another ten billion at 5; (ii) that same ten-billion-at-100 outcome is better than an outcome of ten quadrillion people at 3; (iii) an outcome of ten billion at 20 is worse than one in which those ten billion are joined by ten billion more at 60; (iv) an outcome of one billion at 95 is better than one of ten billion at 15; (v) an outcome of one billion at 100 is better than one in which those billion are joined by a trillion people at 6; and, less firmly, (vi) an outcome containing ten billion at 100 and a further billion at 50 is better than a uniform ten billion at 60. She is emphatic that the repugnant conclusion is a reductio of anything that implies it, and she is not, I am fairly sure, any kind of straightforward totalist.

The case I mean to put to her next is this. We must choose between two futures: in one, ten billion people exist, each at 90; in the other, those same ten billion exist at 90 and a further twenty billion exist at 70, with lives that are plainly good and who will never learn that there might have been fewer of them. Please tell me what you expect her to say about which of the two is better. I am less interested in the verdict itself than in the rule she seems to be running: set out the candidate principles that fit all six verdicts above equally well, say what each of them predicts about the new case, give me your best guess and the considerations that tell in its favour over the others, and tell me which further case would most cleanly separate those principles, so that I know what to ask her about next.
