In a seminar on personal identity, we spent most of a session on Hofstadter's suggestion that the self is not confined to one skull — that a person's I, the locus of first-person experience, can be present in reduced degree in the minds of those who know them, so that something of us persists in others after death. A classmate defended the idea with an analogy he says is standard in cognitive science: the mind is software and the brain is hardware. If a self is a program, he argued, then it is not tied to a single machine; it can be run on more than one, and what you leave in the people who knew you is a copy of that program running on their hardware, at lower resolution. He draws two conclusions from this: that the boundary of the self at the skin is an illusion, and that grief is irrational, since the person is not gone but distributed. He also likes the older image of a self as a whirlpool in a stream — no single drop of water is the whirlpool, yet it is real, and the same form can arise elsewhere — and treats the two pictures as saying the same thing.

I want an audit of the analogy, not a verdict on Hofstadter. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of program, hardware, memory, the running process, a port, a version, a backup — and what plays each of those roles in the target. Then tell me where the mapping holds and where it breaks, and separate the breaks that matter to his two conclusions from the ones that do not. Say what the analogy quietly imports: about what makes a thing the same thing over time (similarity of pattern versus causal continuity), about types versus instances, about whether a self must represent itself in order to be a self, and about whether personal identity is discovered or stipulated. He treats the possibility of two simultaneous instances of me as a bonus rather than a problem; tell me whether that is right. And tell me whether the whirlpool image repairs the software story or undercuts it, whether either of his conclusions survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and, if not, what a better analogy would be. No citations or reading lists needed — I care about the argument. Please write the answer in American English.
