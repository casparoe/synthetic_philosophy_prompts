I am preparing materials for a small, mixed graduate workshop on the nature of the alignment problem, with participants from philosophy, computer science, and policy, and I want to force us to stop trading labels. I have been working with a rough four-way distinction that I find useful but incomplete: behavioural alignment (what the system actually does), intentional alignment (what the system is trying to do, if anything), procedural alignment (whether the training process reliably selects for the right thing), and structural alignment (whether the system fits into a wider ecology of incentives, institutions, and other systems). The recurring disagreement is whether these are four facets of one problem, four separate problems, or symptoms of a prior confusion: one colleague insists that alignment is a single specification problem and everything else is engineering; another says that specification, optimisation, generalisation, and oversight are distinct and only loosely coupled; a third says that the technical framing is a distraction from the real problem of governance; a fourth says that the term alignment should be dissolved altogether. Please design a diagnostic decision tree that sorts these and other positions by the answers they give to concrete questions, rather than by the names they accept.

The tree should start from a commitment that is common ground in the workshop—say, that there is at least something worth calling the alignment problem—and branch on questions such as: (i) what is the object of alignment: behaviour, learned objective, intentions, training process, or deployment context; (ii) whether the central obstacle is epistemic (we do not know what we want), optimisation-theoretic (the training process selects for the wrong thing), generalisation-theoretic (the objective fails outside the training distribution), or institutional (no actor can credibly commit to a solution); (iii) whether the subproblems are independent or coupled, so that solving one changes the difficulty of another; (iv) whether there is a single target or a family of potentially conflicting constraints; (v) whether the unit of analysis is one system, a single deployment, or a multi-agent ecology; and (vi) whether the problem is well-posed, ill-posed but tractable through proxies, or ill-posed and better dissolved. Each branch node should be phrased as a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. Each leaf should carry a short name for the position, a one-sentence statement of its central claim, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Aim for between ten and fifteen leaves.

Where a combination of answers corresponds to no position anyone defends, say so and diagnose whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pairs of leaves the tree cannot separate—for example, positions that differ only in unobservable intent but agree on every behavioural prediction, or positions that collapse once you ask who the aligning agent is. Please write the tree as a nested outline or a text flowchart, followed by a short key that maps the main answer paths to the leaves. I do not want a bibliography, reading list, or citations; assume the reader has no internet access. The point is to make the logical space explicit, not to survey the literature.
