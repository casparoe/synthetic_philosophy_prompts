I am a third-year philosophy student in a seminar on the ethics of influence, and I am trying to settle a question before I write my final paper: how my instructor draws the line between manipulation and legitimate persuasion. Over the term she has given verdicts on a run of cases, and I want to work out the rule she is actually running before I commit to a reading of her.

Her verdicts, in the order she gave them:

1. A supermarket that plays slow music and diffuses a bakery scent to keep shoppers browsing longer, with no notice to customers, is manipulating them.
2. A charity mailer that leads with a photograph and a short paragraph about one named child, makes no false statement, but never mentions that it chose this format because a single identifiable victim reliably draws more donations than statistics about thousands, is manipulating its readers.
3. A financial adviser who recommends a particular fund while concealing that he earns a commission on it, and who warms the client up with flattery and a personal story, is manipulating her.
4. An op-ed that is plainly labelled as paid advocacy and that gives honest reasons for its policy proposal is engaged in legitimate persuasion.
5. A checkout page that arrives with the add-a-dollar-to-charity box already ticked, and with no notice that it is ticked, is manipulating the buyer.
6. A lecturer who tells a student that one research path is harder than the other, states which she would choose and why, and discloses that she runs a lab that would benefit if the student chose that path, is engaged in legitimate persuasion.
7. A gambling app that pings a user with a message suggesting a win is due, moments after a loss, without disclosing that the timing is engineered to encourage further play, is manipulating the user.
8. A teacher who tells a student plainly that the reading is long, that she would still assign it, and why, is engaged in legitimate persuasion.

The case I have to predict is this. A charity's fundraising letter opens with the same vivid story of a single named child as in case 2. This time, however, the first paragraph states in plain terms that the letter is using one child's story because research shows donors give more in response to a single identifiable victim than to statistics about many, that it does not claim this child is more deserving than any other, and that the writer's aim is to raise as much money as possible. Every claim in the letter is true, and neither the technique nor the writer's motive is concealed.

I would like you to do four things. First, set out the candidate rules that fit her eight verdicts equally well, and show briefly that each really does fit all eight rather than only the obvious ones. Second, say what each rule predicts about the fundraising letter. Third, give your best guess at her verdict and the reasons for preferring one rule over the others. Fourth, name the further case, as close to the edge of her pattern as you can put it, that would best tell the surviving rules apart, and explain why that case rather than another would do the work. I am less interested in whether she is right than in what she is doing.
