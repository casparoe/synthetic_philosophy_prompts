I'm an undergraduate currently enrolled in a course on cooperative game theory, and I'm also deep in a research project on multiobjective optimization, so I keep trying to read the two literatures through each other and I'd rather have the translation done properly than done loosely. Take the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution: the rule that, given a feasible set of utility pairs and a disagreement point, returns the point where the segment joining the disagreement point to the ideal (utopia) point meets the boundary of the feasible set, and that is pinned down by weak Pareto optimality, symmetry, invariance under positive affine rescaling of each player's utility, and individual monotonicity — conspicuously without Nash's independence of irrelevant alternatives, which is what makes it a rival to the Nash product solution rather than a refinement of it. Restate that solution, and the standoff between it and the Nash solution, in the vocabulary I actually use: feasible regions and the Pareto frontier, weighted-sum and epsilon-constraint scalarizations, reference-point and achievement-scalarizing methods, the ideal and nadir points, and the whole question of how a scalarization earns its choice of weights. I'd like the translation to be as faithful as the subject allows — don't quietly import optimization's assumptions and then call the result a translation.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which of the bargaining axioms come through cleanly as statements about feasible regions and scalarizations, which ones only survive if I fudge something (I suspect the disagreement point is doing illicit work — in optimization there is no threat point, only a lower bound or a reservation level, and those are not the same object), and which have no counterpart at all in how multiobjective methods pick a point off the frontier? I want to know specifically whether the optimizer's silence about fairness is a genuine blind spot or whether the bargaining theorists' axioms are just a disguised way of choosing weights, and, going the other direction, whether anything the optimization vocabulary can say — about convexity, about nonconvex frontiers where the utopia-point segment may not even intersect the frontier, about the geometry of the feasible set rather than its bare extension — exposes a limitation in the axiomatic picture that its own proponents would rather not discuss. Treat the mismatch as the interesting result, not as noise to be smoothed over.
