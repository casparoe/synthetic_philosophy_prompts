About a month ago I learned that a close friend had, over the better part of a year, been telling me things that were true — every sentence of them — while arranging them so that I would draw a false conclusion about something that mattered a great deal to me. He was not so much apologetic as precise: he pointed out, correctly, that he had never said anything untrue, and seemed to think that settled the matter. What unsettled me was my own reaction. It stung more than a straightforward lie would have, which is not what I would have predicted about myself, and I have been trying to work out whether that reaction is a moral insight or merely a bruise talking.

At dinner last week I described the whole business to two friends of mine — Ravi, a lawyer, and Cora, who took a master's in philosophy before switching fields — and they took it up with some relish. Ravi said there was a real distinction here and that my reaction was misplaced: a lie is a false assertion, a misleading remark is a true one, and the law itself treats a false statement under oath differently from conduct that merely leads someone astray. When you arrange the truth, he said, the hearer still has to do the inferring, and that is the hearer's business — a reasonable person asks a follow-up question. Cora replied that this rests everything on the boundary between what a sentence literally says and what it merely implicates, a boundary that linguists and philosophers have been fighting over for decades and that is far blurrier than it looks, since context supplies much of what a sentence is heard as saying; and that in any case the boundary is morally beside the point, because what is wrong with lying is not the falsity of the words but the fact that you set out to install a false belief in someone, routing around their ability to weigh the evidence — something that can be done just as well with true sentences. The morally interesting category, she said, is deception, and lying is one technique for it. Ravi came back with the objection that this makes liars of advertisers, and of anyone who says "lovely to see you" when they would rather be elsewhere; worse, it seems to exculpate the transparent liar — the witness who denies something everyone in the room watched him do. That man deceives nobody, so if deception is the wrong, he has done nothing wrong, which is absurd. Cora conceded that lying is not only about deception, but said this does not rescue Ravi's line: it shows that the wrong of lying has more than one source, and one of those sources — the standing expectation that you will tell me what you actually think — is violated just as much by a true sentence engineered to mislead. She added that the engineered version is often the worse of the two precisely because it is deniable, leaving the speaker an exit and leaving the person misled without standing to complain, which is more or less exactly what she believes happened to me. Ravi said that made deniability sound like cowardice rather than a mark of greater wrongdoing, and that "more calculated, therefore worse" is not a principle he would accept without argument, since some calculated acts are plainly better than their impulsive counterparts.

I could not referee any of this, and I would rather not simply pick the side that flatters my hurt feelings. Could you go through the exchange move by move — Ravi's initial distinction, Cora's reply about the instability of the said/implicated line, her deception-first account, Ravi's two objections, her concession and the deniability point, and his last rejoinder — saying for each whether it is right, partly right, or wrong, and why? Then, if you can, tell me what both of them are missing, and whether the question I actually care about — whether there is a moral difference between lying and misleading — has a single answer, or whether it splits into several that the two of them have been running together.
