ok, here's my situation. I run the study-abroad office at a mid-sized university — about 140 students a year, 18 destinations, and every one of them comes back or emails me from abroad with some version of the same question: do I actually have to do this? Do I have to tip 20% when the service was mediocre and the whole arrangement seems gross? Do I have to stand in a line nobody else is standing in? Do I have to wait at a red light at 1am on an empty street when everyone else just walks? Is it rude to eat on the train? One student who kept refusing to tip got into a shouting match with a server, another got pulled up by her host university for something that's completely unremarkable here, and one cheerfully paid a small extra fee to jump a queue at a public office and I still don't know what I think about that.

What I want is a one-page procedure I can put in the pre-departure handbook, that students will actually read on their phones. Something they can run in about two minutes in the moment, that tells them: follow it, don't follow it, or this is a judgment call and here's who to ask. It has to be honest about which steps are just my opinion dressed up as a procedure.

I'm not a philosopher but I've been reading around, and here's the pile of distinctions I've got, in my own words. Tell me which ones survive contact with the actual philosophy, which are junk or secretly the same as each other, and what single thing I'm missing.

- Some local practices are pure coordination: everyone does X because everyone expects everyone else to do X, and it could just as easily have been Y. Which side of the escalator, which door, whether you line up or crowd.
- Some are coordination, but the thing being coordinated is a scarce good, so the practice is doing fairness work — who gets the taxi, who's next.
- Some happen to be the local way of doing something you'd owe anyway: not sticking someone with a cost you created, not humiliating the person serving you.
- Some are backed only by the fact that people will be annoyed if you don't. I keep wanting to say annoyance isn't a harm, but I'm not sure it's nothing either.
- Some are actively bad — they encode a hierarchy, or make you the one funding something unjust, or require you to treat someone as beneath you. I assume the answer there is no, but I want the procedure to get there for the right reason rather than because I find it icky.
- A colleague gave me a regularity/norm distinction I like: sometimes everyone does X but nobody would care if you didn't, and sometimes nobody does X but everyone would care if you did. Those feel like completely different situations and my current draft treats them the same.
- She also says the convention people actually follow and the convention people expect *you* to follow come apart all the time in tourist-facing settings, because the service sector enforces things the locals themselves don't bother with. That one I don't know what to do with.

Two things I need the procedure to be honest about and can't work out how to state:

1. Whether being a visitor changes anything at all. My instinct is visitors get less latitude, not more — you're taking the benefits of a scheme you didn't help build, so not doing your bit looks like free-riding. A colleague says the exact opposite: you never agreed to any of it, so you can't be bound by it. Both of us sound right and I don't think we are.
2. Legality. Our general counsel wants a line in the handbook saying follow local law. I think that line is both too strong and too weak — there are unwritten conventions that matter far more than laws nobody enforces, and the fact that something is illegal doesn't by itself settle whether a student should do it. But I am not writing anything that tells a 20-year-old to break the law in a country where I can't help them. I need the procedure to keep those two things seperate and to send the student to the on-site coordinator the moment they pull apart, instead of inviting them to decide for themselves.

What I need from you, in this order:

Bottom line (~120 words): the paragraph I can paste straight into the handbook email.

Your distinctions, sorted (~300 words): which of my bullets is real, which is junk or just another bullet in disguise, and the one thing that's missing.

The procedure (~700 words): numbered steps in the order a student should run them, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, the points where the answer is simply no, and the points where it stops and hands off to a human. Keep it to something a jetlagged 20-year-old can execute.

The two-minute card (~120 words): the same thing as a bullet list they can screenshot.

Three runs (~600 words): run the procedure step by step on (a) tipping at a US sit-down restaurant where the server's pay depends on it and the service was mediocre, (b) crossing on a red light at night in a city where everybody does it and nothing's coming, and (c) paying a little extra to jump a queue at a public office. I want to see it give at least one answer I won't like, and I want you to say so out loud when it does.

Where this misfires (~250 words): the steps that are really me making a value call and pretending it's a procedure, the cases the procedure can't settle, and what I should be telling the coordinators.

What I still have to decide (~80 words).

Don't give me reading lists or citations, I won't chase them down — just tell me what the distinctions are and where my version of them goes wrong.
