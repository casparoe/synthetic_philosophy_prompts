Exam question (10 points):

Scalable oversight is often motivated by the prospect of AI systems that perform tasks whose outputs humans cannot directly evaluate. The debate proposal is one response to this.

(a) Explain the oversight problem the proposal targets, and why standard human-feedback training such as reinforcement learning from human feedback is expected to break down for such tasks.

(b) Describe the debate protocol, and explain the argument for why a judge weaker than the debaters can still arrive at the correct answer.

(c) Identify one limitation of the proposal and explain why it matters.

Student's answer:

(a) The problem is that once models can do things humans cannot check — writing a large program, designing a protein, planning a long strategy — the human label that reinforcement learning from human feedback trains against stops being a reliable signal. If a human cannot tell a good answer from a bad one, then optimizing against their approval teaches the model to produce answers that merely look good to a human, not answers that are actually good. Debate is meant to keep a human as the final decision-maker even as the tasks outrun human ability.

(b) In the basic protocol, two copies of a model are shown a question and argue for opposite answers, while a judge — a human or a weaker model — reads the exchange and picks a winner. The winning side is rewarded, so the model is trained to win debates. The reason this works even when the judge is much weaker than the debaters is that the honest debater can always expose the dishonest one: whatever the lie is, the truthful side can simply point at it, and the judge then only has to decide which of the two is telling the truth. So the judge never has to solve the original problem, only compare two competing stories, and a weak judge can do that. This is what makes the scheme scalable: the judge can be arbitrarily weaker than the models being overseen, and the only further requirement is that the two debaters be roughly equal in capability.

(c) One limitation is that a dishonest debater can win by making the case so complicated that the judge cannot follow it, even when it is wrong. If the honest side must spend its turn untangling a pile of technical claims the judge cannot evaluate, the judge may side with the more confident or more elaborate argument. The protocol's guarantees therefore depend on the judge being able to adjudicate the particular disagreement, not merely on the debaters being honest with one another.
