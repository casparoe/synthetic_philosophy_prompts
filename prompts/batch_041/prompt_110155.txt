I chair the animal-welfare and ethics committee at a large public aquarium, and at Thursday's meeting we vote on whether to list our octopuses as intentional agents under our enrichment and handling policy — a designation that would commit us to enriched housing, individual enrichment plans, a bar on invasive procedures without separate review, and keeper training in reading apparent preferences and moods. Our staff are split. The physiologists argue that the animals are stimulus-response systems and that a good design-stance account — sensory input, drive states, learned association — predicts everything we observe, so the listing would be anthropomorphism with a budget line attached. The keepers reply that the intentional stance predicts the animals better and more simply, and that this is what should settle it. One member has floated a tie-breaker he read about, drawn from a three-stances framework (physical, design, intentional): treat a system as having beliefs and goals only when the goal-and-belief description picks out a real pattern — that is, when it compresses the behavioural record better than any competing description that forgoes those terms, and goes on doing so on new data rather than merely redescribing what we already have.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the vote: a clear recommendation — adopt the test, adopt it with stated limits, or reject it — with reasons my colleagues can actually argue with, and an honest statement of the strongest case for whichever side I end up ruling against. Two matters need facing squarely. First, if the test works, does it tell us anything about the octopus, or only about the convenience of our descriptions — and if the latter, why should the committee not simply decide on other grounds? Second, can the design-stance side always be met by building a richer stimulus-response model, since a sufficiently detailed model fits any data, and if so does the test have any bite at all? Then say how the test would treat a further case on our books — a slime mould colony that reliably finds the shortest path through a maze to food — and identify the points where the committee will be exercising judgment rather than applying a rule.
