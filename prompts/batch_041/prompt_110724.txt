Panpsychism holds that phenomenal properties are fundamental and pervasive: that the basic entities posited by physics each possess some form of experience. Explain why the truth of panpsychism would not, on its own, license the conclusion that fundamental physics is a domain of moral concern — why universal experience does not entail universal suffering. I am not asking for a survey of the positions that have been taken on this; I want the inferential joints exposed.

Separate three claims and say what is required to move from each to the next: first, that fundamental physical entities have experiences; second, that those experiences carry valence, that is, that they are good or bad for the entity having them; third, that there are subjects whose welfare those valenced states constitute. For each transition, state the additional premise needed and how plausible that premise is independently of panpsychism. In particular, address whether a panpsychist is entitled to pan-sentience — the claim that the micro-level constituents of the world are bearers of morally relevant suffering — or whether valence is a separate and optional addition to the panpsychist picture.

Then take up aggregation in its ethical rather than its metaphysical form. Granting, for the sake of argument, that micro-subjects with valenced states exist, does the moral weight of a great many very small pains sum in the ordinary way, is it capped or discounted, and does the answer change if those micro-experiences never combine into a single macro-subject? This leads to a sharper difficulty: if it is indeterminate which micro-experiences belong to which subject — if the combination relation is not settled by the physics — then it may be indeterminate who is suffering, and how much, and the moral ledger cannot be drawn up even in principle. Say how seriously this threatens the ethical project, and whether it is a reason for indifference toward micro-experience or for special caution about it.

Finally, consider two worlds with the same complete physical description: in the first, the fundamental entities' experiential states carry valence; in the second, they are phenomenally present but valenced in no way. Say what, if anything, morally distinguishes them, and whether a panpsychist can state the difference without appealing to something over and above the physics. Use this to say at what grain of physical description the moral facts would have to be located, and whether the moral status of a composite being derives from, displaces, or merely accompanies that of its constituents.
