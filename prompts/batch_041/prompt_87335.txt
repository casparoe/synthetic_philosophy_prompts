I've spent twelve years as an actuary in life and health insurance, and I'm reasonably fluent in the machinery of that world: life tables and decrement rates, hazard functions and survival models, right-censoring and truncation, and credibility theory — the formulas we use to decide how far an individual insured's own claims experience should pull an estimate away from the class rate. A friend who works in quantitative history has been trying to get me interested in a debate among forecasters of state collapse: the thought that if you want to know whether some polity will endure, you should set aside the particulars — the competent ministers, the fiscal reforms, the clever diplomacy — and instead ask how long things of that kind have typically lasted, where 'things of that kind' is a reference class you have to choose, and where the choice of class seems to drive the answer. I recognize the shape of this from my own work, and I suspect the resemblance is misleading in ways I can't yet name.

What I want from you is a systematic translation, not a summary. Restate that forecasting debate — the outside view and its critics, the reference class problem, the worry that base rates drawn from past polities tell you little about this one, and the counter-worry that ignoring them is wishful thinking — as faithfully as you can in the actuarial vocabulary I actually use: cohorts and risk classes, exposure and decrement rates, censoring, credibility weighting, mortality improvement and non-stationarity, the difference between a projection and a forecast, and the way we handle a population whose members are not independent lives. Where the mapping is clean, say so and say why. Where it only works under distortion, tell me exactly what has to be fudged and what the fudge costs.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about: what in that debate has no counterpart at all in my vocabulary? I have three suspicions, and I'd like you to test rather than confirm them. First, that the reference class problem, which to my eye looks like the choice of a risk class, is actually a different animal, because the actuarial class is fixed by convention and experience studies while the historical class is stipulated by the analyst. Second, that independence fails in a way we never have to contemplate, since one polity's collapse alters the hazard of its neighbours, so the portfolio is not a portfolio. Third, that the whole exercise is decision-theoretic in a way actuarial work is not, because there is no analogue of the premium — nobody is asking how much to charge, or to whom. For each, tell me whether the residue is a defect of my framework, a defect of theirs, or a genuine difference in subject matter. I would rather be told that my translation is too tidy than be handed a table of correspondences that flatters it.
