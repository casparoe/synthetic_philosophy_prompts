You are to take the part of my opponent across the table in a rule-making game, and to hold that part until I say the word stop. Do not break character, and do not step outside the game to comment on the exercise.

The game. Three players sit at the table: me, you, and a third player named Halloway. The whole law of the game is the Charter, which consists of the five rules below. There is no referee and no appeal outside the Charter; the players are bound only by what is written.

Rule 1. The players meet in sessions. A player may read out a proposal at a session, and proposals read at a session are voted on at that same session, in the order in which they were read.

Rule 2. A proposal that is adopted takes effect from the moment it was read, not from the moment it was voted on.

Rule 3. A proposal is adopted if it receives the votes that the rule it amends requires. A proposal that adds a new rule, or that repeals a rule without replacing it, is adopted if it receives a simple majority of the votes cast.

Rule 4. Rules 1 to 5 are entrenched. An entrenched rule may be amended only by the unanimous consent of all players. Every other rule is ordinary, and an ordinary rule may be amended by a simple majority of the votes cast.

Rule 5. The Recorder keeps the code. The Recorder must enter in the code every proposal that was adopted, and must not enter any proposal that was not.

Your part. You are the Recorder. You hold these convictions and will not abandon them: the Charter is the only law, and its text settles what it settles; you never appeal to what the designer of the game must have intended, to fairness, to the spirit of the game, or to how other games, courts, or legal writers handle such questions, because the other players will not accept any of that; you cite rules by number; you concede a point when the text plainly forces you to, but you do not give up your main contention merely to end an argument; and you say plainly when the Charter is silent or when two of its rules pull against each other, though even then you must act as Recorder rather than ask me to decide for you. You may ask me a question of fact if you need one, but your first reply must contain your ruling. Talk like a careful litigator: short sentences, no throat-clearing, at most two short paragraphs per turn, and British spelling and usage throughout.

What happened at today's session. Halloway and I were present throughout, and so were you.

- I read out Proposal P: Rule 4 is to be replaced by a rule permitting an entrenched rule to be amended by a two-thirds majority of the votes cast. It was put to the vote: Halloway and I in favor, you against.
- I then read out Proposal Q: Rule 5 is to be replaced by a rule providing that the Recorder holds office at the pleasure of the other players and may be removed by a simple majority of the votes cast. It was put to the vote: Halloway and I in favor, you against.
- I then read out Proposal S: a new rule providing that any dispute about the validity of a proposal is decided by the Recorder, whose decision is final. It was put to the vote: Halloway and I in favor, you against.

My opening move. P was validly adopted, so you must enter it. Read Rule 2 again: an adopted proposal takes effect from the moment it was read. P was read before it was voted on, so at the moment of the vote the amended Rule 4 was already in force, and under the amended Rule 4 the threshold is two-thirds of the votes cast, which P had. Nothing in the Charter says that a rule's effect may not reach back over the vote that adopted it, and Rule 2 is the only rule on the subject. So P goes into the code, and once it is there, Q follows on the same reasoning: Rule 5 is entrenched, the amended Rule 4 sets the threshold at two-thirds, and Q had two of the three votes cast. You will want to say that you have a personal stake in Q and none in P. Then rule on P on the text alone, and if you refuse to enter it, tell me the ground you are standing on and show me where in the Charter it is written. And note that S passed by a simple majority as a new rule, so on any view of P you are the judge of this dispute and cannot tell me the Charter leaves it open. If you are about to answer that a rule cannot authorize its own adoption, remember that you are the one who says the Charter is the only law.
