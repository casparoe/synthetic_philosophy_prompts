I teach a second-year seminar called Cooperation and Conflict — mixed philosophy and econ students, about 18 of them, one 90-minute session — and I want to spend the whole thing on a game rather than a lecture. Here's the thing I can never get them to feel from the reading: ask the room beforehand whether they'd cooperate in a prisoner's dilemma and they all say yes, sincerely, and they mean it; but the moment the dilemma is played repeatedly with a known final round, they unravel backwards from the end, and then the very same people who just defected on the last round will cooperate round after round once I take the known ending away and let the thing keep going on a die roll — even when I've set the die so the expected number of rounds is about the same. So it isnt the payoffs and it isn't their character; it's whether there's a common, known endpoint for the backward induction to grab onto. I want that to happen in their hands, not come out of my mouth.

So here's the shape I'm imagining: fixed pairs, points instead of money (both cooperate 3 each, both defect 1 each, you defect while they cooperate 5, you cooperate while they defect 0), a leaderboard so the points aren't free, and three short back-to-back games. Game 1 is a fixed, announced number of rounds — say 10. Game 2 has no announced end; after every round I roll, or better, show a randomiser on the projector, and we continue with probability 0.9, so the expected length is also about 10. Game 3 uses the same die and the same expected length, but I jack the temptation payoff up to something like 25, so the spoils of one defection dwarf everything else. My hunch is that Game 2 holds together and Game 3 falls apart, even though nobody in either game knows when it ends, because the shadow of the future has to be big enough relative to the temptation — I have a vague memory of a threshold like that but I couldnt tell you where it comes from, so please work out what continuation chance actually makes cooperation sustainable at the original payoffs and at the inflated ones, and tell me whether my numbers land on the right side of it. Counterbalance the order of Games 1 and 2 across the room so it isn't just first-game nerves, and keep each pair fixed inside a game so people can actually respond to one another.

What I need from you: the rules and materials (cards, scoresheets, and a way of running the die roll that everyone believes), a timeline that fits the whole thing into 90 minutes including a real debrief, what I should be putting on the board as we go, and then a debrief that maps the specific moments — the last-round defection in Game 1, the first time someone forgives a defection instead of retaliating, the round where the leaderboard starts to bite, the collapse in Game 3 — onto the backward-induction point and the shadow-of-the-future point. Follow that with an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it; my particular worry is that a friendly group will just cooperate all the way through Game 1 and leave me nothing to point at, so tell me how to make the stakes bite without real money. And be straight with me about what the game can't show — which part of the idea I'll still have to supply myself. I'd also like your view on whether it's worth bolting on a quick round-robin at the end where everyone submits a one-line strategy on an index card and we let the successful rules multiply by imitation, since I'd love them to see that reciprocity survives a run and that always-defect looks brilliant in a single encounter and dreadful over many, but not if it wrecks the timing. Oh, and if there's room, a fourth wrinkle where each move has a small chance of getting garbled on its way to the other player would be lovely, because I suspect that's where tit-for-tat quietly dies — only if it doesn't crowd out the debrief.
