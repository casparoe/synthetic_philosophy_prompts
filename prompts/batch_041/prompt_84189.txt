As I've got it, the view is that there's an asymmetry between the two cases: if you'd bring into existence someone whose life goes badly, that's a bad thing to do, because they'd have been better off never existing at all; but if you'd bring into existence someone whose life goes well, that isn't a good thing, and not bringing them into existence isn't a loss to anyone, because there's nobody there to be deprived. So the first kind of person is worse off for existing and the second kind isn't better off for existing. I think this is Benatar's thing but I might be mangling it, and my flatmate reckons it's obviously just sour grapes dressed up as a principle.

What I want is a thought experiment built to order, for a short write-up I'm doing. The point of the exercise is to show him the view isn't just gloom — it hangs on one specific claim about whether coming into existence can ever be better for the person who comes into it, and I want the case to make that claim visible rather than just assert it. Specs: it has to be a choice between two options that a normal adult could actually face — no science fiction, no billions of future people, no more than three people whose lives are at stake, and no razor-thin margins (dont get the result by making two lives differ by a hair). The two views in play — the asymmetry above, and the plain "total" view that just adds up how well everyone's life goes and says more of that is better, including for people who wouldn't have existed otherwise — have to agree completely on every fact about how well off each person is; the split has to come out of the principles, not out of hidden uncertainty about the facts. And I want them to reach opposite verdicts about which option is better, not just different shades of the same verdict.
