Until a few months ago I treated this as an exam topic. Then a colleague I respect was dismissed for something she did after our manager had arranged matters so that she had no real alternative, and I found I blamed her anyway. I have been trying to work out what I actually think ever since, and my verdicts do not obviously fit together. I am not after a survey of the literature; I want the principle, if there is one.

Here are my verdicts. All but the fifth are cases in which the agent does something wrong, and I am asking only whether she is blameworthy.

1. Vera falsifies the audit on her own initiative. Unknown to her, a colleague had rigged the system so that had she hesitated, the falsification would have gone through anyway. I blame her.
2. The same, except that the rigging would have produced a different and worse act — a larger fraud, say. I still blame her for what she actually did.
3. Omar's hand types the falsification while a chip moves it. He watches, appalled, and would have stopped it if he could. I do not blame him.
4. Priya's decision to falsify is produced, in the actual sequence, by a chip. But the chip works only through the resentment she already has; it adds nothing to her reasons, and without it she would have been genuinely torn and might have refrained. I do not blame her.
5. A lifeguard whose reasons left her no room at all — she could not have done anything but dive — is praiseworthy, not merely excused. So the absence of alternatives does not by itself block credit.
6. Someone who could have refrained only in some trivial way — by doing it a second later, or with the other hand — is no better off for it. That kind of alternative does nothing for me; I blame her the same.
7. Nadia was raised into cruelty and is now cruel. Nobody interferes with her; every option is open. I blame her.

My provisional rule is this: you are answerable for what issues from your own reasons, unless something outside you intervened in the actual sequence that produced it. But that rule lets the chip excuse Priya and not Nadia, and I cannot say why a chip counts as an intervention and a decade of conditioning does not, except that one is a device and the other is a life — which I suspect is not a reason.

Is there a principle that gets all seven, or close? State it precisely, with the case that motivates each clause; if you need two principles rather than one, say where they divide. Say what it predicts for a case I have not listed (choose one yourself), and which of my seven verdicts it cannot accommodate, and why. Answer in no more than 800 words, in British English, and tell me how confident you are in the account and which point you are least sure of.
