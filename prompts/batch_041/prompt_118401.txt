I'm opening a unit on the knowledge argument with Lois Lowry's The Giver instead of the Mary's room setup, since every kid in the class has already read it, but the more I poke at it the more I think it isn't a Mary case at all and I want a reading that says why. Mary is a subject who has all the physical facts and is missing one experience; Jonas's community has surrendered the memories wholesale, color along with them, so they're not missing a facet, they're missing the file. And the memories don't arrive as facts, they arrive by touch, first-person and whole -- Jonas gets the sled ride and the rainbow handed to him the way Mary would if the world just beamed redness into her head, which is exactly what the thought experiment is supposed to rule out. So: does the novel dramatize the intuition the knowledge argument needs, or does it smuggle in a fantasy of transferable experience and then cash the intuition for free? My suspicion is that what Jonas actually gains is less a new quale than the capacity to be moved, and that the gap the book really cares about is between knowing the world is fine and having any reason to care about it -- which would make it a story about the connection between phenomenal knowledge and value, not about whether physical information is complete. Take the ability reply and the acquaintance reply seriously and tell me which one the novel honestly read ends up supporting (I bet neither, or both badly), where it resists the reading, and what it shows about the gap that the papers don't.
