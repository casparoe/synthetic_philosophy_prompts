**Draft: "Dominance without tears"**

The claim I want to defend is that "solvable by iterated dominance" is not a property of a game. It's a property of a game *plus* a decision about which strategies are allowed to do the dominating, and that decision is not innocent — it decides whether the notion is even about rationality.

Setup. One player picks among three options T, M, B; the opponent will play either L or R. Just the chooser's payoffs:

        L     R
T       1     0
M       0     1
B      0.4   0.4

(I've left the opponent's payoffs out — more on that below.)

B is strictly dominated: play T half the time and M half the time and you get 0.5 in each column, strictly better than 0.4. But B is not dominated by T (T gets 0 against R, which is less than 0.4) and not by M (mirror image). So if "dominated" means "dominated by some other single pure option," B is clean; if it means "dominated by some mixture," B is out. The two readings give different answers, and that's before we've even started iterating.

Now the part I actually care about. Why are we allowed to throw out dominated options at all? The usual story is the sure-thing principle: if one option is at least as good as another no matter what the world does, a rational chooser won't take the worse one. That's a state-by-state argument and it needs no probabilities — that's the whole appeal, it's supposed to be the bit of decision theory you get for free. But look at B. Is there any single pure option that beats B in both states? No: T beats B against L and loses to it against R, M is the mirror. The only thing that beats B everywhere is the mixture — and the mixture beats B everywhere only because we've already decided that the value of the mixture in a state is the average of the values of the pure options it mixes. That averaging step is not the sure-thing principle. It's an assumption about how lotteries are valued. I think it's a small assumption (just: the value of a lottery is the probability-weighted average of the values of its outcomes) but it is an assumption, and it's exactly the kind of thing a theory of expected utility is supposed to *deliver*, not presuppose.

So the thesis: the reading of "don't play dominated strategies" that actually bites — the one that gets you to the rationalizable set instead of something weaker — already contains a chunk of expected-utility theory. If that's right, then the standard textbook move of selling dominance reasoning as a pre-theoretic rationality constraint, the thing you assume *before* you build a theory of risky choice, is backwards. You can't use dominance solvability to motivate expected utility without going in a circle.
