I am trying to settle my own views on conscription, professional armies, and armed neutrality, and I have ended up with a set of verdicts on particular cases that I cannot get to hang together. I would like the principle, or the small set of principles, that best fits them — stated precisely enough that I can apply it to cases I have not considered — together with an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which of them I should therefore give up.

My verdicts, roughly in the order I wrote them down. I grew up in Switzerland and now live in a small country that has never had conscription and belongs to no alliance.

1. Swiss-style universal liability, under which every able-bodied man is liable, trains in a militia unit, and remains subject to service for decades, seems to me legitimate and not merely tolerable. I never consented to it and neither did anyone in my cohort. That the 2013 initiative to abolish conscription was defeated, and that a 2025 proposal to replace male conscription with compulsory civic duty for both sexes was defeated by an even larger margin, reassures me without doing, I think, the real justificatory work.

2. The Swiss levy on men declared unfit for service — three per cent of annual income until the age of thirty-seven, capped at eleven years — strikes me as acceptable and even well designed. I notice that it falls on incapacity rather than refusal, so that the fit pay in service and the unfit pay in money.

3. A pure lottery: all eighteen-year-olds, women included, drawn by lot for a fixed term, medical exemptions only, no student or occupational deferments, no substitutes for sale. This seems to me the fairest arrangement I can describe, and the one I would choose.

4. Gender-neutral liability, as in Norway from 2015 or Sweden's system after 2017, is plainly better than male-only liability, and a state that still conscripts only men is resting on something it can no longer defend.

5. Individual conscientious objection, with a genuinely civilian alternative, seems to me right. Switzerland's alternative is about half again as long as the military service it replaces; Finland's is a flat twelve months against a military minimum of about five and a half. The longer term does not strike me as a punishment for holding a belief, though I see the argument that it is one.

6. A blanket exemption for a whole religious group, of the kind Finland used to grant Jehovah's Witnesses until 2019, seems to me wrong — not because the convictions are insincere, but because a group is being let out of a shared burden on grounds that, if they were good grounds, would empty the draft.

7. Selective objection — a man willing to defend his own country but not to fight in what he regards as a war of aggression — should not be an exemption at all. He should serve or be punished.

8. A volunteer force that pays enough to draw in the poor, the rural, and those with nothing else to sell seems to me worse than the lottery in (3), even though no one in it is coerced and everyone in (3) is. I would rather coerce the well-off than buy the poor.

9. Letting a man pay a fee to be excused from a draft, or hire a substitute to go in his place, as the Union army did during the American Civil War, seems to me straightforwardly unjust, and any modern version of it would be too.

10. Hiring foreigners to carry risks that citizens will not carry — Gurkhas, the Foreign Legion, contractors — seems to me unjust even where the foreigners enlist freely and are paid far above what they could earn at home.

11. In a real emergency, conscripting only those with scarce and needed skills — surgeons, pilots, network engineers — seems to me acceptable, even though it abandons equal shares entirely.

12. Conscripting prisoners, with sentence reductions, seems to me worse than conscripting ordinary citizens, even if the prisoners sign up and even if the alternative is idleness.

13. Armed neutrality of the Swiss or the older Swedish and Finnish kind — a small rich country arming heavily, conscripting broadly, staying out of other people's wars — seems to me legitimate, and better for the world than its alternatives. I would add that conscription is easier for me to justify where the conscripts cannot be sent abroad, and harder where they can.

14. A rich country with no army that shelters under another's guarantee — Iceland under the American umbrella, Japan's arrangement — seems to me parasitic rather than merely imprudent. A country that gives up its army while under no such guarantee, as Costa Rica did in 1948, seems to me admirable.

15. The case that prompted all this. The country I now live in is debating a universal national service lottery: every eighteen-year-old, both sexes, drawn by lot for eighteen months of service in the armed forces, civil protection, or care work, with the option of declining all of it by paying a levy of one per cent of income for ten years. I approve of the lottery and am uneasy about the levy, and I cannot say why the levy is worse than the exemption tax in (2) or the longer alternative service in (5).

Where I come apart. The lottery in (3) against the volunteer force in (8): I prefer coercion to exploitation, which is not a preference I can defend in general. The lottery in (3) against the skill-based call-up in (11): (3) seems fair because it takes everyone, (11) because it takes the ones who matter, and if fairness means equal shares then (11) is an outrage. Individual objection in (5) against the group exemption in (6) and the selective objector in (7): I let conscience out through one door and bar it at two others. And in (2), (5), (8) and (9) taken together: extra months or money may be given in place of service, it seems, but not used to buy safety. My guess is that the difference is that in (2) and (5) the state sets the price for a whole class, proportionately to means, while in (9) a flat fee only the well-off can pay buys exemption from anyone's draft, and in (8) the market sets the price of a poor man's risk. I would like to know whether that is a real distinction or a rationalisation.

My own tentative principle, which I expect you to break: a state may impose a burden of risk only on those who are also its beneficiaries, and only under a rule that cannot be revised to spare anyone without putting someone else in that person's place. That seems to me to get (1), (3), (8), (9), (10), (13) and (14). It seems to condemn (5), and probably (2) and (11), since in each of those someone is spared at someone else's expense.

What I would like from you:

- The principle or principles that fit best, stated precisely, with the case that forces each clause to be there and the case that each clause gets wrong.
- An explicit statement of whether one principle can do the work or whether two are needed, and if two, which of my verdicts each is doing the work for.
- Your verdicts, under the principle, on at least two cases I have not put to you: one close to mine, and one that tests the edge of the principle.
- Which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, which of them I should abandon, and whether you would rather keep a complicated principle that saves all fifteen or a simple one that overturns one or two. If you overturn one, say which and why that one rather than another.
- I am not asking for sources or reading; I want the principle, the argument for it, and the cases against it.
