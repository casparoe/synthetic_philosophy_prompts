I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and next month I have been asked to run a ninety-minute session for about a dozen newsroom colleagues — reporters and editors on the business and labour beats, none with any background in moral philosophy or labour history — as part of a series we are calling the future of work. I do not want you to explain the material to me. I want you to plan how I should explain it.

The idea I need them to leave with is not that some jobs are hard, or that work-life balance matters. It is the sharper claim that the line between work and leisure is a political distribution of time rather than a fact of nature: that someone decides who owes their hours to whom, that the line has been drawn differently at different times and is drawn differently, right now, across countries. Build the session so that this claim arrives as a conclusion the room has been walked to, not as a thesis I announce at the top.

What I need from you:

1. The order in which the components must go in, with the dependency stated at each step — what has to be in place before the next thing can land, and why. I expect the Protestant work ethic has to come early, since the whole modern argument is conducted inside a premise it installed; tell me if I am wrong, and where else it belongs. The 35-hour week and karoshi are both in my brief. Tell me which should carry the session and which should be held back as a contrast, and why.

2. The single example the whole thing hangs on. It needs to be concrete enough to picture, to admit a distinction between fewer hours, flexible hours, and time that is owed to no one at all, and to survive being pushed on by sceptical journalists. Say what the example must have and what it must lack, and if karoshi is the right carrier, tell me what I have to do to keep it from collapsing into a story about Japan.

3. The misconceptions my colleagues will arrive with and form, and the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off. I would guess at least these: that leisure is the same thing as laziness or consumption; that the work ethic is a religious relic which secularised away; that overwork deaths are a cultural peculiarity of one country rather than an institutional failure; and that a right to leisure is a luxury only rich economies can afford. Add the ones I have not seen.

4. One question I can put to the room at the end that would show me whether they have got the idea rather than merely been moved by it — something that would separate a colleague who can now tell a distribution of time from one who still hears a preference about it.

5. What I should openly decline to explain at this level, and say so out loud. I would rather leave a gap I have named than blur a distinction I cannot do justice to in ninety minutes.

A note on the material I want you to build with. Do not reach for the famous set pieces unless they are doing real work. I would rather the session turn on a distinction that popular coverage rarely makes crisply — between having fewer working hours, having control over which hours you work, and having hours that are unconditionally your own — and I want the arc to show why those three come apart, so that a shorter week does not get confused with flexible scheduling, and neither gets confused with the stronger claim that some time is owed to no one.
