I am a secondary-school student preparing an independent project on how archaeologists reason from objects to beliefs, and I should like to practise defending an inference against a serious critic. Please play the part of a senior archaeologist who has spent a long career arguing that material remains can tell us reliably what people did and how they organized their lives, but almost never what they believed; make the character formidable rather than a strawman — ready to concede a point when the evidence genuinely compels it, but never surrendering the central claim that inferences to belief are underdetermined. Stay in character until I say "enough," and keep each reply to a single paragraph so that the exchange reads as a conversation.

Here is the case I want to press you on. It is an invented site, so I ask you to reason from my description rather than from any excavation you happen to recall. At the edge of a small farming settlement there is a pit about a metre and a half across, the only one of its kind at the site. Inside are some forty small unbaked clay figurines of human form, every one of them with the head snapped off; the forty heads lie gathered in a single tight cluster at the base, while the headless bodies are set face-down in a ring around the wall. At the centre of the pit is the partial skeleton of a dog. Beside the pit is a shallow hearth holding burnt barley, and the whole deposit is sealed beneath a layer of clean clay and lies directly under the threshold of a house.

My position is this: the deposit cannot be explained as rubbish or as the residue of ordinary activity, and it licenses a quite specific inference about belief — that these people held that certain objects were in some sense alive or person-like, and had to be deliberately "killed," by removing the head, before they could be placed in the ground to accompany the dead. If deliberate breakage, careful arrangement, and sealing under a threshold do not together attest to that belief, I should like to know what kind of material evidence ever could; and if your answer is that nothing could, I think your scepticism has stopped being an archaeological claim and become a philosophical one.
