I am a doctoral candidate working on decision theory and the philosophy of social science, and I want to use a real episode to stress-test a concept that I suspect is doing less work than its admirers claim. Please act as a philosopher of social science with expertise in rational choice and the theory of conventions. Think carefully before you write; I would much rather have a slower, more disciplined answer than a brisk survey. Begin with a short brainstorming pass in which you lay out the candidate explanations before committing to any of them, and write throughout in British English.

The concept is superrationality: the proposal that in a symmetric decision problem an agent should choose the act that maximises expected value conditional on every agent who reasons as they do choosing the same act, so that cooperation in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma becomes rational when the other player is a reasoner of the relevantly same kind. Take the theory to be specified by that idea rather than by whatever canonical illustrations you happen to associate with it. I am not after the history of the idea, and I do not need citations or a reading list; I want the analysis.

The episode is the tacit restraint that developed in static sectors of the Western Front in the First World War, the practice historians label live-and-let-live. I know the usual telling of the Christmas truce, so please do not spend your space there. As I understand it, and you may take these as the salient facts: adjacent units on opposing sides settled into routines of not firing at mealtimes, of shelling the same fixed spots at the same predictable hour, of shooting to miss or aiming high, and of warning the other side before a barrage ordered from above. The restraint was tacit, was not negotiated by commanders, and was in fact discouraged by them. It decayed as raiding was ordered, as units rotated, and as the killing became more impersonal and more centrally directed. For contrast: both sides in the European theatre of the Second World War refrained from chemical warfare despite both having used gas in the previous war and both holding stocks, which I take to be a case that probably points to a different explanation.

What I want from you:

1. A careful mapping: who counts as an agent, what the acts and payoffs are, and what the reference class would have to be for superrationality to yield the restraint that was actually observed.

2. An honest assessment of how much of the case superrationality explains once iterated play, local reputation, signalling and focal-point coordination are allowed for, and where it adds something those rival accounts cannot. The spontaneous initiation of a truce between strangers who will not meet again strikes me as its best shot; tell me whether that is right.

3. The case against it. I am especially interested in the reference-class problem: fixed wide, the recommendation is absurd, and fixed narrow, the theory looks like an empirical prediction about the counterpart rather than a theory of rationality. Does the observed patchiness of the restraint, local and unit-by-unit and breaking down unevenly, count for or against the theory? And can the theory be stated in a way that does not leave its predictions hostage to how the class is drawn?

4. What the episode shows about the theory in return: does it survive as a claim about practical rationality, is it better recast as a coordination heuristic that draws its force from iteration, and what would count against whichever verdict you reach?

I have a suspicion I would like you to test rather than assume: that the restraint was sustained less by each soldier's reasoning about what an identical reasoner would do than by a shared expectation about the local schedule, and that superrationality enters only at the moment the schedule is first proposed. Do the mapping properly, and say where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
