I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who works in foreign policy about whether Kant's essay on perpetual peace is a serious theory of international order or a piece of Enlightenment wishful thinking, and we kept colliding over a case that neither of us could fit cleanly into the essay: the Congress of Vienna of 1814–15 and the Concert of Europe it inaugurated. My friend's position is that the Concert refutes Kant outright. Kant's first definitive article requires that the civil constitution of every state be republican, and his second requires that the right of nations rest on a federation of free states; my friend adds that Kant regarded a mere balance of power as a precarious substitute for a genuine league of peace. Yet the Vienna settlement restored and entrenched monarchies, was engineered by Metternich, Castlereagh, and Talleyrand as a great-power directorate rather than any federation, and — on the usual telling — kept the European great powers from a general war for the better part of a century. If that is right, the case looks like a counterexample: durable peace without republics and without Kant's league. I think the inference is too quick, but I could not say precisely why, and I would like you to adjudicate.

Please act as an expert on Kant's political philosophy and on the diplomatic history of post-Napoleonic Europe, and work slowly and carefully rather than reaching for the standard summary. Begin with a brief brainstorm of the possible ways the case and the theory might connect, then set out, in your own words, the architecture of Kant's argument — the preliminary articles, the three definitive articles, and above all the distinction he draws between a treaty that merely suspends hostilities and a league meant to end war as such — so that we are clear about what the theory actually claims before we test it. Then do the mapping in both directions. In one direction, treat the Vienna settlement and the Concert as a real episode and ask what Kant's framework says the actors faced and should have done, and which features of the episode it explains: can the Concert's longevity be recruited in support of any part of Kant's program (his emphasis on publicity, on the separation of powers, or on commerce and the commercial spirit as a pacifying force), or does it simply falsify the necessity of republicanism? In the other direction, ask what the episode does to the theory: if the powers kept the peace through consultation, mutual restraint, and a shared stake in the status quo rather than through republican constitutions or a genuine federation, is the first definitive article refuted — or was the Concert only a partial and exclusionary peace, a peace among a club of dynasties purchased by repression at home and war abroad, which never put the theory to a fair test? Be explicit about the disanalogies, and about the questions on which Kant's framework is simply silent.

Conclude by saying which reading you find most defensible, and what the episode shows that neither the essay nor the familiar democratic-peace literature quite captures. Where a factual detail would change your verdict, flag it rather than assuming it.
