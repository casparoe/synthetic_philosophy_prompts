A friend of mine enjoys constructing arguments that are airtight until you notice that they prove something nobody believes, and this time I cannot find the exit. He calls it the mugging. Here it is, laid out as he gives it.

(1) A choice of mine is rational exactly when no other choice open to me has a higher expected value, where the expected value of a choice is the sum, over the ways things might go, of the value of each outcome multiplied by its probability.

(2) A stranger stops me in the street. Hand over your wallet, he says, or I will have a googol of people — ten to the hundredth — tortured for years. He offers no evidence that he can do this, but I have no evidence that he cannot, and nothing rules it out.

(3) So the probability that refusing costs a googol of people years of torture is some small positive number; call it p, and suppose for argument's sake that it is one in a googol. A one-in-a-googol chance of a googol of people being tortured works out to one person tortured. That is still far worse than the loss of my wallet, so refusing has a lower expected value than handing the wallet over.

(4) So I should hand it over.

(5) Nothing in steps (1) through (4) depends on the man being a stranger, on the harm being torture, or on the request being a threat rather than an offer. A cheerful man who offers to create a googol of happy lives on condition that I hand him my wallet gets the same verdict by the same arithmetic.

(6) Therefore, for any request anyone makes of me, if the good promised or the harm threatened is large enough and the probability that it is real is small enough, complying with the request has a higher expected value than refusing it.

(7) But I cannot comply with every such request: there can be any number of strangers, and their demands can conflict with one another, so nobody could follow a rule that says to give in to each of them.

(8) So (1) is false, and rationality is not the maximisation of expected value.

I would rather keep (3) and (5) than give them up; the arithmetic seems to me beyond dispute, and (5) seems like plain honesty about what the argument actually uses. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the answer is (1), I need to know what replaces it, and what the replacement says about the ordinary precautions where small odds of large harms are the whole point — seatbelts, insurance, giving to disaster relief. If the trouble is somewhere in the middle, tell me whether (8) still follows once the trouble is repaired.
