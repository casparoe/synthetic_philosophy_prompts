I belong to a small decision-theory reading group that meets fortnightly, and a few weeks ago one of the members brought in a chapter from a popular book on probability — I never got the title, and since the chapter was read aloud and then summarized back to me by someone else, what follows is my reconstruction rather than a transcript. The chapter's treatment of the St. Petersburg game runs roughly as follows: 'A fair coin is tossed until it comes up heads; if the first head appears on toss n, the player receives 2^(n-1) dollars. The expected payoff is therefore 1/2 + 1/2 + 1/2 + ..., which never converges, so the game is worth infinitely much. Anyone who maximizes expected value must be willing to pay any finite sum to play, and indeed to stake their whole fortune on a single toss; yet almost nobody would pay more than a few dollars, and many would pay only a few cents. The classical theory of rational choice is refuted by a coin. Bernoulli's resolution was to observe that money has diminishing marginal utility — the hundredth dollar matters less than the first — so the utility of wealth grows like its logarithm, which is bounded. Once utility is bounded the game's expected utility is finite, and the paradox evaporates: a rational agent pays a modest entry fee and no more. The modern consensus is that this dissolves the problem entirely. No casino has the infinite capital needed to back such a game, and the game cannot be played indefinitely, since no one can keep tossing a coin forever; the infinite expectation is a mathematical artifact of a situation that cannot arise in the world. The deeper lesson is that unbounded utility functions and unbounded payoffs are unphysical, so the paradox is best read as a historical curiosity that taught economists to reckon in utility rather than money.'

I would like an audit of this passage. Take it claim by claim — sentence by sentence, if that is cleaner — and for each one tell me whether a working decision theorist would accept it, wince at it as a simplification that goes a step too far, or reject it outright; in the last case I want to know precisely what is wrong and what the corrected statement would be. I am not asking for a fresh exposition of the paradox itself; I have sat through the standard story twice and can look it up. What I need is to know which of these sentences I can keep, which need a qualification attached, and whether the chapter's closing moral survives once the earlier sentences are fixed. Two things in particular nag at me. First, the slide from 'the utility of wealth grows like its logarithm' to 'utility is bounded' looks to me like a conflation of two different ideas, and I suspect the resolution depends on which of them is doing the work; if the paradox goes through finite without any boundedness assumption, I would like that said explicitly, along with which of the chapter's two repairs is actually load-bearing. Second, the person who runs our group insists that the interesting case is the risk-neutral agent — someone for whom a dollar is always worth exactly a dollar — and that everything the chapter says about Bernoulli's fix is beside the point for that agent. Is that right, and what does the paradox look like once one separates the question 'what is the expected monetary value of this game?' from the question 'what should I pay to play it?'
