I'm in a reading group that splits its time between decision theory and macroeconomics, and we keep relitigating a line that gets attributed to Keynes — the one about there being matters on which we simply do not know, as opposed to merely having a low or middling degree of confidence. A friend of mine, who manages a pension fund, uses it as a conversation-stopper whenever anyone tries to put a number on the returns to a thirty-year infrastructure project. Her view is that the whole point of Keynes on uncertainty is that such numbers are a fiction, and that anyone who assigns probabilities there has already misunderstood the situation. Another member of the group thinks this is overblown: on his reading it just means the probabilities are close to half, or the variance is enormous, or the evidence is thin — none of which threatens expected-value reasoning as such. I suspect both of them are trading on an ambiguity, but I can't articulate the options well enough to say who, if anyone, is entitled to their conclusion.

What I want is a menu rather than a verdict. Lay out the distinct precise claims that this one line could be making, ordered from the trivial to the radical, and for each say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something actually false — and, crucially, whether it would license my friend's refusal to quantify. The dimensions that seem to do the real work are these: is the claim about indeterminism in the world or about our epistemic position; is the problem that no degree of belief is rationally required, that such a degree exists but is not numerically measurable, or that it is measurable but not comparable with other probabilities; is the failure located in the agent's credences or in expected-value maximization as a decision rule; and is the practical upshot paralysis, or reliance on convention and gut feel, or merely very wide error bars? I'd also like to know which of these readings sit comfortably with the more technical side of Keynes's own account of probability — the idea that probability is a logical relation between evidence and hypothesis, and the distinction between the probability of an argument and what he termed its weight, where weight tracks the balance of relevant knowledge against relevant ignorance — and which readings would make his own practice in that book look incoherent. Finally, tell me which reading people most likely have in mind when they invoke the line in an investment or policy setting, and whether any reading that genuinely supports refusing to quantify is one a careful reader should accept. If the honest answer is that no precise reading supports the rhetorical use, say that too.
