I have no formal training in history or archaeology, but I have been thinking on my own about how we draw the line between legitimate historical revision and pseudohistory. A friend of mine, also an amateur, has a long record of verdicts on this question, and I want to know what he will say about a new case. His verdicts so far are these. He says the idea of Atlantis as a literal advanced civilization is pseudohistory, but the search for a real Bronze Age catastrophe behind the story is not. He says ancient aliens is pseudohistory. He says the Norse arrival in North America is legitimate history, even though it was once dismissed as fringe. He says the theory that most ancient history was invented in the Middle Ages is pseudohistory. He says Holocaust denial is pseudohistory. He says the popular claim that the Library of Alexandria was destroyed in one great fire is a myth but not pseudohistory. He says the claim that ancient Egyptians had electrical devices is pseudohistory. He says the claim that some ancient documents were forged is legitimate history, even when it challenges the consensus. He says the claim that the Dark Ages were not uniformly dark is legitimate. He says a claim that a lost city has been found is legitimate if it is published in a peer-reviewed journal, but pseudohistory if it rests on one amateur's reading of satellite images. And he says the claim that aliens built the pyramids is pseudohistory, while the claim that paid laborers built them is legitimate.

Now the new case. A well-credentialed archaeologist publishes a peer-reviewed article arguing that a famous ancient city was not a city at all but a seasonal ceremonial camp. The argument is careful, uses the standard evidence, and engages with the counterarguments, but it contradicts almost every other specialist in the field. My friend has not read the article; he only knows this description. What will he say: pseudohistory or legitimate history? More importantly, what rule is he actually running? I can see at least two possibilities: a rule about method, where pseudohistory is marked by bad evidence, unfalsifiable claims, cherry-picking, and no peer review; and a rule about consensus, where a claim is pseudohistory if it contradicts the settled judgment of the relevant experts. His earlier verdicts seem to fit both, but they may come apart on this case. Tell me which rule you think he is using, which of his earlier verdicts is the closest precedent, what each rule predicts about the new case, and which further case would best show which rule he is really committed to.
