In a long argument I had recently about a conspiracy claim — the claim being that a certain government program existed, was later wound down, and was erased, with the participants bound to silence and the records destroyed — my interlocutor kept repeating "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence" every time I observed that no document, no participant, and no trace of the program had ever surfaced. I could not tell whether he was asserting something trivially true, something substantive and probably false, or something that quietly presupposes the very point at issue. Give me the menu.

Lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, ordered from the near-vacuous to the strong, each stated as a claim I could actually assess. I expect the list to include at least: a deductive reading (no finite body of observation can refute a hypothesis); a probabilistic reading (about what a missing observation does or does not do to a hypothesis's posterior probability, and under what assumption about the chance that the evidence would have existed had the hypothesis been true); and a normative reading (about when one may believe something, as against when one may merely fail to disbelieve it). For each reading, say what would make it true or false, whether it is a platitude, a defensible thesis, or a falsehood, and — the part I care about — whether it actually licenses the move he made: treating the total absence of traces as no reason at all against the claim.

Two distinctions I especially want drawn out. First, the difference between a hypothesis that merely permits the absence of evidence and one that predicts it, and whether, once that distinction is on the table, his use of the slogan retains any force. Second, whether the slogan does the same work when the missing evidence is the kind one would expect to survive (a program's worth of personnel, procurement, and paper) as when it is the kind one would expect to vanish. Where the answer turns on a technical notion — likelihood, confirmation, defeat, burden of proof, detection power — state the notion plainly rather than gesturing at it, since I intend to take this back to him.

Finally, separate what is settled among people who study evidence and confirmation from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus.
