I'm doing a part-time health law conversion course and I keep getting stuck on advance directives — the living-will type forms where you write down, while you're still compos mentis, what treatment you'd want if you later lose capacity. The bit doing my head in isn't the seminar stuff about autonomy versus best interests, it's the actual paperwork. My aunt is a ward clerk at a district general and she showed me (heavily redacted) the standard form her trust uses. Part 1 is a refusal section: you can write that you don't want antibiotics for a chest infection, or don't want to be admitted to hospital at all, and if it's valid and applicable the team has to follow it even if you are now sitting up in bed cheerfully asking for the antibiotics and they would save your life. Part 2 is a preferences section: you can write that you'd like a bed in a home near your sister, or that you want your daughter in the room, and the form says in as many words that this part is not legally binding and will be taken into account. Same document, same signature, same afternoon — binding when it says no, a polite suggestion when it says yes. Meanwhile a friend of mine with bipolar has the opposite setup. Her crisis plan says, in effect, if I stop taking the lithium and start getting grand and sleepless, admit me, and the team do lean on that heavily; but when she's unwell and refusing, they detain her anyway and explain that the refusal is the illness speaking rather than her, which is the same move in reverse — the present self disqualified, the earlier self trusted. And I notice that in the dementia case everyone says the earlier self is the real one because the later one is compromised, and in her case everyone says the later self is the illness because the earlier one is compromised, and nobody seems bothered that these two arguments are mirror images.

What I want to know is what this practice actually presupposes. It looks to me as if the whole apparatus assumes there are two people in one body — the one who signed and the one now living in it — and that some procedure can identify which of them is the real one. Is that right, or am I reading philosophy into admin? If it is right, which theory of the person does it commit to, and which rival views does it quietly rule out? If it's not right, can the asymmetry be explained with no view about identity at all — something about a refusal being a permission and a request needing someone to actually do something, or about who carries the burden of proof, or about the earlier self having no idea what the later state is like because she has never been in it? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, then tell me honestly which parts the practice strictly presupposes and which parts it merely fits nicely with, and where it's underdetermined so that two or three different theories would all license the same form. Then tell me what would have to change in the paperwork and on the ward if we replaced the implicit view with (a) the present self always wins, (b) the earlier self binds absolutely, or (c) a story-based view on which the person is whoever can still make sense of the whole life. Please write it in British English and skip the lecture on autonomy versus best interests, I've had that one twice. No reading lists or citations, I just want the argument.
