I have no academic training, though I have spent a couple of years reading about animals and ethics on my own, and there is one real episode I keep coming back to because I cannot get the usual arguments to fit it. I would like you to analyse it for me.

The episode is the Oostvaardersplassen, a fenced nature reserve of about 56 square kilometres in the Dutch province of Flevoland, on land reclaimed from the sea in the 1960s and originally intended for industry. The wet part turned out to be valuable for water birds; the dry part was filling up with willow scrub, which would have ruined it for those birds, so the managers brought in large grazing animals to keep the land open: Konik horses, red deer, and Heck cattle, a breed produced in the twentieth century to stand in for the extinct aurochs. From the beginning the idea was that these animals would live as wild animals: outdoors all year, no winter feeding, no veterinary treatment, no castration. The reserve is fenced, has no large predators, and has no corridor to the wild areas further east, so the animals cannot leave and nothing limits their numbers except weather and hunger. The policy was to let them starve in hard winters. In the winter of 2017 to 2018 nearly 3,300 of them died that way. There were demonstrations, people were arrested for throwing hay over the fence, and the province commissioned a policy report which was approved in July 2018: the rewilding framing was dropped, the herds were to be reduced, partly by shooting animals rather than letting them starve, and that in turn produced a second round of protests and lawsuits. Please take all of that as given; I am not asking you to check it, and I would rather you did not spend time on details I have not mentioned.

The theory I have been trying to think with is the position that says wild animal suffering is a serious moral problem and that we should be willing to intervene: that an animal is not better off for having been left alone while it slowly starves, that the naturalness of a process does not by itself justify it, the way the naturalness of a disease does not make it wrong to treat the disease, that we already intervene in nature on an enormous scale, and that the amount of suffering in nature is so large that how we act matters a great deal. The opposing position, which I find harder to dismiss than I would like, says we should hold back: that ecosystems are too complicated to meddle with without making things worse, that these particular animals are ones we put there in the first place so the whole thing is a human artefact and proves nothing either way, and that there is something valuable about a wild animal's life being its own rather than managed.

What I want is a case analysis that runs in both directions. Take the theory and tell me what it says each of the parties was facing and what it says they should have done: the reserve managers who set the policy, the provincial government that reversed it in 2018, the people who threw hay over the fence, and the people who wanted the herds shot quickly. Say whether the option the theory recommends was actually available to them. Then tell me which features of the episode the theory accounts for well, which it misdescribes or ignores, and where it simply has nothing to say, rather than forcing it to speak. Finally, tell me what the episode shows about the theory: a confirmation, a complication, damage, or a kind of case the theory never anticipated. In particular, the animals were brought in as a tool for the birds, so a welfare cost was deliberately imposed on some animals for an ecological benefit; say whether the theory can handle that kind of trade-off or not.

Two distinctions of my own that I would like you to use, and to push back on if they are not worth having. First, keep apart the authorship question, whether a human action put an animal where it is, and the wildness question, whether the animal's life is governed by ecological processes rather than human design. In this case humans put the animals there and fenced them, so authorship is total, and yet the whole point was that they would live wild lives, so the two come apart. I want to know whether the theory actually needs the idea of wildness once you see that. Second, keep apart intervention on an individual, such as feeding or shooting a particular animal, and intervention on the regime, such as changing the stocking numbers, the fencing, the predators, or the corridors. The public argument seems to have been almost entirely about individuals, and I suspect the harder questions are about the regime. There is also a third thing I cannot sort out: what counts as leaving them alone here, given that the place they are left alone in is a fenced polder we built, with no predators and no way out, and so not the world the animals evolved in.
