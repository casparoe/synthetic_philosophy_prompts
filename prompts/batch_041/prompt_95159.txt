I run civic engagement for a city of about 300,000 — two staff, no in-house research capacity — and after a long and ugly fight the council has authorized a pilot citizens' panel on a housing question: sixty residents drawn by lottery, meeting over four weekends, with a mandate to recommend, and a public promise that any recommendation clearing two-thirds goes straight to a referendum. If the pilot goes well, the panel becomes a standing institution with a fresh question every year; if it goes badly, the idea dies with it, and I'm not naïve about the fact that my process choices will be read as choices about who gets to count. I've read enough of the theory of sortition and lottocracy to grasp the shape of the case for it — that a randomly constituted body has no faction to reward and no base to please, and that the lottery is doing justificatory work rather than merely picking people — but the theory seems to stop exactly where my job begins. Almost every operational decision I have to make looks as though it smuggles in a normative one, and I have no way of telling which ones do.

Please write me a decision procedure my team could actually run, in order, for the choices that will recur every cycle: the sample frame, and whether to stratify, on which variables, and how much of the mirror-the-city ideal I can trade away for deliberation quality; what to do when the acceptances come in skewed, as they always will, and the people who say yes are systematically unlike the city; whether to pay members, and how the amount changes who accepts; how to replace dropouts mid-cycle without letting my own facilitators quietly engineer the composition; how to handle expert testimony without either drowning the panel or steering it; whether the recommendation should bind, advise, or merely trigger a referendum; and the meta-questions I cannot dodge, of who chose the question and who gets to choose the rules. For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer and what I should do when I can't get one, mark clearly where the procedure stops with a no, and mark where it has to hand the decision to a human because the question is one of values rather than design. Be honest about the places where the procedure will misfire or merely dress up a judgment call as a rule.

Then run the procedure on three cases: one where I would expect the panel to be the wrong instrument altogether; one where the sample frame forces a choice between drawing from the whole city and drawing from the neighbourhood that will live with the outcome, a choice that will look to half of council like fairness and to the other half like rigging; and one where the honest response to attrition is to accept a body that no longer mirrors the city and to defend it on deliberative rather than descriptive grounds — a conclusion I suspect is right and dislike, so I want the procedure to reach it rather than me to feed it in. Assume I have six weeks to set this up, that everything touching eligibility has to be signed off by council's counsel, and that whatever I adopt I will have to explain to a hostile committee in plain language. Don't bother with citations or a reading list; refer to positions and arguments as you need them and I will fill in the sources myself.
