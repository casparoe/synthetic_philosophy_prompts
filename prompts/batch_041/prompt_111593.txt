I cover science and ideas, and I've been reporting on the renewed philosophical interest in al-Ghazālī's occasionalism. A philosopher I interviewed has persuaded me of a deflationary version of it: no created thing has causal power of its own; God alone causes; fire does not burn the cotton, God does; and what we call laws of nature are merely the regularities God has freely adopted as a policy, with no necessity residing in the things themselves. What I want is a map of what would count against this. Which of the standard objections should actually move someone who holds it — that it leaves no room for human agency and responsibility if God is the author of every act, that a "policy" or "habit" is itself a disposition and so smuggles back the very causal powers the view denies, that it erases the distinction between law and miracle once every event is directly willed, and that it is insulated because any apparent counterexample can be redescribed as God's doing otherwise? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish what a committed occasionalist would concede from what they would dismiss as question-begging, and tell me honestly whether the view risks anything at all.
