I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been asked to run a single 90-minute session for a mixed group of about sixteen people — engineers, two psychology graduate students, one philosopher — at an interdisciplinary reading group on decision-making. What I want them to feel rather than hear is the force of the bounded-rationality idea: that "be rational" and "maximize" are not the same instruction, and that a crude stopping rule with an aspiration level does most of the work that optimizing was supposed to do.

Design the session around a sequential search game with real but non-monetary stakes. Participants face a deck of options with hidden values, pay a fixed cost for each look, and may stop whenever they like; I can write a script to control the distribution of values, so the decks can be generated to order. Round one is played freely. Later rounds should force the discovery that a rule of the form "stop at the first option above a cutoff fixed in advance" holds its own against anything the clever players improvise. I have read that under a constant per-look cost with values drawn independently, the optimal policy has exactly that form, so that the crude rule and the optimal rule are the same shape rather than rivals; check whether I have that right, and build the game so it comes out. Tell me whether to let players recall options they passed over — my understanding is that the optimal cutoff is flat when recall is allowed and declines as the horizon closes when it is not. Then shift the distribution and the cost between rounds and let the players watch their cutoffs, and their stated ambitions, drift, including at least one round in which the drift is a mistake.

Give me materials, a payoff scheme in points or chips, exact rules and round-by-round timing, the distributions to script, what to record on a shared scoreboard, and a debrief that maps what happened onto satisficing as a stopping rule with an aspiration level, the difference between judging a decision by its outcomes and judging it by the procedure that produced it, and the thought that behavior is shaped jointly by the environment and the agent's limits. Do not use the words "satisficing" or "bounded rationality" until the debrief. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, including how people will try to game the setup, and be frank about the part of the idea the game cannot reproduce: what about genuinely ill-structured problems, where the option set and the criteria are not given in advance, has to be supplied by me in words.
