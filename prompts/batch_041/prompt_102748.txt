I am a novelist, currently at work on a book whose central character is a woman who cannot stop trying to draw moral lines where, the narrative keeps suggesting, no clean lines exist — she argues with butchers, with entomologists, with a roboticist who builds household devices, and the book is meant to make the reader uncomfortable about how quickly we assign a zero to some minds and a one to others. To keep my own thinking from going soft, I have drafted a short philosophical essay that I intend to place in an appendix, and I would like you to comment on it. The essay runs as follows. The binary of sentient and non-sentient is a fiction we maintain for administrative convenience; what we actually care about comes in degrees along at least three axes. The first is valence: the capacity for states that are positively or negatively toned, pleasure and pain in the thinnest sense, stripped of any suggestion of rich feeling. The second is integration: the degree to which such states are bound into a single subject at a time, rather than flickering in disconnected sub-systems that no one is having. The third is temporal depth: the extent to which the creature represents itself as persisting, so that its pains can be anticipated, remembered, and woven into a life. I then propose that moral status is the product of the three, not their sum, so that a zero on any axis yields a zero overall — which is how I explain why a thermostat, however complex its feedback loops, commands no moral consideration, while an insect, which I take to have a little valence and very little integration or temporal depth, commands a little. To this I add a notion I call hedonic saturation, the ratio of a system's valenced processing to its total processing: a mind that feels keenly but spends almost all of its cycles on unfeeling computation would, on my view, be diluted, its moral weight thinned by the sheer unfeeling bulk around it. And I suggest that the proper unit of moral accounting is not the moment but the lifetime, so that a creature intensely sentient for one second and a creature mildly sentient for eighty years must somehow be placed on a common scale, though I confess I do not know how.

I would like your comments, and I want them to be genuinely critical rather than encouraging. Tell me first whether the framework is accurate as philosophy of mind — in particular, whether the three axes are the right ones and whether they are independent, since I suspect temporal depth may secretly presuppose integration. Second, tell me whether the piece is interesting, or whether it is a restatement of positions that a reader will have met before, and if the latter, what would make it strange enough to be worth reading. Third, and most useful to me, tell me where the argument collapses: whether the product rule is defensible or whether it smuggles in an intuition about thresholds that I have not earned, whether hedonic saturation is a coherent quantity at all or merely a metaphor dressed as a measure, and whether indexing status to the lifetime rather than the moment can be made to work without covertly privileging the kinds of minds that resemble ours. Where you think a step fails, propose the repair you would make rather than merely noting the failure. Please write in American English, and after your prose comments give me a brief structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields: "overall_verdict" (a one-sentence judgment of the framework's promise), "strongest_objection" (the single most damaging criticism, in two or three sentences), "weakest_step" (the specific move in the argument that is least defensible), "axis_by_axis" (an array with one entry per axis, each entry an object with the fields "axis", "assessment", and "repair"), "product_model_verdict" (your ruling on whether status should be a product, a sum, or something else, with a one-line justification), "saturation_verdict" (whether hedonic saturation survives as a coherent notion), "lifetime_indexing_verdict" (whether lifetime accounting can be made workable), and "most_promising_revision" (the single change that would most improve the essay).
