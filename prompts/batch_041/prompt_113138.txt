I am a third-year undergraduate and the student representative on my university's Student Conduct Board, which hears cases of academic and non-academic misconduct and decides sanctions; I am also taking a seminar this term on punishment and moral responsibility. I have a vote on the Board, and next week it hears a case that I expect to divide us. What I would like from you is a prediction of what the Board will do, derived from what it has done, together with an account of the theory of punishment that its record is actually implementing.

The sanctions available to us, roughly in ascending order of severity, are: a formal reprimand; disciplinary probation with conditions (counselling, treatment compliance, no-contact orders, workshops); restitution; community service; a restorative conference with the affected parties; suspension for one or more terms; expulsion. Three further options act on the university's own records rather than on the student: a notation in the internal disciplinary file, a notation on the transcript, and, for a student who has already graduated, the withholding or revocation of the degree. A former chair of the Board used to insist on the difference between sanctions addressed to a student and sanctions addressed to the institution's certification of a student, and would say that a Board which has only the second kind left available is not in the same position as one that can still act on a person. I have never been certain what to make of that remark, but it now seems central to the case in front of us.

The record, as I would summarise it:

1. A second-year student paid an online service for an essay and submitted it as their own. The conduct was planned and concealed until an instructor confronted them, at which point they admitted everything, cooperated fully, and expressed remorse. No prior record. The Board imposed two terms of probation, forty hours of community service, and an integrity workshop, and expressly declined to suspend. Its written reasons conceded that the conduct was serious and deliberate and that the sanction had to register that, but held that a suspension would have cost the student a year of study, and that the workshop and the service were sufficient to ensure the misconduct was not repeated.

2. A first-year student paraphrased two sources without acknowledgement in a draft that was never submitted. The student was the first in their family to attend university, was unfamiliar with the conventions, had not intended to deceive anyone, and had misunderstood the instructions about citation. The Board imposed a grade penalty on the assignment and required a citation workshop, and declined to open a disciplinary file at all. Its reasons were that there had been no deception, that the student could not reasonably have known the rule, and that the workshop addressed the actual problem.

3. A student spray-painted a slur on a stairwell wall in a residence hall. No prior record; at the hearing the student maintained that it had been a joke and expressed no remorse; two students in the building reported that they were frightened. By a vote of six to one the Board imposed a two-term suspension, sixty hours of service, and a bias-education programme. The majority's reasons stressed the community's need to see the conduct condemned and the affected students' need to see the university take it seriously; the dissenting member wrote that the majority had calibrated the sanction to an audience rather than to the act.

4. A student took sixty dollars from a roommate's wallet and returned it two days later, unprompted, after the roommate mentioned the missing money, with an apology. No prior record. The roommate asked the Board to impose nothing. The Board required restitution and a restorative conference and imposed neither probation nor service. Its reasons were that the harm was small and had already been repaired by the student before the Board was involved, and that anything heavier would have endangered the student's scholarship and made future compliance less likely rather than more.

5. Two students, acting together on a single plan, fired a paintball gun from a dormitory window at a passing bus. One shot struck a passenger in the eye and caused permanent injury; the other missed. The Board imposed identical sanctions on both, a one-term suspension and a restorative conference with the affected passengers, reasoning that the injury was a matter of chance and that what the Board had to judge was what the students had chosen to do.

6. A student cheated on a final examination using a concealed device. They denied it when first questioned and admitted it only when shown video evidence. No prior record. The course was a prerequisite for a licensed professional programme. The Board suspended the student for one term, required the course to be retaken, and placed a notation in the internal file. Its reasons cited the continued denial during the investigation as an aggravating factor and observed that the professional programme would need to know.

7. A student in a first manic episode, undiagnosed at the time, shattered a glass door and pulled a fire alarm in a residence hall at three in the morning, disrupting the building and injuring no one. The student was hospitalised and is now in treatment. The Board imposed no suspension, but placed the student on probation conditioned on continuing treatment, ordered restitution for the door, and issued a no-contact order with the residence staff. Its reasons were that it would not punish an untreated illness, but that it could not ignore that the condition, if untreated, had put others at risk, and so the conditions were designed to keep the treatment in place.

8. A graduating student took sole credit for a group project. The other members had already graduated and told the Board that they wanted no sanction and did not want the matter pursued; the student had already apologised publicly at a department event. The Board issued a formal reprimand for the internal file and declined to do nothing. Its reasons were that the affected students' wishes had been taken seriously but that the Board's task was not only to satisfy them, and that the file had to record that the work was not the student's.

The new case is this. In the final weeks before graduation, a student used a borrowed staff credential to enter the student information system and changed their own grade in a required course from a C to an A. They also raised the grades of two classmates, one of whom had asked them to do it and one of whom had no knowledge of it. The changes were never detected by anyone. Eight months later, after graduating and starting a job, the student confessed to the instructor and then to the Board, unprompted, saying they could no longer live with it. The Registrar has confirmed that the changes could be reversed; one of the classmates has already used the altered grade to enter a graduate programme, the other to hold on to a job offer, and both would be affected by any correction. The student has no further access to university systems, has not been accused of anything since, and no one has asked the Board to act. The confession is the only reason the Board knows anything happened.

What I would like from you is the following. First, the candidate rules that the record supports, stated precisely enough that they can disagree with one another, including any rule that fits the Board's outcomes but not its recorded reasons, or its reasons but not its outcomes. Second, what each surviving rule predicts for the new case, including what each says about the record-directed sanctions, given that the ordinary ones are no longer available against a graduate. Third, your best guess at what the Board will actually decide, with the reasons for preferring one reading of the record over its rivals and with the closest analog among the eight earlier cases identified. Fourth, the further case I should watch for, or ask the Board to state reasons about, that would most cleanly separate the readings that survive. Fifth, if the record genuinely underdetermines this case, what principle the Board would be committing itself to with each of the choices actually open to it.
