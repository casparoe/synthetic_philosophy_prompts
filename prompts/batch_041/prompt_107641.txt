QUESTION 4 (25 marks)

"The P versus NP problem is not merely a technical question about computers. If P ≠ NP, then there are problems that no physically realizable computer — and therefore no physically realizable mind — can solve in a reasonable time, and this is a limit in principle rather than a limit of engineering."

Assess this claim. In your answer you should (i) define P, NP and NP-completeness, and say what NP-completeness does and does not tell us about the difficulty of a problem; (ii) distinguish what would follow from a proof that P ≠ NP from what would follow from a proof that P = NP; and (iii) say what further assumptions are needed to move from a theorem about Turing machines to a claim about human minds.

STUDENT'S ANSWER

The question asks whether P versus NP marks a limit of reason rather than a limit of engineering. I shall argue that it does, in a sense stronger than the merely practical.

Definitions first. P is the class of decision problems that a deterministic Turing machine can decide in time polynomial in the length of the input. NP is the class of decision problems for which a proposed answer can be checked in polynomial time; equivalently, the class of problems that a nondeterministic Turing machine can decide in polynomial time. A problem is NP-complete if it belongs to NP and every problem in NP can be reduced to it by a polynomial-time reduction. The Cook–Levin theorem establishes that Boolean satisfiability is NP-complete, and a long series of reductions has shown the same for planning, scheduling, propositional inference and many other problems of practical importance.

It matters that NP-completeness is a statement of relative difficulty. It says that an efficient algorithm for any one NP-complete problem would yield efficient algorithms for every problem in NP. It does not by itself say that NP-complete problems are hard: that further claim is the conjecture P ≠ NP, which is still open, and which is in any case a claim about worst-case behaviour — if it is true, then for every algorithm there are inputs on which it takes more than polynomial time. It says nothing about typical inputs, and, because the classification is asymptotic, nothing about the input sizes that actually arise.

Someone might think that this last point softens the limit. It does not. An agent cannot choose the instances it is presented with, and it cannot know in advance that the instance before it is an easy one: a procedure that could certify instances as easy would itself be an efficient decision procedure for the problem, which by hypothesis there is not. Since hard instances cannot be identified and set aside, they have to be counted as typical, and the agent must proceed as though every instance were hard. Its remaining resources are heuristics that may fail and approximations that may be too coarse to be useful. Nor is this a matter of better hardware or cleverer programming, since the difficulty attaches to the problem and not to any particular machine. And since planning and logical inference are among the NP-complete problems, the central operations of practical reason are subject to the same limit.

The step from machines to minds is the Church–Turing thesis. Every physically realizable computing device is at most polynomially slower than a Turing machine, which is why P and NP can be treated as model-independent classes; the limit therefore does not attach to any particular technology. It holds for any system whose behaviour is computable, and so for the human brain if the brain is such a system.

By contrast, if P = NP then every NP-complete problem would have an efficient algorithm, there could be no one-way functions, and the public-key cryptography on which secure communication depends would be impossible. The two possibilities are therefore not symmetric: one yields a limit in principle, the other removes it.

The conclusion is that the claim in the question is defensible. If P ≠ NP, there are problems that no physically possible agent can solve efficiently, and this is a limitation of reason rather than of engineering. What the argument does not show is that any particular instance is hard, or that human beings will do badly on the instances they actually meet; those remain empirical questions, and it would be a mistake to read complexity theory as a prediction of failure.
