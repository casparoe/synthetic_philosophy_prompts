For the past several months I've been reporting on the effort to get machines to learn what people want by watching what people do — recovering an agent's preferences from its choices, then optimizing on the agent's behalf. Over dinner, a researcher who has worked on these methods laid out an argument that the whole enterprise rests on a mistake, and I have not been able to find the error. I want to put it to you exactly as he gave it to me.

(1) To prefer one option to another just is to be disposed to choose it over the other when both are available; preferences are attributed in order to explain and predict choices, and have no content apart from that.

(2) What we actually observe is a single history: the choices an agent made in the situations it faced. What it would have chosen in situations it never encountered is, by definition, never observed.

(3) For any such history there are infinitely many preference rankings that would have produced it. Whatever the agent did, there is a ranking that puts precisely those actions on top; another that puts them on top by a different margin; another that agrees with the first about everything observed and disagrees about everything else. Two agents who differ only about situations they never met will generate identical evidence. And even among the rankings that fit the data, several are behaviorally equivalent — the same choices come out optimal under objectives that differ by a constant factor or a constant offset, and under some that differ by more than that.

(4) Evidence that fits infinitely many incompatible hypotheses, and that comes out exactly the same whichever of them is true, discriminates among none of them and gives no reason to believe any one over the others.

(5) Therefore watching what someone does can never tell us anything about what they prefer.

(6) But (5) proves far too much. We say, and are entitled to say, that a colleague prefers tea to coffee, that a toddler prefers the swings to the sandbox, that the dog wants to go out. Economics, ordinary mind-reading, and any moral assessment of someone's motives all presuppose that behavior is evidence about preferences. Nor is the form of the argument special to preference: any finite body of data can be fit by infinitely many curves, so (4) would equally show that no empirical hypothesis is ever supported by evidence.

(7) So at least one of (1) through (4) must be false.

Which one? I would like to keep (1) — I don't understand what a preference would be with no connection to choosing — but I am open to being told that this is the expensive one. What I need for the piece is not only which step fails but what it costs to drop it: if I concede the point, do I have to give up on there being facts about what people want, or only on a particular picture of how we get at them? And is there a version of the researcher's worry that survives, so that I don't overcorrect and tell my readers that these systems simply read preferences off behavior?
