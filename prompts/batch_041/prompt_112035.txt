I've been turning over an argument that two people I know had last month, and I can't settle it in my own head. Our neighbor Dan told a group of us, flatly and with no hedging, that the Elm Street bridge was closed for repairs. He was right; it was closed. But afterward it came out that he'd only caught a glimpse of a sign while driving past, hadn't really read it, and had filled in the rest from a general impression. Rosa and Marcus took opposite sides, and it went like this.

Rosa: Dan said something true, and true is all a statement has to be. If what he said matches the facts, there's nothing to criticize. We don't get to complain about how he got there.

Marcus: Then lucky guessing would be a model of good speech. If he'd guessed and happened to be right, would you say he did nothing wrong? The whole reason we distinguish asserting from speculating is that an assertion is supposed to be backed by something.

Rosa: Fine, maybe truth is too low a bar. What if the rule is just that you have to believe what you say? Dan believed it.

Marcus: Same problem, one step over. A confident guesser who believes every guess passes that test too. Believing something with no basis behind it isn't the kind of backing we're after. You have to know it.

Rosa: But knowing is asking too much. Nobody's certain of anything, so on your rule we could never say anything about tomorrow's weather. And it gets worse. Suppose Dan had checked the city's website that morning and seen the closure listed, and suppose the website had been tampered with so the listing was fake — he'd have had excellent grounds and the right answer, and you'd still say he didn't know, so by your rule he'd have spoken wrongly even though there's nothing he could have done better. You're holding him to account for a fact he couldn't reach.

Marcus: Knowing doesn't mean being certain — it means having a basis that makes doubt unreasonable. Dan had no basis at all; he had a glimpse and a guess. And yes, the website case is awkward, I'll give you that. But maybe the point of the rule isn't to grade the outcome at all. It's to grade how much care went into the saying.

Rosa: Care comes in degrees, though, while "you may say this" sounds like a yes or a no. Where does enough care begin? If you can't say, I'm not sure you're describing a rule. And here's the part that really bothers me: you keep sliding between "his statement was defective" and "he did something wrong." Those are different claims. A statement can be second-rate — arrived at carelessly, worth less than it sounds — without the person being to blame for making it. To blame him you have to point to something he should have done instead.

Marcus: Usually those come apart, I agree. But not here, because the better move was right in front of him: he could have pulled over, or read the sign properly, or simply said "I think." When the fix is that cheap and he skips it anyway, the defective statement and the blame travel together.

Can you sort this out for me? Go through it turn by turn and say who has the better of it at each step — not who won overall, because I don't think either of them is simply wrong. I'd particularly like to know how much weight Rosa's last point carries and whether Marcus's reply to it meets it. And if there's a distinction they're both missing, or a case that would decide between them, I'd like to hear it.
