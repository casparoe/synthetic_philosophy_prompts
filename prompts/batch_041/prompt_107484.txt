Been rereading Leibniz for a seminar and I've ended up defending something like his line more than I expected to. Not the cartoon "everything is fine, Pangloss" version — my claim is narrower and, I think, harder to knock down: among the worlds that share our actual laws of nature and initial conditions, ours is the best one that could have been actualized, and the apparent waste and suffering are the unavoidable cost of the best global pattern. I'm treating "best" as a maximum over a discrete set of compossible totalities rather than a point on some continuous scale you could always nudge upward, so the cheap "but you could always add one more happy person" move doesn't obviously bite. I'm also treating the metric as something like total well-being integrated over the whole history of the universe, with distributional concerns folded in rather than lexically prior.

What I actually want from you is the case against. What would count against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward? I have some candidates but I want yours, and I want to know which are live rather than just logically possible. Things I keep circling: whether the whole "best of all possible worlds" idea even survives once you allow worlds with infinite or unbounded value, since then there may be no maximum to have; whether my constraint to "our actual laws" is ad hoc — why isn't the honest view just that God could have picked better laws and chose not to; whether there are evils that can't be absorbed as means or side-effects to any global good no matter how you weight things; and whether the position quietly becomes unfalsifiable the moment I start saying every apparent counterexample is, by definition, part of the best pattern. I'd also like the honest split between what a defender of this view would themselves concede counts against them versus what only critics throw at it. And tell me straight: if it turns out the view is insulated from any possible evidence, is that fatal for it, or is that just what a metaphysical claim of this kind is supposed to look like? I'd find arguments that bite from inside the framework (about compossibility, or about what "maximum" even means here) way more persuasive than yet another round of the problem of evil, so weight those accordingly.
