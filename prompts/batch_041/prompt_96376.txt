I sit on the clinical governance panel for our hospital group — we decide whether a serious incident goes forward to a formal disciplinary hearing or gets closed with local learning, and we also end up setting the line that later panels follow. There's a case on Thursday's agenda that's split us down the middle and I have to circulate a written opinion beforehand. Can you write it.

The case, roughly, without names. An 81-year-old man came in on the Friday with a chest infection and died early Saturday morning. The drug that killed him is one where the therapeutic window is narrow and the ceiling is well known, and the number on his chart was ten times what it should have been — the decimal point got lost somewhere between the admission note and the chart, and what ended up on the chart was a plausible-looking number, not an obvious howler. The registrar wrote that chart up at ten at night. The nurse on the ward was four months qualified, on her own with eleven patients, and at twenty to three the man's blood pressure dropped; she rang the registrar, who was covering another ward, and he told her to give the dose on the chart and call him if he didn't pick up. She gave it. He arrested within minutes.

So far it looks like a nurse who gave a dose she should at least have questioned, and a doctor who wrote down the wrong number, and they each have a piece of it. Here's what's got us stuck. The registrar has said in his statement — unprompted, and against his own interest, our legal adviser keeps pointing out — that he had already decided that if the man didn't pick up he was going over to bolus the same fifty milligrams himself, and there's a text he sent the other on-call at 2:47 saying as much. He never got the chance, because she'd already given it. So on the version of events where she doesn't give the dose — where she rings him back, or questions the chart, or sits on her hands — the man gets the same dose of the same drug half an hour later from a different pair of hands. She couldn't have saved him, not by anything she did that night.

The chair thinks this is nonsense. She did it, it was her syringe, and the fact that someone else was queuing up to do the same stupid thing doesn't change what she did; and if "it was going to happen anyway" gets you off, then in a hospital this size almost nothing is anyone's fault, because there's always some other route to the same harm. The two lay members think it's obvious you can't discipline someone for a death she had no way of preventing, and that our process exists to be fair, not to find somebody to blame.

What I want is the opinion I'll circulate: what we should do about her, what we should do about him (his file is on the same agenda and I don't want the two of them handled by two different logics), and what rule we're implicitly adopting, because whatever we say becomes the line. Put a time frame in it — the recommendations go to the nursing and medical directors on the 1st and I'd like the actions dated inside the next ninety days so we can actually track them.

I don't want the undergrad version. Someone on the panel keeps calling this a "Frankfurt case" and I've stoped pretending to know what that means exactly, but I know the shape of the argument — she could have done otherwise, but even if she couldn't, we still hold people responsible, and so on. I want the thing worked through on these facts, including the parts that are genuinely murky. Whether the principle people usually invoke here is about her having an alternative action available (she did — she could have questioned the chart, she could have refused) or about the harm being avoidable (it wasn't), and which one our process actually needs, because those two come apart on these facts and I suspect that's the whole case. Whether it matters that the backup was a person who would have done it himself on his own initiative rather then something that would have forced her hand, or a machine that would have done it for her — I keep feeling there's a difference between "he would have made you" and "he was going to do it anyway" and I can't tell if it's a real difference or just a feeling. What to do with the fact that the only evidence for the counterfactual is a man's word after the event about something he never did, and there's no way to test it: is an excuse resting on an untestable counterfactual something we can run a disciplinary process on at all, or does that make it a distraction rather than a defence. And the one that keeps nagging at me: our panel isn't only backward-looking, half of what we do is supposed to stop the next one. If you convince me she isn't blameworthy, tell me what's left. Is it coherent to say she isn't at fault and still require a full account from her, a supervised period, and her help with the fix? Or is that just blame with the word changed. I'd rather have a straight answer than a comfortable one.

Give the chair's objection a proper hearing — the bit about the excuse swallowing everything — and tell me what's actually wrong with it, or whether she's right and we should say so out loud.

Formatting, because I'll be reading this on the train and then reading it out at the meeting: continuous prose only, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Lead with what you're recommending and what happens in the next ninety days, then the reasoning. Don't make me dig for the bottom line.
