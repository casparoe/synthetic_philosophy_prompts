I am preparing a handout for a third-year undergraduate module that combines formal logic with epistemology, and the sessions on the justification of deduction always stall in the same place. My students can reproduce the tortoise's challenge — a reasoner who grants the premises and the conditional and still declines to grant the conclusion — and they can produce two or three replies, but they cannot see that the replies differ in kind, and they cannot say what each reply is buying or what it costs. I want to replace the reading with a diagnostic instrument: a decision tree that takes a student from a set of concrete verdicts to a single position on what, if anything, justifies our deductive inferences, with every position identified by its claim rather than by the philosopher supposed to have held it.

Please build that tree. Work from the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they permit — and only then locate the positions in it.

The entry point must not be a question of taste such as whether deduction needs justification at all; a quiz-taker should answer it without knowing where it leads. I want two concrete cases used, and you may add one or two of your own. First, the proof-checker case: someone is handed a formal system whose rules are presented as explicit permissions, checks that every line of a proof is licensed by some rule, and then reports that he still sees no reason to affirm the final line. What is your verdict — he has failed to grasp the rules, he is irrational, he is merely unwilling and nothing is epistemically amiss, or the request for a reason is malformed? Second, the revision case: what would have to happen for you to abandon a basic rule such as modus ponens — a counter-instance turning up in some odd domain, a simpler rival system doing more explanatory work, or nothing at all, because the rule is not the sort of thing that can be withdrawn?

The tree must respect three distinctions I rely on in teaching, and each should be a branch node somewhere: (a) warrant for the rule (a reason to think the rule preserves truth) as against warrant for the move (a reason actually to take the step), which may come apart; (b) whether the warrant is local, attaching to each rule on its own, or holistic, attaching to the whole practice of inferring at once; (c) whether the tortoise's demand is answered, dissolved, or simply refused.

The tree must be able to reach at least these seven leaves, each described by its claim: (i) the regress is vicious, so there is no warrant, only a practice we are trained into; (ii) the regress is benign because rules of inference are permissions rather than premises, and the demand for a further premise to license each step rests on a category mistake; (iii) the warrant is a non-inferential, a priori insight into the validity of certain inferences; (iv) the warrant is empirical and holistic, and logical principles are in principle revisable; (v) the rules fix the meanings of the logical constants, so accepting the premises while rejecting the conclusion is a mistake about meaning rather than a failure of rationality; (vi) we are entitled to our basic inferential dispositions by default, with no evidential warrant at all, since any challenge to them presupposes them; (vii) the question is ill-formed, because justification is internal to a practice and the tortoise has simply declined to join it. You may add further leaves if the logical space demands them.

For each leaf I also want two things a quiz-taker never thinks to ask: what would defeat the position, and what is its most awkward commitment.

The tree must be honest about its limits. List the answer patterns that lead nowhere and diagnose each as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate, and supply a question that might separate them if one exists.

Keep to no more than nine branch nodes and between seven and nine leaves. Write for third-year undergraduates who have taken one course in logic. Do not organise anything around names or labels, and do not cite or quote any text.

Give me the whole thing as a single JSON object with the following fields. "entry_node": the id of the first question. "branch_nodes": an array of objects, each with "id", "question", "answers" (an array of objects with "answer" and "leads_to", the latter being a node or leaf id) and "why_this_separates_them" (one sentence). "leaves": an array of objects, each with "id", "position_name" (a short descriptive name, or unnamed if nobody defends it), "core_claim" (one sentence), "answer_path" (an array of strings, each of the form node id: answer), "warrant_for_the_rule", "warrant_for_the_move", "what_would_defeat_it" and "most_awkward_commitment". "unoccupied_paths": an array of objects with "answer_pattern", "diagnosis" (one of incoherent, unattractive, unexplored) and "why". "inseparable_pairs": an array of objects with "leaves" (an array of two leaf ids), "why" and "question_that_might_separate_them" (or null).
