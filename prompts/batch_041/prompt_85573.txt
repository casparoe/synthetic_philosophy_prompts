I am a novelist, about eight books in, and for the past year I have been trying to write down the ethic I actually work by when I put words into the mouths of people who really lived. I have a rough principle, but my own verdicts on cases do not sit under it comfortably, and I would rather be handed a precise principle that costs me something than a vague one that flatters me.

My tentative principle is this. I think of what I do as borrowing: a real person's name and life are on loan to me, and I pay the interest by keeping the borrower recognizable — by which I mean that a reader who knew the person, or who has read what they left behind, would not have to unlearn anything afterward. Invention, on this view, is licensed wherever the record is silent and forbidden where the record speaks. I call this the loan principle, and I suspect it is either vacuous or false, but I cannot yet say which.

Here are my cases and my verdicts on them.

1. A novel about a real nineteenth-century astronomer, documented to the eyelashes in letters and journals, invents a long private conversation between him and his sister in which he says things he never wrote, but which are continuous with positions he took in print. My verdict: not merely permissible but the whole point of the exercise.

2. The same novel has him, in that same unwitnessed conversation, say something contemptuous about his sister's intelligence, which nothing supports and which cuts against the tone of every surviving letter he wrote to her. My verdict: wrong, and wrong in a way I feel before I can justify it.

3. A film invents a sexual relationship between two real, still-famous Victorians, both married to other people, about whom nothing on the subject survives. Both have living descendants who speak publicly about the family. My verdict: I flinch, and when I interrogate the flinch I cannot find ground under it — the record is silent, they were adults — so I conclude it is permitted, and I do not like concluding that.

4. A novel merges three real women of the same circle into one character, gives the composite the documented deeds of all three, changes the name, and discloses the merger in an afterword. My verdict: fine; often better history than a dutiful parade of three thin characters.

5. A novel about a real town keeps its real, unremarkable inhabitants under their real names — the baker, the sexton, the shopkeeper — and gives the shopkeeper a murder he did not commit, as a subplot. Nothing of him survives; no descendant will ever read the book. My verdict: sharply not fine, and more sharply than in case 3, which I cannot square with the fact that the Victorians in case 3 are the ones with living relatives.

6. A novel gives a real colonial administrator, on record as having done considerably worse, one specific invented atrocity, while omitting the documented ones. My verdict: I find this nearly fine, and the verdict embarrasses me.

7. A woman survives in the record as a single line in a parish register; an enslaved man survives as a name in an inventory. A novel gives each of them a full invented inner life. My verdict: not only permitted but, I think, required of anyone who wants to write about them at all.

What I want from you is not a survey of what critics or other novelists have said, and certainly not a reading list. I want the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply them to a manuscript in front of me. For each clause, tell me which of my seven cases motivates it. Then give me your own verdicts on at least three cases I have not listed, including at least one chosen to make the principle fail. Then tell me, honestly, which of my seven verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which one I should surrender, and whether the choice between saving all seven at the cost of complexity and saving a clean principle at the cost of one verdict is a real choice or a false one. Finally, address the borrowing metaphor directly: does it do any work, or is it a euphemism? If the debt is real, who holds it, what discharges it, and does it matter that the debtor cannot correct the ledger? I am interested in whether the wrong here is done to the dead person, to the record, or to the living reader — and whether those come apart in any of my cases.
