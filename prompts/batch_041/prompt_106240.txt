I am assembling a small anthology of poems that are also pieces of philosophy, and I would like you to write a pantoum on the Principal Principle and the problem of undermining.

The thought I want the form to carry is this. The Principal Principle says that a rational agent who knows the objective chance of some outcome — and knows nothing about that outcome beyond what the chance itself already licenses — should simply take the chance as her own credence: if the chance of rain is one in three, she should believe rain to one in three, neither more nor less. But on the standard view that chances are nothing over and above the world's total pattern of fact, the future included, learning the chance is itself a way of learning something about the future; the very information that was supposed to govern the credence seems to make the credence redundant, or worse. The customary escape is to rule such information inadmissible, and I want the poem to register how unsatisfying that escape is.

The pantoum suits this because its lines return, but never in the same position, so a line can enter as an act of deference and leave as a kind of betrayal; and because its last stanza folds back into its first, so the poem circles rather than concludes. Write sixteen to twenty lines — four or five quatrains, rhyme optional — set in one concrete scene: a single speaker who has just been told the exact chance of one event (a coin, a remission, a machine that will either hold or fail) and who is trying, in real time, to make the number her own belief. Let the recurrence of the lines do the philosophical work. Do not explain the principle or the objection in so many words, and do not resolve the tension — I would rather it stay open, the way a circle stays open at the point where it meets itself.

Accuracy matters more than ornament. Someone who has thought about chance and credence should be able to identify, from the poem alone, both the requirement of deference and the worry that knowing the chance undermines it, and should find nothing in it that misstates the problem; someone who has not should still find a poem about the strange experience of trying to believe a number. You need not name any philosophers or cite anything.
