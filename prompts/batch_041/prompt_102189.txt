A colleague in my department is preparing a lecture course on nineteenth-century philosophy of history and has circulated a handout on Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life. Before I comment on it in front of the students I should like to be sure of my ground, and one paragraph in particular has been nagging at me without my being able to say precisely why:

"Nietzsche distinguishes three kinds of history, each answering to a different need. Monumental history serves the man of action, who requires models: it scours the past for greatness to imitate, at the cost of idealising and generalising the record. Antiquarian history serves the man who reveres and preserves: it cherishes the small, familiar, ancestral things of his own place and lineage, at the cost of mummifying life and shutting out whatever is new. Critical history serves the man who suffers under a burden: it hauls the past before the tribunal and passes sentence on it, so that he may be free of it. Nietzsche's sympathies are not in doubt — critical history is the highest of the three, and the burden of the essay is that we should exchange reverence for critique. The remedy he proposes for our over-historical age is the 'suprahistorical' standpoint: the attitude of the beast that lives unhistorically in the present, forgetful and therefore content. In short, the essay is a plea for the moderns to cultivate forgetfulness, and the test it applies throughout is utility — history is to be judged by how far it makes us happy and helps us to get on."

Please audit this paragraph for me. I do not want a general essay on Nietzsche, and you need not supply references or quotations — I have the text to hand — but I do want the summary tested claim by claim. Distinguish (i) what a Nietzsche scholar would accept as sound, (ii) what is a defensible simplification that nevertheless misleads a reader who has not read the essay, and (iii) what Nietzsche explicitly denies or what the essay does not say. I am uneasy about two matters in particular. First, whether the summary's account of the relations among the three species of history is faithful: whether Nietzsche ranks them as the summary does, and whether he treats them as alternatives to be chosen between or as functions that must be held in balance. Second, whether the summary has dealt correctly with the essay's closing discussion of the remedies it proposes for the historical malady, and whether it has properly distinguished the two things Nietzsche sets side by side there. Where the summary is wrong, state the error and say what the essay actually commits him to; where it is right, say so plainly, so that I do not over-correct a competent piece of work. Finally, tell me whether the pedagogical moral the paragraph draws at the end survives your corrections or ought to be rewritten.

Please write your answer in British English.
