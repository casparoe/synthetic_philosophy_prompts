I'm the chair of the clinical ethics committee at a community hospital, and I've got to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting on a case that has us split right down the middle.

The short version: a 78-year-old woman was diagnosed last week with advanced pancreatic cancer. Her three adult kids, who've been at every appointment, caught the oncologist in the hallway and asked him not to tell their mother. Their line is that in their family, and in the community they come from, news like this goes to the eldest son first and he decides how much to share and when -- telling her flat out would strip away her hope and cut the family out of the one thing they know how to do. The oncologist says our policy gives the patient a right to know her own diagnosis, and he doesnt want to sit there and lie to her. He also pointed out, kind of sharply, that the kids' reading of "their culture" might just be what the kids want. One of our members, a palliative care doc, wants us to adopt something she calls relational consent: the family, not just the patient, is the unit that consents, so long as the patient has at some point named a family member to handle her medical info (she did -- her son is her listed contact). She'd wrap that in a two-key rule: the team can hold back the diagnosis only if the named proxy and the attending both agree, the team never affirmatively lies, and the hold gets revisited every 72 hours with an automatic trigger to disclose if the patient asks straight out. Another member says that whole thing is just paternalism with a passport.

What I need from you is the memo itself. Give me a concrete recommendation for this patient, with a time frame, and then a going-forward policy, because we are absolutely going to get more of these. Two things I care about. First, dont let us just chant "autonomy" and roll the family, and dont let us treat "East Asian family values" as a monolith that overrides whatever this particular woman would actually want if someone asked her -- I've watched committees make both of those mistakes. Second, the family's ask has two pieces: keeping the information from her, and taking over the decisions. I have a hunch those come apart, so please dont just lump them together -- tell me whether our policy should treat them the same or differently, and why. And whatever you end up recommending, take the strongest version of the side you're ruling against seriously, because I have to look these people in the eye on Thursday.
