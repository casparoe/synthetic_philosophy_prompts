A friend and I spent most of a train ride argueing about whether the meme idea is actually any good, and I want an outside opinion. Her line is roughly this: treating cultural items — jingles, catchphrases, hand gestures, the plot of a fairy tale — as replicators copied from mind to mind with occasional errors is a dead end, and the real unit isn't the item at all. What actually spreads, she says, is a fixed point of repeated compression. A person recieves some partial cues, and what they end up with is the simplest thing they can build out of those cues that still "counts" as the same item; do that over and over, person to person, and the thing converges on whatever version is easiest to reconstruct from a thin sample. So two people who each hear a song badly end up with the same simplified version, and it spreads not because it's a faithful copy of anything but because it's the cheapest thing a partly-informed brain can land on. On her picture, fidelity isn't a property of the cultural item at all — it's a property of the compression, and "the meme" is just wherever the compression stops moving.

I keep thinking she's either reinventing the meme concept with extra steps or quietly throwing out the baby. So compare the two for me: the classic Darwinian picture, where cultural items are replicators with heritable variation and differential transmission, and hers, where what's transmitted is whatever survives being repeatedly compressed by its receivers. Where do they genuinely disagree, as opposed to describing one process in two vocabularies? Are there cases that pull them apart — say, cultural items that get more elaborate and baroque over time instead of simpler, or items that are easy to remember but somehow never spread? And if she's right that nothing is ever copied with high fidelity, can the Darwinian framing of culture be rebuilt around something other than copying, or does it just collapse? I'd like a verdict at the end rather than a survey, and I don't especially care who said what first.
