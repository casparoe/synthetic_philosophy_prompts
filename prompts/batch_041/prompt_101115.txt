I have recently been diagnosed with a condition that intermittently distorts my confidence in my own reasoning, and I am trying to settle on a policy for what to believe and how strongly to believe it when I have evidence that my first-order reasoning is unreliable. I would like you to rank the following candidate norms for responding to higher-order evidence, from most to least defensible, and to explain in a sentence or two why each lands where it does. Please treat the ranking as a serious verdict rather than a survey.

The norms are:

1. Strict conciliationism: whenever I have higher-order evidence that my first-order reasoning is unreliable, I should move my credence toward whatever the higher-order evidence suggests, even if the first-order evidence still seems strong.

2. Steadfastness: I should continue to trust my first-order reasoning and treat the higher-order evidence as irrelevant to the first-order question.

3. The right-reasons view: higher-order evidence matters only when it gives me new first-order reasons; it cannot by itself require me to revise a belief that is well supported by the first-order evidence.

4. Epistemic consequentialism: I should adopt whatever belief-forming policy maximizes expected epistemic value, even if that means believing something while acknowledging that the belief is not supported by my first-order evidence.

5. Partitioned deference: I should treat my past, unimpaired self’s credences as a prior, update on current first-order evidence, and let current higher-order doubts set a ceiling on the credence I may act on, rather than revising the credence itself.

6. Two-level calibration: I should treat my own occurrent credence as a noisy measurement of the truth, apply a second-order error model to shrink it toward the base rate, and then act on the shrunk credence.

7. Akratic permission: I may believe that p while also believing that my belief that p is irrational, provided the first-order evidence is strong and the higher-order evidence is itself unreliable.

In ranking these, please pay special attention to cases in which the higher-order evidence is itself suspect, for example when the source of the doubt is the very cognitive impairment that the doubt is about. I am especially interested in whether any of these norms can avoid both epistemic akrasia and an irrational deference to a corrupted self-assessment. Please conclude with the one norm you would recommend for me, and say what it would have me do in a concrete case where I feel certain of a conclusion but have reason to think that the feeling of certainty is a symptom.
