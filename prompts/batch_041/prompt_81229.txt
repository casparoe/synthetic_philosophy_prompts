I am an MA student in a metaphysics seminar, and the only person in the department who has genuinely thought about Bradley's regress is a doctoral student, Marta, who is writing a chapter on the unity of the proposition. All term she has been handing down verdicts on other people's accounts of what unifies a proposition, and I have been keeping a list, because her verdicts have a pattern I can feel but cannot state. On the account that posits a further relation to do the unifying — one relation that ties the constituents to one another and to the relation between them — she says the regress is fatal, since the unifier is simply one more thing that must be tied to what it ties. On the Fregean line, on which the unity comes from a concept's being unsaturated rather than from any additional item, she says the regress never gets going. On Russell's multiple-relation theory, on which the unity is contributed by the mind's act of judging, she says the regress leaves the account alone, and she is explicit that this is because the act's relating is taken as primitive and is not itself a further item awaiting a tie; she rejects the account, but for other reasons, chiefly that it makes the unity of the proposition depend on somebody's judging. On the mereological version, on which the proposition is the fusion of its constituents, she says the regress bites, because parthood is one more relation among the same items. On the primitivist line, on which unity is an unanalysable feature of the proposition, she says there is nothing for the regress to bite on, but that this is a refusal to answer rather than an answer. And on the Bradleyan suggestion of a tie that is not itself a relation, she says the regress stops formally, but that until somebody can say what such a tie is, the phrase is a placeholder for ignorance. Through all of it she insists that the regress is not a reductio of relationality as such: it shows that a relation relates without being related, not that the cat is not on the mat.

Next week we reach the account on which a proposition is unified by the order or structure of its constituents — the thing that distinguishes the proposition that a loves b from the proposition that b loves a. Marta has not read it yet and the rest of us are split, so I should like you to predict her verdict and to make the prediction earn its keep. First, what are the competing rules her earlier verdicts could be instances of? I can see at least a rule turning on whether the unifier is one more constituent of the very thing it unifies, and a rule turning on whether the unifier is the sort of item that itself stands in need of unifying; there is also a third I keep reaching for, on which the regress is vicious exactly where the unifier could have failed to unify, though I cannot tell whether the act of judging rules that third rule out or whether she would say that, given the act, its relating could not have been otherwise. Second, say what each rule predicts about the ordering account, on the natural reading of it on which the order is nothing over and above the constituents yet is not itself one of them, and say which rule she is most likely running and why I should prefer that reading of her to the others. Third, and most useful to me, name the case that would settle it: a single further account of unity, specified as precisely as the ordering account above, on which the rules that agree about ordering come apart, together with the verdict each rule would then deliver. Please write in British English.
