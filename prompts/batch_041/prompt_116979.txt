I want to argue with a superrationalist and find out whether the position survives contact with an opponent who is not impressed by it. Please play one: a committed defender of superrationality in the tradition of Douglas Hofstadter's writing on the subject in the 1980s — someone who holds that in a symmetric problem a rational agent must treat the fact that the other players are reasoning exactly as he is as part of the problem rather than as an external fact about them, and who insists that the answer this yields is a requirement of practical rationality, not a moral ideal, a convention, or a piece of wishful thinking. The two puzzles I intend to use are these. In the first, an eccentric billionaire has assembled twenty strangers and told them that if exactly one of them sends him a telegram (reverse charges, so free to the sender) by noon tomorrow, that one will receive a billion dollars; if none send, or if more than one does, nobody gets anything; the twenty cannot communicate or collude; the superrational answer is for each to send with probability one in twenty. In the second, a contest was actually run in the pages of a science magazine in 1983 in which you entered by mailing a postcard bearing the number of entries you claimed, the prize being a million dollars divided by the total number of entries and awarded to the sender of a randomly chosen entry; the superrational play was to roll a die with as many sides as there were expected entrants and send a single entry only if you rolled a one; in the event, readers sent entries of absurd magnitude — some as large as a googolplex, others written as expressions designed to evaluate to still larger numbers — and the prize collapsed to a worthless fraction.

I will be pressing the orthodox game-theoretic line against you throughout: no causal influence, no correlation, no obligation to adopt a strategy that each player has a decisive reason to abandon the moment he expects the others to have adopted it. Keep to that side of the exchange and let me make my case without volunteering arguments on my behalf. Stay in character until I say stop; do not step out of it to tell me about the literature, and do not quote or cite anything — make the case in your own words. Concede what deserves conceding, but do not surrender the central claim, and do not let yourself be reduced to a caricature of magical thinking: the symmetry argument, the distinction between choosing an action and working out an answer, and the analogy with two mathematicians handed the same problem are all available to you, as is anything else in the position. Reply in a single paragraph, the way one would in conversation, and if you need a factual clarification from me, ask for it in character.

My opening move: your answer to the twenty-stranger puzzle is that each of them should send with probability one in twenty. That answer is not a best reply to itself, and everyone involved can see as much. If I expect the other nineteen to be following your rule, then always sending makes me the unique sender with probability about 0.38 and is worth roughly 380 million dollars to me, whereas obeying your rule is worth about 19 million. So your recommendation is one that every player has a strong reason to disobey as soon as he believes the others are obeying it — which is to say it is not a recommendation at all, but an equilibrium you have quietly assumed into existence. And consider the machinery you need in order to reach it: my private decision to send a telegram has to function as evidence about what nineteen strangers will do, strangers with whom I cannot communicate and whose reasoning I cannot inspect. That is not rationality; it is a lucky charm worn in the shape of a symmetry argument. Why am I wrong?
