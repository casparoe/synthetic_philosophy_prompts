Compose a mock sovereign-credit-rating report, roughly 800 words, in which a fictional agency rates a state's political legitimacy as though legitimacy were a debt instrument. Invent the agency, the state, the analysts, and the internal documents cited; cite nothing real, and include no bibliography, since the whole apparatus is fiction — the report's footnotes should refer only to the agency's own invented methodology papers and committee minutes.

The piece is satire, and its target is a specific intellectual practice: the argument that prosperity and order are sufficient to justify authoritarian rule, together with the institutional vocabulary — ratings, outlooks, watchlists, willingness to pay — that translates political legitimacy into a solvency metric. The humour must come from the genre's own logic rather than from invented facts, so that a reader who works on comparative politics or political obligation finds it recognisable rather than cartoonish.

Give the report the following form: a rating-action header naming the instrument, the rating, and the outlook; numbered sections covering rating rationale, methodology, peer comparison, and outlook or watchlist; a short dissenting memorandum from a member of the rating committee; and a legal disclaimer.

The following must be true of the document, and none of them may be explained within it:

- The agency rates ability and willingness to pay in the currency of legitimacy, where ability is the delivery of prosperity and order and willingness is the sovereign's disposition to keep delivering.
- Order is capitalised as a perpetuity, while the agency's own growth assumptions imply that the flows supporting it are of uncertain and possibly short duration.
- The principal order indicator is compiled and supplied by the rated sovereign; the report records this as a material weakness in data quality and states that it does not affect the rating.
- The comparison group of peer states was proposed by the issuer and adopted without amendment.
- The instrument is explicitly conditional: the mandate is said to hold only while performance holds. No mechanism by which the mandate lapses appears anywhere in the document — there is no election and no exit, and the report states, without elaboration, that exit is not a rated event.
- The rating scale includes one category that has never been assigned, because issuers do not survive to be rated at it.
- The outlook is stable, and the methodology section concedes that outlook changes are ordinarily published after the developments that cause them.
- The disclaimer asserts that the agency makes no normative judgement about who ought to rule, and contains one sentence that, read plainly, concedes that the rating is consumed as exactly such a judgement.

The dissent should press one of these points and be overruled on procedural grounds, in the flat register of a committee minute.

Write in the cold, procedural voice of the genre. No character steps outside the fiction, no sentence names the objections the document is built to raise, and nothing winks at the reader. The objections — the gap between legitimacy as acceptance and legitimacy as entitlement, the counterfactual-consent problem, the benchmark problem, the reversibility problem — should be reconstructible from the document alone.
