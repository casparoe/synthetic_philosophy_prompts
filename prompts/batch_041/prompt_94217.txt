I am helping a zoo and aquarium welfare committee prepare the ground for a dispute it keeps re-litigating: which animals have phenomenal consciousness at all, and how confident anyone can reasonably be about the borderline cases. Before we argue about policy I want a map of the underlying disagreement. Please produce a decision tree rather than an essay.

Build it from the logical space first, not from a roster of names. Every internal node should be a question a committee member could answer without already knowing which verdict it commits them to; every leaf should carry a short name for the resulting position together with one sentence identifying its most awkward commitment. Keep any single path to no more than six questions.

The distinctions we care about, in roughly the order they should sort people, are these. First, whether phenomenal consciousness is a threshold property that some animals have and others simply lack, or something that varies along separable dimensions — richness, valence, temporal thickness, unification into a single subject — so that a question such as whether a bee is conscious is ill-formed until one specifies the dimension and the cut-off. Second, whether one's criterion of attribution is a set of behavioural and physiological markers that nearly everyone accepts as evidence (flexible goal-directed behaviour, trade-offs between pain and other motivations, self-administration of analgesics, rebound after anaesthesia) or is derived from a substantive theory of consciousness, in which case one's verdicts shift with the theory, since global-workspace, recurrent-processing, and higher-order accounts do not carve the animal kingdom the same way. Third, whether valenced experience can come apart from mere phenomenal presence, so that an animal might have experiences with no good or bad feel to them, or the reverse. Fourth, whether attribution requires a single integrated subject rather than a federation of semi-autonomous sub-systems. Fifth, how far a positive result in one species licenses an inference about its whole clade.

The leaves should include at least the following: consciousness confined to animals with a layered cortex; consciousness extended to birds on the strength of their pallium; consciousness grounded in conserved midbrain and brainstem structures, hence present in all vertebrates; the same plus cephalopods; the same plus arthropods; a view requiring only some minimal form of integrative processing; a graded or dimensional view; a deflationary view on which there is no determinate fact about borderline cases independent of our attributive practices; and a view on which the facts are determinate but inaccessible to us. Add whatever further leaf the space demands.

Then, separately: list the combinations of answers the tree cannot reach and say of each whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely undefended; flag any pairs of leaves the tree cannot separate in practice, with the reason; and give a table applying every leaf to one fixed set of cases — a honeybee, an octopus, a trout, a newborn human infant, a decorticated mammal with an intact midbrain, and an anaesthetised animal — marking each entry as conscious, not conscious, or indeterminate on that view. No citations and no reading list; I want the structure of the disagreement, not its literature.
