I am setting the final examination for a 400-level seminar on consequentialism, and the essay section needs to do better than ask students to state the cluelessness objection and then list the replies they have been taught. The question I want should force them to decide where the objection actually bites and to defend that decision against the stock replies. Because the exam is closed-book, and because I do not want to reward recall of a reading list, the question must be answerable by a student who knows the arguments but not who made them; the model answer you write for me should likewise refer to positions by description rather than attaching them to authors or titles.

Here is the apparatus I want the question built around. In the last two weeks of the course I introduce a distinction between two ways an agent can be clueless about the consequences of an action. The first, evidential cluelessness, is ignorance of the empirical facts: the causal chain runs on further than anyone can trace, so the agent does not know what will happen. The second, evaluative cluelessness, survives even the complete empirical story: the agent knows exactly what will happen and still cannot rank the resulting states of affairs, because the value theory supplies no ordering — as it may not where outcomes involve astronomically many future lives, infinite or unbounded value, or goods the theory treats as incommensurable. My claim in lecture, which I want the exam to put under pressure, is that the three standard replies to the cluelessness objection — that the agent should maximize expected value anyway, that robustly good actions can be identified without tracing consequences, and that cluelessness afflicts every moral theory and so cuts both ways — are each aimed at evidential cluelessness, and that at least one of them fails outright once evaluative cluelessness is on the table.

Please produce three things. First, the essay question itself: a single question that a well-prepared student could answer in seventy-five minutes, that names both forms of cluelessness explicitly so a grader can see at a glance whether the candidate has engaged with the distinction, and that cannot be answered by reciting the objection and the three replies. It should require a verdict rather than a survey, and it should not presuppose that the objection succeeds — a candidate must be free to argue either way, but must commit. Second, a model answer of roughly eight hundred words, written as a strong student would write it under time pressure, that would earn full marks; it should apply its verdict to at least one concrete decision, so that the answer is not purely abstract. Third, a marking scheme of six must-hit points, each with a stated mark value, summing to twenty, with one line per point on how candidates typically lose it, plus a note on the two or three recurring confusions that should cap a candidate's mark, and a closing sentence on what separates a competent answer from a first-class one.

Two constraints on the model answer. It must not depend on any citation, and it must not attribute a view to a named author or work; where it needs a label, it should describe the position. And it must end with a short, explicit section saying what would change its verdict — which consideration, turning out one way rather than the other, would move it to the opposite conclusion — and where its reasoning is most likely to fail. Give that section its own mark in the scheme, so that students can see the self-assessment is part of the exercise.
