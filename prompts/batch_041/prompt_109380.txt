ok, so my mech interp reading group has been going in circles for about three months and I want to force the two loudest people in it to actually sit down and write something together.

Here's the fight. One of them — call her the realist — thinks that when we find a feature in a sparse dictionary trained on a model's activations, or a direction a linear probe can read off, or a small circuit that does a recognizable job, we're discovering something about what the network is really representing internally. Not a handy fiction, not an artifact of our tooling — the actual joints. The other one, the deflationist, thinks every one of those findings is downstream of choices we made: the dictionary size, the sparsity penalty, the probe's hypothesis class, the baseline you patch against, the distribution you sampled. On his view "the model's representation of X" is a stance we take toward a system, not a fact we uncover, and if you swapped out the tooling you'd get a different but equally good list of "the model's features."

What kicked it off was a throwaway experiment someone ran. Two dictionaries, same model, different sparsity penalties. They disagree about which features exist — where one has a single clean feature the other has three overlapping ones, and vice versa. Both reconstruct the activations fine, both get rated as highly interpretable by the humans we showed them to, both steer behaviour when you add the decoder directions back in. Then someone ran the same dictionary pipeline on a model with the same architecture but random weights, and a few "features" still passed the usual tests. And when we trained a dictionary on model A and transplanted individual features into model B, some did the same causal work and some did nothing at all. The realist says the ones that transplant and survive intervention are the real ones. The deflationist says transplantability is a property of your dictionary and your data, not a fact about the network's furniture.

What I want from you: write the joint statement these two could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I need (1) a numbered list of claims they both accept — about what the tools actually establish, what's uncontroversial, what they jointly predict; (2) the precise points where they still differ, each written in wording its own side would accept, and non-vacuous (no "well it depends what you mean by representation" weaseling); (3) the cruxes, i.e. the sub-questions where settling one would actually move somebody; and for each crux, a concrete test, case or arguement that both sides agree would bear on it, plus each side's prediction about how it comes out, written down in advance. I suspect one crux is whether causal efficacy is enough to earn the word "representation" or whether it just shows you found a knob, and another is whether there's any fact of the matter about which of the two dictionaries is the right one — but don't just take my list. If part of the dispute turns out to be verbal — if it's really about what work the word "representation" is doing rather than about the network — say which part and why, and say what the substantive residue is once that's stripped out.

Don't strawman either of them, both are sharp and neither is confused. No citations, no reading list, no "see the literature on X" — I don't want a bibliography, I want the arguement. Keep it to a page or two and make it something we could actually hand to the group.
