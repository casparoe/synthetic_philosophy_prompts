my reading group is finishing a term on promises and the last session is on why they bind at all, and i'd rather close with a poem than a summary. so: a pantoum, four stanzas, sixteen lines, strict on the repetition rule — lines 2 and 4 of each stanza come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next, and the last stanza has to fold back onto the opening lines. i dont care about rhyme or meter. make the promise itself the opening line, so the poem has to come back around to it whether or not it ever gets discharged.

what i want it to carry is the bit we kept circling and that none of the standard answers quite gets. the usual suspects are that promising is a made-up virtue that pays off socially, that it's about not disappointing someone you've got counting on you, and that it's a practice already up and running in the community so the obligation comes from the practice rather than from the single act. the thing none of them handles well is the direction it all points. a promise hands the person you made it to a kind of authority over what you now owe, and that authority is one-way: they can release you at any moment, for no reason at all, and the release works — which is strange if what's really doing the work is their interest in getting the thing, since they can toss that interest and the promise was still binding right up until they did. you can't release yourself. nobody else can release you either. and it holds even when nobody relied on anything and no one ends up worse off, so a broken promise can be a wrong without being a harm, and the wrong is owed to that one person and not to the room.

so of the four lines that keep coming back, make one the promisee's release and make another one sound like the promiser letting himself off the hook. that second one has to recur in every stanza and never once do any work, and by the end it should be plain which of the two lines actually moves anything. someone who's read the litrature on this should be able to point at the two of them and say which is which without me explaining it.

verse only please. no headings, no section labels, no bold, no bullet points or tables, no epigraph, no notes after it telling me what you were up to — i'm reading it out loud to the group and i dont want scaffolding.
