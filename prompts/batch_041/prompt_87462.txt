For about a year now a small reading group I sit in on — two philosophers of science, a physician who works in a bone-density clinic, and me — has been going around and around the naturalism/normativism dispute about health and disease, and I've come to suspect the disagreement is structurally more interesting than either side admits. The naturalist position, in the version we keep rehearsing, says disease is a value-free biological category: some part or process fails to make its species-typical contribution to survival or reproduction, where typicality is fixed statistically over a reference class. The normativist position says no such analysis can ever be completed, because nothing counts as a disease unless it is bad for the person who has it, and badness is evaluative, so the normative element sits at the foundation rather than at the edges. We know both positions and we know the standard objections; the trouble is that nothing moves. The naturalist offers a cleaned-up analysis and someone produces a case it misclassifies; the normativist presses the case and the naturalist replies that a contested intuition is being smuggled in as data. Our physician keeps returning to conditions diagnosed by distance from a young-adult reference value rather than from the patient's own age-matched peers, and observes that whether she has a case on her hands depends on which comparison class the analysis picks — and neither side has a shared procedure for deciding that.

My hypothesis, which I'd like you to treat as a hypothesis rather than an assumption, is that the two camps are answering different questions under one heading: one is offering an explication of a concept fit for research classification, the other an analysis of the concept as it actually functions in clinical judgment and in public argument about who counts as ill, and that most of the apparent disagreement is downstream of that, with a smaller substantive remainder concerning whether a goal-free notion of biological function can be made to work at all. Our physician pushes further and suggests the whole thing is a dispute about whether disease is a natural kind rather than a practical kind whose extension is settled by the purposes of whoever is classifying — I can't tell whether that reframing dissolves the impasse or merely relocates it.

Diagnose the impasse for me. Is it verbal, and if so which parts of it, and what would actually be lost by saying so? Is a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work — for instance whether the reference-class problem admits of a non-arbitrary solution, or whether malfunction can be specified without appeal to what is good for the organism? Is it a disagreement about which cases count as data, given that the contested cases (aging, infertility, conditions once classified as disorders and no longer, the periodically shifting thresholds for hypertension and for bone density) are exactly the ones each side needs? Or do the two sides simply start from intuitions that neither can argue for? Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a change in the question — and whether any of it is available; if you think the dispute is better abandoned than resolved, say so and say what should take its place. Please don't survey the literature at me; I want the diagnosis and the reasons for it, and I want to know which parts of the debate are live and which are artifacts of how the question is framed.
