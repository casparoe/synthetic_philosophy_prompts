I am a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been arguing with a few friends from a distributed-systems reading group about an argument that I first thought was a clever way to show deontological constraints are incoherent, but now I suspect it proves too much. The argument is mine, though I am sure it is not original. I will call it the quota argument because I keep picturing rights as rate limits: each person has a quota of one unit of protection against being killed, and the question is whether a rule that forbids killing one person to save five is really saying that one quota unit outweighs five. My friends say this is just the paradox of deontology, but that label has not helped me see where the reasoning fails, if it does.

The argument goes like this. (1) It is wrong to intentionally kill one innocent person even when that is the only way to prevent five other innocent people from being killed by someone else. (2) If (1) is true, there must be a reason why it is wrong. (3) The only plausible reason is the harm to the one person: killing destroys a life and violates that person's right not to be used as a mere means. (4) But the five also have a right not to be killed, and if I do nothing, five lives are destroyed. (5) If the reason not to kill the one is the harm to that person and the violation of that person's right, then the same kind of reason counts, five times over, against letting the five die. (6) A reason of the same kind that counts five times over outweighs a reason that counts once, unless there is some further difference between killing and letting die. (7) There is no further difference: in both cases I know the outcome, I can prevent it, and the victims are equally innocent. (8) Therefore, if (1) is true, I am also required to kill the one to save the five, since otherwise I am letting five quota violations happen when I could prevent them by causing one. But that contradicts (1). (9) So the deontological constraint cannot be justified by the harm or rights of the person killed; it must be a bare rule that treats one person's being used as more important than five people's lives. (10) A bare rule like that is either irrational or a fetish, so deontological constraints are either unjustified or they secretly collapse into a consequentialist calculation.

I would rather not give up (1), because without it I cannot explain why I should not push someone off a bridge to stop a trolley, and I would rather not give up (7), because I do not see a principled difference between killing and letting die. So I cannot name the problem, but I suspect there is one. Please diagnose the argument for me.
