I want your credence in a proposition about interpersonal comparisons of utility, together with the reasoning that produces it, not a survey of the literature.

The proposition is this: there is a fact of the matter, independent of any convention for representing people on a common scale, about the comparative magnitude of two individuals' wellbeing — that is, not only about which of them is better off, but about whether the difference between A and B exceeds the difference between C and D.

Some background I want you to work with rather than summarise. Preferences and hedonic states are usually represented by functions unique only up to some class of transformations, and nothing about a single person considered alone settles how their scale lines up with anyone else's; so a claim of the kind above must rest on something over and above the individual data. Social choice theory carries a familiar taxonomy of what a comparison rule may presuppose: ordinal non-comparability; ordinal level comparability, which lets us say who is better off but not by how much; ordinal full comparability; cardinal non-comparability; cardinal unit comparability, which lets us compare differences while leaving the zero point arbitrary; and cardinal full comparability. It is commonly said that a maximin rule needs no more than level comparability, whereas anything that sums or averages wellbeing needs comparability of units — so the two halves of the proposition may stand or fall together, or come apart. Two further complications to keep in view: the suggestion that there is a fourth comparative relation, parity, under which two items are comparable although neither is better than the other nor equal to it; and the objection that no single metric of advantage is adequate across persons whose circumstances and capacities differ, so that even level comparisons may be partly stipulative. The device of an extended-sympathy ordering — ranking outcomes from a single standpoint while occupying each person's position in turn — is the usual attempt to supply the missing information; say whether you think it succeeds.

Give me a number between 0 and 1 for the proposition as stated, then decompose it. At a minimum I want:

- your probability that wellbeing has determinate magnitudes at all, as against being vague or incomplete;
- your probability, conditional on that, that comparisons of differences across persons are determinate rather than fixed by a convention we could have chosen otherwise;
- your probability that the level and difference components come apart, with separate numbers for each if you think they do;
- your probability that the correct account of whatever comparability exists is naturalistic rather than irreducibly evaluative;
- your probability that parity exists and, if it does, whether it defeats the proposition or is an instance of it;
- anything else you think belongs, with one line of justification per term and a note on how the terms combine.

If you think the proposition is not well formed as it stands, say so, substitute the nearest well-formed claim, and give your credence in that instead, with the decomposition adjusted accordingly.

Then tell me which term you are least confident about, which one the final figure is most sensitive to, and what argument or evidence would move it most. Two colleagues of mine put the proposition at 0.85 and 0.15 respectively; say where their disagreement most plausibly lives. Finally, give your own figure for the weaker claim that levels at least can be compared across persons, and say which terms of the decomposition change when you move from the stronger claim to the weaker one.

Please write your answer in British English.
