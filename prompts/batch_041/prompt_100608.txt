Last night a friend and I spent most of an evening arguing about Memento, and the argument kept circling back to a question neither of us could settle: when Leonard constructs the story he can live with, is he revising a memory of something that genuinely happened to him, or is the story doing the work of constituting what he experienced in the first place? I want to read the film through the distinction philosophers of mind draw between Orwellian and Stalinesque revision, and through the color phi phenomenon that is supposed to make that distinction empirically pressing. By Orwellian revision I mean the picture on which an experience occurs in a determinate way and is only afterward edited in memory or report, so that there is a fact of the matter about what was experienced and the later narrative is a falsification of it. By Stalinesque revision I mean the picture on which the editing is not a later falsification but part of the staging of experience itself, so that the retrospective story is not a distortion of an independent experiential fact but the process by which the experience is constituted. The color phi phenomenon is the experimental wedge: two dots of different colors are flashed in succession, and subjects report a single dot moving and changing color in mid-flight, with the color change reported as occurring before the second dot is presented. If those reports are taken at face value, the brain seems to revise experience retrospectively; if they are not, it seems to construct the experience prospectively. The philosophical puzzle is whether the Orwellian and Stalinesque models are experimentally or even conceptually distinguishable, given that all the subject ever has access to is the final report.

Please give me a serious reading of Memento through that lens. I am not asking for a plot summary or a bibliography. I want to know whether the film commits to one model or refuses the choice. Where does it dramatize Orwellian revision, as when Leonard genuinely undergoes something and then edits it through tattoos, Polaroids, and notes? Where does it dramatize Stalinesque revision, as when the Sammy Jankis story, the condition itself, and the tattooed facts seem to constitute the only experience Leonard can be said to have had? What should we make of the reverse chronology, which forces the viewer to undergo something like retrospective construction in the very act of watching? Does the film use its structure to make a philosophical point about the timing of experience, or does it merely trade on the ambiguity? And where does the film resist the reading, so that an honest critic has to say the distinction does not map cleanly onto what the film is doing?

I would like the reading to be faithful in both directions: do not reduce Memento to an illustration of a philosophy-of-mind debate, and do not distort the debate to fit the film. End by saying what the film adds to the philosophical discussion that argument alone does not. At the close, tell me how confident you are in the reading you have given and identify the single point in it you are least sure about.
