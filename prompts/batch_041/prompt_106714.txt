Here is the question as it appeared on the paper:

"Question 3. The units-of-selection debate is often introduced as the question of whether natural selection acts on genes, on individuals, or on groups. As the lecture notes argue, that formulation bundles several distinct questions together. The notes distinguish two roles that an entity may play in a selection explanation: the beneficiary, whose fitness a trait is selected to promote, and the mediator, whose properties causally bring about the differential reproduction. Using that distinction: (a) take a trait that is costly to the individual that bears it but raises the reproductive output of the local group, and show how a gene's-eye account and a multilevel (group-selection) account each describe the case, identifying in each which entity plays the beneficiary role and which plays the mediator role; (b) say whether the two accounts can ever differ in what they predict about which traits will spread, and if they can, describe the kind of circumstance that would separate them; (c) assess the claim that, once the roles are distinguished, the debate is merely verbal—a dispute about how to keep the books rather than about how nature works."

And here is the student's answer:

"The debate is really several questions wearing one label, and the notes' two roles are a good way of separating them. I'll take the roles as given: the beneficiary is whatever the trait is selected to benefit, and the mediator is whatever's properties cause the differential reproduction. (a) Consider an alarm call that lowers the caller's own survival but raises the number of offspring the local group produces. On the gene's-eye account, the beneficiary is the gene for the trait: the gene is what is copied, and the trait is selected because it promotes the gene's own propagation. The mediator is the organism, since it is the organism's behaviour—its calling or not calling—that brings about the differential reproduction of the genes it carries; the organism is a vehicle for the gene. On the multilevel account, the beneficiary is the group: groups containing callers leave more descendants than groups of non-callers, so the trait is selected because of what it does for the group. Here the mediator is again the individual organism, whose trait is what makes one group outperform another. So both accounts can describe the same episode; they just put the beneficiary in a different place. (b) Since any case the group-selectionist calls selection between groups can be rewritten by the gene-selectionist as selection between genes, the two accounts cannot disagree about which traits will spread. The group-level description is a convenient bookkeeping device, and the gene-level one is the more fundamental; but they will always yield the same prediction, so the difference between them is one of emphasis rather than of substance. (c) That is why I think the debate is largely verbal. If two accounts are compatible with all the same outcomes, there is no fact of the matter for them to disagree about, so the choice between them is a matter of which description we find useful. The gene's-eye view holds that the gene is the unit of selection—that is, the entity whose properties cause the differential reproduction—whereas the multilevel view holds that the group is the unit of selection in just that sense; and the gene's-eye view, after all, denies that group selection ever really occurs, while the multilevel view insists that it does. But because these are only two ways of partitioning the same reproduction, the quarrel is about words. It would become substantive only if someone produced a case in which the two descriptions come apart, and I don't think such a case can be given."
