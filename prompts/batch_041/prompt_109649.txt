I've been arguing for weeks with two friends about Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility — the one that runs roughly: you do what you do because of how you are, so being truly responsible for what you do would require being truly responsible for how you are; being responsible for how you are would require an earlier act of self-determination whose source you would also have to be responsible for; and the regress never bottoms out in anything that is finally yours. One friend, Dana, puts her credence at about 0.15 that the argument is sound and that it establishes what it advertises — that no one is truly morally responsible for anything, and that this holds whether or not determinism is true, so that libertarian free will is no escape. My other friend, Rafael, is at about 0.55; he is convinced there is a real hole somewhere but cannot say where, and he half suspects the whole dispute is verbal, about a notion of responsibility that no one should have wanted in the first place. I am closer to Rafael, but I do not trust my own decomposition, which is why I want yours.

Please give me your own credence in a single proposition precise enough to be true or false — something like: no human adult is truly morally responsible for any of her actions, where true moral responsibility is understood to require that the agent be the ultimate, self-originating source of the character and motives from which the action flows, and where the claim is meant to hold independently of whether determinism is true. Then decompose it. Price at least the following sub-questions, supplying your own additional terms if you think the list is incomplete: first, the transfer or sourcehood premise itself — whether responsibility for an action genuinely requires responsibility for the springs of that action, or whether a view on which agents are responsible for actions simply in virtue of those actions expressing their evaluative commitments (the answerability or attributability tradition associated with Scanlon and with Angela Smith, on which blame addresses the agent rather than demanding that she have authored herself) can sever the requirement. Second, the tracing escape: whether an agent's deliberate, long-run reshaping of her own character — someone who spends a decade in a regimen of self-modification, each step caused by her existing traits together with reasons she endorses — can ground responsibility for the resulting character without any self-originating first step, and whether the standard reply that this merely relocates the problem onto unchosen capacities, such as the reflective capacity that got the project going, is decisive. Third, the agent-causal escape: whether an undetermined agent-causal power of the sort some libertarians posit could serve as the needed self-moved source, and whether the reply usually attributed to Strawson — that an exercise of such a power at a time is still not something the agent is ultimately the author of — is fair to the position or merely begs the question. Fourth, the deflationary or verbal question: whether the only notion the argument defeats is an ultimate-sourcehood notion that is either incoherent or not required by our actual practices of blame, praise, punishment, and forgiveness, so that soundness would carry much less practical weight than it appears to. Fifth, whether the argument's all-or-nothing structure is a weakness, in that responsibility might come in degrees, so that partial authorship of one's character yields partial responsibility, which may be all we ever had or needed. For each term give me a rough probability and one line of justification, then show how they combine into your final number. Finally, tell me which term you are least confident in, which single term your final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or empirical finding would move you most — and then, on the basis of your decomposition, say where Dana and Rafael most likely disagree: which one sub-question, if it were resolved, would be most likely to bring their two numbers together.
