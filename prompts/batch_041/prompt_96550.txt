I am leading the next session of our department's interdisciplinary reading group, and the topic is the doomsday argument: the claim that if you reason as though you were a random sample drawn from all the humans who will ever have lived, then your birth rank — somewhere in the tens of billions — gives you reason to think the total number of humans is probably not vastly larger than the number who have already lived, a conclusion usually cashed out as a substantial probability that humanity ends or dwindles within a modest number of generations. The audience is roughly fifteen graduate students in statistics and economics plus two physicists. They are comfortable with probability, they have never met anthropic reasoning, and several of them are suspicious of any probability that lacks a chance setup. I have seventy-five minutes and a whiteboard. Do not explain the argument to me; I know it. Plan how I should explain it.

My own reading, which you should correct up front if it is wrong, is that the argument is a probabilistic shift rather than a proof of imminent doom, and that its conclusion concerns the total number of humans rather than a date. That reading determines the order of everything else.

- The order in which the components must come, with the dependencies spelled out. What has to be in place before the argument can be stated in a way that lands rather than sounds like a trick, and which step, if I rush it, will make everything after it feel like sleight of hand? Should the reference-class problem be raised early, as a caveat, or only once the argument has been felt, and why? Should I show that the argument is a shift other evidence can swamp, or does that belong at the end?
- The single example that should carry the session, and what it must have and must not smuggle in. I do not want the numbered-balls-in-an-urn lottery; in my experience it makes people think the argument is a claim about a chance device, which hands them the objection that there is no randomizing device here. If you think that objection is unavoidable with any example, say so, and tell me how the example should be built so that the randomness assumption is the thing under discussion rather than a hidden premise. If you think the bare formulation — you are a random human — should carry it, tell me why it is not circular.
- The misconceptions, in the order they will surface, and exactly where in the sequence I should head each off. My guesses: that the argument predicts a date; that it is simply the Copernican principle and therefore trivially true; that a probability claim requires a randomizing device and so the whole thing is ill-posed; that it must be unsound because anyone at any time can run it, including people who by its own lights would be living in the far future; that the reference class can be gerrymandered, so the argument proves nothing; and that the rival self-indication principle — which makes your existence evidence for a larger world, and which in one version tells you to be nearly certain the universe is enormous — refutes it. Tell me which of these are real objections, which are confusions, and where each belongs.
- What I should leave out at this level and say that I am leaving out. Candidates: the formalism of the two competing sampling assumptions, the Sleeping Beauty literature, the application to fine-tuning, the quantum-many-worlds version, exact numerical estimates, and the parallel arguments that run off your position among all observers in the universe rather than among humans.
- One question to put to the group at the end that would show whether the idea has landed rather than been nodded at, and what a wrong answer to it would look like.
- How to close the session without pretending to settle whether the argument is sound, since I do not think it is settled.
