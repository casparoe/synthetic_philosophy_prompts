Write a parody of a corporate engineering artifact: an internal design-review memo, circulated at a fictional genomics firm, proposing that the human genome be treated as a codebase and put through a refactoring project. Invent the firm and the project (a product with a version number, a plausible-sounding internal office, a date). Around 800 words, formatted as a memo with numbered sections, in the flat, hedged register of a real engineering memo, and including at least three marginal comments from a dissenting reviewer who holds that genetic information is a substantive, real notion rather than a figure of speech.

The humor must come from getting the biology and the philosophy of biology right. The memo should treat as literal engineering facts things that are in fact true of genomes: the genetic code as a degenerate mapping from sixty-four codons onto twenty amino acids and stop signals; the near-universality of that mapping and its documented exceptions, in mitochondria and a few odd lineages; overlapping reading frames; alternative splicing; the absence of any privileged start-of-program; and stretches of the genome that regulate without coding. It should also commit the errors the metaphor invites, and here is where the piece should be sharpest: it should keep asking where the interpreter sits, treat the junk-DNA label as settled, cite the claim that most of the genome is functional as though it were uncontested, and assume a clean split between genome-as-software and cell-as-hardware. The dissenting reviewer is the one who should be allowed to say that information in the content-neutral, uncertainty-reducing sense and information in the about-something sense are two different things, and that the memo keeps sliding between them.

The satire's target is a practice, widespread well beyond molecular biology, of saying that DNA is a code, a program, or a language as though this were a description rather than a metaphor doing unearned work. Exaggerate that real feature; do not invent biology to make the joke land. The irony should stay deadpan, legible to a general reader and funny to someone who knows the literature, without the memo ever admitting it is a joke.

Close with a short, non-ironic author's note in your own voice, about 150 words: which real disputes the piece leans on, where it takes liberties with them, and, since this is the nearest thing to a verdict a parody can give, what consideration would make the satire unfair, that is, what would show that the code metaphor is doing legitimate explanatory work after all.
