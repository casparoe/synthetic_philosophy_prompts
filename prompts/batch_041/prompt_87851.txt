I am assembling materials for a one-semester independent-study project in decision theory and game theory, and I would like you to draft the research proposal for it. The setting is the pair of puzzles introduced by Douglas Hofstadter: the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery. In the Platonia dilemma, some number of people are each promised a large prize if and only if exactly one of them sends in a telegram, with no communication and no binding agreements permitted; naive individual reasoning tells everyone to send, which would spoil it for all, and the "superrational" answer is usually given as each participant sending with probability equal to one over the number of participants. In the Luring Lottery, entrants may submit any whole number of entries, the prize is one million dollars divided by the total number of entries, and a single entry is drawn at random to receive it; here the superrational prescription is said to be a randomization that makes the expected total about one entry, but because the strategy space is unbounded, any number of symmetric prescriptions look equally defensible.

My half-formed worry, which I want the proposal to sharpen rather than merely restate: the superrational prescriptions in both games appear to depend on an unstated assumption that the participants are in the same situation in a strong sense, and in the Luring Lottery the assumption of a shared strategy does not by itself single out a unique action, since there are infinitely many symmetric strategies and nothing internal to superrationality ranks them. I suspect that what presents itself as a decision-theoretic prescription is really a selection principle imported from outside — perhaps payoff-dominance among symmetric equilibria, or a program-equilibrium style argument in which each player picks a rule that would be optimal if everyone ran the same rule — and that once the participants are even slightly asymmetric (different priors about how many others are playing, or about how likely the others are to reason superrationally), the prescription either becomes indeterminate or collapses into ordinary expected-utility reasoning.

Please turn this into a formal research proposal containing the following:

1. A precise statement of the question, narrow enough to have a determinate answer. I would like the central question to be something along the lines of: under exactly what conditions on the game — the number of players and the players' uncertainty about it, finite versus infinite strategy spaces, entry costs, and the degree of asymmetry among players' beliefs — does superrational reasoning yield a unique and well-defined prescription, and when it does not, what supplementary principle, if any, does the real work?

2. An account of why the existing answers leave this open: what the superrationality literature and its rivals (causal and evidential decision theory, team reasoning, program equilibrium, Kantian universalization) each already settle, and precisely where each falls silent.

3. The method — the actual formal objects I would construct and the moves I would make. For example: parameterizing the Platonia dilemma by the players' beliefs and locating the point at which the symmetric mixed-strategy prescription ceases to be unique; building an infinite-strategy variant of the Luring Lottery to test whether any principled selection rule survives the loss of a finite strategy set; and comparing what each rival framework predicts across a small set of constructed cases, including one asymmetric case.

4. What a result would look like and what would count as the project failing. I want to be told what a genuine theorem or taxonomy here would look like, and also the honest failure modes, including the possibility that "superrationality" turns out not to name a single thing.

5. A realistic month-by-month scope for a single semester's work by one person, with a note on which part is most likely to overrun.

Assume I will supply all citations myself, so do not include a bibliography and do not refer to specific papers by author and year; refer to positions and arguments instead. Write the proposal in American English, in a formal register suitable for a fellowship or independent-study application.
