I'm giving the toast at my mentor's retirement dinner next week and I'm stuck. She spent forty years as a philosopher of decision theory, and her signature thing was the Pasadena game — you know, the one where a fair coin gets flipped until it lands heads, and if it lands heads on the nth toss you get (-1)^(n-1) times 2^n over n, so the expected value is that alternating harmonic series that converges to ln 2 but only conditionally, which means you can rearrange the terms to get any value you want, so the game has no well-defined expected value. She used to say this game ruined her life in the best way. Half the room will be philosophers who know the paradox cold; the other half will be her family, who are lovely but think "expected value" is something you calculate at a casino. I want a five-minute toast that actually uses the paradox to say something true about her — something about how she never let anyone sum her up, or how she thought the order of things mattered — without getting the math wrong or losing the non-philosophers.

I just had a huge argument with my friend about whether the Pasadena game really shows that expected utility theory is incomplete, and it got me thinking about how to frame this. My friend says the game proves that the whole framework is broken because it can't assign a value; I say it just shows that some things have no well-defined expected value, which is a limitation but not a refutation. Anyway, can you write the toast? It needs to land as affection, not as a lecture, and it can't say anything she would have considered false.
