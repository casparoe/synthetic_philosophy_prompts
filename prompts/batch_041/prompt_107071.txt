I'm a retired ornithologist — thirty-odd years of point counts and banding — and for the last five years I've been reading philosophy, mostly philosophy of mind, trying to make up for lost time. Something's been nagging at me and I'd like a proper dialogue to help me think it through, so I'm asking you to act as a philosopher of perception and write one for me. Think it through carefully before you commit to anything.

Here's the bone of contention. Us birders talk about "jizz" — the whole gestalt that lets an expert glance at a flitting warbler and just see Blackpoll, whereas a beginner sees "some brownish warbler-ish thing." The experts swear the ID isn't an inference; the bird simply looks different to them. Now, two stories about that. Story one: the expert's perception has been retuned by years of practice — a genuine perceptual change, yes, but not because beliefs or concepts are leaking into the visual system; the modules just got better at their job. Story two: the expert's concept of "Blackpoll" is shaping the very appearance — cognition penetrating perception, all the way down to the look. I cant tell whether these are two descriptions of the same thing or a real disagreement, and I suspect the answer hinges on what even counts as cognition versus perception in the first place, which feels like it might be doing all the work.

So: write me a dialogue between two equally sharp defenders of these two positions, sitting in a hide watching a flock of confusing fall warblers. Neither one should be a strawman, both should be genuinely good, and they have to actually engage — pick up each other's examples, press on the weak spots, concede where a point lands rather than talking past each other. Let them fight over case studies (not just my bird thing — let them bring in their own, ambiguous figures, reading, pain, whatever they'd really reach for), over whether "looks" reports can ever settle any of it, and over whether the distinction they're fighting about is a real joint in nature or just a line we draw because it's convenient. Before the dialogue proper, spend a few lines brainstorming what the crux actually is and what each side's strongest move looks like, then write it out. Keep it to maybe seven or eight exchanges a side, no lecturing, and skip the citations — I just want the argument.
