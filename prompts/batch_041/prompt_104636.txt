So I'm in this little philosophy-of-math reading group and we keep going in circles: everyone grants that any story about mathematical truth has to do two things at once -- give mathematical sentences the same kind of meaning as ordinary empirical sentences, and still let us know about the objects those sentences are apparently about -- and everyone also seems to grant that nothing actually does both. Before we read another -ism I want the spec written down properly. So: give me the desiderata that any adequate account of mathematical truth (and of mathematical reference, since those come apart) has to satisfy -- the case or phenomenon behind each one, not just the label -- and then tell me which are genuinely non-negotiable and which are just the prejudices of one camp wearing the costume of a requirement. I specifically want the ones that keep getting smuggled in without argument: that the account has to let math be necessary rather than contingent; that it has to explain why any of this applies to the physical world; that it has to leave room for a working mathematician to treat some open question as genuinely unsettled rather than as meaningless; that it has to not make mathematical knowledge a special case that breaks the account's own theory of how we know anything else; and that it has to say what's going on when two people use rival constructions of the same thing that give all the same arithmetic but disagree on a structural fact (like whether 2 is a member of 4) -- I think that last one quietly does a lot of work and nobody ever puts it on the list.

The part I actually care about is the tension. Flag any pair or triple on the list you think can't be had together, and say which one you'd sacrifice and why. I've been told the whole dilemma is just semantics pulling against epistemology, but that feels too neat to me and I suspect some of the other requirements are quietly incompatible too -- necessity versus the applicability story, or determinacy versus a deflationary account of what "true" is even doing in arithmetic, or the rival-constructions case versus any view that says the natural numbers are a particular object. And if there's one requirement on the list that by itself knocks out most of the popular positions, tell me which one it is, because then thats the thing we should actually be arguing about on Thursday. Don't waste the space scoring platonism/nominalism/structuralism one by one -- I can look up who says what; I want the criteria themselves.
