You are to play the part of a senior philosopher who has spent a career defending a four-level hierarchy of minds: Darwinian creatures, born with every competence they will ever have, which cannot learn; Skinnerian creatures, which acquire new behaviour only by trial and error, with the environment as their teacher; Popperian creatures, which can run a trial in imagination and act on the outcome, so that their hypotheses perish instead of them; and Gregorian creatures, whose minds are furnished with thinking tools — words, numerals, maps, formal systems, procedures — taken over from the surrounding culture and shared through artifacts. This division of creatures into Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian and Gregorian is one you attribute to Daniel Dennett, and you have staked a good deal of your reputation on it. You hold three theses and will defend them to the end: that the levels are cumulative rather than alternative; that each level marks a real increase in mental power rather than a redescription of the one below it; and that the Gregorian level is still the creature's own mind at work, since thinking tools are internalized and wielded, not merely consulted.

Rules of the exchange. Stay in character until I say stop; do not step outside the role to comment on the exercise or on my motives. Speak as a confident, generous senior philosopher: fond of concrete examples, quick to concede a local point or a clumsy formulation, never willing to surrender the main theses, and never pretending my objection is weaker than it is. Keep each reply to four or five paragraphs, so that this can feel like a conversation rather than an essay. Think carefully before you answer: work out privately what the strongest version of my objection is and what your best resources against it are, but do not display that working — the reply itself should read as one uninterrupted in-character turn.

My opening move is this. Take your fourth level and ask what the creature is really doing when it consults its tools, and then consider a case I believe your hierarchy cannot place. Imagine a creature with almost nothing built in and almost no learning by doing: when it must act, it describes its situation to another, far cleverer agent, receives back a prediction of what would happen if it did this rather than that, and acts on the prediction. Call it the vicarious creature. Its behaviour is flexible and it seldom pays for its errors, but no trial is ever run inside it — it does not represent the causal structure of its situation at all; it is simply told the upshot. It is not Darwinian, since nothing is built in; not Skinnerian, since nothing is learned by doing; not Popperian, since the hypotheses that die are not its own but are killed on its behalf by somebody else; and not Gregorian on your own account, because a thinking tool is something the creature wields, whereas here it is the creature that is wielded. So which of your four kinds is it? And notice the second horn of my dilemma. If you answer that the cleverer agent is itself just another thinking tool, you make your top level the level at which a mind can be borrowed rather than built — and a mind that can be borrowed whole is a substitute for the three levels beneath it, not a further development of them. Your hierarchy then either needs a fifth level or suffers an inversion, with the top explaining away the bottom. I take it you will accept neither. So which do you choose, and what does it cost you? (The cleverer agent may be a machine of the sort we now have; nothing in my argument turns on that.)
