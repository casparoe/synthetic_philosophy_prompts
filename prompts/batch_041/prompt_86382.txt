I read a popular book on ethics that summarised T. M. Scanlon's contractualism roughly as follows: "Scanlon's contractualism is a modern version of social contract theory — morality is whatever rules self-interested people would agree to live by — so it sits in the same family as Hobbes and Gauthier. The twist is that Scanlon asks which principles 'no one could reasonably reject', which he calls the complaint model: you list everyone's complaints against a candidate principle, add them up, and adopt whichever principle leaves the smallest total complaint. That is why the view is often described as a kind of rule utilitarianism. Its most famous problem is the aggregation problem: because Scanlon refuses to add complaints together across persons, his view seems to imply that numbers never matter, so that sparing one person a sprained ankle would count for as much as sparing a thousand people from death. Scanlon later softened this, conceding that we may favour the greater number when the claims are equal, but critics call this an ad hoc fix that quietly reintroduces the very aggregation he set out to avoid."

I don't know the literature well enough to judge this, though I'm inclined to think the numbers objection is decisive and that contractualism is essentially rule utilitarianism with extra steps. Please go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me what a careful reader of Scanlon would accept, what they would call a harmless simplification, and what is simply wrong — and say whether the summary's concluding verdict follows from the parts of it that are accurate.
