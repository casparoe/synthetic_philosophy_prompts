I'm applying for a small internal grant — six months, part-time — and need a short research proposal, call it 1200 words. Here's the half-formed idea, turn it into something with an actual question in it.

Everyone argues about brain death vs. circulatory death as if the two criteria are rival definitions of death and we just have to work out which one is right. I think thats the wrong shape. Both criteria quietly lean on irreversibility, and irreversibility isn't a fact about the patient — it's a fact about us: whether anyone is going to try to restart the heart, whether they're allowed to, whether the kit even exists. So the two criteria aren't competing descriptions of a natural event, they're competing stipulations about when we're permitted to stop and call it. If that's right, then when a patient died is partly a question about our practices, not about the patient.

What I want the proposal to nail down: once you strip out the decision-relative notion of irreversibility, is there any content left in the concept of death at all — can we state a criterion that doesn't smuggle in a fact about what we intend to do? And if we can't, what does that do to the dead-donor rule, which is supposed to protect patients precisely by keeping the declaration of death seperate from the decision to procure?

Please write the proposal: state the question so it has a determinate answer; say why the obvious positions (whole-brain, higher-brain, circulatory) don't already settle it; describe the method — I want this conceptual, not empirical, so what do I actually do month by month, and how do I stop it collapsing into a literature review?; say what a result looks like and what would count as the project failing; and be honest about whether six months part-time is enough or whether I should shrink the question.
