A friend dropped this on me and I can't shake it. It's meant to be the honest, no-weasel version of the "he was a man of his time" defence, run over Jefferson, Churchill and Columbus, and I think it proves far too much — but I can't find the joint.

(1) You're only blameworthy for an act if you could reasonably have known, at the time, that it was wrong.
(2) What a person can reasonably know is bounded by their time and place — if slavery, or empire, or taking land and people from whoever was standing there was simply in the air, taken for granted by nearly everyone they'd ever met, then they couldn't reasonably have known better.
(3) So Jefferson's slaveholding, Churchill's views and policies on India and the empire, Columbus's treatment of the people he ran into: not things we can blame them for.
(4) Nothing in (1)–(3) is special to famous men; the same reasoning covers every ordinary person who went along with their era's cruelties.
(5) So nobody is ever blameworthy for going along with the moral consensus of their time — including us, for whatever our consensus is.
(6) A theory of blame that can't blame anyone, ever, for the worst things people do is not a theory of blame.

I'd much rather keep (1) and (2) than (6); I think "he couldn't have known" is a real excuse and I don't want to hold people to standards they had no access to. So: which step do I drop, why did it look fine sitting there, and what does dropping it cost me? And tell me what's left of the man-of-his-time defence afterwards — my suspicion is that in practice it only ever gets pulled out for figures we've already decided to admire, and if that selective use is the real move, say so and tell me the price.
