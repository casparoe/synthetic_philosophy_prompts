I am not a philosopher by training — I have been reading Rorty on my own and thinking about his neopragmatism and his antirepresentationalism — and there is a real episode I cannot get to line up with the view, so I would like you to help me think it through. Please act as someone who knows American pragmatism and the history of the science wars well, and who is willing to tell me where the theory runs out rather than defending it. Think carefully, and brainstorm first: before you commit to an interpretation, list the plausible ways the episode could be read through the theory, note which of them you are going to discard and why, and only then write the analysis.

The episode, briefly, since you cannot look it up. In the mid-1990s a physicist wrote a paper that was deliberately nonsensical — it mixed fragments of quantum-gravity vocabulary with political and cultural commentary in a way that was meant to be obviously absurd — and submitted it to a humanities journal that published it. He then announced publicly that the whole thing had been a hoax, and the revelation set off a much larger fight, commonly called the science wars, between natural scientists and scholars in the humanities and social sciences. The moral almost everyone drew was that people in the humanities who pronounce on science are relativists who cannot tell genuine inquiry from nonsense, and that their talk about the social construction of scientific knowledge had finally been caught out.

What I want is an analysis that runs in both directions.

First, lay out in your own words what Rorty's antirepresentationalism actually claims, precisely enough that I can see what it is not claiming. I want the distinction between justification and truth as Rorty draws it, what he means by saying that the desire for a mirror of nature is optional rather than obligatory, why he treats vocabularies as tools rather than as more or less accurate pictures, and what he thinks is really going on when philosophers appeal to objectivity. Be careful here: much of the popular picture of Rorty is a caricature, and I would like the real position rather than the one his critics attribute to him.

Then map the theory onto the episode. What would antirepresentationalism say the hoax does and does not show? Does it leave Rorty any ground on which to object to the hoax, or to distinguish the journal's failure from any ordinary case of a journal being fooled? The obvious pressure point is this: if truth is not correspondence but something like what our peers will let us get away with saying, then the hoax looks like a demonstration that the peers let the wrong thing through, and it is not clear the view has anything left to say about why that is bad. I want you to take that objection seriously and give the strongest Rortian reply you can, and then say honestly how much of the objection survives the reply.

Then reverse the direction. Treat the episode as a test of the theory rather than an illustration of it. Is it a confirmation, an embarrassment, or a case the theory simply never anticipated? Does it expose a gap between the descriptive claim that representationalism is optional and any normative claim about how inquiry should be conducted? Does Rorty's insistence that philosophy is not a tribunal over the sciences make him unable to say anything about the episode at all, or does it just relocate what he can say? Where the episode and the theory do not really engage each other — where one of them is simply silent about what the other is about — say so, instead of forcing the theory to speak.

Throughout, keep the mapping disciplined: be explicit about who the relevant knowers are, what is being justified to whom, and what would count as getting it right or wrong. And separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who actually know Rorty and know the episode — there is real disagreement about what his view implies here, and I do not want a contested reading presented to me as the consensus. Where you are unsure, say so, and tell me which parts of your answer are reconstruction on your part rather than anything the view or the episode dictates.
