I am a novelist, and I am writing to you about a difficulty that would embarrass me in front of my own characters. The book I am finishing opens one of its later chapters with a passage from Bertrand Russell's "On Denoting" (1905), and I have now spent several weeks unable to say, in my own words, what the passage means. Here it is:

"By a 'denoting phrase' I mean a phrase such as any one of the following: a man, some man, any man, every man, all men, the present King of England, the present King of France, the center of mass of the solar system at the first instant of the twentieth century, the revolution of the earth round the sun, the revolution of the sun round the earth. Thus a phrase is denoting solely in virtue of its form. We may distinguish three cases: (1) A phrase may be denoting, and yet not denote anything; e.g., 'the present King of France'. (2) A phrase may denote one definite object; e.g., 'the present King of England' denotes a certain man. (3) A phrase may denote ambiguously; e.g. 'a man' denotes not many men, but an ambiguous man. The interpretation of such phrases is a matter of considerable difficulty; indeed, it is very hard to frame any theory not susceptible of formal refutation."

What I think I understand is the surface: Russell is sorting phrases that turn on "a", "some", "any", "every", "all" and "the", and he wants the interest of these phrases to lie in their grammar rather than in what they happen to pick out. What I cannot reconcile is the sentence that seems to hold the paragraph together — "Thus a phrase is denoting solely in virtue of its form" — with the first of the three cases that follows it. If denoting is a matter of form, then "the present King of France" should be as much a denoting phrase as "the present King of England"; but if denoting requires that something be denoted, then the first case contradicts the sentence that introduces it. I take it I am running two senses of the word together, but I cannot find the seam.

My second difficulty is more parochial. What is an "ambiguous man"? I want to know whether Russell is positing an object of a strange kind — a man who is neither this man nor that man but man in general — or whether the phrase is a placeholder for machinery he has not yet produced, and the word "denotes" is being asked to do something other than what I mean by it when I say a character's phrase denotes her mother. Since a novelist's working assumption is that a phrase is meaningful because there is something it is about, I suspect I go wrong at the very first sentence and then compound the error.

Could you read the paragraph closely for me: what "denoting" is being used to mean at this stage of the essay, why the three cases are ordered as they are, whether the third case commits Russell to an extra entity, and what work the word "form" is doing in the sentence I quoted? And if it helps to proceed negatively, I would like to know how this paragraph differs from the view that a phrase means something only if something answers to it — the view my characters keep expressing, and which I suspect Russell is out to destroy.
