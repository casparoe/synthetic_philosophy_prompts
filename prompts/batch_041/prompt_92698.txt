I am a novelist, and the book I have been writing for the past two years turns on a single causal claim: that it is a woman's unacknowledged grief — and not the wet road, and not the pattern of firings in her nervous system — that puts her car into the parapet on the coast road. A philosopher friend, someone who works on mind and brain and whose judgement I ordinarily trust, read the manuscript last month and told me, gently, that the premise is incoherent. When I pressed him, he repeated one sentence three times, as though repetition were an argument: physics is causally closed, so there is nothing left for her grief to do. He then added that if I insisted on the grief being a cause, I would have to accept that the crash had two independent sufficient causes, which he found absurd; that the most I was entitled to say was that the grief accompanies the cause; and, when I objected, that if I disliked that, I could always say the grief just is the brain state, at which point the question of its doing anything would take care of itself. He also mentioned, almost as a throwaway, that some of his colleagues will call the grief a cause if cause is understood as difference-making against a background contrast, but that he regarded this as a change of subject rather than an answer.

I do not know what to make of any of it, and I would rather not spend a year rewriting the book around a sentence I cannot parse. What I want from you is not a reading list — names and dates would only be guesses I have no way to check — but the distinctions themselves.

Take the sentence physics is causally closed, so there is nothing left for the mental to do, and lay out as a menu the precise propositions it could be asserting. Make the dimensions of the ambiguity explicit: what exactly is said to be closed, and closed against what; whether the claim concerns causes, or explanations, or counterfactual dependence, or merely the completeness of some future physics; whether a sufficient physical cause means deterministic sufficiency, or probability-raising, or difference-making relative to a contrast; whether the principle is an empirical finding, a methodological assumption, or a stipulation about what we are to count as physical; whether a cause may stand to a more specific cause as determinable to determinate, so that the two need not be rivals at all; and whether, on each reading, the mental is excluded because it is distinct from the physical or because it is identical with it.

For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something false or question-begging, and say which of them would actually license my friend's conclusion that her grief did nothing, as opposed to merely describing the setting in which the question arises. Finish by telling me which readings leave my novel's premise intact and which oblige me to rewrite it, and what the difference-making reading would have to add in order to count as a genuine answer rather than a change of subject.
