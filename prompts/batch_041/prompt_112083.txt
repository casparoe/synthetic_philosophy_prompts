I'm TAing an intro philosophy of medicine course and next week is my 50-minute section on whether disease is a natural kind or a value-laden category — the naturalism vs normativism thing. Twenty undergrads, mostly pre-med, they've done the reading but I want them to feel the problem rather than recite the two positions back at me. So dont explain the debate to me, plan how I should teach it: what has to be in place before the question even makes sense (I have a hunch it's the fact/value distinction, but not where in the sequence it goes), the one case that carries the whole session, the misconceptions they'll arrive with and the ones they'll form as we go, exactly where in the sequence to head each one off, one question at the end that shows whether it actually landed, and what I should refuse to get into at this level and say so out loud.

For the carrying case I want something invented rather than the DSM-homosexuality or baldness chestnuts — those let them off the hook because they already know the right answer and just apply it. My attempt: a rare variant in one valley population where people sleep about four hours a night, wake up rested, never feel sleep-deprived, and whose sleep architecture on a scan is genuinely, measurably unlike the rest of us. In the valley nobody calls it anything. Move the same people to a city and they turn up in clinics with insomnia, anxiety, performance problems, because the eight-hour world doesn't fit them. Is that a disease? Is it one in the valley? Does the scan matter, or only the clinic visit? Tell me whether this case can carry the session or where it breaks, and what to patch it with if it does. I'm guessing they'll think normativism just means a culture can call anything a disease and there's no truth of the matter, and that naturalism means statistically abnormal equals diseased — but tell me what I'm missing.
