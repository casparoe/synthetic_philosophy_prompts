I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of mind and I've somehow let myself be talked into writing something for the department's poetry evening — the loose theme is 'the mind' and most people are doing the usual consciousness-is-a-mystery stuff, so I want to do the one that only people in my area will get. My area is panpsychism, and specifically the combination problem, which is the bit I actually lose sleep over: grant that experience or proto-experience is fundamental and scattered about at the level of the fundamental physical things, and then why is there any such thing as my experience — one subject, one point of view — rather than just a great swarm of tiny experiences that never add up to anyone? The move where you deny micro-subjects at the base and keep only proto-experiential properties that supposedly constitute a subject once they're arranged the right way has always struck me as pushing the problem down a level, and the move where you start with one cosmic subject and ask why it appears to be many strikes me as cheating in the other direction, though honestly it's the one I find most tempting on bad days.

What I want is a sestina. Six end-words, and I'd like the end-words to be six simple experiential qualities — a colour, a warmth, a tone, a pressure, a taste, an ache, that sort of thing — so that the form's actual rule does the work: the same six words come back in permuted order, six times over, and no matter how they're permuted nothing new ever appears. They should be doing the recurring, not just being repeated. The point I'm after is that the same constituents, endlessly rearranged, never once add up to a seventh thing that is the subject of them — and then the envoi, which is supposed to gather all six words into three lines, is where the poem tries to become one voice, and I want that attempt to be visible as an attempt, and moving whether or not it comes off. If the last three lines do finally sound like a single speaker, the reader should feel the cheat in it; if they don't, the failure should be the point and not a shrug. Don't name the problem, don't use the word panpsychism, and don't let it turn into a poem about writing poems. Someone in the audience who knows the literature should be able to think 'ah, thats the subject-summing problem' and also work out which side the poem has taken. Which is the last thing: please commit. Don't hedge across the options — decide which position the poem is actually written from and let that settle the ending, then give me a short paragraph afterwards saying which you chose and why, and where the poem cheats (I expect it has to somewhere; verse can make combination feel like it happens through cadence alone, which is exactly the kind of trick that ought to make us suspicious). And British spelling throughout, please — I'm reading it aloud and I don't want to be the one who wrote 'color'.
