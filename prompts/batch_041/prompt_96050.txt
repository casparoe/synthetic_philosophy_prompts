In cities across the snowy parts of the United States, a driver who has shoveled out a parked car and the surrounding curb space often leaves a kitchen chair, a traffic cone, or a trash can standing in the cleared spot to hold it. In Chicago the practice is called dibs; in Boston, space saving. The space is public property, normally allocated first-come, first-served, and the objects left in it are, strictly speaking, abandoned furniture in a roadway. The practice nevertheless holds: neighbors leave the chair alone, and a driver who moves it and parks is liable to be treated as having done something wrong. Its boundaries are contested, and the law sometimes supplies them and sometimes refuses to: Boston permits a space saver for forty-eight hours after a snow emergency ends and bans the practice outright in some neighborhoods; Washington, D.C., tickets those who leave markers in the street; Pittsburgh police generally decline to enforce, and the practice persists there; and in most places the whole business lapses once the snow melts. Residents disagree among themselves about how long a claim lasts, whether a driver who merely moved snow rather than shoveling it has any claim at all, and whether a claim outlives the snow.

I want to know what account of convention, and of norm-governed entitlement, this practice presupposes. Begin by reconstructing the theory implicit in what the participants actually do — the shovelers, the neighbors who comply, those who retaliate, the police, the city council — rather than the theory they would state if asked.

Then work through the following. First, does the practice require only what David Lewis's analysis of convention supplies: an arbitrary but stable regularity, arising in a recurring situation of coordination, which nearly everyone follows, which nearly everyone expects nearly everyone else to follow, which each person has reason to follow given that the others do, and which is common knowledge in that sense? Second, if the practice needs more, say exactly what: a moralized expectation of compliance, a willingness to punish defectors, or a conviction that the claim is deserved rather than merely customary. Notice that the practice is defended in the language of labor — the space belongs to the shoveler because the shoveler dug it out — and that this justification is not arbitrary, which sits awkwardly with any account that treats the assignment of sides, spots, or roles as arbitrary. Notice too that Lewis's account gives no obvious place to the fear of retribution that plainly helps hold the practice in place.

Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely fits well with, and identify at least one rival account of conventions, social norms, or entitlements that would license the same behavior. Say where the practice is underdetermined, so that the same actions are compatible with a purely coordinative reading, a sanctions-based reading, and a reading on which the claim is a property-like entitlement grounded in labor.

Finally, say what would have to change — in the participants' behavior, in their justifications, or in the law — if the implicit theory were replaced by a strictly coordinative reading, on which the markers mean nothing once the coordination problem is over, or by the reading on which the space remains public and only the city may allocate it.
