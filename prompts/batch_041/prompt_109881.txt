I retired three years ago after thirty-odd years in a microbiology department, and I've spent a good chunk of the retirement reading philosophy — mostly the stuff about whether you can explain meaning in purely biological terms, since that's the one part of it I might actually have a feel for. I keep running my intuitions past cases from my own field and they don't add up, so I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, they come to. No reading list please, just the principle and where it breaks.

Here's what I think, case by case.

Magnetotactic bacteria. They build little chains of magnetite crystals that line the cell up with the earth's field, which sends it swimming down into the sediment, which is where it wants to be. Those crystals don't represent anything. It's a compass needle, and a compass needle doesn't have an opinion about north.

E. coli. The chemotaxis system is a beautiful thing — the receptors get methylated and demethylated over a few seconds, so the cell is effectively comparing the attractant concentration now with the concentration a little while ago, and that memory decides whether it keeps swimming straight or tumbles and tries a new direction. No content there either. It's a running average, not a claim about anything.

A plant. Shade avoidance: the phytochrome system reads the ratio of red to far-red light, and the seedling stretches toward what it "thinks" is a gap in the canopy. No content. Nobody believes a seedling is representing a gap.

A smoke detector. No content, obviously. Being reliable about smoke doesn't buy you any.

A frog. Its retina has cells that fire when something small, dark and moving crosses the field, and the frog snaps. When a frog snaps at a BB, or at a swinging pendulum in the lab, I want to say it's misrepresenting — representing something as a fly when it isn't a fly. So there's real content there, and it can be wrong.

My dog. She barks when she hears the mail truck two streets over. Her bark means the mail truck is coming, and she's right or wrong about it.

A tumor detector. A student in my old department trained a network to look at photos of agar plates and flag the contaminated ones. It detects contamination. I don't think it represents anything. It's a smoke detector with a better algorithm.

Now the two that break me. Take the frog and the plant. Both are mechanisms shaped by selection to fire (or stretch) when some external condition holds, and both can be fooled — put a seedling by a red-painted wall and it stretches at nothing, and it isn't wrong about anything, it's just doing its thing. If that's the story for the plant, why not for the frog? The only difference I can point to is how many steps sit between the detection and the behavior, and I don't see why the step count should decide whether there's meaning. And take the frog and the tumor detector. The detector was tuned, by us, to fire when contamination is there, and its output gets read by a tech as being about the plate. That looks like the frog's setup exactly. If I say the detector has no content, I feel pushed to say the frog's cells have none either, and that feels wrong.

My best attempt at a principle: a state has content when it's produced by a mechanism whose job is to produce it under conditions that make it correct, and it's then consumed downstream by something that uses it as a stand-in for that condition — something that can be let down when the state is wrong. That gets the bacterium (no consumer), the plant (no consumer), the dog (I'm the consumer and I can be let down) and the frog (the snapping machinery consumes the cell's output). But it hands content to the tumor detector too, since the tech consumes its output, and I don't want that. And it makes me nervous about the frog, because I can describe the plant the same way — the growth machinery consumes the phytochrome signal.

So: is there an account that gets all of these, or do I have to give one up, and which one? I'd rather you tell me which verdict to drop than hand me a complicated principle that saves every verdict by brute force.

Three I haven't made my mind up on, which might be a good test of whatever you come up with:

The edge-detecting cells in my own retina. I want to say they don't represent edges, they're just filters. But the frog's bug detector is also a filter, so I'm not sure I'm allowed to say that.

A frog with no ancestors. Suppose a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a frog just assembles by accident in a pond, with no history behind it at all. I want to say its detector still means fly. But nothing ever gave it a job, so by my own principle it shouldn't.

Somewhere in Hawaii most of the male crickets of one species have gone silent — they lost the wing structures they sing with — because a parasitic fly hunts them by their song. The flies are still built to listen for a song the males mostly don't make anymore. I don't think the flies are misrepresenting anything. I'd like to know what your principle says about that.

One last thing — please answer in American English. I'm American, and the British spellings in half the books I've been reading keep tripping me up.
