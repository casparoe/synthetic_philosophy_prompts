Augustine's two treatises on the subject — On Lying, and the later Against Lying — leave me unsure what he would say about a case he never considered, and I would like you to work it out.

As I read him: a man who asserts a falsehood while believing it true does not lie, however reckless he is; but a man who asserts what is in fact true while believing it false, and utters it as true, does lie. So the verdict tracks the speaker's mind rather than the facts. Augustine also locates the liar's fault in what he calls "the desire of deceiving," and he sets jokes aside as not lies, since the joker gives plain indication that he means no deceit. But he also says the liar's heart is double — and that image by itself would make any utterance at odds with the speaker's mind a lie, jokes included.

Here is the new case. On pain of losing his post, a man must read aloud and sign a statement affirming, as true, a claim he believes to be false. The official who records it knows the claim is false and knows the man does not believe it; the ceremony exists to secure a public submission, not a belief; no one is meant to be persuaded, and no one is; the man intends only to keep his job.

Would Augustine call this a lie? Tell me which of his verdicts commit him, set out the candidate rules that fit those verdicts equally well — the will-to-deceive test and the double-heart test among them — and say what each predicts about this case, since I suspect they come apart. Then give me your best guess with your reasons, and name the further case that would show which rule he is really running.
