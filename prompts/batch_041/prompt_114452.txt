I recently finished naturalising in the country I have lived in for eleven years. I took the oath in a municipal hall with about forty other people, and my mother, who has never left the country I was born in, has been telling relatives that I have changed sides, so the question of what compatriots owe one another has stopped being a seminar question for me and become a family argument. In the reading group I go to, the point that has been bothering me was put as an analogy. Pressed on why citizens may, or must, put one another first, a member of the group said: you do not feed the neighbour's child before your own. You have a duty of care to your own household that you simply do not have to other households, and a nation is a household writ large, a bounded group with a shared history and mutual attachment, entitled to look after its own before it looks after strangers. Two people in the room thought this settled the matter; two thought it assumed what it was meant to prove; nobody could say precisely why.

What I want is an audit of the analogy rather than a rehearsal of the liberal-nationalist and cosmopolitan positions, which I already know. Begin with the correspondence: say what plays the role of the parent, the child, the household's resources, adoption or marriage into the family, and the family's relations with the households around it, and say which of those correspondences is doing the justificatory work rather than merely decorating the picture. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and which breakages actually threaten the conclusion my friend wanted to draw, namely that compatriots have a claim on one another that strangers lack, and that this claim can extend to favouring citizens for scarce goods and to restricting who may enter. I do not want the merely decorative disanalogies.

Four pressures interest me in particular. First, whether a family's partiality rests on a relationship of a kind a large anonymous state can plausibly claim to reproduce or only to simulate, and whether the analogy, if it works at all, supports duties to co-nationals (cultural kin) or to co-citizens (fellow participants in a coercive scheme of law and taxation), since these come apart and the analogy may point at the wrong one. Second, whether it establishes a permission to favour one's own or a duty to do so, since families are usually thought to license the first while the argument needs the second. Third, what it does with the fact that the family is itself an institution of contested internal justice, the household having been the classic site of unearned hierarchy as well as of care, and with the fact that states coerce and tax the very people they are said to be caring for, which is not something a family does to its adult members. Fourth, whether it has anything to say to the boundary problem: a family's membership is at least partly given by biology and history, whereas a state's is drawn and redrawn by policy, so why these compatriots and not the equally needy people just outside the line seems to fall outside the analogy's reach. Close by saying whether the conclusion survives in some weaker form, for instance whether compatriot partiality is better defended as an assigned institutional responsibility than as the value of a relationship, and whether some other analogy (hospitality, insurance, a lifeboat, a club, a neighbourhood) would carry the argument further.
