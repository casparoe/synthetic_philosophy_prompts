I am setting the final examination paper for a final-year undergraduate course and a taught master's option in philosophical logic and epistemology, and I need one essay question on the problem of logical omniscience, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The paper is closed-book, the candidate has forty-five minutes for this question, and it carries twenty marks. No citations are expected and no particular author's formulation may be presupposed, so the question has to be answerable by a candidate who has understood the issue but remembers no names.

The question itself should not be an invitation to recite the familiar shape of the problem — that in possible-worlds epistemic logic an agent knows every logical truth and her knowledge is closed under entailment — followed by a catalog of repairs. I want candidates to have to adjudicate between the repairs and to see what each costs. Please build the question around the following fork, and require the candidate to choose a horn and defend it.

(a) Keep the content of the attitude truth-apt but finer-grained than a set of possible worlds, so that logically equivalent contents can come apart. The price is a semantics in which the agent can be represented as believing a contradiction, and in which logical consequence no longer constrains what she believes.

(b) Keep the content coarse-grained and locate the limitation in the agent's state — a set of representations together with some awareness, attention, or computational component — so that closure holds only relative to what she is aware of. The price is that a failure to see a consequence becomes a fact about her representational resources rather than a fault in her, and an epistemic question has been traded for a question in the theory of computation.

Beyond choosing, the question should require three things of the candidate: to distinguish the two properties that are usually run together, namely knowing all logical truths and having one's knowledge closed under entailment, and to show that a repair may remove one without touching the other; to say what an adequate account must preserve, in particular the truth-aptness of content and the normativity of logic, that is, the fact that failing to draw a consequence one has in some sense already accepted is a defect in the agent; and to say why the problem is at bottom a demand for an account of inference as something an agent does, which neither horn by itself supplies. A strong answer will also notice the hazard facing the second horn, that an awareness clause bolted onto an unreduced closure principle collapses back into omniscience.

For the model answer I would like about seven hundred words, written as a candidate's essay rather than as notes. The marking scheme should consist of five must-hit points, each with the marks it is worth (the five to total twenty), a one-line statement of what an answer must do to earn them, and a one-line note on how candidates characteristically get that point wrong — for instance by treating omniscience and closure as a single property, or by offering an impossible-worlds semantics without noticing that it makes belief non-truth-apt. Close with a single sentence naming the move that separates a first-class answer from a competent second-class one.

Everything the candidate is to read, and therefore the question, the model answer, and the marking scheme, should be in British English: British spelling and idiom throughout.
