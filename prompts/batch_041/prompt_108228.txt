I'm putting together a seminar for next term on group-based preference in selection -- admissions, hiring, board seats, the odd legislative seat -- and I want to stop running it as a tour of cases. What I want to hand them in week one is the spec instead: what does any theory of when it's permissible to give a preference to someone because of their group membership actually have to deliver? Not a defense of one family of views, the requirements list.

So write me that. Give me the desiderata, and for each one the case or consideration that puts it on the list, so none of them look like something I made up over coffee. And I want the criteria to be doing the work here, not a scorecard -- you can wave at the main families at the end (compensation for past wrongs, diversity, role-model arguments, equality of opportunity, anti-subordination, plain old distributive fairness) but I don't want a tournament, I want the standard.

Three cases have to come out right, and they're not the same shape, which is the point: race-conscious admissions at US universities; the Indian reservation system, with its separate buckets for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Other Backward Classes, and the long fight over the creamy layer and over extending it to promotions; and Norway's 40% boardroom rule for public companies (recently pushed out to the private ones too). If a requirement only earns its keep on one of those and quietly falls over on the others, say so.

Some things I want you to sort out, and I'll be annoyed if you just list them as separate desiderata without noticing they pull against each other. The distinction between a policy that changes who ends up in the comparison pool and one that changes how the comparison is made once you're in it -- I have a hunch half the usual desiderata are really about the second and people smuggle in the first without saying so, so tell me if that's right. The levelling-down worry: whatever the theory demands, it had better not be satisfiable by making everyone worse off. The quota-versus-target thing, and whether "flexible goal" is a real third category or a fig leaf. Whether the good being distributed has to be to the group or to individuals, given that the cost, when there is one, lands on individuals. The innocent third party who loses the seat and did nothing wrong, and the equally innocent beneficiary who did nothing to earn the preference. Sunset: is a theory that can never tell you when the preference has done its job actually a theory of anything, or is "temporary" part of the concept. And a requirement that the theory not contradict the boring empirical stuff we actually know about how selection works -- test scores, mismatch worries, what the applicant pool even looks like before anyone gets compared to anyone.

Then, and this is the part I most want: flag any pair or triple of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied, and say which one you'd give up and why. And tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidate theories -- I'll bet it isn't the one my students expect.

Don't give me citations or a reading list, I'll fill that in myself. And keep it in prose, a table is fine if it's genuinely clearer.
