I help draft sentencing policy for a small jurisdiction, and in our working group the phrase 'the punishment must fit the crime' functions less as a claim anyone defends than as an axiom that ends debate. One member invokes it to strike down a schedule of fines that a consultant derived by asking what level of penalty would actually deter each offense, on the ground that a fine fixed by its deterrent effect cannot, by that very fact, be fitting. Another invokes the same phrase to argue that no sanction may ever exceed the harm the offender caused, since anything more would be unfitting. A third reads it as licensing purely ordinal judgments: that we need only be able to rank sanctions as more or less severe in step with the gravity of offenses, never to say how much a given crime deserves. The three positions are mutually inconsistent, yet all are asserted in the slogan's name, and after a year the group has not moved.

I want the phrase broken open rather than defended or debunked. Give me a menu of the distinct precise principles 'the punishment must fit the crime' could be expressing, ordered roughly from the platitudinous to the contentious, and say of each whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false. The menu should at least distinguish: fitting in kind from fitting in degree; a cardinal proportionality requirement (some determinate magnitude of deserved severity) from a merely ordinal one (a monotone ranking of sanctions against offenses); fit to the harm done from fit to the offender's culpability or desert; fit as a side-constraint that consequentialist considerations may not override from fit as one target to be traded off against others; and fit as a matter of the sanction's social meaning or expressive content matching the wrong's meaning, rather than of its severity at all. For each reading, say which of the three members it would actually support, whether any single reading supports all three, and what each reading presupposes that it cannot itself supply — in particular whether the whole idea of fittingness quietly assumes a common scale on which crimes and sanctions can be measured, and what becomes of the slogan if no such scale exists. Conclude with what, if anything, would count as progress for the group: a distinction that dissolves one of the three positions, an empirical finding, or an admission that the slogan was never doing the work anyone thought it was.
