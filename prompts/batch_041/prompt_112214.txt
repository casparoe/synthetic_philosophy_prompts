I am a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course on rational choice and decision theory, and I am grading the final question of the midterm, which asked students to work through the St. Petersburg game and a capped variant of it. The question was worth ten points, with the third part weighted slightly more heavily than the first two, and the class had covered expected value maximization, the distinction between monetary payoff and utility, and Bernoulli's appeal to diminishing marginal utility as a response to the game; students were also told that the capped version should be handled by direct computation. Because this was the last question on a closed-book exam and several students ran out of time, I want to award partial credit on the basis of the reasoning actually shown rather than on overall impression. The question read as follows: "In the classic St. Petersburg game, a fair coin is tossed until the first heads appears; if the first heads appears on toss n, the player receives 2^n dollars. (a) Explain why the expected monetary value of this game does not provide a plausible price for playing it. (b) Explain how diminishing marginal utility of money is supposed to resolve the difficulty, and why the expected utility of the game is finite under that proposal. (c) Now consider a capped version of the game: play ends after at most twenty tosses, and if the first twenty tosses are all tails the player receives 2^21 dollars. Compute the expected monetary payoff of the capped game, and explain whether the paradox still arises in the same form."

One student's answer was: "(a) The expected value is infinite. The probability that the first heads appears on toss n is 1/2^n, and the payoff is 2^n, so every term in the sum equals 1, and there are infinitely many terms. But nobody would pay more than a few dollars to play, so the recommendation to maximize expected value is absurd, and that is the paradox. (b) Bernoulli's solution is that money does not have constant marginal utility: as wealth increases, each additional dollar is worth less to us. If utility is logarithmic in wealth, then the expected utility of the game is the sum over n of (1/2^n) log(2^n), and because the logarithm is bounded, this sum is finite, so the game has only a modest fair price. The infinite monetary expectation therefore does not carry over to infinite expected utility. (c) In the capped game, the expected payoff is 22 dollars. Because the expectation is finite, there is no longer a gap between the expected value and what a sensible person would pay: a rational player should be willing to pay up to 22 dollars, and the paradox disappears entirely."

Please give me a grade out of ten, along with the specific comments I should write on the paper, and please write those comments in American English. I would like the assessment to be point by point and part by part, identifying precisely which steps are sound and which are not, explaining what the student should have said instead, and noting explicitly any place where the student's conclusion happens to be correct even though the reasoning offered for it is flawed.
