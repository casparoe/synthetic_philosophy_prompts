Ok so this is a weird one but I need help settling something. At a dinner last week two friends of mine — Maya, who did a masters in behavioral econ, and Ravi, who's a philosophy PhD student — got into a long argument about a third friend, Dan. Dan has been saying for like two years that he's going to get up at six and work on his thesis, and he basicly never does; he stays up watching stuff and then hates himself about it the next morning. Ravi called it a textbook case of weakness of will. Maya said there's no such thing. They went back and forth for like an hour and I definately couldn't tell who was winning. Here's the gist, as best I can reconstruct it:

1. Maya: What Dan has isn't weakness of will, it's time-inconsistent preferences. Over the course of the evening his preferences actually flip — at 5pm he ranks working tomorrow above scrolling tonight, and by 10pm he ranks scrolling tonight above working tomorrow. At the moment he actually chooses, there's no conflict between his judgment and his action, because his judgment has just re-ranked. Calling it "weakness" assumes a stable ranking that was never there. On the standard discounting model this is just a predictable preference reversal — so strictly speaking akrasia doesn't exist, it's a mislabel for hyperbolic discounting.

2. Ravi: That's a bait and switch. Discounting is a story about what people choose; akrasia is about the relation between choosing and judging, and you can't make the second disappear by redescribing the first. Also Dan *reports* the conflict — he says things like "I know I should work and I'm going to watch this anyway." If you say that report is just another preference talking, then "judgment" is doing no work at all, and you can't explain why he feels guilty or why he literally asks us to hold onto his phone. And the precommitment point: the only reason deleting an app or handing a friend your password makes any sense is that there's a self that will predictably act against its own plan. That's akrasia, not a discount curve.

3. Maya: The guilt is explained too — it's just the earlier self's ranking getting voiced at the later moment. And precommitment doesn't need a will; it's a self that knows its own future discount function and pays a price to avoid the reversal. You're positing a special faculty to explain something the curve already explains.

4. Ravi: But then "should" has dropped out entirely. Dan doesn't merely prefer differently at different times — he thinks one option is *better* and still fails to do it. If all you've got is changing rankings, you've quietly changed the subject from a normative failure to a mechanical fact, and the one interesting question — how someone can knowingly do what they take to be worse — is the thing you assumed away.

Can you adjudicate this move by move? Rate each of the four from clearly mistaken to clearly right and say why, tell me where they're actually disagreeing versus just talking past each other (I suspect it's some of both), and finish with what both of them are missing. Don't cop out with "they're both partly right" — I want to know which specific claims hold up.
