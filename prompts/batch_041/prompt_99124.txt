I am assembling a three-year, roughly four-person conceptual research program around a single question: whether the simulation argument should genuinely lower our expectation for shaping the far future — that is, whether it is a real dampener on fanaticism about the far future, or whether any dampening effect dissolves once the argument is stated carefully. (By fanaticism about the far future I mean the pattern in which astronomically large expected values from far-off outcomes dominate practical reasoning, so that very small probabilities of enormous value swamp ordinary considerations.) Before I commit the money, I want a defended agenda rather than a survey: set out the open conceptual questions such a program would have to answer, and rank the four or five that matter most.

To keep this from collapsing into a standard recitation, treat the following three proposals — which are mine, and which I believe are not anyone's published position — as live candidates for the program's central question, and tell me whether any of them deserves to be:

(a) the 'substrate-neutral value' proposal: that the value of an outcome is invariant between simulation and base reality whenever the experiences involved are subjectively identical, so that the simulation argument can dampen fanaticism only via a separate premise about whether our actions causally reach base reality at all;

(b) the 'simulator-optionality' proposal: that if we are simulated, the simulators may themselves be moral patients or co-beneficiaries of our far-future projects, which could restore rather than reduce the expected value at stake;

(c) the 'self-indication reversal' proposal: that the anthropic step used to reach the simulation conclusion behaves differently under self-indication than under self-sampling (roughly: whether you treat yourself as a randomly selected observer from some reference class, or whether the fact that you exist as an observer is itself evidence favoring hypotheses under which more observers exist), so that the argument's dampening force is an artifact of an unstated anthropic assumption.

For each question you rank, tell me why it is upstream of the others and what depends on its answer; whether it is tractable for philosophers working with thought experiments and decision theory rather than empirical researchers; how neglected it is relative to its importance; and what development over the next two or three years would move it up or down my list. Where two of the candidate questions are really the same question, say so and merge them. Where you think the whole program rests on a mistake, say that too, and say what the mistake is.

I do not need reading lists, citations, or claims about who has said what; I need the reasoning and the ordering, with the trade-offs between importance, tractability, neglect, and upstreamness made explicit. End with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing only the label of the question you rank first, in five words or fewer.
