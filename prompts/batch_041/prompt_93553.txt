In my role overseeing a customer-support chatbot, I recently encountered a case that has made the Gricean distinction between what is said and what is implicated feel less like a seminar topic and more like a liability. A customer asked whether a damaged part was covered under warranty, and the bot replied, “That’s certainly one way to read the warranty.” The customer took this as an implicature that coverage was doubtful and cancelled the service; our engineers insist the model had no communicative intention, and our lawyers say the company never meant to imply anything. I have started calling this an orphan implicature: an implication carried by an exchange that no single person—prompt author, model, or user—intended to convey. I want a dialogue between two equally competent theorists about whether there is any speaker meaning or implicature here at all. One should defend the intentionalist, broadly Gricean view: implicature requires a communicative intention, so the question is who counts as the speaker in a machine-mediated exchange, and perhaps the company or the prompt author does. The other should defend a public-norms view: utterance meaning and implicature are fixed by context, convention, and reasonable uptake, not by private intentions, so the bot’s line can implicate even if no one intended it. Let each respond to the other’s actual arguments rather than to a caricature, and let each use the warranty example to press the other. Present the dialogue as exactly five numbered exchanges, each exchange consisting of one turn from each speaker and each turn no more than two sentences, with no introduction, conclusion, or stage directions.
