I have recently inherited the opening lecture of our first-year survey in economic history, and I have been asked to frame the whole thing around a single question: why Britain and not China? The reading I have been doing suggests that the older story, in which Europe pulled ahead through some unique genius of its own, has been abandoned, and that China was on most measures as commercially and technologically sophisticated as Europe well into the eighteenth century, with the divergence arriving late and quickly. If that is right, then the lecture comes down to identifying what Britain had that China lacked — coal, colonies, secure property rights, expensive labour that rewarded mechanisation, a culture of mechanical tinkering — and choosing which of these to present as decisive, while conceding that the others contributed. Is that the most defensible structure for the lecture? And when a student asks why the divergence did not begin in the Yangzi delta or in the Netherlands instead, what is the strongest answer I can give without overclaiming?
