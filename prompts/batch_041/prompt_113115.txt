I am the activities coordinator at a small residential home for people with advanced dementia, and I have been given a task I increasingly suspect cannot be done. The home's discretionary budget is small, and my manager wants a single ranked list of everything we offer — the choir, the hand massages, the garden walks, visits from family, an hour of music the residents already know — ordered by how much pleasure each activity produces, so that the money goes to whatever sits at the top. When I try to build the list, the quantity I am supposedly measuring keeps changing character from one row to the next. What makes the hand massage worth having seems to be a bodily sensation; what makes the family visit worth having seems to be being recognized by someone who recognizes you; what makes the choir worth having seems to be doing something together with other people, quite apart from how it feels at any given moment. My colleague, who is helping me, thinks I am inventing a difficulty. Pleasure is pleasure, she says: you can see on a resident's face when she is enjoying herself, so we should rank the activities by how long each one holds her attention and how often she smiles, and stop overthinking. My manager takes the opposite line: if the figures are not measuring one and the same thing, then the list is not a ranking of pleasure at all, and I may as well be recording my own preferences. Many of our residents cannot say in words how they feel, so I cannot simply ask them, and I am not persuaded that my colleague's behavioral measures settle the matter. My instinct is that she is right about what she can see in their faces and he is right that the figures do not add up to anything, which is roughly why I am stuck.

What I would like is to understand what is actually going on here, rather than to be given management tips about how to run the exercise. Is the obstacle real, or an artifact of how the task has been set up? What exactly is at stake between my colleague and my manager? And if pleasure turns out to name experiences this unlike one another, what becomes of the thought that the best use of our money is the one that produces the most of it? Please tell me what a careful treatment of the problem looks like, and what I should do — including whether there is a defensible way to build the list, or whether I should go back to my manager and say that the task, as specified, cannot be completed.
