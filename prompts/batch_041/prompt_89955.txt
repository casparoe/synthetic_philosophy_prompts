I am drafting a joint comment on fairness in clinical risk prediction with a colleague, and he has handed me an argument I can neither refute nor accept. The setting is concrete, so let me fix it. Our hospital uses a risk score to rank patients for a scarce intensive care-management program with a fixed number of funded slots each year; the score estimates the probability of an avoidable hospitalization in the next twelve months, and patients above the funding line are enrolled. His argument runs as follows.

(1) Among the patients a tool enrols, the proportion who actually go on to have the outcome should be the same in every group the tool is applied to.
(2) Among the patients who do have the outcome, the proportion the tool enrols should be the same in every group; and among those who do not, the proportion it enrols should likewise be the same.
(3) A tool that fails either condition hands one group a different grade of accuracy than another, and that is precisely what it is for an instrument to discriminate. A just tool must therefore satisfy both.
(4) When the two groups differ in how many of their members would have the outcome anyway — in their base rates — no decision rule can satisfy both. The arithmetic is elementary and turns on nothing about the model, the data, or where the threshold is set; the only exceptions are a perfectly accurate rule, which we will never have, and equal base rates, which we do not have. One of the two conditions must be surrendered, and which one is surrendered is a choice we make rather than one the mathematics makes for us.
(5) So for any pair of groups with different base rates, no risk score can be just.
(6) Different base rates are the rule rather than the exception, and the same argument runs on any partition of the population one cares to draw, since any feature correlated with the outcome will yield groups whose base rates differ. No level of aggregation makes the difficulty go away.
(7) Therefore no risk score is just, deploying one is an injustice, and because nothing in the argument turned on the outcome, the intervention, or the institution, prediction-based allocation is inherently unjust.

He draws the conclusion that we should scrap the instrument and return to unaided clinical judgment. I think that is too much, but each step looks to me like a restatement of the one before it, and I cannot find the joint. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I would most like to keep (1) and (4), and I would rather not give up (2) either; I have no stake in (7).
