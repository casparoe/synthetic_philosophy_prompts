My freind and I have been argueing about ant colonies for about three weeks now and I want you to write an essay that actually settles it. Settles it means you pick a side and defend it. Don't give me a tour of the positions and then shrug at the end, and don't tell me both sides have merit.

Here's what we're fighting about. A colony of a few hundred thousand ants does things that look for all the world like thinking. It holds the nest at a steady temperature. It sends more foragers to the better food source and fewer to the worse one, without any ant having any idea how many other ants are where. It builds bridges and living rafts out of its own bodies. It appears to decide when the whole operation should pack up and move. And none of this lives in any individual ant -- an ant alone is a twitchy little automaton with about five rules and a bad attitude.

My friend's line: the colony isn't thinking, it's just a lot of ants following pheromone rules, and all the mental vocabulary -- deciding, wanting, remembering -- is a convenience we project because it's easier to say the colony decided than to write out every trajectory. Fine. But then he has to explain why he won't say the same about me. I'm a hundred billion neurons and not one of them has any clue what this sentence means. If "it's just parts following rules" kills the colony's mind, it kills mine, and then we're not arguing about ants, we're arguing about whether anything has a mind. He says brains are different because they're integrated, whatever that means. I say he's smuggling in the answer.

So I want to propose a test and have you actually evaluate it instead of just describing it. Take two descriptions of the colony: one that tracks every ant, one that tracks the colony as a single thing with states like hungry and committed to this trail. If the coarse description lets you predict and compress what happens better than the fine one -- if there's information at the colony level that isn't sitting in any ant -- then the colony-level pattern is real rather than a projection, and mental talk about it is tracking something. If it doesn't, my friend wins and it's just bookkeeping.

My worry is that the test proves way too much. The weather compresses beautifully and I don't think it's thinking. Same for the economy, the immune system, a slime mold, the traffic on the interstate. So either the test is too weak and I need a second condition, or I have to bite the bullet and say mind is everywhere there's a real higher-level pattern, which sounds insane but might be the honest option.

There's a second thing I can't get straight, and I want you to take a position on this too. There seem to be two different things we could mean by having a mind. One is being the sort of system you have to describe in mental terms because nothing else works. The other is there being something it's like to be it -- someone home. The colony might pass the first and fail the second, or pass both, or the two might come apart in a way that makes the whole question confused. I don't want a both-are-valid answer.

And here's the part that keeps me up. The ants aren't like my neurons. A neuron isn't running around with its own little life. An ant is a whole animal with its own interests, and the colony's interests can conflict with its own -- the colony sends workers off to die by the thousands and they go. So a colony mind, if there is one, would be built out of subjects rather than out of parts. Is that a reason to think there can't be one? Or is that just what a mind always is, only here at a scale where we can see the seams? Compare a split-brain patient, compare a corporation, and tell me which comparison is doing the real work.
