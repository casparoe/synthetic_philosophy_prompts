I run impact and performance at a mid-sized independent foundation — around sixty grants, roughly forty million pounds, three-year funding cycles — and I am the person who turns our trustees' ambitions into the indicator set against which we renew or wind down funding. Twice a year a batch of proposed indicators lands on my desk, some from trustees, some from programme staff, some from grantees trying to be helpful, and each one carries a consequence: money, renewal, a public ranking, a line in the annual report. I have a team of four, no experimental capacity, no causal model of the sectors we fund, and a board that wants numbers it can quote in public. I have read enough about Goodhart's law to be wary of it, and the original formulation — 'any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes' — describes my working life exactly. But every version of the law I have met stops at the warning, and I want the operating manual: a procedure I can run in about an hour, on paper, with one colleague, before I recommend that we adopt, modify or refuse an indicator.

Please build it out of the four mechanisms that are usually distinguished in discussions of the law, because I am fairly sure they demand different responses and that I have been prescribing the wrong remedy for years. They are: regressional (selecting for an imperfect proxy also selects for the noise in it, so the top of the distribution is systematically worse than it looks); extremal (the relationship was observed in an ordinary range, and a target pushes the system somewhere the relationship no longer holds); causal (acting on the metric changes the underlying situation, so our own intervention destroys the correlation we relied on — the point the Lucas critique makes about econometric relationships used for policy); and adversarial (agents who do not share our goal move the number rather than the thing). For each, give me the symptom my team can actually see in our own data or in a grantee's report, the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer, and the remedy that follows, since I suspect the standard fixes are not interchangeable. Include the step I keep skipping: asking what consequence attaches to this number and to whom, because an indicator that is merely discussed is not a target, and a target is a selection pressure on whoever must report it.

Then run the procedure on three proposals. First, my own favourite and the one I would most like you to approve: a single composite 'impact score' covering the whole portfolio, so that the trustees have one number for the annual report. Second, a grantee-proposed indicator on the number of safeguarding concerns raised by frontline staff, on the theory that more reporting means a healthier culture. Third, an efficiency indicator — cost per person reached — which we would use to compare grantees. I expect the procedure to give me an answer I will not like on at least one of these, and I would rather have that now than in a trustees' meeting. Finally, tell me plainly where the procedure must stop and hand the matter to the board, which of its steps are judgment dressed up as technique, what it cannot do, and what I should do when a target is imposed on us from outside and refusing is not an option.
