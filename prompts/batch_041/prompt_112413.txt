I chair the research-ethics review at a mid-sized teaching hospital and most of what comes across my desk is consent paperwork — a couple dozen protocols a month, plus amendments — and it's where I keep stalling, because my instincts are frankly inconsistent and I know it. The hard ones are always the same three shapes: studies that don't tell participants what they're really testing (deception, or "withholding" that we all agree to call something softer), offers generous enough that they're obviously why someone said yes, and enrollment of people whose understanding is shaky — early dementia, acute pain, low literacy, psych crisis. Can you write me a decision procedure I can actually run on a protocol, in order, that says what has to be true for consent to be valid, which of those three shapes breaks which requirement, and where it stops and hands the call to the full board or to a person rather than to me? Base it on what the philosophy of consent actually says rather than on the regulation, since the regulation is a floor and I keep needing to know what's above it — but it has to be usable by reviewers who've never read that literature, and I'd rather you flag the steps that are really value judgments than dress them up as procedure. Then run it on three cases: (1) a study enrolling people in what it calls a "learning and memory" study, where the real variable is whether they'll cheat on a puzzle once the experimenter leaves the room, with debriefing afterward; (2) a phase 1 drug study paying healthy volunteers $3,000 for two weeks inpatient, in a city where that's several months' rent for the people who tend to sign up; (3) enrolling a 78-year-old with mild cognitive impairment in an antidepressant trial — she can repeat the risks back to me, but keeps saying she's doing it "for the doctor." Give me an answer I won't like on at least one of these, and tell me what the procedure can't settle.
