A slightly odd request, this. I retired four years ago after a career as a field ecologist — thirty-odd years counting plants in quadrats and arguing about disturbance regimes — and ever since I've been working through philosophy on my own. Lately it's been the Stoics, which I realise is a bit of a cliché for a man of my age, but there we are.

I've built myself an arguement I can't get past, and I'd like you to find the seam in it. It's mine, not something I read somewhere. Here it is. (1) The only things genuinely up to me are my own judgements and choices. (2) Everything else — my health, whether my grandchildren are safe, whether the reef survives the next bleaching, who wins the next election — is not up to me. (3) If a thing isn't up to me, then it isn't really good or bad; it's indifferent. (4) And if it's indifferent, I've no reason to prefer one outcome over another, since my preferences can't move it one way or the other. (5) So there's no reason to care about anything outside my own head, and the wise man should be as untroubled watching his town burn as watching it prosper. (6) Which is plainly wrong, and I'm fairly sure the Stoics themselves would have said so — everything I've read of them suggests they wanted people engaged and dutiful, looking after family and city. So some earlier step has to give. Which one, and what does giving it up cost me? If it were up to me I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than (5), but not if keeping them turns the whole thing into a platitude.

Could you write back in continuous prose, please — no headings, no numbered lists or bullet points, no bold and no tables, just the way you'd explain it in a long letter. And in British English, if its all the same to you; I've been over here long enough that 'color' looks like a typo to me now.
