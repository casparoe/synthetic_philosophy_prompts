In my department's informal theory reading group, a colleague regularly deploys the claim that mathematics is unreasonably effective in the natural sciences — Wigner's phrase, though he uses it as a free-standing thesis rather than a summary of an essay — and last week he used it to do two things at once: to argue that mathematical structures are discovered rather than invented, and to close down a debate about whether pure mathematics deserves public funding on the grounds that its eventual physical application vindicates it in advance. I suspect the slogan is doing more work than it can bear, but I cannot say precisely which of its readings would license either conclusion, so I would like a disambiguation rather than a verdict on my colleague.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning with no preamble and without restating my question.

Lay out, as a numbered menu rather than a single preferred interpretation, the distinct precise claims the slogan could be making, ordered from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical, and for each reading give: (i) a one-sentence statement of the claim; (ii) whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, a substantive and dubious thesis, or simply false as stated; and (iii) what evidence would count for or against it. I expect the menu to include at least the following, though you should add any others you think are live: a historical-inductive claim about how often mathematics developed without physical motivation later finds physical application; a claim about the accuracy of numerical prediction rather than mere applicability; a claim about fertility, in which the mathematics forces predictions no one anticipated (the standard example being an equation whose solutions include a partner particle before any such particle is observed); a claim about the direction of explanation, on which physical structure is explained by mathematical structure rather than the reverse; a selection-effect or observer-bias reading, on which we count as fundamental physics only those domains we have already managed to mathematize; and a reading on which the slogan is not a claim at all but a demand for explanation, registering that no current account makes the fit intelligible.

Then address the modifier directly: what exactly is supposed to be unreasonable, and unreasonable relative to what baseline? Say what an account of the fit would have to look like for the effectiveness to count as reasonable, and whether any of the readings above, if true, would make it reasonable.

Next, consider a thought experiment I want each reading tested against: a world whose most fundamental laws require nothing beyond elementary arithmetic and first-order logic, with no continuous structure, no symmetry principles, and no equations beyond those needed to state the laws. For each reading in your menu, say whether that world would make the slogan more surprising, less surprising, or neither, and explain what that tells us about what the reading is actually claiming. I am particularly interested in whether the triviality readings collapse here, and whether the radical readings predict anything at all about such a world.

Finally, tell me which reading or readings my colleague must have had in mind for his two conclusions to follow, and whether either conclusion follows from any reading that survives your audit. Where a reading supports the discovery claim but not the funding claim, or vice versa, separate them explicitly, and flag any place where the two conclusions require incompatible readings of the same slogan.
