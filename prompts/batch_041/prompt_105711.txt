ok so this is a weird one but here goes. I'm 34 and I just got my German citizenship sorted out through my grandmother (she left in the early 60s, whole thing was a paperwork nightmare, took two years and about six different certified translations of the same birth certificate). Anyway the consulate has this little ceremony for people getting naturalized and they asked me to give the short speech at the next one, apparently because I'm "a good example of someone with a long connection" or whatever. It's like 4 or 5 minutes, in English, at a table with coffee and bad pastries afterward.

The audience is the thing I keep going back and fourth about. It's about twenty people being naturalized — a bunch of them are Turkish-German families who've been here for decades, some are recent arrivals from Syria and Ukraine, a few are Brits post-Brexit who are mostly doing it for the passport and will tell you so. Their kids will be there. There's a consulate person who has to say the official stuff before me. So half the room has a very heavy relationship to what Germany did in the last century and half the room is basically joining a country they picked for practical reasons and has no idea why anyone would feel weird about it. I want to say something that works for both and doesn't just be "welcome, here's a passport, Germany is nice."

What I keep circling is Jaspers, The Question of German Guilt — the one that came out of his lectures right after the war. Specifically his thing about the four kinds: criminal, political, moral, metaphysical. I don't want to do a lecture on it, I want it to actually do the work in the speech. Heres the puzzle I can't get past and I want the speech to actually take a position on: Jaspers says political guilt/liability falls on all citizens of a state, regardless of whether you personally did anything, because you're governed by it and it acts in your name. Fine. But then what happens to that when the state keeps going and the citizens keep changing? I'm getting handed this thing by paperwork 80 years later. So is a person naturalized on Tuesday now carrying it? And if yes, what exactly are they carrying, if it isn't moral guilt — because Jaspers is pretty clear moral guilt can't be collectivized and doesn't pass down to people who weren't there. And metaphysical guilt is the one where he says something like everyone is co-responsible for every wrong in the world, but also that it's the one nobody can demand of anyone else, you can only take it on yourself. I want the speech to hold those two apart properly instead of collapsing into either "you're all guilty" or "it's all in the past, get over it."

Things I want it to do:
- Actually use the four categories, but in plain language, without naming them like a taxonomy if that makes it sound like a seminar.
- Take a real position on the inheritance question. I think the honest answer is that the political liability doesn't evaporate — the state is continuous, so the thing you're joining is continuous — but that this is a liability, not a stain, and it's about what the country does next rather than a debt for what it did then. But push on that, don't just take my version. If there's a better way to carve it, carve it.
- Give the new citizens something to actually do with it, since they're the ones who now get a vote in what this country does.
- Have one honest moment aimed at the kids in the room, who are going to inherit the argument whether or not they inherit the guilt.
- Not let the room off the hook and not let it drown either. The Brits doing it for the passport should be able to follow every sentence and not feel accused; the families who've been dealing with this for three generations should feel like I'm not telling them something they already know.
- Don't quote Jaspers. I'm not confident I'd get the wording right and I don't want to be that person at a consulate. Paraphrase in your own words. Also don't put any citations or book titles in the speech itself — I can mention him by name once, maybe, but this is being spoken out loud to people holding coffee.

Also my grandmother is still alive and will probably be in the room, and she has never once in her life said the word "guilt" out loud to me about any of this, so whatever you write has to be something I could say with her sitting three feet away.
