Last month I moved from Berlin to a mid-sized town in the US for a job, and I keep landing in these low-stakes fights about local customs. The one that's really gotten under my skin: my office mate says I have to tip 20% at the coffee place, and when I told him that back home nobody tips and it's not like anyone makes you, he said, when in Rome, do as the Romans do. Same line came up when I crossed an empty street against the light, and when I stood on the left side of the escalator, which apparently is the wrong side here. Now I genuinly can't tell whether he means 'this is just what people do', 'you'll look like an ass', 'you owe them', or something stronger, like I'm actually doing something wrong — morally wrong, not just rude.

Can you lay out the different precise claims that slogan could be making? I want a menu I can argue from, not one answer: for each reading, what it commits you to, whether it's a platitude or contentious or flat out false, and which of them would actually get him to 'you have to' rather than 'it'd be easier if you did'. A few things I'd like the menu to sort out, since they're where I keep getting stuck:

- Does 'the Romans do it' have to mean most people do it, or just that enough of them do it that the expectation is common knowledge? Someone told me that conventions in the technical sense are about expectations lining up rather than head counts — is that the same thing the slogan is doing, or a different animal?

- Does it matter whether the practice is arbitrary versus one that actually helps or hurts people? If the tipping norm ends up screwing over the workers it's supposed to help, does that change the verdict?

- How long do I have to be here before it stops being about me as a visitor? Does it apply to me if I'm a guest in someone's house but not if I've signed a lease? And is there a reading where the reason isn't on my side at all — where it's about what the locals are entitled to expect from me, so the obligation runs in the other direction?

- Is there a reading that's just false — the relativist one where whatever the locals do is by definition right? Because that would make the slogan tell me the local treatment of, say, women or foreigners is above criticism, and I don't think that's what he meant, but I want to see where the line is.

And at the end, tell me which reading people are usually reaching for when they use this line to end an argument like ours, and whether that reading can actually carry the weight.
