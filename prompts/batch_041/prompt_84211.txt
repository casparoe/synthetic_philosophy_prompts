I'm a software engineer who works on machine learning systems, and I've spent a couple of years reading the interpretability literature without any formal background in philosophy. There is a dispute inside it that I keep running into and that seems to me to have stalled, and I want a diagnosis of the dispute rather than another summary of the results.

On one side are people who treat the internal states of a network as genuinely representing things. A direction in activation space is described as the model's representation of a feature; a circuit is described as implementing a computation over such features. The evidence offered is that the feature turns up under several independent methods, that it tracks the corresponding property of the input on cases the method was never fitted to, and that intervening on it changes behavior in the way the hypothesis predicts. On the other side are skeptics who accept essentially all of that evidence and deny the conclusion. They say a probe or a dictionary has found a direction that is linearly decodable and causally relevant, which shows that some structure in the activations correlates with the property and is used downstream, but not that anything in the network means or is about that property. They add that the same network admits many decompositions into features that are equally predictive while disagreeing about which features there are, and that choosing among them looks more like choosing a description than discovering a fact.

Here is the case that started me thinking about this. I can construct small models where I know the ground truth by construction, and in them I can write down two descriptions that agree on every input I can feed the model and survive every intervention I can run, yet assign different contents to the same units. I don't know whether that is a fact about my toys or a fact about representation talk in general.

My hypothesis, which I'd like treated as a hypothesis and not as a premise, is that the two sides are not disagreeing about the networks. One side is making a claim about the model, the other about what our methods license us to believe about it, and since weights and activations are fully observable in a way that an electron is not, the usual realist move of inferring hidden structure from predictive success has nothing to do here. A second possibility is that the word representation is doing at least three jobs at once — carrying information about something, mediating behavior causally, and having content that could be correct or incorrect — and that each camp means a different one of them.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it terminological, two camps answering different questions under one heading, a difference about which evidence counts, a hidden empirical or formal question, or bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for? Engage what each side actually says rather than surveying positions, and tell me which diagnosis the evidence supports. Then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a distinction, an experiment in which two competing ontologies predict differently, a formal result about what a given decomposition method can and cannot identify from the data it is handed, or a decision that the question should be abandoned. If you think nothing will move it, say so and explain why. I don't need citations or a reading list; I want the structure of the disagreement.
