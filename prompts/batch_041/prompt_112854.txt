I chair the board of a small environmental nonprofit — clean water and land conservation, we work in a purple-ish state where the rural counties we need on side are heavily conservative — and Thursday we vote on whether to hire a consultant who wants to redo all our messaging using moral foundations theory. His pitch: progressives talk almost entirely in care and fairness terms, while conservatives also draw on loyalty, authority and purity, so our mailers about "environmental justice" are literally invisible to half our audience; instead we should frame the same policies as stewardship of creation, protecting our homeland and our way of life, keeping the water pure. Three board members are sold. Three think this is a slick way of pretending we don't have a position — we do think the other side is wrong about the science, and about who ends up bearing the cost of the transition — and that dressing up our asks in sanctity language is either manipulation or a quiet concession that their moral worldview is as good as ours. I'm the chair (unfortunatly), so I have to circulate a written opinion before the meeting: a recommendation, a concrete course of action with a time frame (do the training, or pilot in two counties first, or write a policy about when we're allowed to use a frame we don't ourselves hold?), and an honest treatment of the strongest thing the other side has going for it. I don't want a literature review or a reading list — I want something I can hand out that the three holdouts can actually respond to.

The thing eating at me is that the consultant keeps sliding from a description to a prescription. He says the theory is purely descriptive, it just maps the intuitions people happen to have, and then in the same breath says we should therefore speak to all the foundations. And I noticed he's still using the old five-foundation story, because I read somewhere that the team behind it split fairness into two — equality and proportionality, so there's a version with six now — and if proportionality turns out to be the bit nearly everyone endorses while equality is the polarizing bit, then the tidy "conservatives have a broader moral palate than liberals" line that his whole strategy rests on gets shakier, and the strategy does too. Also one of my board members keeps saying that even if the psychology is all correct, the fact that a frame works tells you nothing about whether the thing being framed is true or just. I can't tell whether that's a fatal objection to the whole proposal or just a reason to be careful about how we present it.

So: does the psychology of left and right actually tell us anything about who's right, and what should we do on Thursday? Be direct about how confident you are, and tell me what would change your mind — which piece of evidence or argument would flip your recommendation, and where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
