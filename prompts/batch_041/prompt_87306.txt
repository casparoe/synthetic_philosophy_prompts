I'm in a graduate seminar on value and normativity, and we have spent several weeks circling the wrong kind of reasons problem without anyone stepping back to ask what a solution to it would even have to accomplish. I have a term paper to write, and before I commit to a line I want the specification itself: the desiderata that any account of the right kind of reasons -- the feature that separates a reason bearing on whether an attitude is fitting from a reason bearing only on whether it is prudent, useful, or socially rewarded to hold -- must satisfy.

Please give me the list, with the case or consideration behind each item, and be explicit about which items are non-negotiable and which are merely parochial demands of one camp (fitting-attitude theorists, buck-passers, reasons-firsters, and so on). The account I have in mind is meant to do a specific job, so the desiderata should be the ones that matter for that use rather than for a general theory of reasons: it has to be strong enough to let a fitting-attitude analysis of value answer the demon objection, the case in which a threat gives you a reason to admire someone, so that a naive version of the analysis seems to imply that the person is admirable.

I would expect the list to have to cover at least: the demon case and its relatives; the parallel cases for belief, emotion, desire, and intention, and whether a single account can handle all of them or the right kind of reason is attitude-specific; the object-given versus state-given distinction and whether it is the same distinction as right-kind versus wrong-kind or a rival to it; the requirement that the account not be circular (if value is to be analyzed in terms of right-kind reasons, the right kind cannot itself be defined in terms of value); the requirement that it preserve the link between value and reasons that made the analysis attractive in the first place; and the constraint that it not make pragmatic reasons for attitudes simply impossible. Sort these into genuine requirements and mere preferences of one side.

What I most want is the part where the list turns against itself: if some subset of the desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied, say which subset, what has to be surrendered, and whether that is a reductio of the fitting-attitude project or just a signal that the right kind of reason has to be taken as primitive. Also tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate accounts. I do not need a full scorecard of named theories; the weight should be on the criteria and their motivating cases.

I want to test whatever you say against three cases of my own, which I do not think are in the standard literature. First: a media analyst whose employer's recommendation algorithm surfaces content to her only when she reacts to it with indignation, so that she has a strong career reason to be indignant at a given post -- is that a reason of the right kind for indignation, and if not, what exactly distinguishes it from the demon's threat? Second: a novelist who cannot write well unless she sincerely believes in her characters, and who has a standing reason, once the belief is installed, to keep believing -- does the theory count that reason as bearing on the belief's fittingness, or only on its utility? Third: a person who arranges to have an attitude implanted in herself by a pill or a hypnotist, and who then, by design, finds the attitude fitting -- must the theory say that the original prudential reason transmits into a right-kind reason, or must it insist that no amount of engineering the attitude can change what the object makes fitting? Treat these as stress tests and say where they land.

Finally, since this is for a paper, I would like you to be explicit about your confidence: tell me how sure you are of the overall verdict, and single out the one point in the whole answer you are least sure of.
