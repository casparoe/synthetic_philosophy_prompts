I want to pressure-test a position I have come to hold about historical understanding, and I want the case against it mapped rather than merely listed.

The position is this. Historical interpretation and evaluation cannot be carried out from anywhere but the present: the historian's questions, salience judgments, and evaluative standards are supplied by the historian's own moment, and the aspiration to assess past agents "by the standards of their own time" is either unattainable or, where it appears to succeed, a covert present-day judgment wearing period costume. On this view, presentism is not a vice to be corrected by better method but a condition of any historical understanding at all. The practical upshot I take from it is deflationary: the familiar demand that we avoid anachronistic judgment is largely an injunction we cannot obey and do not in fact obey, and the interesting question is not whether we judge the past by present lights but which present lights we judge it by and whether we are honest about doing so.

I recognize this bundles several claims that could come apart, and I would like the answer to keep them apart. At least: (a) a descriptive-causal claim that present concerns inevitably shape which pasts we find interesting and how we narrate them; (b) an epistemic claim that there is no standpoint available to us from which a genuinely past-centered evaluation could be issued, so the "view from the period" is a regulative fiction; and (c) a normative claim that present-centered evaluation is not merely unavoidable but legitimate, so the guilt attached to anachronism is misplaced. I hold all three but with different confidence, and I suspect (a) is nearly trivial, (b) is the load-bearing one, and (c) is where I am most exposed.

What I want from you is the exposure. Which observations, arguments, intuitions, or results from adjacent areas would count against this position — and I mean the ones that should move me, not the ones that are easy to answer. I am particularly interested in defeaters of the following shapes, though I do not want to be limited to them:

First, cases where the position's own machinery seems to collapse. If "the standards of the time" are invoked to explain why a past agent acted as they did, note that past societies were internally plural — there were dissenters, reformers, and minority moralities in most periods we care about — so the appeal to a unitary "spirit of the age" may be doing explanatory work that the actual historical record does not support. If that is right, does it undercut the position or strengthen it? I genuinely cannot tell.

Second, the distinction between explaining conduct and evaluating it. It seems plausible that a historian can explain a past atrocity entirely in period terms while still declining to evaluate it, and equally plausible that declining to evaluate is itself a present-day stance. I want to know whether this symmetry is real or whether one of the two directions is privileged.

Third, the internal-versus-external standards contrast. Evaluating a past mathematician's proof by mathematical standards, or a past general's decision by the standards of the military art as then practiced, does not look like presentism at all; it looks like competent appraisal within a practice. Does the position have to treat all such cases as covertly presentist, and if so, is that a reductio?

Fourth, the asymmetry between blaming someone for acting on standards we now reject and blaming them for failing to know what they could not have known. There is a live literature on whether moral ignorance excuses, and I would like to know whether importing it helps or hurts me — in particular whether it lets the presentist off too easily by relocating the whole dispute into epistemology.

Fifth, whether the position has quietly qualified itself into unfalsifiability. If every apparent counterexample — a historian who really does seem to render a past-centered verdict, a reformer who really does seem to have judged their own age by standards not of that age — can be redescribed as a present judgment in disguise, then I need to be told what observation would have counted against the position, and whether the absence of one is fatal or merely the normal condition of a thesis this general.

For each defeater, tell me how decisive it would be: fatal, seriously damaging, awkward but survivable, or merely rhetorical. Tell me which of them a committed defender of the position would themselves concede as counting against them — not only what critics would say. And where a defeater seems to work only against one of my three sub-claims, say which, since I would rather salvage (a) and (b) than lose all three.

What would move me most is a case where the very demand for period-centered judgment turns out to be a present-day value — a value about respecting agency, or about not imposing our categories — such that the anti-presentist is caught in the same net they cast over everyone else. If that case can be made, I want it made sharply. If it cannot, I want to know why not, and what the position would have to concede to avoid it.
