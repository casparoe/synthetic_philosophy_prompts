I've been working on the indicative/counterfactual distinction for a while now, and I've talked myself into a position that I suspect is too neat. The view is this: there's no deep semantic difference between indicative and counterfactual conditionals. There's one conditional operator, and the apparent difference is entirely a matter of pragmatics -- specifically, the counterfactual form carries a presupposition or implicature that the antecedent is false (or at least not live), and the past-tense morphology is just the conventional way English signals that. On this view, the would in counterfactuals is not a distinct modal with its own semantics; it's the same modality you get in indicatives, just colored by the falsity presupposition. The old Goodman-style cotenability apparatus, on this picture, is a red herring: it's trying to do semantic work that's really just presupposition projection.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against this. Not just the usual but Anderson cases show counterfactuals can have true antecedents point -- I know about those, and I've got a story about them (the presupposition can be locally accommodated or canceled). I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Which ones would a committed defender of the pragamtic view have to concede as counting against them, and which would they dismiss as question-begging? I'm especially interested in:

- cases where the indicative/counterfactual distinction seems to survive even when the falsity presupposition is explicitly canceled or where the antecedent is known to be true;
- data from languages that mark the distinction differently (or not at all) -- does the pragmatic view predict the right cross-linguistic distribution, or does it overgenerate?
- acquisition data: if it's just a presupposition, kids should get it whenever they get presuppositions, but if it's semantic, there might be a different developmental profile;
- embedding facts: the distinction seems to behave differently under negation, in questions, in conditionals, under only if, etc. Does the pragmatic view predict that, or does it have to stipulate?
- the relationship to tense: if the past tense is fake, why does it interact with real tense the way it does? (e.g., If it had rained yesterday... vs If it rained tomorrow...)

Also, I'd like an honest assessment of whether the view risks anything at all, or whether it's been qualified into unfalsifiability. If the answer is that it's well insulated, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or is it just what you expect from a pragmatic account?

I'm a PhD student in linguistics, so feel free to get into the weeds -- but don't just list the literature. I want the actual structure of the dialectic.
