The argument over whether a lie may ever be told for a good end has been running, in one form or another, at least since Augustine's two treatises on lying, and I have yet to see anyone on either side give ground. I am teaching the material again next term, and I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another round of it. No reading list or citations, please; I know the literature.

The absolutist holds that no lie is ever permitted, and grounds this not in the harm done but in the duplicity of the speaker. Augustine's formulation is that "that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind," and he locates the fault in "the desire of deceiving in the uttering of his mind," adding that the liar's fault is the same whether or not anyone is actually deceived. The wrong, then, is a fact about the speaker's will, and it survives the case in which the lie helps everyone and injures no one; the jesting falsehood is exempted on the ground that the joker evidently means no deceit.

The permissivist -- here I mean both the card-carrying consequentialist and the ordinary person who finds the murderer-at-the-door case decisive -- grounds the wrong in the relation to the hearer: the violation of trust, the manipulation of another's mind, the corrosion of the practice of testimony. Where the hearer is not wronged, or where the alternative is an atrocity, the falsehood is at worst a venial fault. Both sides agree that malicious deception is wrong and that lying is a defect; they part only on the exceptionless cases, and there they trade stock examples. (The modern definitional literature divides on whether a lie need be intended to deceive at all, which suggests the two camps may not even be arguing about the same class of acts.)

Two cases that neither side's stock examples cover are, it seems to me, where to press.

(i) A falsehood told in earnest to someone I know will not believe me: a pursuer who has already seen through me, a torturer who knows I am lying. No one is deceived, nothing is taken up as true. Does the absolutist's prohibition still reach it? Does the permissivist's permission have anything left to license?

(ii) The converse case, which Augustine's criterion seems to license and which most modern definitions forbid: telling the truth with the fixed intention that the hearer take it for a lie and act on the opposite. On the double-heart criterion this is a lie; on most modern accounts it is not, since what was asserted was true.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that this is a compound impasse: a conceptual disagreement about what the prohibition prohibits -- a fact about the speaker's will or a fact about the hearer's exposure -- plus a disagreement about whether any moral prohibition can be exceptionless, plus a disagreement about what an intuition about a case is evidence of. If the first is doing the work, then each side's central examples are non-probative for the other -- the permissivist's cases are all hearer-side, the absolutist's all speaker-side -- and they can go on trading them indefinitely.

So: which is it? Verbal, with the two sides attaching different extensions to 'lie'? Substantive-moral, about absolute norms? A hidden disagreement about what counts as a reason? Or two questions -- may this be done? and what ought one to do here? -- answered under one heading? Say where the disagreement about the object of the prohibition actually lives, give the reasons for your diagnosis, and then tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a decision about which of these questions is the real one, or nothing at all, because no further argument could move either side.

End with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that names your diagnosis in a short phrase.
