I am preparing a work-in-progress talk for a small interdisciplinary group and need a Q&A preparation sheet. The audience is not hostile to metaphysics, but it is not on my side either: the chair is a physicalist philosopher of mind who regards panpsychism as a respectable dead end and will go straight for the combination problem; two members are Russellian monists who are content to grant that physics describes only structure and leaves intrinsic nature open, but who insist that nothing normative follows from how one fills that gap; one is a philosopher of physics who will ask what the observable difference is and whether my fundamental property can even be assigned in a frame-independent way; one is a consequentialist ethicist who cares only about whether the view changes any verdict; and one is a graduate student in animal cognition who will ask where the line falls in practice.

My thesis, in brief: if the intrinsic natures that physics leaves unspecified are phenomenal, the most economical specification of them is not merely qualitative but hedonic — some fundamental states carry a primitive valence, a felt positivity or negativity, and they do so without belonging to any subject. If that is right, ethics does not begin with organisms, or nervous systems, or even subjects; it begins with physics. The scope of moral concern is coextensive with the physical world, and physical processes themselves, not only the beings they compose, fall under moral assessment. I have already committed myself in the draft to the further claim that valence, if fundamental, must be assigned in a way that respects the symmetries of the physics, and I suspect this creates a problem I have not solved.

Give me the objections I will actually get from these people, phrased in their vocabulary, ordered by how much damage they do rather than by how likely they are to be raised. For each: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and an honest note on where that reply is weak. Be specific about the pressure points — I expect the subjectless-valence claim to be the softest, the aggregation of micro-valences to be the messiest, and I am not confident I have any answer at all to the frame-invariance problem. Close with the question I am hoping nobody asks.

The sheet should force me to confront the things I have been avoiding: what my view implies about the moral status of a star, of radioactive decay, and of pair production in a vacuum; whether it commits me to anything like a conservation law for valence, and whether the absence of any such law counts as evidence against me; whether it makes the universe's approach to heat death the greatest good; and whether, if micro-valences exist but cannot be aggregated, the view retains any action-guiding content. Do not tell me the view is unfalsifiable without naming exactly which of its claims could fail and what would show it; and do not soften the combination problem — I need to know how bad it really is for a position that has already conceded that no micro-subjects are present.
