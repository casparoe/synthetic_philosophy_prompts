I retired about three years ago after thirty-odd years of sticking electrodes into monkey visual cortex, and since then I've been working my way through philosophy of mind, plus whatever machine-learning stuff my old students keep emailing me. Something keeps nagging at me. Both fields say the same sentence over and over: this thing represents that thing. I said it for decades without anyone ever asking what I meant by it — this cell represents a vertical edge, this population represents the direction of motion — and now I'm not sure I'd stand behind any of it if someone pushed.

So I've been going through cases and writing down my gut verdict on each. I'd like someone to tell me what principle is actualy generating these verdicts, if there's one at all.

- A photoreceptor. Tuned to light, obviously, and everything downstream depends on it. Verdict: no, it doesn't represent anything. It's a transducer.

- A cell in primary visual cortex that fires to one orientation and not others, whose output the next stage reads out, and whose firing tracks what the animal reports seeing. Verdict: yes, represents the edge. (I know someone will tell me this is also just a filter. That's part of why I'm writing.)

- A language model. A collegue trains a linear probe on a middle layer and it says, at 96% accuracy, whether the sentence is in the subjunctive. But the same probe on a net with random weights gets 90%, because the text statistics leak through regardless. Verdict: no representation — the probe is reading the input, not the model.

- Another group searches for a small set of directions such that any activation is a sum of a handful of them (a dictionary, I think they call it), and one direction, turned up, makes the model talk about bridges in every sentence, and turned down, stops it. Verdict: yes, that one represents bridges.

- A property the model plainly uses — intervene on it and the answers change, every time — but nobody can find a direction, a unit, or a small set of them that carries it. It's smeared everywhere. Verdict: I still want to say it represents the property, and I can't tell you why that feels different from the probe case.

- An attention head doing something mechanical: after it sees A ... A, it puts its weight on whatever followed the first A. Causally central, easy to describe, does exactly what it says on the tin. Verdict: no, that's a mechanism, not a representation of anything. Though I notice I'm using a completely different standard here than in the bridge case.

- A heat map that highlights the word not in a sentence. Verdict: no, that's a picture somebody made.

- A unit that fires for a grab-bag of unrelated things, and that you can't ablate without hurting performance on legal text. Verdict: yes, it represents something, but I couldn't tell you what, which worries me.

- A direction that predicts some behavior nicely, but ablating it changes nothing. Verdict: no, correlation.

Is there one principle that gets all of these? State it precisely enough that I can check it against the list, tell me what it says about a few cases I haven't listed (your pick, make them interesting), and be straight with me about which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. I've got no sentimental attachment to any of them except the V1 one, and if that has to go I'd rather know now.
