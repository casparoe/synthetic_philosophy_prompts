my cousin runs a no-kill animal shelter and she's also a committed suffering-focused utilitarian — she thinks the total amount of pain in the world is the only thing that matters in the end, and that a life that's mostly pain is worse than no life at all. but the shelter's rule is absolute: we never euthanize, no matter what. they'll keep a dog in a run for two years with untreated anxiety rather than put it down, and there's a cat with a spinal injury they're keeping alive on a little cart. they also run a trap-neuter-return program for ferals, which she defends by saying that stopping a cat from ever being born costs nobody anything, while letting one be born into misery would be a real harm. so what does the no-kill practice actually presuppose about death versus suffering? is it secretly committed to killing being wrong in itself, a line you just don't cross — or to the idea that death is bad because it cuts off future good, which seems to need there to be goods and not just pains? does either of those survive her stated view that only pain matters? and which parts of the ethos are really forced by some theory and which are just shelter culture? if she took the suffering view seriously, what in the practice would have to go?
