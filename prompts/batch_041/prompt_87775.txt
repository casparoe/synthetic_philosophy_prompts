I've followed the conciliationism-versus-steadfastness debate for years, and it never seems to move: both sides grant the toy cases and then restate a principle. My hypothesis is that the impasse is an artifact of the setup. Both camps analyze a frozen snapshot — I hold one view, a peer I respect holds the opposite, and the question is how I alone should revise — but the cases that actually motivate the debate are never frozen; the peer's opinion was formed partly in reaction to mine, and mine to his, so we are a coupled system rather than a one-sided update. If that's right, the two sides may be answering different questions under one heading — what a single ideal agent owes to a fixed piece of evidence versus what two mutually-influencing agents owe to each other — rather than disagreeing about a single thing. Is that the correct diagnosis, or does a genuine substantive disagreement survive once the case is made dynamic, say about whose reasoning gets treated as the fixed point? And what, on your diagnosis, would count as progress: a formal treatment of the coupled case, a distinction that dissolves the puzzle, or an argument that the question was ill-posed from the start? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of who said what.
