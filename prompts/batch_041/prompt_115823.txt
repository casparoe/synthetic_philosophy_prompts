I teach an upper-level seminar on evolutionary psychology and want a classification instrument for the standing dispute over whether the field is a science or a collection of just-so stories. Please build me a decision tree that takes a reader from first principles to a named position, rather than a list of schools to pick a favorite from.

Construct it in the right order: first the logical space — the branching questions and the combinations of answers they actually permit — and only then the positions located within it. Each branch node must be a concrete, answerable claim on which the positions genuinely come apart, phrased so that a reader can assent or dissent without already knowing which label it leads to; no node may simply ask "are you an adaptationist?" The distinctions I want the tree to turn on are these: whether there is a pan-human, species-typical architecture that reliably develops across environments, as against plasticity and cultural construction; whether that architecture is massively modular or domain-general; whether adaptation may be inferred from present design and current utility alone, or requires comparative and phylogenetic evidence; whether the standard empirical toolkit — cross-cultural universality claims, the hunter-gatherer analogy, WEIRD samples, the notion of an environment of evolutionary adaptedness — can genuinely test adaptation hypotheses; and whether the just-so-story charge is best read as an accusation about testability or about evidential standards.

The leaves must include, at minimum: the massive-modularity adaptationism associated with the Santa Barbara school; human behavioral ecology and its optimization models; gene-culture coevolution or dual-inheritance theory; developmental systems theory; niche-construction approaches; the spandrel-and-exaptation critique of adaptationism; and a deflationary view on which the field is simply a normal research program whose current troubles are ordinary scientific growing pains. Where two leaves are ones your tree cannot actually separate, say so explicitly rather than papering over it, and give a one-line diagnosis of each unoccupied combination the space leaves open — is nobody there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?

Anchor the discriminating questions in concrete cases rather than abstractions, and use cases that force real work: the cheater-detection findings from Wason-style selection tasks, the mismatch reasoning behind our taste for sugar and fat, incest avoidance and the Westermarck effect, and the dispute over whether language is an adaptation or a byproduct. Include at least one case that separates behavioral ecology from massive modularity — some subsistence-society pattern where an optimization model and a module story predict different things — and at least one that separates gene-culture coevolution from both. Write for graduate students, keep the tree to five to seven branching levels and eight to ten leaves, and do not include any reading list or citations.

Begin your reply with a single sentence naming the distinction the entire space turns on — the tree's organizing principle — then give the full tree, then your notes on the unoccupied combinations and the leaves you could not separate. No preamble and no restating of my request.
