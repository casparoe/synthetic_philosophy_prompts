Problem 5 (20 points). A fund holds bankroll W and is offered a bet on a coin that comes up heads with probability 0.55. If you stake an amount S, then on heads you receive back 2S (so you gain S), and on tails you receive nothing (you lose S).

(a) (8 pts) Suppose this exact bet is offered every day forever, and each day you choose what fraction f of your current bankroll to stake. What f should you choose, and what justifies it? Derive the number.

(b) (7 pts) Now suppose the bet is offered exactly once, and the terms require you to stake exactly half your bankroll — no more, no less. Does the criterion you used in (a) say to take the bet? Show the relevant calculation, and say what, if anything, changes because the bet comes only once.

(c) (5 pts) A classmate says the criterion from (a) is "just expected utility maximization with one particular utility function," so it has no special claim on a rational agent. Assess.

And here's what the student turned in:

(a) The growth-optimal fraction is Kelly's, f* = p − q/b, with p = 0.55, q = 0.45, b = 1, so f* = 0.10. Justification: staking f gives wealth W·∏(1 + fX_i) after n bets, and (1/n)log(W_n/W) → E[log(1 + fX)], so by the law of large numbers the f that maximizes E[log] is the one whose bankroll grows fastest almost surely. The optimum is interior and the growth rate is concave in f; staking the whole bankroll every round drives wealth to zero almost surely as n grows.

(b) Yes, take it. Staking half the bankroll gives an expected gain of 0.5 × (0.55 − 0.45) = 5%, so the bet has positive expected value, and since log is an increasing function, a bet that raises expected wealth also raises expected log wealth, so the criterion from (a) endorses it. The one thing that changes because the bet comes only once is that the law-of-large-numbers argument doesn't apply — but that argument was only ever about how the criterion gets justified, not about what the criterion says, and it says to take any favorable bet, once or many times.

(c) Half right. Kelly is indeed equivalent to expected utility maximization with log utility. But log isn't arbitrary: it's the utility function whose expectation is the long-run growth rate, so an agent who bets repeatedly and cares about growth is committed to it. Maximizing expected wealth, by contrast, is the policy that ends in ruin.
