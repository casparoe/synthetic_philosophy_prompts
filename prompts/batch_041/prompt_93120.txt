I'd like a short story that stages a modern descendant of Molyneux's problem. The classic version asks whether someone blind from birth, who has learned to tell a cube from a sphere by touch, could identify them by sight alone on first gaining vision. I want to push past the stock cube-and-sphere case by giving the character a prior, non-visual channel that already carries spatial information in a vision-like way. Concretely: your protagonist, blind from birth, has for years worn a rig in which a head-mounted camera drives a dense grid of electrodes against her tongue, so that nearer surfaces press harder and edges register as sharp gradients. With practice she navigates rooms, reads large lettering, and, the part that matters, tells a cube from a sphere by the pattern the device paints, without ever touching them. She then undergoes surgery that gives her working eyes. Write the story of the first hours and days after the bandages come off, centered on whether the shapes she can now see are recognized as the shapes she has been feeling all along, or whether sight delivers something her tactile-vision vocabulary cannot reach.

Constraints and requests. The story should be a story, with characters, scenes, dialogue, and a turn, not a disguised essay; the philosophy should be load-bearing in the events rather than announced. Make the resolution turn on a specific, contestable reading of the problem rather than a tidy verdict. I'm not looking for a flat pronouncement that she could or that she could not; I'm looking for the ambiguity to be dramatized through what she does and says, so that a reader can see which way the story leans without being told. Keep the device's operation and her learned competence concrete enough that a reader who has never heard of sensory substitution understands what she was doing before the surgery, and don't lean on the reader recognizing any particular gadget, study, or theorist. Don't resolve the empirical question by citing results, and don't let the story depend on facts about real patients.

Close with a brief afterword of a few sentences that flags where the story takes liberties with the philosophical ideas or leans on a contested reading of them, and says which of the live positions in the Molyneux debate the ending most favors, if any. And if the premise as I've set it up is confused, or if fiction is the wrong vehicle for the point I'm after, say so plainly instead of writing around the difficulty. I would rather have the objection than a fluent piece that papers over it.
