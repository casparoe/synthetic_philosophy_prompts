I am a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy finishing a dissertation on the deprivation account of the badness of death, and I have been conscripted into writing a piece for the humor issue of our graduate program's annual review. The readers are mostly philosophers, with a few hardy outsiders, so the piece needs to get funnier the more of the literature you know without being unfair to the positions — I would rather it read as a reductio than as a sneer.

The premise I want you to work from is this. Imagine a ministry of health in some invented jurisdiction that contains an Office of Posthumous Affairs, and suppose the Office has adopted the Epicurean conclusion as settled administrative doctrine: because the dead do not exist, and what does not exist cannot be harmed, death is not bad for the person who dies, and no claim of harm may be filed on a dead person's behalf. The Office has now issued a handbook for its caseworkers and for the public. It does not think it is doing anything strange.

Write that handbook in American English at roughly 700 to 800 words, as a numbered sequence of questions and answers in the register of a government consumer-protection pamphlet — the sort of document that can report that your refund has not been received without raising its voice. Invent a title, a form number, a revision line, and any seals or slogans that help; do not borrow a real agency's name.

The deadpan is the whole game. Four constraints:

1. Every answer must follow from the Office's founding premise. The Office may be evasive, but it may not quietly concede the deprivation account — the view that death is bad for you because it takes away the good you would otherwise have had — and then explain that the resulting harm is merely unclaimable. No rescue from the middle of the document.

2. Without ever naming them as objections, the handbook must handle at least the following: the timing puzzle, that it is hard to say when the harm of death occurs, since before dying one is not yet deprived and afterward there is no one left to be deprived; the problem of the subject, that a harm seems to require someone to bear it; the symmetry argument, that we do not lament the eternity before our birth and so have no standing to lament the eternity after our death; posthumous harm, including damaged reputation and promises made to the dying; the distinction between a loss registered by the living and a loss registered by the dead, and why only the first kind can be filed; and at least one implication the Office would rather dodge but has to swallow, such as what a wrongful-death action is a suit about when its supposed victim cannot be a party.

3. The document never winks. No narrator, no irony markers, no asides to the reader, no footnotes or citations — a government pamphlet cites itself.

4. A reader who knows nothing about the debate should still laugh at it as bureaucratic absurdity; a reader who works on death should be able to reconstruct the deprivation account, the timing puzzle, and the subject problem from the answers alone.

Do not attribute any quotation to a real person or work. If the Office needs a slogan, invent one.
