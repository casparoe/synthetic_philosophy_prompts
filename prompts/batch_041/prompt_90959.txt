Here's the idea. Everyone reads Carroll's tortoise as a puzzle about *justification*: the tortoise accepts the premises, accepts the conditional, and then, when asked to accept the conclusion, asks for one more conditional to be written down, and on it goes. The standard replies are all about how the regress stops or why it doesn't need to stop — you can't demand a reason for every rule; accepting a rule is a matter of training or disposition rather than belief; the tortoise is just a stipulated creature who isn't rational, so nothing follows. Fine. I think all of that is answering the wrong question.

My hunch: the tortoise isn't short on justification, it's short on a *kind of state*. Everything on its list — every proposition, every conditional, every principle you write down for it — is the same shape: it's the sort of thing that can be true or false, believed or doubted. Call those premise-shaped states. A transition from premises to conclusion is not premise-shaped. So no matter how many premise-shaped things you hand the tortoise, you haven't given it the one thing it's missing, and that's why the regress can't be closed by adding premises — not because reasons run out, but because you're adding the wrong kind of thing. The tortoise is basically a semantic engine: it assigns truth values to sentences, and it never enters the proof search.

If that's right, the interesting question isn't "how do we justify deduction" but: can a transitional state be appraised as correct or incorrect at all, and if it can't, what happens to the claim that logic is normative for thought? We want modus ponens to be a norm of reasoning. But a creature that accepts every relevant conditional can still not be reasoning in accordance with it, and there is no further premise that fixes this. So either the norm is addressed to something other than the reasoner's reasons, or "being justified in reasoning" means something quite different from "having a justification." I also want this connected to the related worry about whether someone could adopt a rule of inference they don't already follow — treat that as part of the landscape, not as something I need to cite.
