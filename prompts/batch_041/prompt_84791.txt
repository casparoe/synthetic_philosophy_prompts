I'm in a seminar on translation and poetic form, and we've been spending time with Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot — his long meditation on translating Clément Marot's short poem to a sick young lady, and the way every rendering has to trade off sense, sound, syllable count, rhyme, and the poem's other formal features. I went looking for more Marot and found the prefatory letter to his early collection L'Adolescence Clémentine, which is in a sixteenth-century French I can only half read. There is a sentence near the end of that letter that I keep stumbling over:

"Et là commencerons par la premiere Eglogue des Bucoliques Virgilianes, translatée (certes) en grande jeunesse : comme pourrez en plusieurs sortes congnoistre : mesmement par les Couppes feminines : lesquelles je n'observois encor alors : dont Jan le Maire de Belges (en les m'apprenant) me reprint."

What I take from it is roughly that Marot is presenting his youthful translation of Virgil's first eclogue, and that readers can tell it was written when he was young, in particular from the "Couppes feminines" — which he says he was not yet observing, and for which Jean le Maire de Belges reproved him while teaching him. But I don't know what those "couppes feminines" are doing in this context, why their absence would be a tell of immaturity, or what the reproof amounted to. I have a vague sense that French verse of the period had rules about where the caesura falls and about masculine versus feminine endings — that a line can end on a stressed syllable or on a mute e, and that this affects the count and the feel of the line — but I cannot reconstruct how that becomes something a translator is supposed to "observe," nor why a translator of Virgil into French would be judged by it.

Could you walk me through the passage? In particular: (1) what the relevant formal constraint or constraints are, and what "observing" them involves for someone composing French verse in that period; (2) why a failure to observe them would be audible or visible to a contemporary reader as a sign of inexperience; (3) how this connects to the larger problem the seminar is about — that translating a poem into a target language means submitting to that language's formal constraints, so that "preserving the form" is never a matter of copying the source's form but of finding an equivalent discipline in the target; and (4) whether Marot's remark suggests he came to see formal constraint as something that could be learned and applied rather than as a merely ornamental addition. I don't need citations or a reading list; I am trying to understand the mechanics and the stakes.
