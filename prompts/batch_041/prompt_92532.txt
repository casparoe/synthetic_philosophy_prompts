I am preparing a session for a graduate seminar on collective mentality, and for this meeting I would like the discussion to run off a work of fiction rather than off secondary literature. Please write a critical reading of Orson Scott Card's Ender's Game through the question Hofstadter presses in the Ant Fugue dialogue in Gödel, Escher, Bach — the dialogue with the ant colony Aunt Hillary — namely: can a colony, a nest, a swarm, a hive, be a single mind, or is what looks like a mind there only the appearance of very good coordination?

I want the reading to hold several things together, none of them merely decorative.

First, the Ant Fugue's claim in its strongest form: that the colony can be a genuine subject because there is a level of description at which the ants are not small animals but signals in a system, and that the colony becomes a mind in the full sense only when it can represent itself as one thing — when there is something it is like to be the colony, over and above there being something it is like to be each ant. Say whether the second requirement is a further condition or only the first one restated, and what difference the answer makes.

Second, the obvious rejoinder: a brain is also a colony, and nobody asks the neurons for permission. If the isomorphism between nest and brain is doing real work, locate precisely where it stops doing work — what a brain has that a nest lacks, and why that difference bears on mentality rather than merely on efficiency.

Third, a point from the biology that fiction about ants almost always suppresses: no ant is in charge. Queens do not command; colony-level behaviour emerges from local interactions among bodies that know nothing of the whole. The familiar picture of the nest as a kingdom with a mind at the top is a distortion, and any colony mind worth the name will be decentralized.

Then bring all of that to the novel. The Formics are presented as a species whose intelligence is concentrated in queens and whose workers behave as instruments; the war with them is fought twice; Ender's final battle is not a simulation, and it ends the species; the novel calls what he did xenocide, and leaves him at the end with the last queen's cocoon and a grief he must speak for. Ask where the novel thinks the Formic mind is, and notice how much of its moral force depends on the answer. If the hive is one mind, what did Ender destroy — one being, a great many, or a civilization that was never the right unit of accounting? If the workers are instruments rather than persons, does xenocide still name the crime, or does the novel need the Formics to be a people in the ordinary sense before Ender's guilt can take the shape the novel gives it? Throughout, keep distinct the two questions of whether a system can be treated as an agent — one with attributable attitudes and a procedure for settling them — and whether it has experiences at all, and say which of the two the novel needs the Formics to be and which one the Ant Fugue's colony is meant to be.

Two things to keep the reading from collapsing into summary. Take the novel's implicit metaphysics to four cases it never stages, and say what it implies about each: a single worker surviving alone after the queen is dead; a queen surviving with no workers left; a colony whose workers are replaced one by one by mechanical relays that do the same jobs; and a colony that splits into two nests that then diverge. Where the novel does not decide, say so, and construct the reading rather than pretending the text supplies it. And be honest about resistance: identify the scenes or structural choices — Ender's empathy, the novel's investment in individual interiority, the fact that the aliens receive a voice only late and through a single body — that push against the colony-mind reading, and say what the novel would have to give up in order to accept it. Close by saying what a novel can make vivid about distributed minds that the Ant Fugue and the group-agency discussion cannot.
