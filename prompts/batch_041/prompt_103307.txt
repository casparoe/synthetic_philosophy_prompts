I'm halfway through a philosophy master's and last night I lost an argument I still think I should have won. It began as a domestic squabble: my flatmate wants to replace the lawn in our shared garden with gravel, on the grounds that keeping a lawn means routinely killing an enormous number of small invertebrates, and I said that was an absurd reason to give up a garden. Three hours and two pots of tea later he had written the following on the back of an envelope and announced it as a proof that I was in the grip of "species prejudice". He is a committed effective altruist with a spreadsheet for his donations and a habit of saying that anyone who resists this kind of thing is simply "using a different decision theory", so I would like a proper diagnosis rather than a shrug.

The argument as he set it out:

(1) A being's moral weight is proportional to the probability that it is sentient. So a collection of n beings, each with probability p of being sentient, carries n × p units of moral weight, and the harms to them are to be added up.
(2) Ordinary human activity affects astronomical numbers of invertebrates. A square metre of soil or turf holds, by most counts, hundreds of thousands of small invertebrates — nematodes, mites, springtails, ants, worms — and mowing a lawn, driving a car, cooking a meal or building a house disturbs thousands of square metres. Whatever the exact figures, the totals run to billions per person per year.
(3) The probability that at least some of these invertebrates are sentient is not negligible. The science is genuinely unsettled, and on the evidence available it would be reckless to put that probability below, say, one in a thousand.
(4) So the expected number of sentient beings affected by an ordinary action is in the hundreds of thousands or millions — far greater than the number of human beings affected by anything any of us does.
(5) If the harm to one probably-sentient invertebrate is of the same order as the harm to one human — and (1) appears to put them on the same scale — then the expected welfare stakes of ordinary actions are dominated by invertebrates.
(6) Therefore we are required to put invertebrate welfare above nearly every human interest: no mowing, no driving, no cooking that kills, unless the action prevents a comparable invertebrate death toll; and if invertebrate lives are on balance bad, the same premises instruct us to work for their extinction.
(7) Nor does the reasoning stop there. If any small probability may be multiplied by any large population, then for every p > 0 some n makes the product dominate everything else; bacteria, plants or arbitrary physical systems, at a probability of 10⁻⁴⁰ and a population of 10⁸⁰, would generate obligations outweighing all human concerns, and no action would ever be justified.
(8) My flatmate holds that (6) and (7) are the price of consistency and that anyone who balks has quietly abandoned expected-value reasoning. I think (6) is monstrous, (7) is absurd, and something in (1)–(5) must be broken.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look so plausible when it was written down? If you think the argument is valid and the only real options are giving up one of the premises, tell me which premise is cheapest to lose and what each alternative costs; if you think there is a specific slip — an equivocation, a scope error, a step that is valid only on a reading the premises do not actually license — name it and show what it was concealing. I would particularly like to keep (2) and (3), since I do think there is a real chance that insects feel something and I do not want to buy my way out of the conclusion by pretending the numbers are small or the probability is zero, and I would rather not lose (1) either, because without some way of weighting probabilities I have no idea how to think about moral uncertainty at all. What I most want to know is what becomes of the project of taking insect sentience seriously once the repair has been made: does the concern survive in a weakened form, or does the whole thing collapse? Please answer in British English, and please do not send me to the literature — I want the argument taken apart, not a bibliography.
