I sit on the emergency-powers working group of my state's constitutional revision commission — nine of us, appointed by the legislature, we meet every other Tuesday, and I'm the one who gets stuck drafting the written opinions we vote from. Next meeting we take up Proposal 7, which would add an article to the constitution letting the governor suspend, by proclamation and for 90 days at a time, renewable without any limit by a simple majority of both chambers: the open-meetings law, the competitive-bidding statute, and the rules on shifting money between agency accounts. The trigger is a "threat to public safety or fiscal stability" as the governor determines it. There's no judicial review while a declaration is in force — courts get to look at it only after it lapses. One of my colleagues, Dana, has been reading up on the fall of the Roman Republic and now argues this is exactly how republics die, not by one tyrant grabbing power but by crisis powers quietly becoming routine. She keeps bringing up the Senate's emergency decree against the Gracchi — she says it was advice rather than law, and that's precisely what made it so handy — and Sulla's dictatorship, which unlike the old six-month dictatorship came with no end date. Another member, Rob, thinks the Rome stuff is a lazy doom loop: no judicial review, no free press, and elections that actually turned people out, so what does any of it tell us about a state that has all three? I think Dana's onto something but reaching, and Rob's being smug about it.

The reason I'm not sleeping: back in 2019 we had a flood, and the governor used an existing statute (not the constitution) to suspend bidding for about 14 months. The audit afterward found roughly $90 million in no-bid contracts, a chunk of it to campaign donors, and the part that stuck with me is that the auditors found nobody had broken a rule — because the rules were off. So the legality question doesn't seem to be doing the work people think it's doing, and I don't want to write an opinion that just says "trust the courts."

Can you draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Tuesday? It should land on something concrete — reject it, pass it, or pass it with specific amendments (and give me the actual amendment language, not just "add a sunset") — say what the working group's next step and timeline should be, and engage Dana's Roman argument and Rob's reply seriously enough that each of them would recognize their own position in it, including the strongest thing the losing side has going for it. Skip the reading list; I dont need citations, I need reasoning I can defend from the dais. American English, please.
