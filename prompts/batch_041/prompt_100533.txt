For most of my career I took it for granted that the world is, as physicists like to say, written in the language of mathematics, and I never asked why that should be so. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy of mathematics, and I have arrived at a deflationary reading of Wigner's puzzle that I would like you to stress-test.

I now think that what goes by the name of the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics is really two quite different things that get lumped together. The first is the ordinary fact that mathematics built, or rebuilt, to fit a given domain describes that domain well; since the formalism is shaped by the very data it must fit, this is engineering rather than miracle. The second is what I would call free leverage: a formalism developed for its own sake, or for some unrelated purpose, that later proves to be the exact and predictively fertile language of a domain its inventors never contemplated. I do not deny that free leverage occurs. I claim only that it is rarer than the folklore suggests, and that it requires no pre-established harmony to explain. Two mechanisms suffice: mathematicians have cultivated an aesthetic of generality, symmetry, and invariance that systematically steers the surviving corpus toward domain-agnostic structures, and we remember the hits while quietly discarding the enormous body of pure mathematics that never found a home in physics. On this view the appearance of a single grand mystery is an artifact of a favorable denominator.

What I want from you is a map of this view's exposure. Which cases of free leverage, if any, would resist the aesthetic-plus-selection account, and which of those would be genuinely decisive rather than merely awkward? What does my view predict that the harmony view does not, and where does it predict nothing at all? What would a defender of the stronger thesis concede as counting against it, as opposed to what only its critics allege? And I am aware that a deflationary account of this kind risks sliding into unfalsifiability, since any striking success can be redescribed after the fact as just the sort of generality we should have expected; tell me honestly whether my view has been insulated in that way, and if so, whether that is a fatal defect or an acceptable price. Please rank the considerations from fatal to merely awkward. I should add that I am least moved by appeals to the sheer number of past successes, and most moved by any case in which a mathematics we had good reason to regard as useless or perverse turned out to be indispensable.
