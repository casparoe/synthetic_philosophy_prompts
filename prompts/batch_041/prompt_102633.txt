I am a graduate student in philosophy preparing a seminar presentation on Mary Shelley's Frankenstein, and I want to read the novel through the ethics of care rather than through the rights-and-justice framework that dominates discussions of its ethics. Most treatments of the novel's moral world run through duty, recognition, personhood, or the creature's claim to rights; I am interested in whether the care tradition does something genuinely different or merely relabels those moves. What I want from you is a reading, not a summary: where the care lens brings a scene or a choice into focus that the justice lens flattens, where the novel resists the care reading and the honest critic must concede the resistance, and what the novel shows about care that the theoretical literature does not.

To keep this from becoming a name-dropping exercise, I will state the specific care-ethics ideas I want you to work with, so that we are operating from the same understanding. Noddings treats caring as a relation rather than a virtue and locates its core in engrossment, a receptive attention that displaces the carer's own projects onto the cared-for. Kittay develops the figure of the dependency worker, the person who performs the sustained labor of caring for a dependent, and argues that this labor tends to make the worker's self transparent, her own interests receding behind the dependent's. Tronto divides care into four phases -- attentiveness, responsibility, competence, and responsiveness -- and holds that a failure of care can occur at any one of them. Please use these as instruments for the reading rather than as slogans to be applied.

With those in hand: is Victor a carer who fails at one identifiable phase, or is he something the framework cannot quite name, a creator who undertakes a relation and then refuses to inhabit it? Is the creature a dependency worker in anything but an ironic sense, given that he tends the De Laceys and later demands a companion to care for and be cared for by? Does the novel's ending, with the creature mourning over Victor's corpse, vindicate the idea that care is a relation one cannot simply exit, or does it expose the tradition's inability to say what is owed to the dangerous and the unforgivable? I would like a verdict at the end: does Shelley dramatize something the care tradition has missed, or does the novel's bleakness suggest that care ethics is a philosophy for a world less broken than hers? Stay close to scenes a reader would recognize -- the creation and Victor's flight, the creature observing the De Laceys, the demand for a female companion, the final pursuit -- and avoid long quotation, since I will have the text at hand.
