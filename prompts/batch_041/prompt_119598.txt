I have a job talk in three weeks — thirty minutes plus Q&A, in the evolutionary-behavior area of a psychology department — and I come to it from a philosophy of biology background, which means most of the room will be hearing my framing for the first time. The room is mostly orthodox evolutionary psychologists: they take massive modularity more or less for granted, they reason in terms of what a trait is for, and they have a well-rehearsed set of replies to the spandrels critique that they deploy the moment anyone says the word constraint. Also in the room: two behavioral ecologists from the biology side who sit on the search committee and who care mainly about whether anything I say is testable, one philosopher of biology who is skeptical of everybody, and a comparative-cognition person who thinks this whole argument is a parochial fight among people who study the same two species. The dinner afterward is with the two ecologists, so the Q&A is not the only thing that matters.

My talk argues two things this audience will not like. First, that the spandrels critique has been misread for four decades as a claim about how much of the phenotype is adaptive — a claim about relative frequencies — when its actual content is a claim about the burden of proof: the demand that adaptationist hypotheses be treated as hypotheses, with a null model and a stated error cost, rather than as the default reading of anything that looks designed. On that reading the debate was quietly settled by methodological practice rather than by any biological discovery, and what is still worth arguing about is not how adaptive the phenotype is but what evidence would make a byproduct explanation worse than an adaptation explanation. Second, and more provocatively, that the frequency question is not merely unanswered but ill-posed: adaptationist practice individuates traits by the very functions whose existence it is trying to establish, so any census of adaptations is circular before it starts, and the fact that everyone keeps demanding the census is a symptom of the confusion rather than a way out of it.

I already know the canned responses — the four questions, the charge that I am conflating levels of analysis, the accusation that the critique is sociobiology by other means, the suggestion that the paper's own examples have not held up. I do not need those explained; I need to know how they will land in this particular room and what comes after them. Write me a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so questions I will actually get, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each one phrased the way the person who asks it would phrase it — the modularity people, the two ecologists who will want to know what my alternative actually predicts, and the philosopher who will press on what work constraint and byproduct are doing in my argument. For each, give me a two-minute reply, a note on where that reply is soft, and what I should concede before I am forced to. End with the question I am hoping nobody asks, and the one I should refuse to answer as posed. I do not need citations or a reading list; I need the shape of the exchange.
