I'm working up a short paper on judgment aggregation and I have a draft argument I'd like you to go after. Not a line edit — I want to know whether the argument is actually correct, whether it's interesting, where the weak joints are, and what I'm not seeing. Here's the gist.

Everyone introduces the discursive dilemma with the same three-judge contract case. Three judges, each individually consistent: two think there was a valid contract, two think the defendant did the thing, only one thinks both, so voting issue by issue makes the defendant liable while voting case by case makes him not liable. Then you get the standard impossibility result — you can't simultaneously have universal domain, collective consistency, anonymity, systematicity and responsiveness — and the standard menu of fixes: vote the premises and let the conclusion follow, vote the conclusion and back-fill the premises, or give up on collective consistency and concede the group doesn't really have a view.

My complaint is that this framing buries the thing that does the actual work, which is the order of the vote. Take the same profile — TTT, TFF, FTF over (contract, breach, liable) — and run a sequential procedure where you settle one issue at a time and then restrict later votes to the options consistent with what's already settled. Go contract-first and you get liable. Go liable-first and you get not liable: liable loses the majority, breach wins, and contract is then forced to F by the constraint that liability is breach-and-contract. Same voters, same individual judgments, same logical constraint, different answer. So the group's "judgment" isn't a function of the profile plus a fairness axiom at all; it's a function of the profile plus a sequencing, and sequencing is exactly what nobodys axioms are about. Anonymity and systematicity are silent on it, and responsiveness is silent on it too, since both outcomes are perfectly responsive to the profile, just to different features of it.

If that's right, some things follow. First, the premise/conclusion distinction is a special case of the ordering question rather than an alternative to it: premises-first is one ordering, conclusion-first is another, and the general object is the set of orderings. Second, in a court the ordering is supplied by substantive law — the elements of the offence fix the sequence — which is why the doctrinal paradox surfaces as doctrine about ratio decidendi rather than as a paradox about voting: the majority's reason for deciding is whatever the elements-first ordering throws up, and the precedential force of the case is a by-product of that sequencing, not of anything the judges individually endorsed. Third, in a legislature or committee the ordering is set by whoever holds the agenda, so results normally read as limits on what groups can rationally believe are really limits on how much the chair can get away with. Fourth — and thats the part I'd most like you to attack — I think this makes the collective-rationality literature somewhat misdirected: the group is perfectly consistent under any fixed ordering, so the dilemma isn't that consistency and responsiveness collide, it's that there's no ordering-free notion of the group's view for them to collide in. Which pushes the normative question from "should the group be consistent?" to "who gets to sequence, and what makes a sequencing legitimate?" — a question about procedure and authority rather than about rationality.

I want the strongest objection to the argument; whether it's actually novel or is just the well-known point about agenda manipulation wearing a hat; what the example does and doesn't establish; what I'd have to add to make the argument watertight; and what would have to be true for it to fail outright. For each point, say how confident you are, and flag whichever of the five you're least sure about.
