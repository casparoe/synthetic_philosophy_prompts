Over the past few months I have found myself in a small tenants' collective where the same argument keeps resurfacing. Several of us think that in a one-shot standoff with our landlord each of us should act on the assumption that the others will arrive at the same decision we do — that 'we all hold out' can be rational even though holding out pays only if the others do too, and defecting pays best if they hold out. A friend with an economics background insists this is wishful thinking in formal dress, since my decision does not causally influence anyone else's. I take this to be the dispute that has run for decades under the name of superrationality, and it has the same inert quality: the sides agree about the payoffs and about the causal structure, and still nothing moves.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement, or are the two sides answering different questions under one name — one asking what an agent should do given the causal facts, the other asking what an agent should do given that it is one of several instances of the same reasoning process? My working hypothesis is that the impasse rests on an unstated disagreement about what individuates a decision problem: whether a problem is fixed by the physical situation or by the agent's algorithm and evidence, so that 'the same problem' means something different on each side. If that is right, is superrationality even a claim that can be true or false until that prior question is settled? And if the disagreement is more than verbal, what would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, or a change of question?
