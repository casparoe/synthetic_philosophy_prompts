I need a teaching plan, not an explanation of the topic. In three weeks I am leading a ninety-minute session for a lab meeting of animal-behavior researchers — roughly a dozen graduate students and postdocs who run experiments on insects and fish, none of them with formal training in philosophy of mind. They arrive with two opposed reflexes: that the question of which animals are conscious is a straightforward empirical matter that better experiments will eventually settle, or that it is unanswerable anthropomorphic hand-waving. My aim is not to deliver a verdict but to leave them able to handle the question themselves, with its difficulty intact rather than dissolved.

I want the plan built around two commitments, because they are the parts I find hardest to sequence. First, sentience is to be treated as a cluster of capacities that can come apart — for instance, the capacity for valenced experience, the capacity to integrate information across sensory modalities, and the capacity to locate an experience as one's own — rather than as a single on/off property, so that a creature may score high on one and low on another; the sequence has to make the audience feel why that is a substantive proposal and not a dodge. Second, I want a puzzle planted early that the rest of the session must earn its way around: two systems can display exactly the same behavioral profile — the same discriminations, the same avoidance, the same reports — while differing in whether they are built from recurrent feedback loops or from a purely feedforward cascade; if a theory of consciousness says the recurrent one is conscious and the feedforward one is not, then no behavioral experiment can ever separate them, and if it says both are, it owes an account of why. I do not want the moral of that puzzle stated outright; I want the ordering of the material to make it unavoidable.

So give me the order in which the ideas must come, such that each step is forced by the one before it: what has to be in place before a question like "is a bee conscious?" even has a determinate sense, what depends on what, and where the sequence would break if two of the pieces were swapped. Give me the single concrete case that should carry the whole session — which animal, which behavior, and why it does this job better than the octopus or the dog — and say what that case must have and what it must conspicuously lack in order to do its work. List the misconceptions they will bring and the ones they will form in the room; I am expecting at least "consciousness is intelligence," "no cortex, no experience," "consciousness is all-or-nothing," "we will settle this by locating the right brain area," and "this is just anthropomorphism." For each, tell me exactly where in the sequence to head it off and what to say at that point. Tell me how to check, in the last ten minutes, whether they have understood the shape of the question rather than merely been impressed by it. And tell me what to leave out at this level, and how to say so without leaving them feeling that the interesting part is being withheld.
