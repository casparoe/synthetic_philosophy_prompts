I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar in comparative epistemology, and I would like you to write the handout's centrepiece: a diagnostic decision tree that sorts a reader from a starting commitment to a named position on the pramāṇas, the candidate means of knowledge. The students will already have met the standard inventory — pratyakṣa (perception), anumāna (inference), śabda (testimony, cognition arising from the words of a competent speaker), upamāna (comparison, the source by which a word first learned from a description is applied to a thing newly encountered, as when one is told that a gavaya is like a cow and later judges of some strange animal that it is a gavaya), arthāpatti (postulation, as when, knowing that a man is stout and that he eats nothing by day, one concludes that he eats at night), and anupalabdhi or abhāva (cognition of absence) — and the seminar's organising question is whether that inventory is closed or still open. Two subsidiary questions drive the session: whether testimony is a source in its own right or a disguised form of inference, and whether a source with no speaker behind it can be testimony at all.

The tree should be built from the logical space rather than from a roster of schools: first the branching questions, each phrased as a concrete case or a claim a reader can answer without knowing which position it leads to, with each branch labelled by the answer that selects it; then the actual positions located in that space; then a note on which answer-combinations are unoccupied, with each empty slot diagnosed as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Take it as a standing test that a candidate counts as a distinct source only if there are cases in which it yields knowledge and no combination of the other admitted sources does.

The branching questions must include at least these: whether a source counts only when its cognition is true (yathārtha), or whether a source is one that reliably produces cognition and truth is a further matter; whether the stranger's report that the bridge is out, believed on no independent evidence about the bridge or the stranger, is knowledge from a source distinct from inference; whether the gavaya case is a source of its own; whether the stout man case is a source of its own; whether knowledge that there is no elephant in the courtyard is perception of an absence, inference from a failure to perceive, or a source of its own; whether the list is meant to exclude memory, and if so whether the exclusion rests on a requirement of novelty — that the object of the cognition not already be known — and whether making that requirement explicit re-sorts any leaves; and whether pramāṇa names a real kind at all, or only a pedagogically convenient grouping. You may add further nodes, for instance whether a pramāṇa is the instrument of cognition or the cognition itself, or whether perception has a non-conceptual stage that is itself a source; add them only if they genuinely separate positions.

The leaves must include at least the following, whether or not any school occupies them: a perception-only position; a perception-plus-inference position; a position that admits testimony and stops there; a position that goes on to admit comparison; a position that admits postulation as well; a position that admits absence as well; a position that keeps every classical case but explains them all as inference; a position that keeps the list while replacing the classical warrant with causal reliability; a deflationary position on which the taxonomy is an artifact of pedagogy; and whichever leaf, if any, turns out to be the home of two contemporary cases that the tree must be able to place — (a) a system that answers a question by retrieving and paraphrasing sentences from a large corpus written by unknown authors, where no single identifiable speaker stands behind the answer and the system cannot assess what it retrieves, and (b) a reading taken off an instrument whose reliability the reader is unable to assess.

For each leaf, give the name of the position and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where you are confident which school occupies a leaf, attach the school's name — the widely repeated attributions run from the perception-only position, through perception and inference, through the three-source lists that add testimony, through the four-source list that adds comparison, to the six-source lists that add postulation and absence — but the tree's structure must not depend on those names, and where the fit between a leaf and a school is doubtful you should say so rather than force it. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and say what further question would be needed to separate them. Present the tree as an indented outline, with the surrounding prose kept brief enough that the whole handout could be printed on two sides of a page; aim for eight to eleven branching questions and ten to fourteen leaves.
