I spent thirty-odd years as a physical chemist doing combustion and air-quality modeling, retired three years ago, and have been reading philosophy of science since — slowly, and mostly badly. Last month the old lab group had its annual dinner and we spent two hours argueing about the thing we used to argue about in the corridor: does it matter that the assumptions in our models were false? My line was that I invented numbers for things nobody has ever measured — a mixing rate inside a burner can, a deposition velocity for a particle species that doesn't quite exist — and the model still called the stack concentrations right, so who cares. Then a colleague said this was just Friedman's old move, the one Samuelson nicknamed the F-twist: assumptions don't have to be realistic, only the predictions matter, and the more important the theory the less realistic its assumptions tend to be. He said it like it ended the discussion and it didn't end it for me, because I don't think I ever actually believed it while I was doing the work. So before I read any more of this literature I want the specification first: what does any adequate account of idealization in scientific modeling have to deliver?

Give me the requirements list. Each requirement with the case or consideration behind it, and mark which ones are non-negotiable and which are just what one camp happens to want. Here's the material I want it answerable to, because this is the part I've never seen handled together. (i) Cases where I could in principle undo the lie with a limit and the answer would survive — my mixing-rate fudge: make the mixing rate infinite, the reactor becomes perfectly mixed, everything is solvable, and if you put the finite rate back the answer moves a little but not catastrophically. (ii) Cases where the lie is load-bearing and no such limit exists — an infinite population in a genetics model, a lattice spacing sent to zero, a thermodynamic limit where the phase transition only appears because the system is infinite, zero viscosity in a fluid. Here the result doesn't survive de-idealization, it's an artifact of it, and yet we treat it as knowledge. (iii) A parameter that stands in for everything you left out and is fitted to data — which is how we all actually worked, and which seems like a different animal from a clean limiting-case idealization, though I can't say why. (iv) The awkward fact that when a student finally measured one of my fudged quantities and I put the honest number in, the model got worse at predicting. (v) That I can legitimately ask what would happen with zero friction or zero viscosity when nothing has zero of either. (vi) That two incompatible idealizations of the same system, with incompatible pictures of what's going on inside, can both predict well and both seem to explain. (vii) That students and postdocs learn from these models rather than merely despite them.

Then: flag any pair of requirements you think can't be jointly satisfied — that's the part I most want, since I suspect (ii) and (iv) are in some tension with the whole idea that models explain. And tell me which single requirement does the most work excluding candidates. Keep the weight on the criteria; if there's room, one line each on where the two or three best-known positions land, but no reading list, I'll find the literature myself. Bullets are fine, under 800 words.
