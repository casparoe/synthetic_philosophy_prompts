I write a column on science and ideas, and for a piece on the independence axiom I have been using myself as a subject. Here are my verdicts on several lotteries, with outcomes in dollars. In the first, A pays $1,000,000 for sure; B pays $1,000,000 with probability 0.90, $5,000,000 with probability 0.09, and nothing with probability 0.01. I take A. In the second, C pays $1,000,000 with probability 0.10 and nothing otherwise; D pays $5,000,000 with probability 0.09 and nothing otherwise. I take D. In the third, I am shown the same C and D as a two-stage tree: first a 0.10 chance to proceed to a second stage, otherwise nothing; if I reach the second stage, I choose between $1,000,000 for sure and a lottery with a 0.90 chance of $5,000,000 and a 0.10 chance of nothing. At the second stage I take the sure $1,000,000, which means I would take C over D when the tree is displayed. I am also drawn to a simpler rule: minimize maximum regret against the best available alternative. Please tell me what principle, or small set of principles, captures these verdicts. State each clause precisely, give the verdict that motivates it, say what it predicts for a case I have not listed—say, a three-stage version, or the same lotteries with the first stage described as a coin flip whose outcome I learn only after choosing—and tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and which I should give up. I also want to know whether these verdicts force me to reject the independence axiom itself, or only the assumption that a two-stage lottery can be replaced by its reduced form. Answer in no more than 600 words, and lead with the principle you would endorse.
