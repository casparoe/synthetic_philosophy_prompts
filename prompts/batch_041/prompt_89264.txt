I have recently moved to a city whose everyday conventions are not mine, and a question I had always taken to be idle has become uncomfortably practical: which of the small rules of daily life — how one queues, when one tips, whether one waits at an empty crossing — are genuine moral obligations, and which of them bind a newcomer who has never learned them? I have been asked to lead a sixty-minute session on this for an adult evening class in philosophy. The students are intelligent non-specialists with strong and largely unexamined opinions, and several of them are themselves recent arrivals. I do not want the answer to the question; I want a plan for teaching it.

Set out the order in which the components must be introduced, and why each depends on what precedes it. My own rough sequence is: first, that some requirements are merely conventional; then, that a convention can nevertheless be obligatory; then, and only then, the visitor problem. Tell me whether that order is sound, where it breaks, and what has to be added before the visitor question can even be posed.

I am inclined to build the session on a single case: a visitor who joins a long, patient queue at a bakery and, reaching the counter, does not tip, in a city where tipping is expected and where the queue is a custom rather than a rule. I suspect the case does too much at once, running the locals-and-visitors question together with a different question about what kind of convention each practice is. Say whether it should carry the session; if not, propose the case that should, and specify what it must have and what it must lack.

I want to try out a distinction of my own, and would like the plan either to use it or to tell me where it distorts the material: conventions whose point is coordination — a queue, where any one rule will do provided everyone knows it — against conventions whose point is expression or signalling, such as a tip, which says something about the giver and is illegible to anyone who has not learned to read it. I am not confident that the moral status of the two is the same, and I would like the session to make the students feel the difference.

Then give me the misconceptions the students will bring or form — I expect 'if it is only a convention, it cannot bind anyone' and 'if it is illegal it is immoral, and if it is legal it is permitted' — the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off, and which of them cannot be headed off at all and ought instead to be left standing as a genuine disagreement.

Finish with one question I can put to them at the end that would show whether they have grasped the structure of the problem rather than merely settled on an opinion, and with what I should openly refuse to cover in the time available. Please write your answer in British English.
