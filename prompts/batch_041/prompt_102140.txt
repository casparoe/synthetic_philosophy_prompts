I am a doctoral student in philosophy of cognitive science, and I am second-marking the scripts for a second-year undergraduate module on consciousness. The question below was worth 20 marks. I would be grateful if you would mark the answer that follows, giving a mark out of 20 and the corresponding degree band (first, 2:1, 2:2, and so on), followed by the specific comments I should pass on to the candidate. I want the feedback to be point-by-point — which claims are sound, which are not, and how much each error should cost — rather than a general impression of the script. The marks were allocated roughly as follows: up to 6 for the account of the Cartesian theater and Dennett's objection to it, up to 6 for the multiple drafts model, and up to 8 for the discussion of colour phi.

Question: 'Dennett says that the Cartesian theater is a myth. Explain what he takes the Cartesian theater to be, set out the multiple drafts model that he offers in its place, and assess how that model handles the colour phi phenomenon. In particular, does the evidence from colour phi adjudicate between the readings Dennett labels Orwellian and Stalinesque?'

Answer: The Cartesian theater is Dennett's name for the picture in which the various products of perception and thought are delivered to a single place, at a single time, where an inner witness inspects them. Dennett's principal target is Descartes' substance dualism, in which the immaterial soul surveys the contents of the brain from the pineal gland; his argument is that no such meeting point exists, since there is no region of the brain to which all the relevant signals converge, and that dualism should therefore be rejected. In place of the theater Dennett offers the multiple drafts model. On this account the brain continuously generates many parallel and partial streams of content, which are revised and overwritten as processing continues. There is no inner observer, no final version of a given experience, and no privileged moment at which content crosses a threshold into consciousness. A content counts as conscious, rather, in virtue of being sufficiently distributed and durable to shape later processing and the subject's reports; consciousness is thus a matter of the functional organisation of the drafts, not an additional inner show.

The colour phi phenomenon is the case in which a red spot and then a green spot are presented in succession, and subjects report a single spot that travels across the gap and changes colour in mid-flight. Dennett asks whether the intervening colour is manufactured before the subject becomes aware of it, as in a staged show trial (Stalinesque), or whether the subject is first aware of the earlier colour and the record is subsequently edited, as in Orwell's ministry of truth (Orwellian). He concludes that the Stalinesque reading is the better one, since the brain constructs the intermediate content before it is witnessed; but because the two readings are behaviourally indistinguishable, no experiment can force the choice, and the fact that we are tempted to ask which is true shows how deeply the Cartesian theater picture is embedded in our thinking.
