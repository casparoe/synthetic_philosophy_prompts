For my high school philosophy club I have to write a dialogue between two students who disagree about borderline cases, and I'd like you to draft it. The case they are arguing about is a colour chart: a continuous strip of shades running from unmistakably red to unmistakably orange, with no visible seam anywhere in the middle. The question is whether the word "red" stops applying somewhere along that strip.

One of them is an epistemicist. She holds that there is one particular shade — a single one — at which "red" ceases to be true, that which shade it is is a fact about the meaning of the word, and that no amount of looking, measuring, or asking speakers could ever tell us which shade it is. The other is a supervaluationist. He holds that there is no such shade: "red" is true of some shades and false of others, and simply unsettled about the shades in between, so that "this shade is red" may be neither true nor false even though "this shade is red or it is not" is true.

Neither of them may be a pushover, and each has to answer the other's actual objection rather than slide past it. At a minimum, have the supervaluationist press this: if our use of the word is the only thing that could fix its meaning, and the supposed cutoff is fixed by facts about our use that we cannot survey, what makes it a fact at all, rather than a distinction with no difference it could ever make? Have the epistemicist press this: if "red" is merely unsettled in the middle, then when two speakers disagree about a shade in the middle they are not really disagreeing about anything, which is hard to square with how such arguments feel from the inside. Then let each of them raise a cost the other's view has to pay — for the supervaluationist, that "definitely red" seems to need a boundary of its own, and that his logic has to give up classically valid patterns of inference; for the epistemicist, that a boundary no possible investigation could reveal looks arbitrary rather than discovered. Let one of them concede something small, and let neither win outright.

About 1,000 words, no philosophers' names and no citations, and please don't let them settle on "it just depends how you define the word."
