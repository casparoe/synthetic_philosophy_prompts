Been going round and round on Sen's liberal paradox and I keep being told there are two clean ways out of it, and I'm starting to suspect they're the same way out wearing different hats.

The paradox, roughly: two people, one book, three options -- one of us reads it, the other of us reads it, or nobody reads it. Each person gets to be decisive over the pair that's plainly their own business: if I'd rather read it than have nobody read it, society ranks me-reading above nobody-reading; if you'd rather not read it, society ranks nobody-reading above you-reading. Now add the mild Pareto rule: if we both rank one option above another, so does society. But you'd rather be made to read it than have me read it, and I'd rather you be made to read it than read it myself -- so we both rank you-reading above me-reading, and Pareto puts that in the social ranking as well. Stack those up and the social ranking loops: me-reading beats nobody-reading beats you-reading beats me-reading. Something has to give and I dont want it to be the liberal bit.

Escape one: the problem is the preferences we let in. The loop only closes because we're counting our rankings about the other person's reading. Drop the nosy, other-regarding preferences, keep each person's ranking of their own reading, and the Pareto step has nothing left to push with. Nothing about the framework changes; the input list just gets shorter.

Escape two: the problem is that we treated the preferences as fixed inputs. Make each person's ranking over their own pair conditional on what the other does -- I rank me-reading above nobody-reading only in the world where you aren't reading, and flip it in the world where you are -- and the profile that generates the loop never arises. Nobody's preference gets thrown out, they just get indexed to the rest of the situation.

Here's what I want: compare these two properly. Are they genuinly different, or is conditioning just the exclusion move with extra steps? Does one of them need a substantive story about which preferences are really someone's own business, while the other needs a story about when a conditional ranking counts as the same preference and when it's a different one? And tell me whether escape two even gets a grip on the original case, given that the three options there exclude each other -- there's no world where we're both reading, so what exactly is the ranking being conditioned on?

Then test both against a case I actually care about: someone whose ranking over their own private pair has been shaped by other people's nosiness -- they've internalized the disapproval and now really do prefer not to read. The ranking looks self-regarding on its face, but it wouldn't exist without the interference. Which escape handles that, and does it handle it without turning into a filter on preferences' histories rather than their contents?

Finish by telling me which one you'd defend, what it costs you, and whether there's a minimal version of the paradox that survives both. If there is, build it. If there isn't, say why the two of them together close it off.
