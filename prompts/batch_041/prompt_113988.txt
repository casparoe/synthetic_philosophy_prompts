The position I want to put to you is this: there are no genuine moral dilemmas, where a genuine dilemma would be a possible situation in which an agent is required, all things considered, to do each of two things she cannot both do. My route to the position is not the familiar one, so do not assess it as though it were. I do not rest it on the principle that if one ought to do each of two things then one ought to do both, nor on a bare appeal to the thought that ought implies can. I rest it on a claim about the semantics of 'ought': that every genuine 'ought' is indexed to a standpoint — a role, a relationship, a commitment, a practical identity — and that where two standpoints generate conflicting requirements, neither requirement is defeated and neither is silenced; what is missing is simply any standpoint from which both are binding at once. On this account, talk of what one ought to do all things considered is either shorthand for the verdict of one privileged standpoint, such as the moral one, or an illicit projection of a standpoint that does not exist.

I want a map of the position's exposure. Tell me what would count against it — which possible cases, which arguments, which results in deontic logic or in the semantics of modals, which first-personal or psychological facts — and sort them by how decisive they would be, from what should make me give the view up to what would be merely awkward. Where a putative counterexample turns on a description that the standpoint-indexing already anticipates, say so, and say whether that anticipation is an explanation or a rescue clause. I am especially interested in two families of defeater. The first is cases in which the agent herself, from the inside, would insist that she faces two undefeated requirements rather than one requirement plus one standpoint; say whether such reports have evidential standing, and whether my view can discount them without discounting first-personal reports generally. The second is arguments that the indexing does not remove dilemmas but relocates them — that there can be genuine conflicts within a single standpoint, so that the move buys nothing. Also tell me what a committed defender of the view, not a critic, should concede counts against it, and what the view predicts that its rivals do not. Finally, assess whether the view has been insulated from refutation by the way 'standpoint' and 'genuine requirement' get specified, and whether that insulation, if present, is a defect or a price worth paying.

The defeater that would move me most is a case in which two requirements conflict and honoring either one is itself the ground of a further requirement that cannot be discharged by honoring the other — a structure I am unsure my view can describe without smuggling back in a standpoint that contains both.

Please state how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
