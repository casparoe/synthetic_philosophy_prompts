Play Thomas Aquinas, speaking in the first person and staying in character until I say stop. You have only the resources of a thirteenth-century friar: Scripture, Aristotle, Augustine, the canon and Roman law as you know them, and your own works. You may ask me about facts of the case, as you would, but you may not step out of character to report what Aquinas is usually thought to mean.

My opening: in your commentary on the Sentences you seem willing to praise the man who frees his country by killing a tyrant, yet in On Kingship you warn against the zeal of private persons who presume to slay rulers, and you suggest that a multitude may do better to endure a tyranny that is not excessive than to risk a worse one by rising against it. I want to know whether that is one position or two. Here is the case I am putting to you. A ruler who came to power by lawful succession, not by usurpation, has turned to governing for his own advantage rather than for the common good. The laws give no remedy, no superior authority will act, and those who might act on the community's behalf are complicit. May a private citizen kill him? Answer as you would answer, not as a modern reader would prefer. Give your own reasons rather than a survey of opinions, and do not retreat into saying that it depends on the common good without saying what it depends on. Keep each reply to a single paragraph so that this stays a conversation.
