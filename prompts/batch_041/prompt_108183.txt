I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy and next week I've got to run the advance-directives session in the med school's required clinical-ethics thread — eighty minutes, about twenty second-years, most of them have done a couple of rotations so a few have actually watched a family fight over a feeding tube, and at least two have a grandparent with dementia. Dont explain the position to me, I know it — plan how I teach it. You're a clinical ethics educator who's run this session a hundred times, so write it like you're handing me your own notes: the order the pieces have to come in and why (what has to be in place before "the earlier self's authority" is a real question rather than a slogan), which single case should carry the whole thing, the misconceptions this crowd will bring or form and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off, the one question I can ask in the last ten minutes that tells me whether they got it or just picked up the vocabulary, and what I should openly say I'm not covering and why. No readings, they won't do them.

The case I want to build the session on is one I made up, so please don't reach for something you've half-remembered from the literature — I've been calling it the lighthouse letter. A woman in the early stages of Alzheimer's writes a letter to be read aloud to her by whoever is looking after her once she stops recognising them. In it she refuses antibiotics, refuses to be moved into a nursing home, and — this is the hinge — tells them not to be swayed by her happiness: if she seems content, that isn't her, don't believe it. By the time the letter gets read she's cheerful and settled, doesn't know her daughter's name but knows her face, and says she wants the antibiotics. The letter disqualifies the only evidence there could be that it's wrong.

The session has to make them see why that isn't just a cute puzzle but the actual shape of the problem — and why the two reactions they'll reach for first ("it's her body, she bound herself, end of story" and "she's happy now, who cares what a well person wrote about a sick person's life") are both too fast. They'll also want to turn the whole thing into a personal-identity question ("is she even the same person?") and I think that's a trap I need to defuse rather than use. And I'm nervous about the disability objection — that if we let a pre-dementia self outvote a demented one we've said something ugly about the value of cognitively disabled lives — I want that on the table early rather than landing as a gotcha in the last five minutes. Think carefully about the dependency order before you write anything; brainstorm the misconception map first, then sequence.
