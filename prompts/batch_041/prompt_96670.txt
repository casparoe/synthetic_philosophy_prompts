I have been trying to work out the Molinist account of providence and creaturely freedom, and I have arrived at a dilemma I cannot see how to escape. On that account, God's middle knowledge is his knowledge — logically prior to any decree of his will, and true independently of it — of the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom: for every possible creature, and for every possible set of circumstances in which it might be placed, God knows what that creature would freely choose. Because this knowledge precedes the decree, God surveys the whole field of what creatures would freely do before he decides what to create; and because he also decides which creatures to make and what circumstances to put them in, he can select the combination that yields the outcome he wants. It seems to follow that if God wills that all be saved, he can simply create a world in which all freely come to him. The usual reply is that no such world may be feasible for him to actualize. But I believe I can defeat that reply by staging the creation: God creates a first generation, knows exactly what each of its members would freely do in the circumstances he has assigned them, and so knows what history will result; he then creates a second generation whose circumstances include the free choices of the first; and he repeats the procedure indefinitely. Since the range of possible circumstances is unlimited, and each generation's situation can be fitted to the free choices of the one before it, it seems that God can steer the sequence toward any outcome he chooses, including the salvation of every creature.

If that is right, the feasibility reply collapses, and I am left with the original dilemma. The existence of anyone who is not saved then shows either that God cannot bring about the universal free salvation of his creatures, which limits his power, or that he does not will it, which limits his goodness. Which horn should I take, and why?
