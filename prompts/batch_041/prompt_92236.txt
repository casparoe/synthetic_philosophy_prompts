I host a small interview podcast. My next guest is a philosopher of the social sciences whose recent book argues that the phenomena we habitually bundle under labels such as 'multipolar trap', 'race to the bottom', and 'Moloch' are not one phenomenon but several structurally distinct ones, and that the shared vocabulary does real harm: it advertises a single family of remedies where the structures require different ones, and it makes the situations look more inescapable than they are.

Her taxonomy, as I understand it, distinguishes at least four things. First, escalation dilemmas proper: symmetric arms races and iterated prisoner's dilemmas in which each party would prefer mutual restraint, each has a dominant reason to defect, and the available exits are coordination devices, enforceable agreements, or altered payoffs. Second, commons problems: shared resources with thresholds and congestion effects, where collective depletion occurs even though no single party's choice is decisive. Third, selection traps: cases in which the difficulty lies not in any agent's choice but in the composition of the population of agents, so that more restrained parties are outcompeted and displaced, and in which the word 'trap' is doing selection-theoretic rather than game-theoretic work. Fourth, proxy traps: principal-agent and bureaucratic structures in which everyone optimizes a measurable stand-in for what they actually want, and the stand-in drifts. Her claim is that these share little beyond a mood, and that the mood is what the word 'Moloch' actually names.

I would like eight to twelve interview questions that do more than invite her to restate this. Please make sure the set includes: (i) a question pressing whether the disunity thesis is substantive, since 'different mechanisms differ' is nearly trivially true — what would it take for the unity claim to be false, and might the vocabulary be diagnostic rather than explanatory? (ii) a question about the normative baseline that 'bottom' presupposes, since a race to the bottom requires a metric and a privileged level, and I want to know who sets it and whether her account can do without one; (iii) a question about endogenous preferences — if participants come to want what the race delivers, whether anyone is still worse off, and whether the framing covertly assumes fixed preferences; (iv) a question about where responsibility lands once the mechanisms are disunified, specifically whether the vocabulary relocates blame from persons to structures and so supplies an excuse for individual defection; (v) a question demanding a worked case in which such a race was actually escaped, with the structural story of how, together with a case in which competition pushed standards up rather than down and whether her taxonomy can absorb it; and (vi) a question that forces her to choose between 'one phenomenon with many instances' and 'many phenomena under one word', and to say what evidence or argument would settle the matter.

For each question, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and a short note on what she would have to concede for the question to have landed. She has a habitual reply to the disunity objection — that the vocabulary is diagnostic, that naming a pattern is what allows you to see it — so at least two questions in the set should be ones this reply cannot dispose of. Order the questions so that the pressure builds rather than shuffling topics. Keep every question answerable from her own argument and from reasoning: do not ask her for reading lists, literature, or empirical citations, and avoid definitional or background questions. Finally, tell me which two questions are most likely to produce a genuinely new answer rather than a rehearsed one, and why.
