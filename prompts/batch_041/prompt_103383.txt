I'm building a case-study section for a reading group on epistemic game theory and I'd rather you write the analysis than me, since I keep sliding into the textbook version. The episode is the run on Northern Rock in September 2007 — the first run on a British bank in a century and a half, and a good one for this because it happened in public and in about four days.

Here's the whole story, so we dont have to argue about facts: the bank had grown fast by borrowing short in the wholesale markets and lending long on mortgages; those markets seized up in August 2007; it went to the Bank of England for a liquidity facility; the BBC got the story and ran it on the evening of Thursday 13 September; by Friday morning there were queues outside branches; the queues were on every front page and every news bulletin, so by Friday afternoon everyone could see everyone else queuing; and the Chancellor announced a guarantee of existing deposits the following Monday, which stopped it. That's all I need from you on the history — don't go looking for more, and don't give me a bibliography.

What I want is the case run through the machinery. Two things I want kept apart, because I think the standard telling fudges them: (1) common knowledge of the payoffs, or of the bank's soundness — everyone knows, everyone knows everyone knows, and so on up the hierarchy; and (2) common knowledge of rationality, in the sense the backward-induction literature cares about — everyone knows everyone is rational, everyone knows everyone knows it, and so on. Say what each of those is actually doing in a bank-run story, and whether the run even needs the second one at all, or whether plain best-response-to-beliefs gets you there without it.

Then the piece of kit I want you to introduce and explain as you go, because I don't want to assume the reader already has it: almost common knowledge, i.e. common p-belief — each depositor assigns at least probability p to the run happening, at least p to every other depositor assigning at least p, and so on up the hierarchy. The point of the notion, and this is the bit I find interesting, is that you can be arbitrarily close to common knowledge and still lose the coordination equilibrium. Use it. What does it predict about the threshold at which queuing becomes self-sustaining, and does the Thursday broadcast plus the Friday queue actually map onto it?

Then the part I really care about: where the theory breaks. Things I want taken seriously — a lot of the money left by phone and over the internet rather than through the doors, so why did the visible queue matter so much more than the headline; deposit protection at the time was only partial, so was the run irrational, or was it a correct response to a genuinely uncertain guarantee; the lender-of-last-resort facility was itself public knowledge and that arguably made things worse rather than better; and the run made the bank's position worse, so the belief that the bank was in trouble became partly true as a result of people acting on it — a reflexive loop the standard two-equilibrium story doesn't have a slot for. Also, why that bank and not the ones in comparable trouble — does anything in the machinery do the work of salience, or is that simply outside it?

Finish by running it back the other way: does the episode confirm the coordination account, is it an anomaly the account has to swallow (an insured run, a run in a system with a central bank), or is it a case the theory never anticipated? And say plainly where the theory is silent rather than making it speak. I'd much rather have an honest gap than a forced fit.
