You are a philosopher of science with a specialist's command of the realism debate — the no-miracles argument, the pessimistic meta-induction, entity realism, structural realism, and the "divide et impera" reply to the meta-induction. Please reason carefully and in stages, and begin by brainstorming rather than committing early to an answer. I do not need citations, reading lists, or a survey of the literature; I want your reasoning about the record below.

I am writing a paper on whether any form of selective realism survives the pessimistic meta-induction, or whether selectivity is always hindsight — a rule that only looks principled because we already know which posits happened to survive. My central test case is a senior colleague whose published work and seminar remarks give her a long and explicit track record of verdicts about whether the theoretical posits of past science were really something in the world or merely useful fictions. She has never stated her criterion in a single sentence, which is why I am asking you. Her record, as I have reconstructed it:

- Phlogiston: never a real thing.
- Caloric: never a real thing.
- The luminiferous ether: never a real thing, even though the equations of the wave theory of light survived the transition to relativity.
- Newton's absolute space and absolute time: never real.
- Bohr's stationary orbits: never real — though she grants that the energy levels and the spectral regularities they were introduced to explain are real.
- Atoms: real.
- Electrons: real.
- Mendel's factors, that is, genes: real.
- The electromagnetic field: real.
- Neptune: real; she treats its prediction from the perturbations of Uranus and its subsequent discovery as a model case.
- Vulcan: never real — the planet posited to explain the anomalous perihelion of Mercury.

Three cases have now come up in our seminar, and I need to predict her verdicts on each.

(a) Quarks. Introduced in the 1960s to organize the hadron spectrum and now indispensable to the Standard Model; but they are confined, and no isolated quark has ever been observed or, on the standard theory, ever can be. Everything we know of them comes from scattering experiments, jets, and the symmetries of the theory.

(b) Dark matter. Posited to explain galactic rotation curves and now load-bearing in the standard cosmological model, including its account of the cosmic microwave background; but decades of direct and indirect detection have produced no confirmed signal.

(c) The Higgs field. Posited in the 1960s as the mechanism by which elementary particles acquire mass, with a nonzero value in the vacuum, and now associated with a particle detected at CERN's Large Hadron Collider; the field itself is not something anyone can isolate or manipulate, only infer.

Please do the following, in order.

1. Brainstorm the candidate rules such a philosopher might be running — the no-miracles realist rule, the pessimistic meta-induction, entity realism about manipulable entities, structural realism about retained formalism, the "divide et impera" rule about posits essential to a theory's success, and any further rule of your own that fits at least as well. State each rule precisely and say which of her verdicts it accommodates and which it cannot.
2. Identify which rules survive her record intact, and state the survivors in the form of a criterion I could apply to a new case myself. Point to the individual verdicts that do the discriminating work: which single verdicts eliminate which rules.
3. Predict her verdict on each of the three new cases, attributing each prediction to the rule that generates it.
4. Say which of the three is the sharpest test of the surviving rules, and which rule you would bet she is actually running, with your reasons and a rough sense of your confidence. Where the record underdetermines a verdict, say so, and say what further fact about her would resolve it.
5. Name one further case — real or invented — whose verdict would settle the matter, and explain why the record as it stands cannot settle it.
6. Finally, answer the question my paper turns on: is her pattern a principled selective realism, or hindsight in disguise? If it is hindsight, state exactly what an honest version of her view would have had to commit to in advance.

Be decisive where the evidence allows and explicit where it does not. I am less interested in a catalogue of positions than in what her record actually commits her to.
