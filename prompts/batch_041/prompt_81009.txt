I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist, and the habit I cannot shake is this: when someone invokes a theorem, I want to see its conditions of application written out on the table. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy, and I keep encountering a move for which I have no name. It goes like this. Someone notes that by Gödel's incompleteness theorems, any formal system rich enough to contain arithmetic must leave some truths unprovable within it. From this it is concluded that no rule-governed system — a machine, a legal code, a decision procedure, a scientific method — can ever be complete, and that something non-formal (insight, understanding, conscience) must therefore be doing the real work. What strikes me is that the left-hand side is never checked: no one asks whether the system in question can encode arithmetic, whether it is recursively axiomatizable, whether it has a provability predicate, or what "unprovable within it" would even mean for a statute. And when the gap is pointed out, the claim retreats to something weaker — that every system has limits — which nobody disputes and which no longer supports the conclusion. I have watched the same shape appear elsewhere: indeterminacy borrowed from quantum mechanics to license free will, unpredictability borrowed from chaos theory to license claims about societies, the second law borrowed to declare some project doomed. So I have two questions. First, does the specifically Gödelian version of the move have an established name and a body of criticism attached to it, and if so, what precisely is the error, stated as a diagnosis rather than a sneer — as against the uses of undecidability that I am told are legitimate, in formal verification and computability theory, which I would like to see distinguished from it? Second, is there a name, or can you coin one, for the general pattern: a theorem carried out of the domain in which its preconditions hold, with the conclusion then made unfalsifiable by weakening — and how does that differ from an honest analogy, from metaphor, and from the ordinary appeal to authority? Please answer in under five hundred words of continuous prose, leading with the name or the coinage and its definition, then the boundary cases. A bare label will not help me; what I want is the carving. I am not asking for citations, only for the analysis.
