I'm teaching the memory unit in a first-year seminar and I want to close it out with Lois Lowry's The Giver. Half the class read it in seventh grade, none of them have thought about it since, so it's a good test case -- familiar enough that nobody needs selling on the plot, unfamiliar enough that nobody walks in with a canned take.

I gave them a handout with a few distinctions I want the reading to actually use. First, living or communicative memory -- the past as a community carries it informally, in stories and habits and jokes, usually not more than a few generations deep -- versus cultural or archival memory, the past as stored in records and institutions, which can outlive the community that made it and needs nobody to be remembering it. Then, inside the archive, the split between what a community keeps in active view and treats as binding, and what's just sitting in storage, unused. Third, a thing an anthropologist friend of mine calls structural amnesia: communities don't simply forget inconvenient ancestors, they quietly retrim the family tree so the past lines up with current politics, and nobody involved experiences it as a lie. And I've thrown in the second of Nietzsche's Untimely Meditations, where forgetting is treated as closer to digestion -- a capacity you need in order to act at all, not a defect.

What I want from you is a reading of the novel through all that. The setup is already strange: in Jonas's community the past hasn't been lost, it's been confiscated and concentrated in one person. Everyone else lives in a present tense with no depth, which is exactly why the elders have to go to the Receiver whenever they hit a decision the community has no memory for. Jonas's training is a course in what it would be to have a history again -- snow and a sled and sunburn first, then hunger and war, then the video of his own father injecting the smaller twin. And the plan he and the Giver land on, him running so the memories go back into the community, is a claim about what a community owes itself.

The question my handout keeps circling is the one I want you to answer: when a community's lived memory and the record come apart, which gives way? The Giver is a funny case, because the conflict isn't between two rival accounts so much as between a community's narrative of its own past -- Sameness as deliverance from chaos, taught in the schools and not actually false -- and a record that only exists because one man is holding it in his head. So is the novel finally on the side of returning the memories, i.e. an archive that isn't anybody's living memory isn't really memory at all? Or does it give its strongest case against that, given Rosemary -- the apprentice before Jonas, who took her own life, and whose memories, on the Giver's account, spilled back into the community? And what do I do with the ending, the sled, the fact that the book won't tell me whether he and Gabriel make it? If the whole moral hangs on the memories getting back to the community and the book won't say whether they do, does that undercut the reading, or is it the point?

I also want the honest edges. Nietzsche and Lowry are natural allies up to a point, since the community's painlessness is roughly the price he says you pay for it, but the community isn't just forgetful, it's engineered, and I dont think the book is finally Nietzschean -- tell me where that comparison breaks. And give the Chief Elder her due: her claim that the community chose this, that they gained control of a great deal and had to let go of some things in exchange, is an argument, not just villainy, and I want it stated at full strength before you knock it down. Where does the novel resist the memory-versus-history frame entirely?

Be committed. Give me an actual reading with a verdict rather than a seperate menu of options, and if a scene cuts against the line you've taken, say so instead of smoothing it over -- I'd rather bring a reading to class that the students can fight with.
