The notion of a free-floating rationale — a reason that does genuine explanatory work in nature even though nothing in nature entertains it, so that we may say truly that a bird feigns a broken wing in order to draw a predator from its nest while denying that the bird, or anything else, represents that end — has been in circulation in philosophy of biology for decades, and the dispute over whether it is a legitimate notion shows no sign of converging. One party holds that the rationale is merely a compressed report of a causal fact: to cite it is to cite selection history, and there is no reason 'there,' only a cause that reason-talk abbreviates. Another holds that functional and rational attributions are irreducible and genuinely normative — a heart that fails to pump is not merely unusual but defective — and that this normativity belongs to the world rather than to our projections. A third treats the rationales as real but deflated patterns: proper objects of prediction and explanation, without any commitment to a reasoner.

I want a diagnosis of the standoff rather than a verdict on the merits. What kind of disagreement is it? A substantive dispute about what exists in nature; a verbal one about the word 'reason' (or 'function'); a disagreement about what it takes for an explanation to be satisfying; or two camps answering different questions under a single heading — one about the causal history of a trait, the other about its normative standing? Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually claims, and say whether the impasse shows the question to be ill-posed or merely hard.

My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, not a conclusion — is that the deadlock owes less to any disagreement about selection than to an unnoticed slide in 'reason' between an explanatory sense (a consideration that explains why something is so) and a normative sense (a consideration that counts in favour of something and can be flouted). Free-floating rationales are unproblematic in the first sense and paradoxical in the second, and each camp may be defending one sense while hearing the other. Test the hypothesis; if it fails, say where the distinction is benign and the disagreement remains substantive.

Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a decisive case, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected, and why. As a probe, take a setting with no biology and no reasoner in it: a search or evolutionary algorithm that settles on a policy best predicted by a rationale that no part of the system represents and that misdescribes the mechanism actually generating the behaviour. Does the notion of a free-floating rationale earn its keep there, or does that case expose it as a description we have mistaken for a thing?
