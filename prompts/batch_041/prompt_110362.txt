I host a small monthly philosophy podcast — a few hundred listeners, mostly grad students and stubborn amateurs — and next month's guest is a philosopher who's spent years defending what I'd call the gradual replacement thesis: swap out a person's neurons one at a time for functionally identical silicon parts, and at no point does the person stop being the same person; the end product is the same person on different hardware. Her line is that identity is carried by the continuity of the cognitive process and the causal-functional role of the parts, not by the carbon. I want you to take the role of someone who's thought hard about personal identity for a long time and is trying to actually corner her, not just moderate a friendly chat. Think it through carefully before you start writing, and open your answer with a short brainstorm of the real pressure points — including ones that don't have a stock name in the literature — before you turn them into questions.

I know the standard objections (the copy problem, branching, the Sorites worry about the hundredth neuron, biological naturalism about simulation not being duplication) and I don't want an hour of her swatting softballs. Give me eight to ten questions, in a sequence that builds: a couple early on to pin down her view precisely (what exactly has to be preserved — functional profile, causal powers, representational content, the timing of the dynamics?), then the harder ones. For every question, add a note on the answer she's most likely to give plus a follow-up keyed to that answer rather than a generic "but what about...".

Some things I definitely want covered somewhere:

- The chaining worry. If each individual swap is really a copy-and-delete, a tiny teletransportation, then the whole procedure is a very long chain of tiny teletransportations — does the chaining change anything, or just hide the problem? Force her to pick one, and don't let her answer by pointing at how smooth the process is.
- The inside story. Whatever the third-person facts are, what does it look like from the first person — and is there any way to make the first-person verdict evidentially relevent rather than just a report of how it seems?
- The pause. Her view presumably needs the process to be uninterrupted, or maybe it doesn't. If you freeze the half-replaced brain for a week, or run it at a thousandth of speed, does the answer change? Press on whether there's a principled line here or whether continuity is doing unexamined work.
- The original who stays put. Suppose nothing is destroyed — the biological person is still there, awake, watching. What does she think happened? Is she willing to accept one-to-many identity, or that there was no fact of the matter all along? If she goes for no fact of the matter, follow up on whether that's a discovery or a decision, and if a decision, on what grounds we're supposed to make it.
- Whether any of this is really about identity at all, or about what matters / survival / prudential concern. I suspect she'll want to seperate those, so I want a question that makes her say why the seperation is legitimate and not a dodge.

Please avoid jargon where a plain description would do — the audience won't know the terms, and I'd rather the questions be sharp than scholarly. No "what drew you to this topic," no "what would you say to skeptics," and don't ask her to recommend reading, she'll just list her own work. Write it as something I can basically read aloud, with the follow-ups clearly marked.
