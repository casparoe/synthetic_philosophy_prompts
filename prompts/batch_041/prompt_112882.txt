I am a professor of philosophy at a mid-sized public university, where I hold an endowed chair and, for the past fourteen months, have also served as department chair — a job I took reluctantly, and mainly because the one colleague willing to take it would have made everything worse. I mention the office because the question I want to put to you cannot be answered by asking what a private person should do. Whatever I decide here, I decide for forty-one faculty, six staff members, and about ninety graduate students who get no vote in it, and who will live with the consequences long after I have gone back to my seminars.

Here is the situation. A senior colleague of mine — tenured, protected, and by general agreement the reason we have lost three graduate students to other programs in four years — has for a long time treated students with a contempt that stops just short of anything our procedures will recognize. The complaints are all about tone, or about what he meant, or about a seminar he ran in a way that was technically permitted. Three chairs before me concluded that any formal proceeding would fail and would tear the department apart in the attempt, and I have reached the same conclusion on the merits. There is exactly one route that would work. He has, for the past two years, been running an unauthorized independent study for the son of a major donor — a real violation of our rules, though a minor and easily forgiven one. If I put that in front of the dean, framed as one instance of a pattern rather than as the isolated lapse it is, the dean would be compelled to act, and my colleague would be removed from graduate teaching or gone outright. To do this I would have to overstate what I know, and I would have to use a student's confidence against her wishes: she is the one who told me about the arrangement, she trusts me, and she asked me not to do anything that would identify her. I could keep the letter of that promise and break it in substance. I have been turning this over for three weeks with two friends, one of whom is a criminal defense attorney and thinks I am being precious about it.

What makes this genuinely hard is not the tradeoff but the fact that I can no longer tell what kind of act I would be performing. I have taught for twenty years that a person in office who does something wrong for the sake of the people he answers for is still guilty of the wrong — that the guilt is not a fee you pay to make the act permissible, and that if you do not feel it you should not have done it. I have also argued, in print, that institutions should be designed so that no one is ever placed in this position: a system that requires a chair to lie is a defective system, not a noble one, and the honest response to it is reform rather than sacrifice. And I have to admit two facts that I suspect change the analysis rather than merely complicating it. First, I would enjoy this. My colleague has spent four years trying to end my chairmanship, and there is a version of this story in which I am not the reluctant servant of the common good but a man settling a score with a respectable excuse. Second, I have more or less decided that if I do it, I will write to the dean afterward and confess what I did, and resign the chairmanship. I cannot tell whether that plan makes the act better, because I am willing to pay for it, or whether it is a way of buying permission in advance and cashing it in, or both at once.

I want your advice on what to do, and I want you to be willing to tell me that my framing is the problem — if what I have described is not really a case of doing wrong for good reasons but an ordinary abuse of office wearing philosophical clothes, say so plainly and show me where the costume is stitched. I am less interested in a survey of positions than in a verdict I could act on, so please organize your answer as follows, in American English: (1) "The shape of the case," roughly 200 words, saying what kind of problem this actually is and whether my description of it is accurate or self-serving; (2) "The case for keeping my hands clean," roughly 350 words, giving the strongest version of the argument that I should refuse; (3) "The case for doing it," roughly 350 words, giving the strongest version of the argument that I should; (4) "What my two admissions change," roughly 300 words, on the personal enjoyment and the pre-arranged atonement; (5) "My verdict," roughly 250 words, stating what you would advise and what it costs; and (6) "If the verdict is yes," roughly 150 words, on what the honest version of doing it would look like.
