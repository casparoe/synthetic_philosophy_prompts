For a volume I am co-editing on teleology and explanation in biology, a colleague has contributed a short background entry on Dennett's notion of a "free-floating rationale." I would like a careful audit of it before I send her my comments. She writes, roughly, as follows:

"Dennett's idea of a free-floating rationale is that a piece of behavior can have a reason that explains it even though no one ever entertains that reason. The digger wasp returns to her burrow, checks that it is empty, and drags in the paralyzed prey; if the prey is nudged a few inches away while she is inside, she will haul it back to the entrance and begin the inspection over again, however many times one repeats the trick. There is plainly a reason for this sequence — it is the sequence that, in the wasp's ancestral environment, reliably got her offspring provisioned — yet the wasp herself has no grasp of it. Dennett's claim is that the reason is nonetheless genuinely present in the wasp, stored as an unconscious representation that drives the behavior, so that she may be said to act for that reason without knowing it. This is what makes the rationale 'free-floating': it has been detached from the conscious deliberation of a human agent and lodged instead in the organism's subpersonal machinery. He concludes that the difference between a creature that acts for a reason and a mere mechanism is one of degree rather than kind, and that we should adopt the intentional stance toward the wasp — treating her as though she wanted to provision her young — because it is simply true that she has this goal. Critics reply that since nobody entertains these reasons, calling them reasons is at most a manner of speaking, and that since natural selection already accounts for the behavior, the free-floating rationale does no explanatory work at all; the notion is therefore idle and may be dropped."

I have taught this material often enough to suspect that the entry gets the broad contrast right while turning something central inside out, but I cannot put my finger on it, and the closing objection looks to me like a disagreement being dressed up as a refutation. Please go through the entry clause by clause. Tell me what a defender of Dennett's position would accept as fair, what would make him wince as an oversimplification, and what he would say is simply wrong — distinguishing, in particular, claims that misdescribe the notion from claims that merely overstate it. Then say whether the critics' closing argument, as she has stated it, follows from anything accurate in the entry, and whether the free-floating rationale does any work that ordinary evolutionary explanation cannot already do on its own.
