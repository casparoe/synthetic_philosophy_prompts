I am teaching Frankenstein alongside Turing's account of how a machine might come to think, and I want a reading that does justice to both. Turing's proposal is to build a child machine and educate it rather than to program an adult mind; he is confident that a machine raised that way would eventually be hard to distinguish from a person. His imitation game is played under a condition "which prevents the interrogator from seeing or touching the other competitors, or hearing their voices." The creature is the case Turing never considers: adult in body, newborn in mind, educated by no one who knows he exists. He learns language by watching the De Laceys teach Safie, teaches himself to read, and becomes articulate enough to argue his own case. His education succeeds. What destroys him is being seen: the one conversation in which he is treated as a person is with the blind father, and it ends the moment the sighted family returns; later, when he pulls a drowning girl from the water, he is shot for it.

Where does the analogy hold, and where does it break — he is assembled from human remains rather than built, and what he wants is to be loved, not to be undetectable? Is he evidence for Turing's optimism about education or against it? Is Turing's stipulation that the interrogator not see the machine merely methodological, or does the novel show it doing moral work, the test's blindness being the only condition under which a fair hearing was ever possible? And what does the novel supply that Turing's picture lacks: a teacher who stays. Victor abandons his pupil at the first moment, and the creature's one request is for a companion, the need for a peer that Turing's solitary learner, examined by a hidden interrogator, has no place for. Keep to the novel rather than the films, and do not quote it — I want the argument, including where the novel resists it.
