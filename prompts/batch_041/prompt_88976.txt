Write a short story that transposes the structure of middle knowledge into an ordinary, contemporary, non-supernatural setting.

Take the following as given, and do not have any character expound it as theology or attribute it to anyone: on one traditional account of providence, there is a body of truths about what each possible free creature would freely do in each set of circumstances in which it might be placed — truths that hold before anyone decides to place it anywhere. These truths are supposed to be about genuinely free acts (so the one who places the creature neither causes nor coerces the act), to lie outside the placer's control (the creature's own choice is what fixes the answer), and yet to be contingent (the creature could have done otherwise in just those circumstances). What this supposed knowledge buys is a distinctive kind of power: the power to arrange circumstances so that free beings freely do what the arranger wants, without ever compelling them. Two standing objections: first, nothing seems to make such truths true — ground them in the creature and the creature's character is doing the choosing; ground them in the arranger and the arranger is; leave them ungrounded and they are brute; second, such counterfactuals seem to hold only when the circumstances are described thinly enough to be indeterminate or thickly enough that the choice looks settled in advance.

Requirements:

- The arranger must be a human person or institution working by ordinary means — assessment, records, interviews, modelling — and must be fallible and contested. No gods, angels, oracles, prophecy, time travel, deterministic simulations, or predictive machinery that settles the question by fiat. Any instrument in the story must be the kind a real organization could plausibly have. Keep the setting entirely clear of religion and theology: a reader who has never heard of the doctrine should be able to follow the story completely.
- The arranger must never coerce, deceive, or manipulate anyone into a choice; it may only choose the circumstances. This constraint is the engine of the story and must remain visible throughout.
- The plot must turn on a placement decision: the arranger can put a particular person into one of two situations, takes itself to know what that person would freely do in each, and prefers one outcome. Show the decision and its aftermath.
- Give both objections a competent voice — one character who holds that the arrangement leaves the freedom, and therefore the credit or blame, intact, and one who presses that it does not, or that the counterfactual has nothing behind it. Neither may be a fool, a strawman, or a villain, and neither may win by assertion.
- Include one scene in which the description of the circumstances is itself at issue: someone argues that the prediction holds only for an extremely thick description of the situation, and that any thinner description makes it indeterminate or false.
- Do not resolve the case. No revelation that the arranger is a fraud, no vindication, no conversion, no verdict from the narrator.
- Do not name or cite real philosophers, theologians, or texts, and do not let the narrator explain the concept to the reader.

Length: no more than 1,500 words.

Format: the response is the story and nothing else. Begin with the story's first sentence; no title, epigraph, framing note, preface, or commentary before or after.
