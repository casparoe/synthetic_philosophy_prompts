So I've been stuck on this thing for about a month now and my friends are tired of hearing about it, so I'm hoping you can help me get it out of my system. A buddy of mine who reads alot of philosophy told me about this argument — I think the guy's name is Strawson — that nobody has ever really been morally responsible for anything, and the whole thing is weirdly simple. You do what you do because of the way you are. To actually deserve credit or blame for what you do, you'd have to deserve being the way you are. But you didn't pick your own genes, your upbringing, your personality, your character — and even if you had picked it, you'd have had to be a certain sort of person to make that pick, so it just goes backwards forever, and if it ever does stop, it stops at a choice that came out of nowhere, which isn't something you can be praised or blamed for either. My friend says it applies even to God, which kind of broke my brain.

So here's what I want: write me an advice column. Not a real one — a fake one for a small-town newspaper, with an editor's note at the top and then four letters and four replies, maybe 650 words all told. The columnist is an invented woman with a folksy byline who has swallowed this argument whole, and she answers every single letter with it, deadpan, no winking at the reader, no "obviously this is crazy" anywhere in the piece. The letters should be ordinary: a guy who wants to stay furious at his brother who stole from him, a woman eaten up with guilt over a car accident she can't undo, somebody who wants a little credit for donating a kidney to a total stranger, and a small business owner asking how to fire an employee who keeps messing up. Every reply has to stay genuinely consistent with the argument — so she can't give herself a pass either, and if she catches herself resenting her own neighbor partway through the column she has to own that out loud without quietly sliding into compatibilism. The funny has to come from the argument itself: the endless going-backwards, the impossibility of yanking yourself up by your own bootstraps, the way it cancels praise and gratitude just as thoroughly as blame (she should be honest that she's got nothing to give the kidney guy), the bit about God, and the uncomfortable fact that after all of this, the practical stuff — locking people up, keeping an eye on the dangerous ones, saying nice work — carries on exactly as before, just with nothing underneath it. Somebody who's never heard the argument should be able to follow the column, and somebody who's argued about it for years should think she's playing fair instead of knocking down a strawman. American English, please.
