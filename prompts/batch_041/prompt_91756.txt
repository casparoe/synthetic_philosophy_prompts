Over the past few years I have found myself defending a position I first ran into in Buddhist-adjacent corners of the rationalist and effective-altruist blogosphere, and which I have come to call tranquilism: the claim that what ultimately matters in a life is the absence of craving and suffering — not the satisfaction of desire, not the accumulation of positive experience, and not the completion of projects. Everyone grants the modest claim that suffering is bad and ought to be reduced; the distinctive tranquilist claim is stronger, namely that positive goods either reduce to the absence of craving and suffering or do not matter at all, so that a life with no craving and no suffering is the best life, and whatever goods one thinks are forfeited in reaching it were never goods to begin with.

I have been arguing for this with an analogy I keep reaching for and now lean on fairly hard. Craving and suffering, I say, are like an alarm system — more precisely, like a smoke detector whose sensitivity has been cranked up by evolution until it shrieks at the faintest wisp. It is a mechanism that once protected us from genuine threats but now, in conditions it was never tuned for, produces mostly false alarms, and its operating cost (anxiety, dissatisfaction, the endless treadmill of wanting) far exceeds its benefit. If the alarm just is the suffering, and the fires it warns of are rare or imaginary, then silencing it is straightforwardly good, and the ambitions and longings I would lose in silencing it were merely the noise. I am aware that this is adjacent to the wireheading and experience-machine debates, and part of what I want to know is whether the analogy quietly trades on the fact that tranquilism is not hedonism — it promises less craving, not more pleasure — and whether that distinction survives scrutiny.

A friend I argue with insists that the analogy is doing all the work and smuggling in the conclusion. I would like you to audit the analogy rather than adjudicate tranquilism directly. Specifically:

1. Give me the correspondence table. Say what plays the role of the fire, the smoke, the sensor, the alarm noise, the sensitivity setting, the cost-benefit ledger, and the person who decides to disconnect the thing — and flag any place where the target has no clean counterpart, or where a single element of the source has to be split across two elements of the target (for instance, whether craving is the sensor, the noise, or both).

2. Say where the mapping breaks, and distinguish the breaks that matter for the particular conclusion I am using it to support from the ones that are merely picturesque.

3. Say what the analogy quietly imports that the target may lack — for example, a designer with a purpose external to the system, a mind-independent fact about which alarms count as false, and the assumption that the signalling function can be stripped out without disturbing the states it signals.

4. Tell me whether the conclusion I want — that the goods I would lose are noise, and that no craving at all beats well-tuned craving — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy, read literally, supports only the weaker conclusion that the alarm should be recalibrated rather than removed.

5. If a better analogy is available for the tranquilist's claim, sketch it and say what it would let the argument carry that the smoke detector does not.
