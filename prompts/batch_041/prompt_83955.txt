I got into an arguement with a friend in a seminar on rational choice a couple weeks ago and I still haven't been able to settle it in my own head. His claim was that "preference" in the revealed-preference tradition is either a triviality or a con: either it just means "the choice you made", in which case it says nothing, or it pretends to be about a state of the person while being defined entirely by behaviour, in which case it's circular. I don't think thats right, but every time I try to defend the position I end up reciting the weak axiom and sounding like a textbook, which is exactly what I don't want.

What I actually want explained is a narrower thing I keep circling. When someone's choices come out looking inconsistent -- they take A over B when offered together, B over C, but then C over both when all three are on the table, or they pick differently from the same menu depending on how it's described or what they saw a minute earlier -- there seem to be at least three very different ways to respond, and I suspect they're not interchangeable. One is to redescribe the object of preference: what the agent is really ranking isn't the items but items-under-a-description, or bundles that include the act of choosing, or (a version I've only heard gestured at) the menu itself, on the grounds that being offered something can carry information about your situation or about what kind of person you are, so menu-dependence isn't a violation of anything. Another is to keep the framework and say the agent erred, or that the data is noisy, so what you need is a stochastic model of choice rather than a deterministic preference relation. The third is to say the axiom was never a claim about minds at all but a constraint the theorist imposes on a data set in order to summarise it, and that a data set can fail to have a tidy summary without anyone being irrational.

Please explain how those three responses actually relate to each other -- are they rivals, or can you consistently hold all three, and if so what does each do that the others can't? And I especially want the cost of the third one. My suspicion is that welfare economics, the whole business of ranking policies by what people would choose, quietly needs preference to be a fact about the person rather than a convenient way of compressing a data set, but I can't say precisely where the summary-view runs out and the substantive view has to come in. Along the way, say something about why the weak axiom is so weak if what you actually observe is choices from large menus rather than pairwise comparisons, and what has to be added to get a genuine ordering back. I'd rather have the conceptual skeleton than the formal detail. Keep it under 700 words, one continuous argument rather than a bulleted list, and don't tell me who said what or where to read more -- I just want the shape of the thing.
