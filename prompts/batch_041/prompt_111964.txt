I host a small interview podcast about strategy and statecraft. I have no formal training in the field — I'm self-taught, I read what I can get hold of, and I work things out on my own — so I need the questions themselves to carry the analytical weight rather than my framing. Next week's guest is an independent defense analyst and former reserve officer whose long argument I find both compelling and slightly suspicious. His thesis, as I would summarize it, is this: armed neutrality is a commitment problem rather than a capability problem. A small state's deterrent value rests less on what it could do in a war than on the adversary's belief that its population would actually turn out and fight, and the only hard evidence of that belief is a conscription-based reserve spread across the whole society. Professional armies, on his account, are worse than useless for this purpose: they are small enough to be destroyed or decapitated early, and they are politically easy to abandon, so they advertise precisely the weakness a neutral state cannot afford to advertise. He adds two claims I want to press on. First, that the cost of service should be high and widely distributed, and that the real constraint on the model is not the defense budget but the point at which evasion becomes politically mainstream. Second, that this is why the Nordic and Alpine cases matter: Finland trains a large share of each male cohort and Switzerland's electorate has repeatedly refused to abolish the obligation, while Sweden let its system lapse and then brought it back in a much thinner, more selective form.

Please act as an expert in comparative defense policy, civil-military relations, and deterrence theory — someone who knows this material well and can also tell when an argument is doing rhetorical work rather than analytical work. Think carefully before you write, and begin by brainstorming: list the four or five places where this thesis is most vulnerable, and the strongest version of the objection at each, before drafting anything.

Then write me eight to ten interview questions. None of them should let him simply restate the thesis; each should force him to commit to something. For every question, give me in one or two sentences the answer he is most likely to give, and then a follow-up keyed to that answer. Please include at least one question on each of the following, though you can order them however makes for the best conversation:

- How he would measure his own threshold — the point at which evasion becomes politically mainstream — and whether Sweden's history is evidence for his model or against it, given that Sweden's Cold War system reached the great majority of men while the system it brought back in 2017 reaches only a few percent of the age cohort.
- Whether a highly selective draft, one that most people are confident of being exempted from, still emits the signal he says it does, or whether it degrades into a professional army with extra steps and a lottery attached.
- The legal escape hatch: Switzerland offers a longer civilian alternative to military service, and Finland offers a civilian alternative as well. Does an orderly way out hollow out the commitment, or does it strengthen it by making the service of those who stay a genuine choice? Push him on which answer his own theory requires.
- A case that seems to cut against him: a state with a large conscript reserve and a mobilized population that was invaded anyway. Ask what that does to the thesis, and whether there is any answer that does not amount to saving the theory after the fact.
- The alliance question. Ask him to explain, on his own terms, why states with strong conscription traditions nonetheless moved toward collective defense, and whether his thesis predicts that neutral states with broad reserves should not need allies — and, if it does not, whether the thesis can be tested at all.
- Who pays. The obligation falls on the young, and in practice on young men, even where the draft is formally open to both sexes. Ask whether conscription is a tax on the people with the least political power, and how he answers the fairness objection without conceding that a professional force is the more just arrangement.
- The civic argument. He also suggests that service makes citizens, which is a different claim from the one about deterrence. Ask how the two arguments interact, and what becomes of the civic argument in a country where the army belongs to the government rather than to the population.
- What "total defense" adds. Ask whether his model needs the wider apparatus of civil defense, territorial units, and a prepared civilian population, or whether a trained reserve is sufficient on its own.
- Falsification. End with what evidence would make him abandon the commitment thesis, and what he would say if the honest answer is that nothing would.

Constraints: he should not need to name scholars, cite studies, or recommend reading; if a question needs a concrete case, use the ones I have given you or another you are confident about, and say plainly where you are unsure of a detail rather than asserting it. Do not put positions in his mouth beyond the thesis I summarized — where you think he would say something different, mark that as your guess. Keep the questions in the register of a serious but non-academic conversation, and write everything in American English.
