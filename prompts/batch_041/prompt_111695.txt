I run a hardware store and sit as the citizen member of our county heritage commission, which has to approve the wording of every interpretive plaque we install. The item before us next month is a plaque at the old mill site, and it has split us. The mill's owner gave the land for the public library and left money for the school, and he also ran the mill in the 1890s with practices that would be criminal today — children working the floor, wages paid in scrip redeemable only at his own store. The historical society's draft says only that he was "a leading figure of his era." Two commissioners want the plaque to state plainly that he exploited children; two others say we have no business imposing today's morality on a man of his time. The chair has asked me to write the commission's opinion before the vote.

I have no training in history and have been reading on my own. I understand historians have a word for the error of judging the past by the standards of the present — presentism, I think, though I may be using it loosely. What I cannot accept is the opposite rule, that we may describe people only in the terms they accepted themselves; under that rule no plaque could ever say that anything was wrong, and I do not think a commission should be in the business of tidying up the past. So I have devised a test, and I would like you to try to break it. I call it the available-objection test: before we call a past practice wrong, ask whether anyone at the time, with only what was then available to them, actually made that objection. Where someone did — where contemporary voices called the scrip theft — the conduct was already criticizable on its own terms, and our plaque may condemn it without pretending we invented the insight. Where no one said it and no one plausibly could have, the plaque should describe the practice and the standards of the time and let the reader judge.

Please write the opinion I will read aloud at the meeting: a clear recommendation on this plaque, including the exact sentence you would have it carry, and a general rule for our future plaques, with the reasoning. Engage seriously the strongest objection to my test — that in most times and places the people who might have objected were silenced, illiterate, or dependent, so "nobody said it" reveals power rather than morality — and also the opposite objection, from the commissioners who think any contextualizing is an excuse. Say whether a body like ours can ever really escape the standards of its own time, or whether choosing which facts go on a plaque already commits us to present-day values. I need no sources; I need the reasoning. End by telling me what would change your recommendation — what fact about this case, or what argument, would lead you to the other verdict — and where your reasoning is likeliest to fail. Keep it under 850 words.
