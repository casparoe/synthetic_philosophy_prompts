1. Andy: the PP is basically the only sane response to fat-tailed risk. Ordinary expected-value thinking quietly assumes the loss distribution is thin enough that the mean converges and you can average over outcomes. For stuff like releasing a self-replicating organism into the biosphere, or anything else you can't undo, the tail dominates — the expectation is either undefined or it's set by the worst case — so "we aren't certain it's harmful" stops being a reason to relax and becomes a reason to hold off. He says this is a theorem, not a vibe, and he kept citing that Taleb paper that formalizes it: precaution only bites in the joint fat-tail-plus-systemic-risk case, and they use exactly that to argue for severe limits on GMOs while calling nuclear comparatively manageable.

2. Burdenofproof: two problems. First, "fat-tailed" is doing all the work and you can't tell from the armchair whether a given risk is fat-tailed — it's an empirical claim about a distribution, and without the distribution you can't invoke the theorem. Second, if you do have the fat tail, the conclusion is just maximin, and maximin is famously insensitive to probability: it makes you refuse any gamble with an unbounded downside, which is nearly every interesting action. Also the PP as it's actually written in treaties is a different animal from the theorem — it's a rule about who has to prove what when the evidence is thin, and burden-of-proof rules don't change the underlying probabilities, they just decide who does the work. At one point he quoted the Wingspread version: "When an activity raises threats of harm to human health or the environment, precautionary measures should be taken even if some cause and effect relationships are not fully established scientifically."

3. Andy: fine, but burden-of-proof *is* decision-relevant when the two error costs are asymmetric and one of them is irreversible. Banning a GMO for ten years costs you some yield; releasing it and finding out it spreads costs you an ecosystem, and there's no undo. So the strong PP just reduces to: when the asymmetry of reversibility is lopsided, tilt toward the reversible option. The "you'd never get out of bed" thing is a cheap shot, because getting out of bed isn't irreversible.

4. Burdenofproof: "irreversible" is a matter of degree and everything's irreversible if you squint — you never get back the decade you spent *not* deploying the thing. And there's the risk-risk tradeoff: German precaution against nuclear plausibly killed people via coal and gas; precaution against Golden Rice plausibly left vitamin A in the deficiency column. So the "inaction" arm isn't a zero-risk baseline, and if you don't count those deaths you're just not doing the arithmetic. Deeper point: you can't be precautionary about everything, so the PP is either empty or it's a rubber stamp for whatever the regulator already feared. Notice it's always the people currently in charge who get to decide what counts as the scary thing.

5. Andy: the risk-risk point is real but it's an argument for doing the PP carefully, not for dropping it, and the rubber-stamp point assumes symmetry when the whole claim is that the harms aren't symmetric. He didn't concede the examples — he said the German number is contested and Golden Rice is doing alot of work — but he granted the general shape. And the reason the PP looks like a rubber stamp is that the treaty versions are stuffed with weasel words, "cost-effective measures," "serious or irreversible," which gut it. Strip those and you get the honest version, roughly: if you can't show it's safe, don't do it at scale.

6. Burdenofproof: that's the version that proves too much, because literally every activity has some nonzero chance of serious irreversible harm, so either it forbids everything or it forbids whatever you happen to dislike — the rubber-stamp objection again, now with better branding. Also, if you really believe the fat-tail story, the move is to go estimate the tails and price them, not to adopt a rule whose whole selling point is that it refuses to look at probabilities. Precaution-as-a-rule is often just a way of dodging the hard epistemic work. And he says there's a selection effect: the cases where precaution looks vindicated (asbestos, CFCs, lead) get remembered, and the ones where it blocked something good don't.

7. Andy: but "go estimate the tails" is exactly what you *can't* do for a one-shot systemic risk with no reference class — that's the whole point of the black swan framing. If the tail is genuinely unforeseeable, the only thing left to condition on is fragility: is this the kind of system that survives being wrong? And he says burdenofproof is smuggling in an omission bias, treating the harm from acting as something you caused and the harm from not acting as just "the world being the world."
