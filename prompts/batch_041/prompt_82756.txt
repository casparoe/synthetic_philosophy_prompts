So I cover politics and ideas for a metro alt-weekly, and I keep bumping into "the will of the people" doing wildly different jobs. Last month our county passed a transit levy with about 58 percent, and at the presser the county exec declined to implement it, saying the vote didn't refelct "the will of the people" -- people had been misled by the campaign, and the real will of the people came through in the listening sessions beforehand. Two weeks later, at a school board meeting, a parent used the exact same phrase to mean count the ballots and stop second-guessing them. I'm writing a piece on how one phrase pulls in so many directions, and what I want from you is a menu, not a lecture.

Lay out the distinct precise things "the will of the people" could be claiming, from the boring end (a majority of those who voted, a majority of adults, a plurality, whatever) up through the loaded ones -- a general will that can be right even when the majority is wrong, a claim about which subset of the population counts as the real people, a counterfactual will about what we'd want if we were better informed or better people, popular sovereignty as the claim that authority flows from the people rather than from any particular outcome or vote. For each reading: is it trivial, substantive and defensible, or actually false? Which reading was the county exec leaning on, and which one was the parent? And which of them, if any, would actually support refusing to implement a measure that passed?

One more thing. Tell me what consideration or argument would most plausibly change your verdicts, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to be shaky -- I'd rather have the disagreement located than smoothed over.
