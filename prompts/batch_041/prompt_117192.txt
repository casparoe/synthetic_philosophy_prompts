Hey, I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so bear with me if I get the vocabulary wrong. I'm writing a book where two of my characters are epistemologists, old friends, and they've got this one fight they've never gotten past, and I want that fight to be intellectually honest instead of the mush I'd write on my own. The technical label, I think, is the conciliationism versus steadfastness debate about peer disagreement. I'd like you to answer as a professional philosopher of epistemology who has spent a lot of time in exactly this literature — and please think carefully and brainstorm out loud for a bit before you commit to anything, because I'm less interested in a tidy answer than in a real diagnosis.

Here's the shape of it as I understand it. Two people are epistemic peers — same evidence, same intelligence, same care, no reason to think either is more likely to have messed up. They disagree. The conciliationist says: if you really do regard them as your equal, you can't just keep your view as is; the fact that someone equally well placed sees it otherwise is itself evidence, and you have no independent reason to think the coin came up heads for you, so split the difference or at least dial yourself back. The steadfast person says: I've got the reasons, and the reasons are what they are; if I trimmed my confidence every time an equally smart person disagreed, the mere fact of disagreement would be pushing me around, and anyway I have to be able to trust my own reasoning or I couldn't reason at all.

What gets me is that the arguments all seem to be on the table, everyone knows them, and nobody moves. So what I actually want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict about who's right. Is this a substantive disagreement, or is part of it verbal — are the two sides even using 'evidence' the same way (does a disagreement count as evidence, or is it just a psychological fact about what two people happen to think)? Are they answering two different questions under one name — the third-person question, what does the evidence support, versus the first-person deliberative question, what am I now to believe — so that each is right about their own question and they're talking past each other? Or is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the heavy lifting underneath?

My working hypothesis, and I want you to push on it rather than just agree: the load-bearing thing is the uniqueness thesis — that there's exactly one rational response to a given body of evidence — and the fact that permissivists deny it. If two people can rationally sit in different places with the same evidence, the conciliationist's symmetry argument loses its grip, and the whole debate is stalled because it's downstream of a question the field hasn't settled. Related hunch, and tell me if I've got it wrong: on the Bayesian version, two agents with different priors can't treat each other's credences as evidence without already agreeing on a likelihood model, which would mean the formal machinery can't even state the problem without assuming away the disagreement. Is that right, or a half-truth?

Two more things, because I want the weird corners. First, a lot of this seems to turn on cases where both people used the same method and got different answers — but what about when they used different methods and got different answers, or when the disagreement is really about whether the method is any good? I suspect those come apart and that the debate quietly slides between them. Second, my novelist's case: what if the peer is a duplicate of me — a copy who shares my whole history and all my evidence right up to last Tuesday, and now holds one different belief? Do I owe a copy of myself the same deference I owe a stranger who's my equal, or is self-disagreement a different animal? And here's the sharper worry: if literally no two people share all their evidence, is the 'peer' in peer disagreement even a coherent category, so that the debate is about an empty set?

So give me your diagnosis, grounded in what each side actually says — verbal, two questions under one name, a hidden formal issue, or bedrock intuition that neither side can argue for — and then, given that diagnosis, tell me honestly what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or nothing, in which case I'll write my characters as permanently stuck and mean it. No bibliography, please — I'm not going to read the papers, I want the shape of the thing. American English.
