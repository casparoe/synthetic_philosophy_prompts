Next Thursday I'm giving the work-in-progress talk in our department and Im dreading the Q&A, so help me prep for it. The paper is on transcendental arguments, and the line I'm defending is that they've been aimed at the wrong target for fifty years: everyone since Stroud has treated them as failed refutations of the external-world skeptic, whereas their real and defensible use is against the revisionary metaphysician -- the person who says beliefs, or enduring objects, or the past, aren't really part of our furniture and we should stop talking as if they are. On my view a transcendental argument never proves that such things exist; it shows that a practice can't coherently disown the commitments it can't help making, which is a claim about what we can't repudiate rather than about what's true. I've been calling these self-exemption arguements. The audience will be: two Kant scholars who know the first Critique inside out and will want to know why this isn't just the second-edition Refutation of Idealism with the metaphysics taken out, a Wittgensteinian who thinks the whole thing is the hinge-proposition stuff in On Certainty done worse, a colleague who works on Stroud and will hit me with the belief-versus-truth gap in the first two minutes, and a philosopher of mind in our department who is an eliminativist about propositional attitudes -- meaning the person my argument is actually aimed at will be sitting in the front row and will get the last question. Write me the Q&A prep sheet: exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no introduction and no closing paragraph, ordered by how much damage they do to me, where each item is one objection phrased the way that particular person would really put it, my two-minute reply, and a note on where the reply is soft. Make the fifth item the question I'm lying awake hoping nobody asks.
