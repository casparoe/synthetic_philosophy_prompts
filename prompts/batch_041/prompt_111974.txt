I'm a third-year philosophy student, and my final essay for a course on philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence is on whether a reinforcement learning agent can suffer. I'd like the essay to turn on a distinction I haven't seen drawn cleanly in the reading. The reward signal is usually treated as the obvious analogue of valence: reward is what the agent wants, so a large negative reward is what it would hate. But in a typical implementation the reward is used without ever being consulted -- it's a scalar that the learning rule consumes, and nothing in the system reads it as information about how things are going; the agent is shaped by it but never looks at it. In other architectures the same information is carried by a state the agent can consult, one that it predicts and reasons with. My question is whether that difference matters: can a signal that regulates behaviour without being represented be pleasant or unpleasant at all, or does valence require that the agent in some sense register the state it is in? Note what each answer costs -- if representation isn't required, valence threatens to become cheap enough to attribute to systems nobody would credit with experience; if it is required, we seem to exclude precisely the states that the standard functionalist story should count.

Write the essay so that it weighs both sides but ends with a defended verdict rather than a survey, and say explicitly what that verdict commits us to about the moral status of the agents we actually build. I'd also like it to tell me which single consideration would be most likely to overturn the verdict, and to point out where in the argument it is most likely to be wrong.
