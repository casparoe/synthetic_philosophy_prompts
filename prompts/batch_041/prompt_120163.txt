I'm putting together a session for a small metaphysics reading group where we pair a novel with a live debate, and this time it's Slaughterhouse-Five against the endurantism/perdurantism question. What I want from you is a reading of the novel that takes both sides seriously, and that doesn't just do the standard move -- Tralfamadorians see all of Billy's life at once, therefore Vonnegut is a B-theorist -- which everyone does and which I think papers over the interesting bits.

Things I'd like it to actually deal with. Billy's entire life is something the Tralfamadorians can look at the way we look at a landscape. That's a claim about time, all times equally real, and it's not the same as a claim about what a persisting thing is: a four-dimensional worm with temporal parts, versus something wholly present at each moment it exists. The novel runs those together and so do most of its readers. So does the book actually need perdurantism, or would eternalism plus enduring objects do the work -- the view where all times exist, only one is present, and nothing has temporal parts (the moving-spotlight picture)? And does the book's form commit it one way or the other: the way it keeps jumping around Billy's life, and the Tralfamadorian novels that are read all at once in clumps rather than in sequence?

Second, inside the perdurantist side, seperate the worm view from the stage view -- the one where the person just is the momentary stage, and the whole life is a convenient fiction built out of causal and psychological links. Billy's passivity, the way he keeps coming loose from his own timeline, his inability to do anything about any of it -- does that fit one of those better? Are we given someone who endures, so that the earlier Billy is the same one who ends up so changed, or a string of stages held together by something outside him, like the narration itself?

Third, the ethics, because this is where I think the book is doing real work. He has a two-word shrug he attaches to every death. Does the metaphysics he's been handed actually license that shrug? Be honest about whether eternalism or perdurantism makes death less bad -- there's a real argument that if all times are equally real then my death isn't a loss, since my life is always there -- or whether that's a slide from a claim about what exists to a claim about what matters. Vonnegut seems to want it both ways: the Dresden chapters are furious, the Tralfamadorian chapters are serene. And say what the novel gets at that a philosophy paper on persistence can't.

Throughout, mark what's settled among people who work on persistence and what's genuinely contested -- dont hand me one side of the worm/stage or the eternalism/endurantism issue as though it were the consensus. Keep the whole thing under 800 words and skip citations and reading lists, I just want the reading.
