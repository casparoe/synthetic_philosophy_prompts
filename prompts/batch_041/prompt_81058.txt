I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas for a mid-size magazine, which means half my week goes to reading philosophy papers and the other half to watching our in-house counsel take a red pen to my sentences. So the lying-vs-misleading question isn't abstract for me. It's the thing that decides whether I write "the study found no effect" or "the study found no effect in the sample tested," and it's also the thing that decides whether we run a correction, a clarification, or an editor's note, which is the three-tier taxonomy we actually use and which I've never once seen explained to a reader.

Here's what I want from you. Take the philosophical debate over whether lying is morally worse than merely misleading — asserting something false versus arranging for someone to believe something false without ever asserting it, with all the awkward cases where the misleading person looks worse than the liar or the liar looks weirdly innocent. Restate that whole debate in the vocabulary I actually use. Not assertion and implicature: substantial truth and the sting of the statement; whether the piece leaves a false impression even when every sentence in it is literally true; the lawyer's question of whether the implication is of and concerning a particular person; the opinion/fact line; false light; actual malice, meaning knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for whether it's false; the privilege some courts grant (and others refuse) for accurately reporting somebody else's false accusation; and the newsroom habit of treating accuracy as a property of each sentence rather than of the piece as a whole. Then give me the residue report. Where the law says the literal truth of your words is no defense if the impression they leave is false, does the lying/misleading distinction survive the trip at all, or does my vocabulary just flatten it into one thing? Where does the moral debate put weight that my vocabulary has no shelf for — the wrong done to the listener's agency, the liar's intent to deceive, the difference between deceiving someone and merely causing them to end up wrong — and is that because a framework built out of textual accuracy plus reputational harm simply can't represent it, or because that part of the philosophers' debate was carrying alot less weight than it looks?

Don't be diplomatic with me about this. And at the end, tell me what would change your mind: if I've mis-stated the philosophical distinction, or if there's a piece of libel doctrine I'm getting wrong or leaning on too hard, say so and tell me how it would flip your verdict — and tell me which step in your own reasoning you'd bet against if you had to pick one.
