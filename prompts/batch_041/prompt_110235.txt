I have been chewing on the story of the digger wasp — the one that drags a paralyzed cricket to its burrow, goes inside to check that all is well, and, if the cricket is nudged a few inches while she is away, drags it back to the entrance and checks again, over and over, as though the loop had no exit. The story is told to make a point about us: if a creature can be locked into a cycle like that, then perhaps our sense of choosing is the same kind of cycle with more steps in it. The argument I keep running into about this has not moved in years, and what interests me is that both sides agree about the wasp. One side treats the loop as a fact about the wasp's machinery — she is rigid, and so are we, only with longer loops. The other treats the rigidity as a fact about our description — at a fine enough grain nothing is rigid, and calling her mechanical tells you about the explanation we picked, not about the wasp.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that this is really two disagreements wearing one word: a question about causes and a question about descriptions, with the word mechanical doing double duty. I would like that tested rather than assumed. So consider a creature I will invent, since the literature I have read does not discuss one: it performs the wasp's exact loop, except that after every third inspection it varies a single step according to the wind. It never escapes the loop, yet the loop is never quite the same twice. Would the first side call that rigid? Would the second? And is there any fact of the matter that could settle which side is right, or only a decision about how finely to describe?

Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, what each side is genuinely committed to once you press it, and what would count as progress — a distinction, a criterion for when a loop counts as mechanical, or a demonstration that the question is ill-posed. If the honest answer is that nothing could move this debate, say so, and say why.
