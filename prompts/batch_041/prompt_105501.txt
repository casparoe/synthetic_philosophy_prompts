For a graduate seminar unit on the epistemology of collective belief, write a short story (roughly 1,200 words, with a title) that dramatizes an information cascade. I want the setting to be a paleontology lab whose members must, one at a time, log a verdict about whether a fossil fragment represents a new species; each student sees only the verdicts already logged and their own private measurement, and the story should make it plain that once the first two verdicts agree, the third student's own contrary measurement is — by her own lights — not worth acting on. Two things matter to me. First, no character should be stupid, timid, or pressured: each person should be reasoning correctly from what she can see, so that if there is an error it belongs to the arrangement rather than to anyone in it. Second, the cascade should be informationally shallow — the story's logic, conveyed through dialogue rather than a lecture, should make clear that everything the lab ends up believing is carried by the first two signals, so that when a technician later re-measures the founding specimen and finds the original reading was a misread decimal place, the whole confident structure is revealed to have rested on almost nothing, and a different pair of first signals would have produced an equally confident opposite consensus. Include a character who suspects the truth early but can see that contradicting the room will cost her something, and let the ending leave the reader genuinely unsure whether the lab's convergence counts as knowledge or merely as agreement. Do not name or define the phenomenon in the text and do not cite any literature; the mechanism should be legible from the story alone. Begin with the story itself, with no preface or commentary.
