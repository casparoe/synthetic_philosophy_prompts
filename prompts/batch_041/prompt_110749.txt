I am a third-year philosophy and cognitive science student currently taking a seminar on practical rationality and decision theory. For my final essay, I need to compare the classical problem of akrasia with the hyperbolic discounting model of intertemporal choice. I do not want a summary of either. The essay should treat them as two competing explanations of a single phenomenon — an agent who judges that some course of action is best and then fails to take it — and ask whether they are actually explaining the same thing.

The comparison should turn on a distinction I want to develop in the essay: synchronic akrasia, where the agent acts against an all-things-considered judgment that is still endorsed at the moment of action, versus diachronic akrasia, where the agent acts against a previously formed intention or resolution. Hyperbolic discounting is naturally suited to the diachronic case: it explains how preferences can reverse as the time of action approaches, so that what was judged best from a distance is no longer preferred when it is imminent. But it is less obvious how it handles the synchronic case, where there need be no change in preferences over time and no earlier self whose resolution is betrayed. Does the hyperbolic discounter have the resources to explain synchronic akrasia, or does the model only capture a temporal inconsistency that is not yet weakness of will in the classical sense? If it cannot capture synchronic akrasia, what is the minimal revision that would let it do so — for example, by allowing a gap between preference and judgment, or by introducing a distinction between local and global evaluations? If it can capture it, what follows for the idea that akrasia requires a divided will or a distinct faculty that is overcome by desire?

Please compare the two accounts on these points. Consider what each must say about the agent's first-person perspective: the hyperbolic discounter looks like someone whose preferences are simply inconsistent across time, while the akratic agent looks like someone who experiences a genuine conflict between what they judge they should do and what they are moved to do. Is that difference merely phenomenological, or does it mark a structural difference between the two explanations? Finally, say which account you think is more illuminating and why, and identify one feature of akrasia that you think neither account can fully capture.
