I'm a novelist — two books, both set in small communities that have curdled from the inside — and I've been given a nine-month research fellowship at a humanities institute, the kind that funds artists to chase a question and expects a written deliverable at the end (eight thousand words or so, plus whatever it feeds into the next novel). I want to spend it pinning down a conceptual question I keep circling, and I need help turning my muddle into a proposal. Here is the muddle. The accounts I've been reading separate two ways a group can end up with a closed picture of the world. One is simple deprivation of information: a bubble, where you never meet the contrary view, and mere exposure tends to fix it. The other is the echo chamber proper, where you do meet the contrary view but have been given reasons to treat every source carrying it as discredited, so exposure entrenches you rather than loosening you. Neither describes what I actually write about. The communities that interest me are not sealed off and do not think outsiders are liars or fools; they read the papers, they are fond of the people who disagree with them, and they will cheerfully grant that the disconfirming evidence is genuine. What they possess instead is a stock of stories — a persecution story, a prodigal-son story, a story about how the faithful are perpetually being tested — into which any inconvenient episode can be slotted and thereby absorbed rather than resisted, and the stories are serial by design, so the present episode is never the ending and the verdict is always still pending. I've been calling this narrative absorption, provisionally, and I genuinely cannot tell whether it is a third mechanism alongside deprivation and discrediting, a species of the second in different clothing, or merely an ornate name for ordinary motivated reasoning.

So: write me the proposal. I want the central question stated so that it has a determinate answer — roughly, whether narrative absorption is a distinct mechanism with its own signature, its own conditions of success, and its own conditions of failure, or whether it reduces to one of the two mechanisms I've described — and I want the case made that the existing accounts leave this open rather than having already settled it in passing. Then tell me how I would actually settle it: what I would do, month by month, across nine months, whether the work is conceptual, empirical, or both, and why that method rather than the obvious alternatives. Be honest about my limits here — I have no training in survey experiments and no appetite for running them, so if the question can only be answered empirically, say so plainly and tell me what the philosophical half of the work still buys me. I would rather be told that the project is misconceived than be flattered into it.

Finally, tell me what a finished result looks like: what I would be able to claim at the end that I cannot claim now, and what would count as the project failing. Include, among the failure conditions, the real possibility that the distinction I am chasing collapses — that narrative absorption turns out to be discrediting under another name, or deprivation seen from a different angle — and say what would make that verdict the correct one. Give me a scope that is realistic for nine months of work by someone with no formal background in the field, and do not bother with citations or a reading list; I will fill those in myself.
