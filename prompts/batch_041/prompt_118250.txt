I teach a small undergraduate seminar on decision theory, and I would like you to design one session of it around a problem I have lately been unable to get out of my own head. For some months now I have been giving a serious share of my income to a cause whose whole case rests on a very small probability of a very large benefit, and at a dinner a colleague pointed out that if I really accept that reasoning I am equally obliged to hand money to any stranger who promises me the world on an event I cannot check. I could not answer him, and I have been going in circles ever since. The session is in three weeks: eighteen students, seventy-five minutes, nothing needed but paper.

I want a game with stakes rather than a discussion prompt, one in which the rules do the work and my framing does none. Give them a budget they must allocate, a printed menu of options with stated probabilities and payoffs, and a scoring rule that, once it is finally applied, shows the option with the smallest probability and the largest payoff to be the one their own arithmetic selects. Include an option whose payoff is infinite, so that the ranking they have been relying on quietly collapses, and include a stage in which a claim they cannot verify has to be taken on faith. They should commit to their choices and only then see the ranking; if they smell it coming, the exercise is dead. Let them find at least one escape route for themselves, and make each escape cost something they can feel, whether that is capping the value of outcomes, ignoring small probabilities outright, refusing to compare, or insisting that probabilities below some bound are not the sort of thing one may act on. I am just as interested in the mirror-image charge, that a principled refusal to be fanatical obliges you to ignore a tiny chance of the best outcome, and I want them to feel its force before they settle which side they are on. By the end they should be convinced that the fanatical conclusion follows from premises they already accept, and that every way of blocking it has a price.

Then the debrief: map each moment of the game onto the corresponding piece of the problem, say what each standard reply actually settles and what it merely postpones, and tell me honestly which parts of this an activity can genuinely test and which it can only gesture at. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which feature of the idea the game cannot reproduce at all and that I will have to supply myself.

I do not need names, dates, or a reading list; I need the moves. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, with no headings, no numbered steps, no bullet points, and no tables. I will be reading it on my phone between classes.
