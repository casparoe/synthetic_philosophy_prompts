I would like you to referee an argument that two friends of mine carried on for most of a long drive while I sat in the back and said nothing. Both are trained in philosophy; neither works on decision theory. By the end I could not tell who had the better of it, or even whether the two of them were disagreeing about the same question. I will give the puzzle they were arguing about, then the exchange as best I can reconstruct it, with the turns numbered.

The puzzle: A traveler is stranded in the desert and will die of thirst unless someone gives him a ride. A driver appears and offers to take him to the nearest town on one condition — the traveler must promise to pay him a hundred dollars upon arrival. The promise cannot be enforced. The driver will drop him off and drive away, the two will never meet or hear of each other again, and no third party will ever learn what the traveler does. The traveler makes the promise, is believed, and gets the ride. On arrival he has the money; paying would cost him a hundred dollars and would change nothing else, since the ride is already given, the driver is unaffected either way, and no one's future conduct toward him depends on what he does.

The exchange:

(1) First friend: He should pay. He gave his word, and a promise is not a device for extracting what you want from someone and then doing as you please once you have it. That the payment is unenforceable and costs him something does not erase the reason the promise gives him; if anything, the situations in which no one can make you keep your word are exactly the ones that show whether you meant it.

(2) Second friend: That is sentiment dressed up as reason. When he is deciding whether to pay, the only question is what paying will bring about. It will make him a hundred dollars poorer and bring about nothing else — the ride is finished and the driver is no worse off either way. So he keeps the money. And this does not make him a liar: at the moment he promised, if he genuinely meant to pay, he said nothing false.

(3) First friend: Then the driver is a fool to take him. If the traveler is rational, the driver can work out that he will not pay, and so should refuse the ride; and the traveler should see this coming. The only way to get the ride is to be someone who really will pay — not to intend to pay just this once, but to have the standing disposition.

(4) Second friend: You cannot manufacture reasons by wanting a disposition. Being the sort of person who pays does not make paying rational at the moment of payment; it makes him the sort of person who reliably does the irrational thing at the end. That he would be glad, beforehand, to have that trait does not make the trait rational. It makes him someone who wants to be irrational, which is not the same as being rational.

(5) First friend: But choosing a character is itself a choice, and in the desert it is made under the highest stakes. He chooses between the sort of person who will pay, who lives, and the sort of person who will not, who is seen through and dies. That the act-level verdict comes out one way and the choice-of-disposition verdict the other does not show the second is illusory; it shows that the act-level verdict is not the whole story.

(6) Second friend: Then notice what the disposition would have to be. It helps only if the driver can detect it, and the driver can detect it only if it is genuine — if the traveler has really become the person who will pay, not merely intends to become one for the length of the ride. So he must actually turn himself into someone who will do, in town, what he will then have no reason to do. If he succeeds, he has arranged to act against his reasons; if he fails, he does not get the ride. Either way, rationality is asking him to acquire a defect.

(7) First friend: That is a paradox only if "rational" must mean the same thing at every level. There is the rationality of the act and the rationality of the policy or the trait. A policy can be rational even when some of the acts it dictates, considered by themselves, are not. The error is treating the act-level verdict as final.

(8) Second friend: Or the error is multiplying senses of "rational" until the awkward conclusion goes away. If paying is irrational, then the one who pays is irrational. Saying the disposition is rational only means it was rational to want to have the disposition — which is entirely compatible with the acts it produces being irrational. You have renamed the problem, not solved it.

I have a vague sense that the whole exchange turns on what exactly rationality is being predicated of — an act, an intention, a disposition, a policy — and that some writers distinguish an act's rationality by what it causes from its rationality as part of a pattern of behavior, while others insist that whatever is rational at the moment of action is all there is. I also have some memory of the phrase "rational irrationality," though I could not tell you what it is meant to name or whether it applies here. My friends raised none of this.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the eight numbered turns, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or right only under an assumption that ought to be made explicit, and justify the rating. Then say who has the better of the argument overall, what each of them is missing, and whether there is a resolution neither of them reached — in particular, whether the answer turns on how we decide what makes an action rational in the first place, and if so, how.
