After four decades in experimental science and a recent, late conversion to the philosophy of mind, I have been reading my way into the dispute over illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, and I am struck less by the arguments themselves than by the fact that the two camps seem no closer after years of exchange. The illusionists hold that phenomenal consciousness — inner felt quality, the what-it-is-like-ness of experience — does not exist; what exists is a set of cognitive and introspective dispositions that produce in us the powerful but mistaken impression that we are acquainted with such qualities, and the proper explanatory target is that impression and its causes. Their opponents hold that the thesis is either false or self-defeating: an impression or seeming of phenomenality is itself a phenomenal state, so the illusionist must help himself to the very thing he denies; or, failing that, he has quietly substituted a different question — why we believe we are conscious — for the original one, which was why and how we are. Each side accuses the other of begging the question, and the accusations have a weary, symmetrical quality that makes me suspect the dispute is not quite what it appears to be.

My own working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that this is not a disagreement about the facts of consciousness but about the standing of a certain kind of evidence and the aim of a certain kind of explanation. In my own field I grew up on deflationary explanations — heat as molecular motion, life as chemistry, water as a compound — and I was taught to regard the disappearance of a supposed entity or force as a mark of explanatory progress. The illusionist invites me to treat phenomenal consciousness the way I treat caloric or a vital force. But I notice that those earlier cases were deflated because the posited entity was introduced to explain something observable and turned out to be idle, whereas the phenomenal realist claims that the thing in question is not an explanatory posit at all but something directly given, and therefore not the kind of thing that can be explained away. If that is the crux, then the disagreement may really be about whether first-person seemings are data that any theory must accommodate, or data that a theory may itself explain and, in explaining, discount.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a verbal dispute about the word 'consciousness'? Two parties answering different questions under one name — the illusionist a question about our judgments, the realist a question about our nature? A clash of bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for, about whether introspection is a faculty that reveals or a faculty that distorts? Or is some hidden empirical or formal question doing the real work, so that the apparent deadlock would dissolve if only we could settle it? I am also curious whether the recent suggestion to set aside the hard problem and attack instead the tractable question of why we are so inclined to believe there is one is a genuine bridge between the camps or merely the illusionist's substitution in a friendlier dress.

Finally, tell me what, given your diagnosis, would actually count as progress: a distinction that recasts the issue, a criterion for when a deflationary explanation is legitimate, an experiment that would decide between the readings, a formal result, or a frank argument that none of these is available and the impasse should simply be accepted. Please engage the specific moves each side makes — the charge of self-defeat, the charge of changing the subject, the appeal to introspective evidence — rather than surveying the literature.
