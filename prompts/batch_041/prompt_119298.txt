I am writing a novel whose climactic scene turns on a rescue: a woman on a flooded causeway can either pull one stranger out of the water or, by spending the same minutes and the same strength, reach a van going under with five people inside it. I am not asking you for a verdict yet. Before I trust any verdict, I want the specification a theory of this choice would have to satisfy — because I have read enough moral philosophy to notice that the familiar camps each buy their answer by refusing to look at part of the case (one says that every person's claim is equal and that the numbers are therefore morally inert; the other says that five deaths are simply worse than one), and I want the whole bill laid out before I put an argument in a character's mouth. Adequate, for my purposes, means good enough that a woman with a minute to decide could act on it and defend the act afterwards.

So give me the desiderata — not a survey of positions, a specification. For each requirement, name the case or consideration that motivates it.

Cases it must get right, at minimum: the plain one against five; the nested case in which option A saves one person and option B saves that same person plus two others, so that nobody is left worse off under B; a case in which the options overlap without either containing the other (one pair against a different pair sharing a single member), so that no one's fate is settled by a larger set simply containing her; a case in which both options save the same number of people but the stakes differ (one will certainly die without rescue, five will lose limbs); a case in which the five can be reached only by a plan that succeeds with a probability, so that expected lives saved can be equal while the certain outcomes are not; a case in which the candidates can speak to one another and propose a lottery among themselves; and a case in which the rescuer's own survival is one of the things at stake.

Distinctions it must not collapse: between the number of claimants and the magnitude of each claim; between a consideration that breaks a tie and one that decides the case; between a claim being weaker and its being outweighed; between what is true of each of the five and what is true of the five together; between the agent's obligation and the claimants' own agreement; and between the ex ante prospect and the ex post outcome.

Constraints it must respect: no trading of one life against a large number of small goods; no verdict that turns on facts the rescuer cannot know; and an answer even when no coin, no time, and no consultation are available, since that is the condition of my scene.

Then tell me which requirement is doing the most work in excluding the two camps I named, and whether any pair of them is jointly unsatisfiable — the pair I suspect is the demand that each person's claim count equally and the demand that the agent save the greater number. If they cannot both hold, say which I should surrender and what the scene loses by it. One line each on how the two camps fare against your list is enough; I will do the arguing in the book.
