I host a small philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends a broadly consequentialist view and argues that the cluelessness objection—roughly, that we cannot know the total downstream consequences of our actions, so the theory cannot guide action—is overstated: on her account we are merely epistemically clueless about distant effects, which threatens our confidence in applying the criterion of rightness but not the criterion itself. Draft eight to ten interview questions that push past a restatement of the objection. I want at least one question each on (a) the distinction between a criterion of rightness and a decision procedure, and whether separating them genuinely rescues the theory from the objection rather than quietly changing the subject; (b) whether the cluelessness at issue is epistemic (we lack information) or evaluative (we lack a grip, even in principle, on how to weigh the distant effects), and why that distinction matters for her defense; and (c) the standard expected-value reply—that we should simply maximize expected value given our evidence—including whether our probability assignments are themselves arbitrary rather than merely imprecise. For each of these, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and close with one question about whether her view collapses into a form of indirect consequentialism that merely reinstates common-sense morality as the operative decision procedure.
