I am a novelist. For four years I have been writing a book about a woman who really lived: in the 1890s she ran a home for foundlings in a river town, was investigated twice by the county board, and was never charged with anything. What survives of her is thin and mostly hostile — a ledger in her own hand, a bundle of letters from people asking her for money, and the transcript of a single interview she gave to an inspector who plainly disliked her. She was not famous. You will not have heard of her, and that is part of my difficulty: there is no public story of her for my book to be measured against, so whatever image my book creates will very likely be the only one most of my readers ever have.

The book is a novel. I have compressed years, invented a sister, and — in the chapter I care most about — put her in a room where no document places her and let her speak in the first person about what she was afraid of and what she did with the fear. None of that chapter is attested. I believe it is true in the way novels are true. I also know that I made it.

Two friends have been arguing about this for a year, and I cannot settle it, and I want their argument written out. One is a documentary filmmaker who has spent her working life in other people's archives; her position, as I understand it, is that a person who cannot answer back has a stronger claim on us than a person who can — that we owe the silent ones more care, not less. The other is a novelist who has never once written about a person who existed; his position is that this is sentimentality dressed as ethics, that the dead hold no property in the meanings of their lives, and that the novel is the only instrument that reaches a life at the depth where it was actually lived. Both are serious, both are better read than I am, and neither is a fool. I lean toward my novelist friend, and I would like that leaning tested rather than confirmed.

Write the dialogue between them, each trying to talk the other out of a position, each yielding where the other lands a real blow. Let them get to these, and let them fight over them rather than merely survey them:

- whether the dead can be wronged at all, when the one wronged must exist to be wronged; and if the harm falls instead on the living — on the few who knew her, or on readers who will carry a false image of a woman they never met — whether that answers the objection or merely moves it;
- whether a novel's refusal to assert anything is a defense, when the book trades on the reader's knowledge that she was real;
- the line my filmmaker friend proposes: invent her inner life if you must, but never give her a deed she did not do. Is that line stable? The interior is precisely what no document reaches and what invention can most freely fill, so the rule may protect the record while leaving the person most exposed;
- whether a false kindness differs from a false accusation. I have made her braver than the record shows in one scene and more cowardly in another, and I do not feel the same about the two, and I want to know whether that asymmetry is defensible or merely squeamish;
- whether the archive is innocent. The papers that survive were written by people with purposes, and the inspector's transcript is a performance in which she had every reason to lie. Fidelity to that may be its own distortion. Press my filmmaker friend here; it cuts against her;
- whether the note I might put in the front matter — a sentence declaring that some scenes are invented — discharges anything, or whether it makes things worse by certifying everything I have not flagged;
- and whether her own words, in that one interview, carry any special authority, or whether the dead person's account is simply one more document among the others.

Some rules for the dialogue. Neither of them should reach for a citation; there is no library in the room, and I would rather hear the arguments than the names attached to them. They should argue about this woman and this book, not about historical fiction in general, though they will have to make general points in order to settle anything about her. Let each of them concede at least one point fully and without hedging. Let them interrupt, press, and follow up on what was actually just said, rather than delivering alternating speeches. And do not force a winner; if the argument ends in a genuine standoff, let it, provided I can see exactly where the standoff lies.

Write it in American English, in prose fit for a reader who is not a philosopher but will notice when one is being faked. Roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words.
