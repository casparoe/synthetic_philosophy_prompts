Putting together the handout for my aesthetics seminar where I have to lay my own cards on the table before we do Tolstoy, and I'd like a sanity check on whether its a stable package before I hand it to people who will enjoy watching it come apart. Here's the list, roughly in the order I'd defend it:

(1) Art is the transmission of feeling. Something is art to the extent that the maker's feeling gets into me. Beauty, pleasure, formal mastery, novelty — none of that is what makes it art.
(2) The marks of the real thing are sincerity, individuality, clarity, and above all infectiousness. If it doesn't spread, it isn't art.
(3) The best art is the art of the highest feelings — the feelings of human brotherhood, the ones you don't need any special upbringing to arrive at. I've started calling these bare feelings: receivable by anyone, no background required. Great art transmits bare feelings.
(4) So the greatest works are the ones the ploughman and the eight-year-old get straight away, and art that only a coterie can catch has been cut down to the size of the coterie.
(5) Verdicts I'll defend in the seminar: I'd take a sincere folk tale over the Ring any day; I think alot of what the concert hall calls its treasures is the upper classes mistaking their own nervous systems for the human one; but I also think there's real greatness in some things almost nobody in my seminar is moved by — the old liturgical chant, say — and that this is a fact about my seminar, not about the chant.
(6) I don't think art is supposed to teach anything. A work that argues its way to a moral conclusion is a tract, not art. But I do think art makes us better, and that this is the whole point of caring about it.

Which of these actually fight each other, which only look like they do, and what's the cheapest repair in each case? Order the real clashes by how much they'd cost me to give in, and then tell me whether one single revision would clean up more than one of them — and what it would be, stated as I'd have to write it in the handout.
