I spent most of Friday evening arguing with a friend who teaches ancient history, and I have come away wanting to settle the question in a classroom rather than in the pub. Her claim was that the Athenians' worst decisions — the near-destruction of Mytilene, the Sicilian expedition, the condemnation of Socrates — were failures of a deliberative machinery rather than of individual character. Mine was that character was doing the real work, and that no institutional arrangement saves a city that produces the men it produced. By closing time neither of us could name anything in the other's position that a room of students could be made to feel, rather than merely nod along to. So I would like you to design the session that decides it.

The setting: an undergraduate seminar I teach on ancient political thought, eighteen students, one hundred minutes, an ordinary room with movable chairs, and one co-facilitator. They will have read nothing in advance; the only text they see is a brief I hand out at the door. They should not be able to identify the historical cases by name until the debrief, and I want no lecture at the start — the point is that they should run into the problem in the doing of it.

What I want them to come away having felt, rather than having been told: that a decision made once, in public, on a single day, by the same body that will also punish, is a different thing from a decision made by a body that can defer, revise, and consult; that the possibility of taking a vote back is itself a procedural accident nobody thinks to guarantee; that admitting a public error is expensive in a way that has nothing to do with the merits; and that a settlement designed to end recrimination can simply relocate it.

For the first part I want an assembly of one day. Give them a city that has revolted and been retaken. The Council has already put a single motion before them — put the adult men to death, sell the rest — and they may amend it but not introduce anything of their own; this agenda control should be visible in the rules. Speeches are capped at ninety seconds, two speakers are pre-assigned to open for and against, and every other participant holds a private interest card that gives them a stake nobody else can see. The vote is by show of hands and is public, except for one half of the room, who vote on folded ballots, so that we can compare afterwards what the two halves were willing to say. Then I, as facilitator, announce that the fleet has already sailed, and allow exactly one motion to reconsider — at a price. Anyone who changes a vote must say so aloud and surrender a standing token; whoever moves reconsideration is exposed to a prosecution, for which I hand a random participant a prosecutor's card and require them to argue that the mover has wronged the people. If the timetable allows, run the whole thing a second time with reconsideration forbidden, so that they can see the branch they were standing on.

For the second part, the same assembly, now pleased with itself, must decide on a great expedition proposed by a charismatic young commander against the warnings of an older cautious one. The rules should do the work: the session cannot be adjourned, the question must be settled today, nobody may speak twice, and any participant who suggests deferring to the commanders' professional judgement is marked with an oligarch card, after which their speech counts for half. Distribute private roles — glory, debt, bribe, fear — and arrange some momentum mechanic so that undecided voters drift towards whoever has spoken most recently.

For the third part, news of a catastrophe. They must now produce a charge against a designated troublesome citizen. Two constraints: the city's reconciliation settlement forbids any mention of past political alignments, so the charge must be one the law still permits, which in practice means impiety or corrupting the young; and the accused, once named, must all be voted on together, in a single vote, so that nobody can separate the guilty from the merely unlucky. One participant is made presiding officer for this vote and must decide whether to put an illegal motion to the room at all, while the room makes clear what it wants. Give everyone a debt-to-the-past card. Some historians read the trial of Socrates as shaped precisely by that amnesty, and I find it plausible, but I do not want to stake the activity on it; build it as a possibility the students can arrive at, not as a fact they are handed.

Then the debrief, which should map each mechanic back to the institution it came from, name the cases, and say plainly which real events I have compressed into one composite. I also want the line drawn forward: publicity and conformity, the single-day plebiscite, the absence of any filter between enthusiasm and commitment, retrospective accountability as the only accountability, and the way a formal settlement can convert vengeance into a different charge rather than ending it.

Please give me the runnable thing: rules, materials and briefs, tokens and payoffs that need no money, a minute-by-minute timeline for the hundred minutes, and the debrief. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it — I expect at least one group to over-act and lose the substance, and I expect the secret-ballot half to spoil the comparison by talking. And be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself: the scale of a real assembly, the real stakes, the religious texture of an impiety charge, and the fact that we are asking students to play at a decision whose cost was tens of thousands of lives. If you think a fourth phase, a different order, or a different number of participants would serve the point better, say so and tell me why.
