I'm a retired fisheries biologist — 68, hung up the waders three years ago — and I've gotten into philosophy the way other people get into golf. There's a reading group that meets in the back room of the pub and next month the topic is moral worth, which means we all have to bring something. I've decided mine should be a parody job advertisement.

Here's the setup, tell me if it works. A company has read Kant properly and taken the idea that only actions done from duty — not from enjoyment, not because they suit you — have moral worth, and applied it completely literally to its hiring. So the ad is for some utterly ordinary job, warehouse or reception, doesn't matter, but every requirement is about why you would do the job rather than whether you can do it. They're only interested in someone who'd do it out of respect for what's right. And of course anyone who sends in an application is doing it because they want the job, which is a want and not a duty, so they're definately in trouble before they start.

Two things I want you to keep straight, because they're the actual meat and I don't want it collapsing into ha ha HR is fake:

- Doing the right thing because you saw it was right is not the same as doing the right thing for some other reason that happens to line up with it — someone who is kind because they want to be liked, say. The ad should have to deal with both kinds of applicant, and it should notice that a motive isn't something you can just look inside yourself and check; and that the moment they start testing for it, everyone filling in the form is acting for the sake of the test.
- If the worth of an action depends on it not being done for a reward, and the job pays, then maybe no morally worthy act is available inside that company at all, and they should end up in a bind they can't advertise their way out of.

Write it deadpan, no winking, as a real posting: bullet-pointed requirements plus a short application form with two or three questions that can't honestly be answered. About 600 words. It gets read aloud to a pub full of people who've read one philosophy book each, so it has to be funny to them and not make the one actual philosophy graduate in the group wince. And it should bite a bit on how real employers now want to know not just what you'd do but why you'd do it.
