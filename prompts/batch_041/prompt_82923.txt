I do a small history-of-ethics podcast (few thousand listeners, half of them grad students) and next month's guest is a historian whose book on how moral revolutions happen comes out in the spring. I've read the draft. Her line is roughly this: slavery and duelling didn't end because anyone won the argument, and they didn't end because the practice stopped paying either. They ended when the people who did the practice could no longer look their own crowd in the eye. Three sub-claims I want the interview to actually get at:

1. She thinks the usual fight between "arguments did it" and "honor did it" is a fake fight, because honor *is* an interest — just not the kind economists mean. On her view honor is the currency the elite's interests are denominated in, so a change in what's honorable is a change in interests, not a change in anyone's beliefs.
2. She has a diagnostic she's definately attached to: before a practice collapses, the rearguard's rhetoric flips from defending it on its merits to defending it as ours — a matter of taste, manliness, "the way things are done here." She says the flip runs about a generation ahead of the collapse, and that you can use it as a leading indicator.
3. She doesn't think argument is useless. Arguments supply the vocabulary for a shame people already feel. The shame comes first.

Write me nine or ten questions to ask her, in the order I should ask them, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give (she's a fast, slightly combative talker and will take the easy exit if I let her). I want at least one that presses on the mechanism — what exactly is the shame attached to, the individual's complicity or the group's standing? — at least one on whether you can tell an honor-shift that caused a collapse from an honor-shift that was a symptom of one, at least one on what an "interest" explanation would even look like if honor just is interest, and at least one on a case where the practice was ended from outside: by a parliament voting against the wishes of the profession, by a colonial administration, by defeat in war. There should be one that goes after claim 2 as a measurement problem — isn't "the rhetoric flipped" just another way of saying they were losing the argument? — one on whether, if shame is the lever, campaigners should stop arguing and start making a practice déclassé, and whether a norm held by status anxiety is one worth having, and one at the end that asks what would make her give the honor thesis up.

Please don't ask her to summarise the book or to place herself in a tradition; I've got an intro for that. And if I've set this up wrong — if "moral revolution" is a category she'd reject, or the argument/honor opposition is something the field moved past years ago and I'm interviewing a straw man — just say so up top in a couple of lines instead of writing me the questions anyway. I'd rather know.
