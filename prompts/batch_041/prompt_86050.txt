I teach the second half of a Kant ethics course (upper-level undergrad) and I'm write the final. I want one essay question, 90 minutes, that cant be answered by reciting the four Groundwork examples — I want them to hit the maxim-specification problem head on.

Here's the case I want to give them. Someone in a meeting grabs a pen off a colleague's desk without asking, planning to put it back afterwards. Two candidate maxims for that same act:

(A) Whenever I need something and can just take it, I'll take it, meaning to return it later.
(B) When I'm stuck in a meeting without a pen, I'll borrow one from a colleague sitting near me and hand it back a few minutes later.

I want the question to force them to run both through the universalizability test and see that A is supposed to fail (universalised, the whole practice of taking-things-and-returning-them comes apart, so the maxim's purpose is defeated) while B sails through — and then to face the obvious objection that the test is rigged by the wording: generalise and you get a contradiction, narrow it and you don't, so the verdict is whatever you describe your way into.

Please write:

- the exam question itself, framed so students have to adjudicate rather than just report the problem;
- a model answer of around 700 words that does the A-vs-B run-through, says which kind of contradiction A is meant to generate and why B's case is different, gives the strongest version of the rigged objection, and then the best Kantian reply — I want them to lean on the point that a maxim has to state the agent's actual principle including the incentive its done from, and to take a stand on whether A's failure is a real contradiction or merely a purpose getting defeated;
- a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves a first-class answer must make and the slips that should cost marks (calling B a contradiction in conception, treating "it just feels wrong" as the test, etc.).
