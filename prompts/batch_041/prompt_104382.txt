Ok so I've been chewing on this for two weeks and I can't find the seam, hoping you can.

I built it for a reading group on the cosmological argument. I skipped the kalam version on purpose, since that one lives or dies on the impossibility of actual infinities and I don't have the stamina for that fight. I wanted the contingency version, and I wanted to run it on the smallest engine I could get away with, so I assumed only the modest principle of sufficient reason: every contingent truth has a sufficient reason, something that explains why it's true rather than not. No God in it, no universe, nothing. Call it P1.

Now the part that's eating me. What does "sufficient" have to mean? I think it has to mean enough. If R is a sufficient reason for T, then given R, T couldn't have failed to hold. Because if R left it open whether T or not-T, then R didn't answer "why T rather than not-T" -- it just made T likely, or permissible -- and the original question is still sitting there. Call that P2, the no-slack principle. It honestly just sounds like the meaning of the word.

So:

(1) Every contingent truth has a sufficient reason. [P1]
(2) If R is a sufficient reason for T, then R necessitates T. [P2]
(3) God exists necessarily, and God's nature is necessary. Nothing in God is arbitrary.
(4) The truth that God creates a world containing me -- call it T -- is contingent. That's the standard picture: God could have made a different world, or none at all.
(5) So T has a sufficient reason R, by (1). Given (3), R has to be God's nature together with the reasons he had for decreeing as he did.
(6) By (2), given R, T couldn't have failed to hold. So T is necessary after all.
(7) That contradicts (4). And nothing was special about T -- same reasoning for any truth whatsoever.
(8) So there are no contingent truths. My socks were always going to be these socks. Not "I couldn't have done otherwise," but "there was never an otherwise."

I know some people just accept (8). I can't, and tell me if my reasons are bad ones. First, it makes P1 idle: a principle whose whole job is explaining contingent truths has no work to do in a world that has none. Second, it makes my own argument pointless -- if everything is necessary, I don't need a cosmological argument to get God, he's necessary regardless, so what did I prove?

Someone in the group said the problem is the theology, that I shouldn't have put God in there. I don't think the theology is doing the work, though; I can rerun the same thing with just "there is something rather than nothing" and a necessary being, and I get the same slide.

So: which line do I give up, and what does it cost me? I'd like to keep (1) and (4) -- I need contingency to be real -- and I'd rather not give up (2), since it looks like a truism about what the word means. But I can't keep everything. Tell me which one to drop, why it looked so good in the first place, and what I'm paying for the drop. Then the thing I actually care about: does the fix you recommend still leave the contingency argument able to do its job and land on a necessary being, or is the fix so expensive that what's left is an argument for something much weaker? I'd rather know that then get a pat answer.

Answer in American English please. No citations or reading lists needed -- I just want the diagnosis and the price tags.
