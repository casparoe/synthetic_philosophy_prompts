I teach a second-year political theory unit — 18 students, one 100-minute block for the liberty topic — and every year the same thing happens: they can recite Berlin's two concepts back at me and they can say non-domination in a seminar, and none of it lands. I want an activity where they run into the distinctions with their feet instead of hearing about them. Nothing gets named until the debrief.

What I want them to come away seeing, roughly in this order. (a) That how much you're interfered with and how free you are come apart in both directions: a round where they're bossed around constantly and don't feel especially unfree, and a round where nobody touches them and they do. (b) That the thing doing the work in that second case isnt the severity of the constraint or how predictable it is, but whether the power over them is answerable to them, i.e. forced to track their interests rather than someone's whim. (c) The one I actually care about: a round where their own options are completely untouched, but somebody else is under a master, and they have to commit to a verdict on whether that changes how free *they* are. Most of them will say obviously not. I want the activity to make that answer feel less obvious without rigging it. (d) A positive-liberty round where the binding constraint is one they've put on themselves, or a majority has put on a minority on the minority's behalf, so the question of which self is doing the constraining stops being rhetorical.

Mechanically I'm imagining one village / household / firm that we re-run with the same people in different power positions, three or four rounds of about 15 minutes, with real but small stakes so they can't just talk: something they have to bet on, a scarce resource they're dividing, a ranking of the rounds that costs them if they get it wrong. Please don't give me the master/slave case, they've read it. I want the power-holder benevolent, reliable, and never actually interfering, and I want at least one round where the constraint is harsher in content but comes out of a process they can contest.

So: rules, materials, the stake/payoff scheme (no real money), roles if you need them, a timeline for the 100 minutes, and the debrief that maps each round onto a conception of liberty. In the debrief be straight with me about the two things the activity can't produce and I'll have to supply myself — I think one is that feeling unfree isn't the same as being unfree, and the other is the whole fight over whether freedom is a status we hold together or just a property of my option set, but tell me if I've got that wrong. Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time: I'm expecting at least one student to say this is just a game about power, and at least one to dig in as a hard negative-libertarian who thinks the benevolent boss is obviously fine, and I need to know how to handle both without turning it into a lecture.
