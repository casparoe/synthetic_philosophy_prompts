I am a novelist, and I have been circling a puzzle about language that comes out of my working life rather than out of any theory, and I would like to know whether there is an established name for the configuration I keep meeting.

Three instances, all of them real to me. First, a word from the dialect I grew up speaking: I use it with complete confidence, so do the other speakers of that dialect, we agree about when it applies, and we correct outsiders who use it wrongly — and yet when I ask anyone, myself included, what it means, we can only produce more sentences containing it, each of which would need the word explained in turn. Second, voice, the central term of every writing workshop I have sat in. No two writers define it alike, and the definitions are not merely different but largely incompatible; even so, a room of writers will independently flag the same paragraphs as lacking it, and the agreement is not random, because when challenged the same writers will defend their judgments at length. Third, a character in the book I am writing now: a fluent impostor who deploys the vocabulary of a profession he has never practised, in the right places and the right registers, and neither the other characters nor the reader can tell — nor, so far as I can see, could anything available to me inside the story tell, since he behaves exactly as a practitioner behaves.

What these share is not vagueness, and not disagreement. It is that the vocabulary does its work — sorts cases, supports inference, gets taught, gets enforced — while nothing anywhere anchors it: not in any single speaker's grasp, and not, as far as I can see, in the speakers taken together. I had assumed such a state must be unstable, that a vocabulary with no floor under it would drift or collapse. It does not. It behaves exactly like a vocabulary that means something.

So: is there a name for this? I suspect the nearest established ideas are not the same thing. One says that meaning is held by a few people who genuinely know and is lent out to the rest of us; but in my cases nobody knows, and there is no expert anywhere up the chain. Another concerns terms that fail to pick anything out; but my dialect word picks out something real, and I can tell when someone misapplies it. A third is the familiar worry that something manipulating words might manipulate them without meaning anything; but that seems to be about one mind and whatever goes on inside it, whereas what puzzles me is a public vocabulary that works and is unanchored everywhere at once.

I do not want a bare label. What I want is the carving: whether this is one phenomenon or several; whether it is genuinely distinct from those neighbours or only a social restatement of one of them; what the edges of it are — whether, for instance, a community whose members all defer to one another, with no one at the base of the chain, can be said to mean anything at all; and, if nothing established fits, what you would call it, defined precisely enough that I can say what falls outside.
