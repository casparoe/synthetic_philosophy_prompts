I'm putting together a session for my social epistemology seminar and I need a handout that does something my usual readings don't. Everyone knows the Condorcet jury theorem by now — each voter slightly better than a coin flip, votes independent, majority converges on the truth as the group grows — and everyone also knows the two assumptions are the interesting part. What I want instead is the man rather than the theorem: Condorcet as a thinker, asked about situations he could not possibly have seen. So the task is to project his views forward, not to report them. Reason from what he actually held — his faith that the calculus of probabilities was the right model for sound judgement, his democratic instincts and his enthusiasm for extending the vote and for universal education, his impatience with bodies that let a small number of people settle things for everyone, and, I think, a certain optimism that better information plus bigger numbers equals better decisions — and then tell me what that package commits him to when it collides with the world we've built. Obviously don't pretend he read any of this; I want the inference made in front of me, from his premises to our cases, and I want you to say plainly when the inference is strained.

Here's what I want covered, and I've made the cases concrete on purpose so you can't just float above them. First: a public broadcaster replaces its panel of three experts with forty language models that each answer the same factual question independently and then reports the majority answer — is that the jury theorem's dream or its parody, and what does he notice about whether the models are really independent of one another. Second: a country where nearly everyone is individually well informed but gets their news through the same two recommendation systems, so that their mistakes have become correlated rather than scattered; I suspect this is where he'd have to admit the theorem was never a theorem about people, only about error, but I want your version. Third: a citizens' assembly picked by lot, given three weekends and a stack of briefings, whose recommendation is then either made binding or left advisory — where does he land, and does his enthusiasm for enlarged, more representative deciding bodies cut against his worry about amateur judgement. Fourth: prediction markets where a handful of very confident traders move the price, which is exactly the kind of aggregation he might have liked and exactly the kind of elite capture he might have hated. Give me his verdict on each and the argument behind it, note where his own principles pull against each other, and finish with the single feature of our situation you think would have baffled him most. Don't hedge everything into mush at the end — he was a confident man and I'd rather see a wrong confident reconstruction than a survey of possibilities.
