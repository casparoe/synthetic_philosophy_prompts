For the past several months I have been sitting in on a reading group on self-reference and machine reasoning, and I have found myself stuck on a question that has become unexpectedly personal. A close collaborator is building an agent that periodically rewrites its own decision procedures, and because I am the group's token logician she has asked me, in earnest, whether she needs to worry about the Löbian obstacle. I keep giving her the standard answer -- that an agent which adopts the schema that its own proofs are true, or which tries to certify a successor at least as strong as itself, can be driven to inconsistency, and that this is why the naive schemes for self-modifying agents do not tile -- but I have gradually realized that I cannot say how much of this is a fact about the world and how much is an artifact of how we formalize the agent. So what I want from you is not a survey but an honest credence, with the reasoning laid bare. Take the proposition, which I will call P: there is a genuine barrier, in the sense that no consistent agent can justify from its own resources alone the trust it needs in a successor at least as strong as itself, and this barrier cannot be removed by any weakening of the trust requirement that still leaves the agent able to improve itself. How confident are you in P?

Please decompose that number rather than asserting it. I would like the sub-questions it rests on, each with a rough probability and one line of justification: whether the relevant trust requirement can be weakened -- for instance to trust in the successor's verdicts on some bounded class of situations rather than in its unbounded proofs -- without losing the capacity to improve; whether bounded or approximate notions of provability genuinely dissolve the obstacle or merely relocate it one level up; whether the obstacle depends on modeling the agent as a single consistent, recursively axiomatizable theory extending arithmetic, and whether anything worth calling an agent must be modeled that way; whether tiling -- each generation certifying the next with no infinite ascent in strength -- is even the right formalization of what self-improvement requires; and whatever further terms you think belong. Then tell me how the terms combine, which one the final number is most sensitive to, and what single argument or result would move it the most.

Two friends have staked out opposite positions and I want to know where their disagreement actually lives. One holds that the obstacle evaporates the moment one stops demanding unbounded self-trust: an agent that needs only to trust its successor's verdicts on a bounded set of situations can be built, and what remains is engineering, not logic. The other holds that the obstacle is a symptom of a deeper mistake, namely that any system worth calling an agent is a single consistent theory of itself, whereas real agents are better modeled as open-ended collections of heuristics with no global consistency requirement at all, so the Löbian result is about a formal object no one was ever going to build. Which of my sub-questions is each of them implicitly betting on, and does the difference between them survive once their claims are stated precisely? I am not asking for a reading list or for citations -- I already know the names -- I am asking for your number, the decomposition that produces it, and your best guess at the weakest link in that decomposition.
