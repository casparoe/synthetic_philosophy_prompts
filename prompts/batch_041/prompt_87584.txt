I spent thirty-five years as a field ecologist studying lake and coastal fisheries, and since retiring I have been reading my way through the literature on common-pool resources and public goods. The habit in that literature, as far as I can tell, is to advance a theory and then defend it by pointing at one favorite case — the overgrazed pasture, the centuries-old irrigation cooperative, the collapsing fishery — so that the argument becomes a contest over whose example is more telling. Before I invest in any of it, I want the specification first.

Please write the desiderata: the list of requirements that any adequate theory of when shared resources are sustained and when they are destroyed must meet. I care about one purpose in particular, so let that fix the bar. I want a theory good enough to let me predict, in advance, which of two superficially similar resource systems will degrade and which will endure.

For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it — the phenomenon the theory has to explain, the case it has to get right, the distinction it cannot collapse (say, between a resource that is nonexcludable and one that is merely unowned, or between a good that is nonrivalrous in consumption and one that is rivalrous only past a threshold), or the empirical regularity it cannot contradict.

Then tell me which of these requirements are genuine and which are prejudices of one camp, and flag any pair or subset that I should suspect cannot be satisfied together. If the list itself is a result — because two things everyone demands turn out to be incompatible — that is what I most want to see. And which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the theories currently on offer?

To keep it concrete, make sure the list can handle at least these: a resource that regenerates only while aggregate withdrawal stays below a rate that no single user can observe; a resource that tolerates depletion up to a tipping point and then flips into a degraded state that does not recover on its own; a system whose infrastructure must be maintained jointly, so that maintenance is itself a second-order public good; a resource from which exclusion is technically easy but legally or morally forbidden; and a global commons with no possibility of the face-to-face monitoring and repeated interaction on which the successful local cases seem to depend. If your requirements cannot be met by a single theory that covers all five, say so plainly — I would rather have a map of the trade-offs than a false unification.

I don't need a bibliography or a reading list; I need the criteria. A brief scorecard at the end, if you like, but keep the weight on the requirements and the cases behind them.
