I'm a third-year philosophy student, and the course I'm taking on the divine attributes has just finished its unit on omnipotence, which means I now have to write the short paper due at the end of it. The seminar has become stuck on a slogan that gets used as though it settled matters on its own: "God can do anything." One classmate ran it into the standard stone case — if God can do anything, then he can make a stone so heavy that he cannot lift it, and either way there is something he cannot do — and treated the resulting contradiction as a refutation of the doctrine entire. What interested me is that nearly everyone in the room, including those who pushed back, seemed to be operating with a different tacit reading of the sentence: one person evidently meant that no external thing can thwart God's will, another that God's power is the source of every possibility whatsoever, another that God can do whatever is intrinsically possible for a being of that kind, and the professor added the suggestion that the real lesson of the stone case is not that omnipotence is incoherent but that it cannot be combined with immutability, since the scenario seems to require God's powers to differ between the making of the stone and the failing to lift it. The only place I have heard the slogan carry real weight outside a classroom is from my aunt, who says it to mean that no situation is beyond hope — which looks like a different claim again, and I don't want to fold it in carelessly.

What I want, then, is a menu with verdicts attached rather than a single favoured reading: the distinct precise theses the sentence could be expressing, laid out from the trivial to the radical, with a note on which are merely definitional restatements of "very powerful," which are substantive and defensible, and which are actually false or incoherent. For each reading, please say three things. First, what it makes of the stone case — whether the case refutes it outright, is a non-starter under it, or refutes it only in conjunction with some further attribute such as immutability or simplicity — and treat the time-indexing question explicitly, since I want to know whether "can lift" must be relativized to a time or a schedule, and if so whether that is an innocent clarification or a substantive concession about what kind of attribute omnipotence is. Second, what work the reading can and cannot do in the contexts where people actually lean on the slogan: the seminar's use of it to close down a debate about incoherence, my aunt's use of it to deny that any situation is hopeless, and the more traditional uses in worship and trust, where the claim seems to concern less the extent of a power than the absence of any rival to it. Third, what a competing reading would have to give up in order to secure the same results. Where you judge that the slogan is being asked to do more than any one reading can support, say so plainly, and if there is a reading that is obviously false but that you suspect is what most people actually have in mind, I would rather hear that than a diplomatic one.
