I work in the two-person civic engagement office of a city of roughly 210,000, and for four years we have run a deliberative panel — thirty residents drawn by lot, an eleven-week cycle, a paid facilitator, expert witnesses, a written recommendation — on whatever the council decides to hand us. The last cycle nearly broke us. The council asked the panel to rank three pre-screened engineering options for replacing the city's lead service lines; one panelist resigned mid-cycle, saying we were a focus group with a budget; a council member described the panel in a public meeting as democracy by lottery; and the recommendation we finally delivered was adopted, which somehow made things worse, because the council now treats the panel as a device that manufactures legitimacy on demand. I have one year to redesign the process before the next cycle. I read The Public and Its Problems last winter on a friend's recommendation and found the first half clarifying and the second half useless for my purposes, which is roughly where I am: convinced that Dewey has the right account of what we are failing at, and unable to see how to run a Tuesday night meeting on it.

What I want from you is a procedure rather than a defense of deliberation — a staged protocol my office can actually operate, built out of Dewey's experimentalism about inquiry and democracy rather than out of the participation literature. Build it from the following material: inquiry begins with a situation that is indeterminate rather than with a well-formed question, so that the first intellectual move is to convert the situation into a problem rather than to accept the question as given; the phases of reflective thinking he sets out in How We Think, treated not as a five-step checklist but as a cycle that can be re-entered at any point; his insistence that ends are ends-in-view, revised in the course of acting, so that a panel's recommendation terminates a stage of inquiry and not inquiry itself; his account of a public as constituted by those who bear the indirect consequences of transactions, which I expect to be the hardest part to operationalize, since some of the affected are absent from any room we can convene (children, renters who will have moved away, downstream water users, people who will never speak at a public meeting); and his attack on the spectator view of knowledge, which gives me a test I want built in explicitly — if residents are only advising while others decide and act, we are not conducting an inquiry, whatever we call the meeting. The protocol should take the form of questions in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the process stops with a no, and the point at which it must hand the matter to a person or a body because the question has become one of values or of power rather than of inquiry. It has to be usable by a facilitator with no philosophy background, in eleven weeks, by two staff, on a budget the city manager has to sign off on, with the council retaining final authority to ratify or reject.

Then run the protocol on three items from our backlog and show the reasoning at each step: first, the lead service line cycle, where the council has already settled the engineering approach and is asking the panel only to rank options; second, a request to site an overdose prevention centre, where the relevant public is precisely the population least likely to attend our meetings and where a neighbourhood association claims to speak for everyone affected; third, a proposal to spend the panel's discretionary fund on a youth mentorship pilot that we have no way to evaluate inside the cycle. At least one of the three should come out in a way I will not like — I would rather learn now that the protocol tells me to suspend a cycle, or to refuse a charge the council has given us, than discover it in month nine with the funding on the line; where that happens, tell me what I lose with the council and what I can offer them instead. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about what the protocol cannot do at all — including the possibility that Dewey's account is simply too demanding for a body with no power to act on what it concludes.
