I am a novelist, and the book I am finishing turns on a long argument between two psychiatrists. The first is a follower of Thomas Szasz, who holds that what we call mental illness is a myth: that apart from demonstrable disease of the brain, a psychiatric diagnosis names a problem in living or a judgment of deviance, and that the concept survives chiefly because it licenses coercion. She insists, as Szasz did, that she is not against psychiatry but against its coercive uses. The second is a clinician and researcher who holds that psychiatric disorders are real illnesses, identified by their course, heritability, treatment response, and the suffering they cause, whether or not a lesion has yet been located. For years each has been answering a question the other did not ask. I want to write the scene in which they finally sit down and produce the document they should have produced a decade ago, and I would like you to draft it.

Write the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two camps. Its purpose is not to adjudicate between them and not to dramatize their exchange, but to be a text both could sign. Please include the following, in this order.

First, a short preamble fixing the scope of the collaboration and the vocabulary: which terms the document will use and which it will set aside, so that terminological preference does not masquerade as substantive disagreement.

Second, a numbered list of claims both sides accept. These should cover what each side is not asserting, what a claim of disease involves, what the concept of mental illness actually does in medicine, law, and ordinary life, and what both would regard as progress in the dispute. Each claim must be phrased so that neither signatory is conceding anything she would refuse to concede.

Third, a joint classification: conditions both agree are diseases, conditions both agree are not diseases, and the contested middle, with a note on why each contested item is contested. General paresis belongs in the first column, and a diagnosis invented to pathologize runaway slaves belongs in the second; I leave the rest to you.

Fourth, the genuine disagreements, each given twice, once in wording the Szaszian would accept as her own and once in wording the clinician would accept as hers, followed by a neutral description of the difference that both can endorse. Where the myth thesis is ambiguous between the claim that the term fails to refer and the claim that the concept does illegitimate work, require each side to say which reading it is defending.

Fifth, a section on whatever part of the dispute is verbal, and what remains once that part is set aside.

Sixth, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each crux, give a concrete case or line of evidence both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance, and a note on whether the crux is genuinely empirical or is a value disagreement wearing empirical clothes. I want at least the historical case of general paresis; the withdrawal of a diagnosis once applied to homosexuality; cross-cultural variation in what befalls people who hear voices; the heritability and treatment-response findings for schizophrenia and the mood disorders; the way a diagnosis alters how people present themselves, so that the category and its instances change each other; and the question of what a biological marker, if one were found, would and would not show. Add any cruxes I have omitted.

Seventh, the practical stakes: involuntary commitment, capacity to refuse treatment, the insanity defense, and the case of a person with severe, treatment-resistant depression who asks for help dying. State what the two sides agree about who may decide what, and where exactly they part.

Eighth, a closing paragraph in which each side states what it would count as decisive evidence against its own position.

Keep the tone sober and non-polemical, with no rhetorical flourishes, and write so that both signatories would be willing to put their names to the result. Do not include citations, references, reading lists, or appeals to authority; the document should stand on the arguments and cases it names.
