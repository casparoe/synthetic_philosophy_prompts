I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of twenty years, and the habit they've instilled in me is to distrust any theory of consciousness that can't say why the question feels the way it does. So what I want from you is a ranking, not a survey, and I'd rather have your reasoning than a reading list.

Below are six candidate diagnoses of the meta-problem of consciousness — the problem of explaining why we find consciousness so hard to explain, or why we are inclined to think there is a hard problem at all. Treat all six as live hypotheses to be judged on their merits, not as positions to be attributed to particular schools or authors.

1. Introspective misrepresentation. Introspection issues representations of our own mental states that are systematically false in one respect: they present sensory and cognitive states as having intrinsic, immediately given qualities, when those states in fact have only functional and relational properties. The meta-problem reduces to explaining why the introspective system would traffic in such representations.
2. Phenomenal-concept opacity. We possess a class of first-person concepts that can only be deployed from the subject's own perspective and that cannot appear in third-person explanations. Any functional account therefore leaves an explanatory remainder, and we mistake that remainder for a metaphysical one.
3. Bounded metacognitive resolution. The self-monitoring system has limited bandwidth and reports internal states in a coarse, discrete format carrying a "quality" tag. The tag is an artifact of the format, the way a low-resolution readout can seem to carry information the sensor never had.
4. Broadcast self-ascription. The system must broadcast its internal states to other subsystems and to conspecifics, and the broadcast format requires a single self-ascription that no third-person model of the system can substitute for. The felt obviousness of experience is a standing prediction of a channel that is one-way by construction.
5. Recursive self-model opacity. The self-model contains a component that models the modeling relation itself. Since a model cannot represent its own vehicle as a vehicle, only as content, this component yields a representation of experience that is structurally incapable of being replaced by a mechanistic description.
6. First-person-authority semantics. The vocabulary of experience is governed by norms that grant the subject's sincere reports default authority and treat third-person correction as a category error. Functional descriptions are excluded by the semantics of the terms rather than by the facts.

For each of the six, give (a) a rating from 0 to 10 for explanatory power, meaning how much of the target phenomenon it actually accounts for; (b) a rating from 0 to 10 for dissolution power, meaning how much it would make the hard problem stop being a problem rather than merely relocate it; (c) a tier from S down to D for the two combined, weighting them equally unless you state and defend different weights; and (d) one sentence explaining why it lands where it does. If two candidates strike you as the same hypothesis in different clothes, say so, merge them, and rank the merged list.

Then rank the candidates from best to worst, note which one you would bet on if forced to choose, and give a single number between 0 and 1 for your credence that the meta-problem has a purely deflationary solution — that is, that one of these diagnoses is essentially correct and nothing is left over. For your top two, name something that would discriminate between them. Don't hedge by declaring that all six are complementary; if you think they are, say so explicitly and then rank them anyway. Please write in American English.
