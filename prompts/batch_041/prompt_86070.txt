For the past several months I have been reporting a feature on the migration of military vocabulary into ordinary political speech — the way organizers, administrators, and party operatives now describe zoning hearings and curriculum fights as fronts, and elections as engagements in a war that never ended. The bookish version of the claim, which several of my sources cite, and which one of them — a political theorist I have been quoting at length — states far more aggressively than the others, runs like this. Clausewitz held that war is the continuation of politics by other means; Foucault, in the 1976 lectures at the Collège de France published as Society Must Be Defended, proposed the inversion, that politics is the continuation of war by other means, so that law, institutions, and economic arrangements are the residue of past struggles, silently reinscribing a relation of force that no treaty ever settled. My source draws the stronger conclusion: that courts, legislatures, and universities are not neutral fora but the same conflict prosecuted with slower weapons, and therefore that appeals to civility or to neutral procedure are either naive or the winning side's way of asking the losing side to stand down.

I would like to know how much of that the analogy can actually carry, and I would rather have the audit than a verdict. Begin with the correspondence table: element by element, what in the war the image invokes is supposed to map onto what in political and institutional life — the combatants, the front, the battle, the armistice, the victor's law. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and, more usefully, sort the breaks into those that matter for the use my source is making of the analogy and those that are merely decorative. Along the way I want three things pinned down. First, which war is doing the work here: the interstate war Clausewitz had in mind, a civil war, Hobbes's war of all against all, or the later Foucauldian mutation in which a society is said to be defending itself against enemies within? The inversion may be plausible under one of these and nearly empty under another, and I suspect my source has not chosen. Second, which features of the source domain are being imported without argument — that a conflict has two sides with settled interests, that force is its medium, that battles decide matters, that the participants know they are at war — and whether my source's conclusion survives once those are subtracted. Third, what to make of the fact that Foucault put the inversion forward as a hypothesis rather than a finding, and that on his own account the war in question is one that peace conceals rather than one anyone is presently fighting; does that qualification leave the claim intact, or does it weaken it past the point where it can license the conclusion my source needs?

Finally, tell me what the claim would have to look like to be testable in the institutions I actually cover: what I should expect to see if the analogy is doing explanatory work, and what would count against it. If a different image would carry the point better — or if the point does not need an image at all — say so, and say why.
