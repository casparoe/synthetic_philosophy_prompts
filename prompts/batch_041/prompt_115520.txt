For the past four years I have taught logic and set theory at a small liberal arts college, and next month I have to deliver the commencement address: roughly eight minutes, outdoors on the quadrangle, in front of about four hundred graduates, their families and the faculty, of whom perhaps half a dozen will be logicians and almost none of the rest will have thought about mathematics since school. I want to build the whole address around Skolem's paradox — the fact that a first-order theory such as ZF, if it has a model at all, has a countable one, and yet that model will cheerfully affirm that uncountable sets exist, because the bijection which would expose its domain as countable from the outside is not itself an element of the model, and the set it calls uncountable is uncountable only relative to its own membership relation. One of this year's graduates, who took my seminar and is collecting the mathematics prize, asked me in office hours why this is not a flat contradiction. I gave her the standard answer — countability is not absolute, and the missing bijection is the whole story — and she said, quite reasonably, that it sounded like a trick. The address should be my genuine answer to her rather than a reassurance, so please write it.

Two things have to hold. First, the philosophy must be honest. I do not want the paradox wheeled on as a decorative metaphor — some line about there always being more to a life than can be listed — and then quietly abandoned; the conceit and the mathematics have to be answerable to one another. That means taking a view on the dispute between the reading on which the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem exposes a genuine relativity in our talk of the uncountable (Skolem's own inclination, at least on some readings, and the reading that made Zermelo object so fiercely) and the deflationary reading on which it shows only that a first-order axiomatisation cannot pin down an intended interpretation, the paradox being, after all, a first-order phenomenon. The address has to survive whichever line I take: if I go relativist, I risk telling four hundred people that nothing they care about is really larger than a list; if I go deflationary, I owe them something better than the assurance that the whole thing is merely an artefact of logic and needn't worry anyone. Second, the speechcraft has to hold. This is a commencement address and not a lecture: warm, spoken, pitched to the families at least as much as to the graduates, and comprehensible within its first two minutes to somebody who has never heard of a model. Please include no quotations and no citations — nobody in that field is going to write anything down, and I would rather have nothing in the address that I cannot vouch for myself. A little local detail is welcome: the brass band, the heat, the class's gift of a bench, my colleague in the third row who will be checking the set theory. And I would rather it did not end on the usual inventory of platitudes about the future being unwritten.
