I am preparing a proposal for a one-semester graduate research paper (roughly 6,000 words) in the philosophy of action, and I would like you to write the proposal document itself. My half-formed idea is the following.

Work on collective intentionality and joint action has largely been organized around a single question — what must be true of the participants' minds for them to be doing something together — and the answers fall into a few families: one locates jointness in a shared intention, with the right interdependence and mutual responsiveness; one locates it in a joint commitment that constitutes a plural subject with its own obligations; one treats we-intention as primitive and irreducible. I have come to suspect that all of these families are answering a question adjacent to the one that matters, and that they pay for it in the same way. They handle the clean, fully committed, voluntary cases well, and they either exclude or distort the messy ones — cases where commitment is partial, where each party privately expects the other to drop out, where exit is anticipated and legitimate. Two people co-authoring a paper each suspects the other may abandon; a couple staying together for now; a subcontractor who assumes the general contractor will cut him loose. I do not think these are defective or degenerate instances of joint action. I think they are joint action, and that what makes them so is not a shared intention but a structure of mutual accountability: who has standing to demand what of whom, who owes notice before withdrawing, and who is entitled to exit, and on what terms. The thought I want to test is that joint action is constituted by this division of normative labor rather than by a meeting of minds, and that the mental states the standard accounts fasten on are downstream of it rather than its ground.

Turn this into a proposal with the following elements. First, a precise statement of the question, framed so that it has a determinate answer rather than inviting a survey of views. Second, an account of why the existing positions leave the question open — not merely that they disagree with one another, but that the question I am asking is not one any of them has taken up, and why that gap is not an oversight to be patched but a consequence of how the debate is set up. Third, the method: what I would actually do, in order, over the course of a semester, and why this approach rather than the obvious alternative of simply adding more cases to the existing debate. Fourth, the form a result would take, and what would count as the project failing — I would rather the proposal name a real way for it to come out badly than pretend it cannot. Fifth, an honest assessment of scope, including what might make the project too large for one semester and how I should trim it if so.

Two constraints bear on the method. The account I am proposing has to distinguish joint action from mere interlocking, or it overgenerates: two strangers approaching on a sidewalk, each adjusting to the other and coordinating smoothly, have no shared plan and no standing to demand anything of each other, and I want to be able to say why that is not joint action while the co-author case is. And the account has to say something about a case I have not listed above — please specify at least one further case it predicts, and say which way it cuts. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I will supply those myself. Refer to the positions and arguments on their own terms rather than to who held them.
