You are a philosopher specializing in the ethics of historical wrongdoing, collective memory, and comparative moral judgment. I would like you to write a rigorous essay for a fictional seminar volume, The Limits of Comparison. Before you begin writing, think carefully and spend a few sentences brainstorming the main positions one could take on the question below, identify the strongest version of each, and only then commit to a line of argument. Do not include citations, references to particular authors, or reading lists; argue from the merits of the positions themselves, so that the essay stands on its own for a reader with no access to any outside sources.

Background. A fictional research institute, the Center for Historical Severity Assessment, has proposed a "Severity of Historical Wrongs Index" (SHWI) for use in museums, school curricula, and reparations deliberations. The proposal has three parts.

First, a set of dimensions on which any atrocity is to be scored, each on a 0-10 scale: (1) Magnitude, the number of people killed, displaced, or otherwise subjected to the harm; (2) Intentionality, the degree to which the harm was aimed at as an end rather than foreseen and accepted as a side effect; (3) Organization, the degree to which the harm was planned, bureaucratized, and carried out by a durable institution rather than emerging from chaos or a short-lived uprising; (4) Ideological exclusion, the degree to which victims were marked as lying outside the community of moral concern; (5) Irreversibility, the degree to which the losses — lives, languages, archives, practices — cannot be recovered or repaired.

Second, an aggregation rule. The five scores are combined into a single number, but the index explicitly permits ties and disclaims any claim that a one-point difference on a given dimension is meaningful. It advertises itself as ordinal, not cardinal.

Third, a "lexical priority" option. Users who wish may order the dimensions lexically, so that a difference in Magnitude, however small, outweighs any difference on the remaining dimensions.

The institute's defenders describe the index as a tool for transparency rather than a verdict. Its critics call it obscene. Your task is to adjudicate.

Write an essay that does all of the following:

(a) States and defends a verdict on whether the comparative ranking of atrocities is possible, and, separately, whether it is obscene. These two questions may come apart, and you should say explicitly whether, on your view, they do.

(b) Examines whether the ordinal/cardinal distinction actually rescues the index or merely relabels the underlying problem, and whether the permission to leave ties unresolved is a genuine concession to incommensurability or an evasion.

(c) Tests the index against at least two concrete hard cases of your own construction. For instance, consider a regime that deliberately starves a region to crush a rebellion and foresees mass death as the means, versus a movement that pursues a war of conquest and, without aiming at civilians, knowingly destroys the infrastructure on which a particular minority's survival depends; suppose the index scores the two nearly equal. Also construct a case in which the lexical option reverses a ranking that most people would find compelling. Say what the index gets wrong, if anything, in each case.

(d) Gives an account of what kind of objection the charge of obscenity actually is — moral, epistemic, or political — and assesses whether it succeeds. Do not treat the word as merely rhetorical.

(e) States and addresses the strongest objection to your own conclusion, and says what the index may and may not legitimately be used for, even if it survives your objections.

Conclude with a definite verdict rather than a survey of difficulties; a qualified verdict is acceptable if the qualifications are doing real work. Aim for roughly 1200-1800 words of dense, self-contained argument.
