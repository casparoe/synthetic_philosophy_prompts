A friend of mine defends the British Raj, and his entire case turns on one move: he says we should treat the empire as a business and draw up its balance sheet. Put the railways, the telegraph, the legal codes, the administrative service and the abolition of sati on the credit side; put the land revenue, the deindustrialisation, the famines and the massacres on the debit side; strike a total; and if the credit column wins, the empire was, all things considered, a good thing and its critics are simply refusing to do the arithmetic. He calls this the only honest way to argue about it, and I notice the other side tends to answer in the same currency — they call it a drain, a siphon from colony to metropole, which is the same picture with the sign flipped. I would like the analogy itself audited rather than another round of figures.

Begin with the correspondence table. In this picture, what plays the role of the assets, the liabilities, the reporting entity, the unit of account, the opening balance, the reporting period, the auditor and the shareholders? Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and which of the breaks matter for the use my friend is making of it. I am especially interested in three: whether a single net figure is available at all when the items are incommensurable, since a railway and a famine do not obviously share a unit; whose ledger this is, given that the credits and the debits fall on different people, so that a net gain for the whole may be a loss for nearly everyone inside it; and what the opening balance is supposed to be, when the baseline against which improvement is measured is itself an effect of the conquest. The drain metaphor should be assessed on the same terms — if it shares the balance sheet's assumptions, say whether it can survive the objections that sink the ledger, or whether both stand or fall together.

Finish by pricing the conclusion: does my friend need this analogy to reach the verdict that the empire was justified, or does the arithmetic support only something weaker, such as that some benefits accrued? If the balance-sheet frame cannot carry the weight, say what frame would do better, and what that frame would have to give up.

Lead with your verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate my question.
