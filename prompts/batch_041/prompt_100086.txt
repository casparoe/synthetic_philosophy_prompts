"Consider a physician who must assign a probability that her patient, Ms. K, carries a particular recessive condition. She has four sets of statistics available: (i) the prevalence of the condition in the population at large; (ii) its prevalence among people of Ms. K's ancestry; (iii) its prevalence among people who, like Ms. K, have a certain mildly elevated blood marker; and (iv) its prevalence among first cousins of known carriers, Ms. K being one such cousin. The four frequencies differ substantially. (a) Explain why the mere existence of these four classes generates a problem for assigning Ms. K a probability. (b) A colleague proposes the following rule, which he attributes to a statistician named R. Alvares: assign the probability given by the most specific reference class for which reliable statistics are available. Does this rule resolve the difficulty, or does it merely relocate it? (c) What would a thoroughgoing subjective Bayesian say about the whole dispute?"

The student's answer reads:

"(a) Ms. K is a member of all four classes at once, and each class carries a different frequency, so the statistics alone do not single out a probability for her. This is the reference class problem: a probability is a property of a class, but what we want is a probability for an individual, and the individual can be slotted into indefinitely many classes. It is worth stressing that this is not a defect of our data. Even an ideal observer in possession of every frequency would face exactly the same choice, so the difficulty is conceptual rather than a matter of ignorance. Once we see this, it follows that the probability we end up reporting is arbitrary — any figure between the lowest and the highest of the four frequencies is as defensible as any other.

(b) Alvares's rule, as stated, does not resolve the difficulty; it relocates it. 'Most specific' is ambiguous — smallest class? class defined by the most predicates? class that is a subset of every other? Ms. K's marker-class and cousin-class need not be nested, so neither is more specific than the other, and the rule is silent. And where one class is nested in another, the more specific class is usually the one with the sparsest data, so a demand for maximal specificity fights against the demand for reliability; the rule tells us to maximize both and gives no exchange rate. We are left needing a further rule for choosing among candidate classes, which is the original problem one level up.

(c) A thoroughgoing subjective Bayesian would say that the dispute is largely an artifact of a frequentist picture. For her a probability is a degree of belief, not a frequency, so there is no fact of the matter about the 'correct' class and nothing to read off; the four frequencies are simply evidence to be conditioned on. She would therefore advise picking the class that maximizes entropy, giving a maximally noncommittal prior over the four candidate classes, and then letting the data do the rest."
