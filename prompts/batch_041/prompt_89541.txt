Write a poem for me to read at the close of a panel on deception in public life. I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I have spent the past year reporting on a species of deception that keeps its hands clean: the minister, the press officer, the company spokesperson, the negotiator, the person who never says anything false and yet leaves a false picture standing in the room. I want a poem that performs that distinction rather than describing it.

The formal spine should be a refrain: a single sentence that is strictly true and that recurs at least four times, each time in a context that makes it carry a different and false implication. The repetition should itself be the deception, so that the reader can watch the same true words change what they convey. Somewhere near the end I want one line that is, by contrast, a flat lie — a sentence the speaker believes false and asserts anyway. Someone who knows the philosophical literature on lying and misleading should be able to point to that line and say exactly why it is the only lie in the poem. About twenty lines, no fixed metre, and in a spokesperson's voice. Please do not let the poem sneer at her; the whole interest of the case is that she can defend every word she has said.

The ideas the poem should be faithful to: the gap between what a sentence asserts and what it merely conveys, so that the hearer's own expectations, and not the speaker's words, finish the false picture; the way the misleading speaker recruits the hearer as a co-author of the deception and keeps the credit for truthfulness; the presupposition that smuggles in something false without ever stating it; the tactic researchers have called paltering, the active use of truthful statements to convey a misleading impression, and the fact that people who do it tend to regard it as less dishonest than lying, which is precisely the judgement I want left open rather than settled; and the harder cases — the true statement made in the hope that it will be disbelieved, the true answer that misleads through what it omits, and the speaker who cares nothing for whether her words are true and so is neither lying nor quite misleading.

Then add a short note, no more than 150 words, on where the poem takes liberties with the ideas. Say plainly which parts of the lying-versus-misleading distinction are settled among people who work on it and which are genuinely contested — for instance, whether a true statement made with the intention to deceive is as bad as a lie, whether misleading can be the graver wrong because it exploits the hearer's trust and leaves them nothing to complain about, and whether implying a falsehood ought to count as lying at all. Do not present a contested view as the consensus, and do not resolve the disagreement on my behalf.

Please write in British English.
