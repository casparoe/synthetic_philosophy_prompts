For the better part of a year I've been reporting on a corner of decision theory that goes by the name of imprecise probabilities, the view that a rational agent's uncertainty is better represented by a set of probability functions than by a single one, and I keep colliding with an objection from a source of mine, a decision theorist of the classical persuasion, that I can neither wave off nor pin down. Let me set it out as fairly as I can, since if I have garbled it, the garbling is part of what I need to hear about. (1) Whatever else is true of a rational agent, she must be able to say of any two options open to her that she prefers the first, prefers the second, or is indifferent between the two; there is no fourth possibility. (2) A preference ordering of that shape, if it is coherent, can always be represented by one probability function and one utility function, so that she prefers one option to another exactly when its expected utility is higher under that probability function. (3) Now consider the imprecise agent, whose rule -- the one usually called maximality -- is to treat an option as permissible when no alternative does better in expected utility under every probability function in her set. That rule always leaves her something to do; whatever situation she faces, at least one option is permissible. (4) And when two options are both permissible because the members of her set disagree about which is better, she is indifferent between them: she has no preference either way, and that is what indifference is. (5) So her conduct always reveals a complete ordering of the options before her, and by (2) some single probability function and utility function capture it. (6) The set was therefore doing no work; anything she can be described as doing with imprecise probabilities can be described just as well with precise ones, and the extra machinery is either notational or incoherent. He regards this as a proof that the program has no subject matter. He never did tell me what coherence amounts to here beyond obeying the usual axioms, and I let it go at the time.

What keeps me from printing it is a corollary I can construct myself. If (4) is right, then genuine indecision does not exist. The colleague who cannot choose between two job offers, the voter who cannot say which of two candidates is the lesser evil, the buyer who cannot decide whether the house is worth the asking price -- on this argument they are all simply indifferent and do not know their own minds, and anyone who insists she is torn is either confused about her own preferences or irrational. That strikes me as proving far too much, but when I try to say exactly where the argument overreaches, I end up gesturing at a difference between not preferring and preferring equally, and he tells me that is a distinction without a difference. So: tell me which step to reject, why it looked so innocent that a careful person would write it down, and what rejecting it costs the imprecise probabilist. If the honest answer is that the program simply denies (1), that it never promised a complete ranking in the first place, then I want to know what she is left holding, and whether she must accept some penalty I have heard alluded to but never properly explained, something about sequences of trades or bets that can leave an agent with incomplete preferences worse off than she began. If instead the argument is sound and the program really does collapse into precise probability, say so without softening it; and if I have misdescribed the position, or the question as I have framed it is confused, tell me so plainly rather than answering around the confusion. Write it the way you would write me an email -- continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
