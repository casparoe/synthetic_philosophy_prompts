After thirty-odd years as an experimental physicist I retired, and somewhat to my own surprise I have spent the last several years reading philosophy in earnest. I now sit in on a graduate seminar in metaphysics and epistemology, and through a chain of flattering misunderstandings I have been added to the referee list of a journal that publishes work in the philosophy of science and its borderlands with epistemology. I therefore read three or four submissions a month, and I have found that I can usually follow a paper's formal arguments but am at sea whenever its central move is a thought experiment. I cannot reliably tell when I am being shown something about a concept and when I am being worked on by a piece of stagecraft. Having spent a career around real apparatus, I am perhaps over-sensitized to the difference between an instrument that reveals a property of the world and one that merely produces a vivid effect, and I suspect the distinction has an analogue here, but I do not know how to draw it.

What I would like is a procedure — a one-page, ordered set of questions I can actually run at my desk in the forty minutes I am willing to give a manuscript before writing my report — for judging what evidential work, if any, a thought experiment in a paper is doing. Please give me the questions in the order I should ask them, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, mark the point at which the procedure returns a hard stop (the case is doing no evidential work and the paper must be assessed on other grounds), and mark the points at which the honest answer is that this is a judgment call to be handed to the author or to a colleague rather than settled by me. I want the distinctions that must survive the simplification made explicit — in particular, I think I need to be able to tell a case that functions as a counterexample from one that functions as a positive illustration, to tell an intuition being treated as data from one being treated as a prompt to build a better theory, and to separate the stipulations that are relevant to the concept under discussion from the incidental details that are doing the persuading. I should add that I do not want a reading list, citations, or pointers into a literature I have not read; whatever the procedure needs must be stated in it, in terms I can understand without prior allegiance to any named position.

Then run the procedure on three cases so I can see it work: a Gettier-style counterexample to a proposed analysis of knowledge; a science-fiction case about personal identity under teleportation or uploading; and one case of your choosing that is widely found persuasive but that your procedure will probably rule inadmissible — I would rather you pick a case where the verdict is likely to annoy me than one where it flatters me, and I want you to say plainly which feature of the case drives the verdict and what someone who disagrees with you would say. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: the kinds of paper or case where it will misfire, how I might notice that it is misfiring, and which of its steps are judgments dressed up as procedure.
