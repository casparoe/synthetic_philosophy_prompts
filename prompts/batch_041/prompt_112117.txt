I run a ten-week evening course on the philosophy of art for adults at a local institute — twelve to fourteen students, very mixed backgrounds, a couple of retired teachers, one lapsed art historian, several who have never read a word of philosophy, and nothing set between sessions. I have ninety minutes for the session I am planning, and I want to spend it on why we go looking for art that makes us sad. The trouble is that if I stand up and give them the shape of the problem and the standard answers, they will nod along and forget it by the following week. I want them to walk into it through their own choices and only notice, at the end, that they have been caught. So please design a runnable session — rules, materials, a timeline, and a payoff scheme using tokens rather than money — in which the group's own decisions generate the difficulty before anyone names it. Participants should not be told in advance what the session is about, and the facilitator should not use the words 'paradox' or 'tragedy', or mention any philosopher or any proposed solution, before the debrief; the opening should be no more than that we are going to choose how to spend an evening together.

My rough thinking, which you should feel free to improve on rather than merely implement: in the first round each participant has a budget of tokens and a slate of eight or so cultural offerings, each with a short and genuinely appealing synopsis, and bids for the ones they would most like to attend. Unknown to them, each card carries a hidden rating on the back — how sad the offering will leave them feeling. The offerings they will most want should, by design, be the ones with the highest sadness rating, so that the reveal lands as a fact about them rather than a fact about the theory. A second round re-runs the bids with the ratings face up, and most people do not change. A third asks each of them to say how much better a sad offering would have to be, in tokens, before they would choose it over a happy one, which gives the group a crude exchange rate between sadness and quality. Then I want two probes that press on the obvious reply that sadness is simply a cost paid for other goods such as insight or beauty. First, a stripping probe: the same story with the wound removed — same understanding, less sorrow — and then a pill that removes the sting afterwards while leaving the insight intact. If sadness were merely a cost, both would be straightforward improvements; I expect them to refuse, and I want the activity to make that refusal visible and awkward. Second, a reality probe: the same narrative as fiction, as documentary, and as something true happening to someone in the room, to expose how much of the pleasure depends on the frame. The closing stake should be real but small — the group should actually sit through a short, genuinely moving passage at the end, chosen by whoever bid the most, so that the emotion is not merely hypothetical.

The debrief should map each moment of the session onto a component of the problem, and lay out the main families of answer without pretending they are equivalent: that the emotion is purged or discharged; that it is compensated by other goods; that it is not a cost at all but part of what is valued; that we enjoy it only because it is safely framed and freely chosen; and that what we enjoy is not the sadness itself but our own responsiveness to it. I also want two less familiar thoughts worked in: that the sadness of art comes pre-shaped — it has a form, a point and an end, which real sorrow does not — and that art may work on two channels at once, holding the emotion at arm's length while inviting us to let it in, with the pleasure arising from the combination rather than from either alone. Please do not lean on names or citations for any of this; describe the mechanisms instead. I also want the distinction between enjoying something and finding it worthwhile to be available at the end, since I suspect my students will report the second while denying the first, and I want to know what to say when they do. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run this — I am especially worried about the student who insists she is not really sad because it is only make-believe, and about the student who genuinely prefers the light options and so spoils the reveal — and be honest about which feature of the problem the session cannot reproduce and that I shall have to supply myself. Write in British English.
