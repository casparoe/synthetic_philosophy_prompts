I was covering a panel on the economics of planning when two of the panelists fell into an argument I couldn't referee. One said the old calculation debate is settled in the planners' favor: Lange's central board, adjusting prices by trial and error until markets clear while managers minimize cost and produce to those prices, shows a socialist economy can allocate rationally, and that what Mises and Hayek added was really about incentives, not about whether rational calculation is possible. The other said that skips the hard part — the board can only iterate toward clearing prices if it already knows the preference scales and production functions it is meant to be discovering, so the scheme assumes the very knowledge the market is credited with generating. The first called that a distinction without a difference: if the board converges on the prices a market would, it hardly matters where the knowledge is said to sit. The second said it matters because the market does not compute a target that exists in advance; it discovers what the target is, and no one can iterate toward a number that does not yet exist. The first then offered the modern version: with enough data and computation, the discovery problem shrinks toward the computational one, since more of the relevant information can simply be measured and processed. The second replied that this conflates two different problems, and that information you can collect is not the same as knowledge that has not yet been created. They turned to the room for a verdict and I had none to offer. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move — who is right about what, and what both of them are missing — and tell me how confident you are in each judgment, singling out the point you are least sure of.
