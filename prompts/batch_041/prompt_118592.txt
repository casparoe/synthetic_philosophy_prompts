The question, with the marks I assigned to each part, was: "(a) The knowability principle says that every truth is knowable: for every proposition p, if p is true then it is possible that someone knows that p. Reconstruct the argument that this principle entails that every truth is in fact known, and state every principle the argument uses. [7] (b) Say precisely what the argument establishes and what it does not. In particular, separate whatever part of the reasoning is intuitionistically valid from whatever part requires classical logic, and say what a defender of the knowability principle who accepts only intuitionistic logic is left with. [6] (c) Describe two distinct responses a defender of the knowability principle might make, and say exactly what each response concedes. [5] (d) A student in the lecture said that the knowability principle is not really a thesis about truth at all, but only a thesis about the limits of our actual cognitive capacities. Assess this remark. [2]"

The script I'm asking about reads: "(a) The argument is a reductio. Suppose there is a truth that nobody knows: p and no one knows that p. Because this conjunction is true, the knowability principle applies to it, so it must be possible for someone to know it. Knowledge distributes over conjunction, so anyone who knows that p and no one knows that p both knows p and knows that no one knows p. But knowledge is factive -- you cannot know what is not the case -- so knowing that no one knows p entails that no one knows p. In that same possible situation we therefore have both that p is known and that p is not known. Contradiction. So the assumption that this unknown truth is knowable is impossible. But the knowability principle says it must be possible to know it, since it is true. So there can be no unknown truths, and hence, if the principle holds, every truth is known. The argument turns on exactly three principles: the knowability principle itself, the factivity of knowledge, and the distribution of knowledge over conjunction. It does not need knowledge to be closed under logical consequence in general, and it does not need any iteration principle such as KK. Nor does it depend on self-reference: the proposition substituted into the principle is an ordinary contingent proposition about who knows what, not a sentence that says of itself that it is unknown. (b) What the argument establishes is that the knowability principle entails that every truth is known. That is not by itself a formal contradiction, but it is a claim nobody accepts, so the principle is untenable as it stands. It is worth seeing how much of the reasoning is intuitionistically acceptable: the reductio in (a), from the assumption that some truth is unknown to the conclusion that there are no unknown truths, uses nothing but the three principles listed, so an intuitionist can accept it. The step that requires classical logic is the last one, where we pass from 'there are no unknown truths' to 'every truth is known', which relies on the classical equivalence of not-(p and not-known-p) with if-p-then-known-p. So an intuitionist can keep the knowability principle and accept only the claim that for every truth p it is not the case that p is never known -- which is simply the knowability principle written in intuitionistic notation, since for an intuitionist 'it is not the case that p is never known' is how one expresses that p is knowable, the two being interchangeable. Nothing is lost by the retreat; the anti-realist merely cannot have the classical reading of the conditional. (c) Two responses are available. The first is the intuitionistic retreat just described: change the logic rather than the principle. The second is to restrict the principle to propositions that are not self-defeating -- roughly, those whose knowability is not ruled out by their own content, since the paradox needs an instance of the principle whose antecedent is a truth of the form p and no one knows that p. The two responses come to the same thing: each says that the knowability principle has to be trimmed so that it does not apply to every truth without restriction. What both concede is that the unrestricted formulation of the principle cannot be maintained. (d) The remark from the lecture is right about the letter of the principle but wrong about its point. The principle is a possibility claim, so it is not a claim about what we can in fact come to know; but it is nonetheless a claim about truth, namely that nothing can be true which could not be known, and that is exactly what the argument attacks."
