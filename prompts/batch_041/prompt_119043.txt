At a reading group I have been sitting in on this term — I am an MA student auditing a seminar, so I am not the one doing the arguing — a dispute broke out that I have not been able to settle in my own head, and I would rather have it adjudicated than take a side. The topic was the familiar complaint, usually credited to Bernard Williams, that a utilitarian account of action requires the agent to have one thought too many. The stock illustration, which nobody in the room disputed, is the man who saves his wife from drowning rather than a stranger: on the utilitarian story he is supposed to think not only that this is his wife, but also that saving one's wife is the sort of act that generally promotes the best outcome, and it is that second, generalising thought that is said to be one too many. What the group could not agree on is whether the complaint is a serious objection to consequentialism or a rhetorical trick that evaporates once the theory is stated carefully.

Owen went first, arguing that the objection attacks a strawman. Consequentialists, he said, distinguish the criterion that makes an act right from the procedure an agent uses in deciding what to do; a sophisticated consequentialist can hold that rightness is impartial maximisation while insisting that people should in fact deliberate from love, loyalty and habit, so that the extra thought belongs to the theorist rather than to the man in the water. Nadia answered that this either leaves the objection exactly where it was — the theory still makes it true that his wife counts as one locus of value among others, so the reason he acts on is displaced even if the arithmetic is never run — or else it requires the theory to keep its real criterion from the very people it governs, which is less a reply than a concession. Owen came back that she was conflating two things, the reason an agent acts on and the justification of the disposition he acts on, and that she was treating the mere availability of a further justification as though it were a further thought the agent has to entertain; he added that the objection, so understood, proves far too much, since the Kantian who asks whether her maxim could be willed as a universal law, or simply the person who wonders whether she is being fair to everyone, also has a second thought. Nadia replied that the objection was never about counting thoughts: it is about the content of the extra one, which treats the beloved as an instance of a kind rather than as this person, and that the charge of proving too much misfires because the Kantian's second thought is still about the particular person and what is owed to him. She then said that the manoeuvre of a justification that is available but never entertained is unstable — a consideration kept by design out of the agent's deliberation cannot guide or check him, and if it does enter his deliberation we are back where we began. Owen's last word was that all this turns the objection into a claim about which reasons are of the right kind for a person to have, which is a further and highly controversial premise about agent-relative reasons, and not a point against the criterion at all.

Neither of them gave an inch, and I honestly cannot tell whether they were disagreeing about the objection or about what a moral theory is even for. What I would like is a point-by-point adjudication: which of Owen's moves and which of Nadia's moves are sound as stated, which are sound only against a weaker version of the other's position, and where the two of them are talking past each other. I would also like to know whether the complaint about the extra thought and the separate complaint about integrity and the commitments that give a life its shape are really one objection or two, and whether an answer to one of them settles the other. Finally, please say what both of them are missing, and give a verdict: does the one-thought-too-many objection survive contact with the sophisticated reply, and if it does, what exactly is left of it?
