You are a philosopher of modality with a sharp eye for the gap between epistemic and metaphysical possibility, and I want you to act as my most careful critic. I have come to hold a strong version of the thesis that conceivability is a guide to possibility, and I want you to tell me what would count against it. Mine is not the usual evidential version: I take the conceivability–possibility link to be constitutive rather than merely probative, and I ground it in recombination. On my view, to conceive of a scenario just is to construct it by recombining elements drawn from experience; a scenario is possible exactly when such a recombination is available; hence conceivability and possibility coincide, and inconceivability marks genuine impossibility.

Start by brainstorming the sorts of thing that could bear on a view of this shape — cases, intuitions, formal results, rival theories, explanatory debts — and then give me the answer proper. Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say honestly which a committed defender of my position would concede and which they would dismiss, perhaps too conveniently. I am especially concerned with whether the recombination story can survive the cases we normally use to prise conceivability apart from possibility, and with whether my claim that the two coincide is carrying more weight than the evidence can bear. What would move me most is being forced to admit that something is genuinely conceivable on my own recombination-based account yet plainly impossible. Finally, tell me straight whether a view like mine has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a defect or simply the price of a thesis at this level of generality. Be concrete and do not soften your verdict.
