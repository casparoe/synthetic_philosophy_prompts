I'm in a reading group on ethics and war, and there's one guy in it who deploys the same move whenever anyone raises sovereignty as a barrier to humanitarian intervention. He says: picture your neighbor's house on fire, and you can hear a kid trapped upstairs. Nobody thinks you need the owner's permission to kick the door in and pull the kid out. Sovereignty, he says, is just the deed to the house — a property right — and property rights don't stop you from saving a life. So (his word) the whole 'you can't just cross a border' objection to intervening in a massacre collapses. He's used it to argue we shouldve been more willing in Kosovo and that people who agonized over Libya were basically fetishizing a line on a map.

What I want is a proper audit of the analogy, not another round of us talking past each other. Lay the correspondence out explicitly — what plays the role of the house, the fire, the child, the owner, the neighbor, the deed, the fire brigade — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. I'm especially interested in the parts that seem to do quiet work: who exactly is the child (a whole population? some subset? does the picture require the rescued party to be passive and voiceless, and is that itself being smuggled in as a premise?), whether a house fire is even the right model for a civil war where the 'fire' might be contested or lit by someone inside the house, and what happens when the thing whose door you're kicking in is also the arsonist — because in Libya it wasn't obviously a house with a fire in it so much as a house that was on fire because of who was running it. And notice the modal slide: his analogy licenses 'you may' but he ends up at 'we should've' — does the picture support any duty at all, given that in the story the rescuer risks basically nothing while a real intervener risks their own soldiers and the neighbors' houses?

Then the bottom line I actually care about: does the conclusion he wants — that the sovereignty objection 'collapses' — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the entire argument? And if you had to swap it out, what's a better picture that keeps the real insight (that sovereignty isn't an absolute moral shield) without importing everything he needs? Keep it concrete and philosophical — I don't need a literature review or a reading list.
