For the last two years I've been a research assistant on a project about personal identity, and what finally got under my skin wasn't the reading list — it was sitting in on my uncle's neurology appointment after his Parkinson's diagnosis, listening to a clinician describe, very cheerfully, the day he might get a deep-brain implant, and then hearing my uncle ask, only half joking, how many parts of him they could swap out before the warranty on him ran out. Since then I've gone down the rabbit hole on the gradual-replacement thought experiment: you take a living brain and replace its neurons one at a time with silicon devices that do exactly what each neuron did — same inputs, same outputs, same contribution to the surrounding network, no detectable functional or behavioral difference at any step — and then you ask whether the person is still there at the end, or whether at some point they quietly ceased to be there and no one could say when. What I want from you is a poem, not an essay. Take the role of a poet who is also a competent philosopher of mind, and think carefully before you commit to a form.

The form should itself be the argument. Write roughly five stanzas — you choose the line length and whether to rhyme; drop rhyme if a stricter scheme would force you to say something false — built around a single recurring line, a refrain, that returns once per stanza but is altered each time it comes back, by one word or one clause, so that by the last stanza it says something the first stanza's version could not have said. The speaker is the person being replaced, addressing someone who is staying with them through it, and the drift in the refrain should track the replacement itself: early alterations should be cosmetic, the kind of thing a person could shrug off, and they should grow less cosmetic as the poem goes, until the final occurrence is either fully foreign or fully unremarkable — the poem should make me feel the force of not being able to tell which of those it is. It should be accurate enough that someone who works on personal identity recognizes the scenario rather than just the buzzwords; in particular it shouldn't cheat by making the replaced person abruptly behave differently, or by treating the silicon as obviously non-conscious, since those are precisely the things the thought experiment is meant to leave open.

Because a poem delivers no verdict, I'd like you to append a short prose note — a few sentences, not a lecture — telling me where the poem takes liberties with the ideas: specifically, where its structure presupposes an answer to the identity question (does it assume that continuity of function is continuity of person, or that it isn't?), which contested reading of the replacement scenario the ending leans on, and what a proponent of the rival reading would say the poem got wrong. End by naming the one consideration that would most change your mind about whether the poem's implicit verdict is the right one — the argument or the empirical result that would make you rewrite the refrain — and say honestly where you think your own reasoning is weakest. Begin with a few lines of brainstorming about possible forms before you write the poem itself.
