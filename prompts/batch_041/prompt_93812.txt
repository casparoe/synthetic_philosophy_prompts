I'm putting together a bunch of prompts where I ask a model to predict what some long-dead philosopher would have thought about things that only came up after they died. This one's Kant, and I want it to be about whether values can be traded off against each other at all.

Here's the setup. Kant draws a line between things that have a price and things that have a dignity: something with a price can be replaced by an equivalent, something with a dignity is beyond all price, there's no equivalent for it. Alot of people read that as him being a value monist — that everything in morality gets weighed on one scale, or at least that reason can always rank any two options against each other. But the modern debate about incommensurability came much later: the idea that two options can both be genuinely valuable and still have no common unit in which to compare them, so a hard choice isn't just a choice where you're missing information or where you feel bad afterwards — it's one where the values themselves don't admit of a common measure. Tied to that are two more modern ideas: moral remainder, where even when you choose correctly you still owe something to the option you didn't take and can't just write it off; and dirty hands, where doing the right thing in a rotten situation still leaves you implicated.

The thing I want him to react to is institutional rationing with quality-adjusted life years. A national health system decides which treatments to pay for by measuring how much health each one buys per dollar, where a year of life at some quality counts as a fraction or a multiple of a year in perfect health, and it funds things up to a cutoff. That treats health, and years of life, as if they sit on one scale with a price. Add the modern cousins: triage protocols for scarce ICU beds that score patients on predicted survival, and frameworks that compare saving one life now against shaving a tiny risk off millions of people far in the future. None of that existed in Kant's day.

So: what would Kant actually say about all this? Please don't just tell me it's complicated or lay out the options and leave me to pick. Take a position, predict what he'd think, and commit to it — and if you think he'd accept some of it and reject the rest, draw the line and say exactly where it falls. Also deal with the obvious counter-reading: his price/dignity split is itself a kind of incommensurability claim, so is he an ally of the modern view or not? I dont want hedging, I want a definite answer with reasons.
