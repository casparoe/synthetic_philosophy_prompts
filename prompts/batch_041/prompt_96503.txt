Ten years ago this spring, my older brother Daniel went back into a burning house on the edge of town to bring out the urn holding the ashes of a neighbor's wife — the neighbor was already safe on the lawn, and Daniel knew it — and the floor came down on him. On the anniversary, the volunteer fire company is dedicating a plaque and giving him its medal for 'service beyond the call,' and I have been asked, as the family's speaker, to give the address. The hall will hold about eighty people: two dozen of Daniel's crew, most of whom believe to this day that going back in was simply the job, and who will hear any talk of going 'beyond the call' as an accusation that the rest of them stop short; our mother and Daniel's widow, for whom it was unmistakably a choice and not a duty, and who will hear 'that was his job' as a way of not thanking him; the neighbor himself, who has never known what to do with the fact that a man died for an urn; and one firefighter who said at the time, in front of everyone, that it was a stupid thing to do and that Daniel put his crew at risk by making them follow him. I don't want to lie to any of them, and I don't want to hand the room a line that lets them stop thinking.

I have been turning this over through a distinction a philosopher friend keeps pressing on me, and I would like the address to carry it without ever naming it as philosophy: between duties that are bounded — you do the thing, you are done, and no one can demand another ounce — and duties that are unbounded, where the role itself never tells you when you have given enough, so that every act is at once a fulfilment and a falling-short. Firefighting looks unbounded in that sense, and that is precisely what makes the medal's wording hard. There is a further snag I cannot get around: by every account, Daniel was never happier than when he was inside a burning building, and he did not experience that second entry as a cost. If nothing was given up, then the usual way of praising a sacrifice — the scale of it, the price, what it cost him — is simply unavailable to me, and I suspect it was never the right register anyway. Please write the address, roughly five minutes spoken, as exactly five numbered movements and nothing else: no greeting, no introduction before the first movement, no closing remarks after the fifth, each movement a short paragraph that could be read aloud, and no attempt to settle for the room whether what he did was required, permitted, or beyond the reach of either word.
