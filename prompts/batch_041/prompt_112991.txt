I'm a high school senior and president of my school's math club, and I've been asked to give the five-minute opening address at this year's induction ceremony. I want to build the speech around the rule-following problem associated with Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein: however many sums I have worked out in the past, those facts by themselves do not determine that I meant addition rather than some deviant rule that agrees with addition on every case I have ever seen but diverges on the next one; what settles what I meant is a shared practice, correction, and the judgment of a community. The audience will be students, parents, and a few teachers, almost none of whom have heard of this problem. Write the address so that the paradox does real structural work — it should explain why mathematics is not a private game, why being corrected is not an insult, and why a club is more than a resume line — without turning into a lecture or quoting anyone. Keep it warm enough for the occasion and honest about what the paradox does and does not show.
