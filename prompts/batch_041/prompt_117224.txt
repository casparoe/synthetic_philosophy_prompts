Next month I am presenting a paper at a joint seminar of early modern philosophers and historians of economic thought. My thesis is that Berkeley's treatment of money in The Querist — his description of money as a ticket or counter, his claim that wealth consists in the power of commanding the industry of others, and his proposal for an Irish national bank issuing paper credit — is not an amateur digression but a deliberate extension of his immaterialism into economic theory: if bodies are bundles of ideas, then money is not a substance but a system of signs whose value consists in the trust and circulation it sustains. The philosophers in the room know the Principles and the Dialogues inside out but have never read The Querist; the historians of economic thought know the pamphlet literature of the 1720s and 1730s and regard philosophical readings of it as anachronistic. Prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how damaging they would be, each phrased the way an early modernist or a historian of economic thought would put it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
