So I joined a small AI lab about three months ago (I do evals, not training) and I've somehow ended up on the commitee writing our model-release policy. Two people on it have been going at each other for weeks now: one thinks anything we build should have its weights put on the internet, the other thinks that's basically handing out ingredients for a catastrophe. They're both sharp and they're clearly talking past each other, and I have to make a recommendation at the end of the month, so I want to force them to write the thing they'd both actually sign instead of listening to another hour of vibes.

So: draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a committed open-weights advocate and a committed closed-development / structured-access safety person, on whether frontier AI should be open-sourced. Not a dialogue, not a verdict on who's right -- a document both of them could put their names to.

What I want in it:

- A numbered section of claims they can both sign. Include claims about the artifact itself (what a release actually contains -- weights, code, data, the recipe, the evals), about what each side's evidence base really supports versus what it only suggests, and about the decision situation (it's a race, it's partly irreversible, we don't control what other labs do).
- Then a table of the residual disagreements, each one phrased so that its own side would accept the wording, with the crux it rests on named.
- For each crux, a concrete case, test, or arguement that both sides agree would bear on it, plus each side's prediction written down in advance. I'd rather have four sharp ones than twelve mushy ones.

A few things I'm pretty sure are buried in there and want handled explicitly:

(a) Whether "open source" even describes what gets released, since weights alone aren't the recipe -- and whether that makes the label a cheat or just shorthand. I suspect half their fight is about this and they haven't noticed.
(b) Whether the risk story is about a lone actor getting capability uplift or about safety guardrails being stripped back by fine-tuning. Those are different claims with different evidence and I think they keep sliding between them.
(c) Whether "someone else would have released it anyway" is a reason or an excuse, and how the two of them should be thinking about marginal versus absolute risk when the counterfactual lab is a real lab.
(d) Whether the safety benefits of openness -- auditing, distributed defense, not having three companies own the world -- actually require frontier-scale weights or would be served by something less.
(e) The artifact existing versus the artifact spreading. I think that's the deepest split and I want it named.

And if any chunk of the disagreement turns out to be verbal -- different senses of "safety", "open", "frontier" -- say which chunk and why, and what's left once you strip it out. End with what both of them agree would actually change their minds, since right now neither of them will name anything.
