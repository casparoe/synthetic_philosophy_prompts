I have just spent an evening arguing with a friend about the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism, and I would like to work out what my own position is actually exposed to. Here is the position. The objection is not, at bottom, a complaint about aggregation in the abstract; it is a complaint about the form of justification. What is wrong with a classical utilitarian justification of a sacrifice is not that it counts gains and losses, but that it justifies the loss to the person who bears it by appeal to a gain that is not hers and that she cannot be said to receive: the justification is addressed to no one in particular, and so is not a justification to her at all. Understood that way, I think the objection survives the standard replies. The reply that utilitarianism takes persons seriously because it weights each person's good equally seems to miss the point, since equal weighting of goods is compatible with treating the persons whose goods they are as locations in a single calculation. The reply that the objection is merely an intuition dressed up as an argument also seems to miss the point, since the claim concerns the structure of a justification rather than any particular verdict.

What I want is a map of what would count against this. Specifically: which counterexamples, formal results, or independent commitments tell against it, sorted from fatal to merely awkward; which of these a defender of my formulation must himself concede rather than dismiss as question-begging; whether the position predicts anything its rivals do not, or has instead been quietly insulated from disconfirmation by the way I have stated it; and, if you conclude that it is insulated, whether that is a defect in the position or a sign that it is well founded.

Three pressure points from the argument deserve particular attention. First, my friend claimed that my formulation is not a distinct objection at all but a restatement of contractualism, and that it therefore inherits contractualism's problems. Tell me whether that charge sticks, and what would show that it does or does not. Second, he claimed that any version of the objection that makes interpersonal aggregation illicit must equally make intrapersonal aggregation illicit — sacrificing a present good for a later good within a single life — and that since no one renounces the latter, the objection either proves too much or else requires an asymmetry between persons and time-slices that no one has stated without begging the question. I want to know how much that costs me. Third, I suspect the objection bites differently on a view that counts experiences or person-moments rather than whole lives, and differently again on indirect or institutional forms of utilitarianism; say which of these it still reaches and which it does not.

Assume I know the standard literature well enough to recognize it, and spend your effort on the structure of the exposure rather than on exposition. End with a final line of the form Verdict: LABEL, where LABEL is exactly one of EXPOSED, PARTLY EXPOSED, or INSULATED, and records your overall judgement of how much the position actually risks.
