I have no training in philosophy and have been working through Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life on my own. The three ways of doing history he distinguishes — monumental, antiquarian, and critical — have begun to look to me like they assemble into an argument that eats itself, and I want you to tell me where it fails, or, if it does not fail, which step I should surrender and what I lose by surrendering it. Here is the argument as I have reconstructed it.

(1) A piece of history is worth anything at all only insofar as it serves life — only insofar as meeting it leaves a person more able to act, rather than merely admiring, or paralyzed, or nostalgic.

(2) Each of the three modes serves life from one direction and wounds it from another: monumental history hands you figures to imitate but flattens the past into usable myth; antiquarian history roots you in what is yours but can petrify into a museum no one can live in; critical history frees you from a past you must escape but can leave you standing on nothing.

(3) So for any given piece of history there is someone it strengthens and someone it weakens.

(4) If the worth of a piece of history is fixed by what it does to whoever meets it, then the same history is worth keeping for one person and worth discarding for another; its worth is relative to the user.

(5) Now take Nietzsche's own essay. Its substance is a claim about us: that we moderns carry too much history, and that the excess is what makes us sick.

(6) A claim about what we actually are can only serve life by being true. If it merely helps to believe that we are over-historical, then the antiquarian's comforting myth serves life just as well.

(7) So either the essay claims to be true about us, in which case its hold on us comes from truth rather than from usefulness, and serving life was never the real standard; or it makes no such claim, in which case it has no more authority over us than any story that happens to flatter.

(8) Either way, the thought that history serves life cannot be the whole account of what history is for. Premise (1) is false.

What makes this concrete for me: my mother wants my grandmother's house kept exactly as she left it, down to the furniture, and my uncle wants it cleared and sold, and each of them is plainly drawing on the past in one of Nietzsche's ways in order to become more able to live — and yet I cannot say that either of them is right about what that house and its past actually were. I would most like to keep (1), since it is the only thing that stops history from being mere trivia; I cannot accept (8). Which step is doing the damage, why did it look harmless when I wrote it, and what would I be giving up if I cut it? And if the argument is really sound and something has to go, tell me which premise is cheapest to abandon and what that costs me.
