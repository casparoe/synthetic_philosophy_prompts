I'm a science reporter at a metro daily and next month I've been volun-told to run a lunch-and-learn for the newsroom — 45 minutes, a conference room with a whiteboard, about twelve people, mostly editors plus our podcast producer and the sports guy, nobody who's read any philosophy since a survey course they dont remember. My topic is Wittgensteins family resemblance, because we spent three weeks this spring having the same fight every time a story touched animal communication: the desk wouldn't let me write that sperm whales have something like language, one editor insisted language requires syntax and recursion, I said nobody can define language without smuggling in exactly the cases they already wanted to include, and we went around like that until the piece shipped with weasel words in it. The sports guy, I should say, will probably get it first, because he has this argument about darts every six months.

So don't explain family resemblance to me — I've read the Investigations once and it mostly stuck. Plan how I should teach it. What has to come before what? I have a hunch you can't reach the part where it isn't just relativism until they've already felt the pull of so-anything-goes, but tell me if that's wrong, and where else the load-bearing joints are. What one example should carry the whole 45 minutes, and what does it have to have and not have? I was going to use our whale fight since it's ours, but I don't know if that's too close to the bone, or if it collapses back into the definitional fight instead of dissolving it. And I assume the obvious move — running through the things we call games — is a trap in this room; tell me why, or tell me I'm wrong.

The misconceptions I'm braced for: that it means categories are arbitrary, or that we can just stipulate a definition and move on; that it's the Rosch prototype thing where some whales are more language than others; that language games just means jargon or wordplay; that he's saying words don't really mean anything. Where in the sequence do I head each one off? What's the one question I ask at the end to see whether it landed, as opposed to everyone feeling clever? And tell me what to skip and announce that I'm skipping — rule-following, private language, the whole is-he-a-relativist mess — so I don't get dragged into a fight I can't win with the ideas editor sitting in the front row.
