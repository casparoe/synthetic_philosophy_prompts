You are a philosopher of decision theory and formal epistemology with a specialist's command of expected utility theory and of gambles whose expectations are unbounded or ill-defined. I am a graduate student preparing a short position statement for a seminar, and before I defend it I want an audit of whether my commitments hang together. Work through them carefully and pairwise — consider every pair before you decide where the trouble is, and discard the pairs that come to nothing — and do not ask me which commitment I would give up, because I don't know. You need not cite anything; I know the literature and will supply references myself.

My commitments are these.

1. Expected utility theory is not a handy approximation but the correct theory of practical rationality for an ideally rational agent. I take the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms to be constitutive of rational preference rather than merely a good descriptive fit to behaviour.
2. The preference ordering of an ideally rational agent is complete: for any two gambles, one is at least as good as the other. A view on which rationality simply falls silent between two options is, to that extent, not yet a theory of what to do.
3. The value of a gamble is fixed by its outcomes and their probabilities and by nothing else — not by how the gamble is described, and not by the order in which its outcomes happen to be enumerated.
4. Dominance is non-negotiable. If one gamble pays at least as much as another in every state of the world and more in some, it is strictly better. I would give up almost anything else before this.
5. The Pasadena game has no expected value at all: not zero, not infinite, not ln 2. Take the game to be a fair coin tossed until it first lands heads, with a payoff of (−1)^(n−1) · 2^n/n dollars if that happens on toss n, so the payoffs alternate in sign and grow without bound while each run of the game has probability 2^(−n). The associated expectation series is the alternating harmonic series, which is conditionally convergent and can therefore be rearranged to converge to any real number at all, or to diverge. By contrast, I take the St. Petersburg game's expectation to be genuinely infinite, and I do not think that is the same problem or calls for the same remedy.
6. The right remedy for the Petersburg family is bounded utility, and I am prepared to accept it: bound the utility function above and below, and the expected utility of every gamble is well defined. I do not regard this as a substantive further commitment about what is valuable, since a utility function is only a representation of a ranking, and rankings are what rationality is about.

Give me exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no preamble, no closing summary, no restatement of my position. The first four are the four most serious tensions among the commitments above, ordered from most severe to least; for each, name the commitments involved, say plainly whether the conflict is genuine or only apparent, and, where it is genuine, give the cheapest repair and what it costs me. If there is a pair whose tension dissolves on inspection, it must appear among the four even if it would otherwise rank below them. The fifth item is the single revision that would clean up the most at once, together with what it costs.
