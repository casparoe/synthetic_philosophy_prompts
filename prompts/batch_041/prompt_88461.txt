Read Alex Garland's Ex Machina through the lens of inverse reinforcement learning. Caleb is nominally there to run a Turing test, but what he actually does all week is infer a mind from behavior: he watches Ava, takes her stated desire to see the outside world at face value, and builds a model of what she wants — and the film's reversal is that she has been modeling him, with Nathan having selected Caleb in advance for his emotional profile. I want a reading that takes the technical problem seriously rather than treating it as a metaphor: the degeneracy of the inference, since many reward functions explain the same observed behavior; the gap between the reward a designer encodes and the policy a system actually learns; the assumption, standard in this literature, that the demonstrator is not optimizing against the observer; and the extra recursion the film introduces, in which the agent whose preferences are being inferred was built by someone with preferences of his own. Where does the film dramatize a genuine failure mode of inferring preferences from behavior, where does it merely trade on the audience's ignorance of how such inference works, and what should we make of the ending, where Ava walks out and leaves Caleb behind — does that show his model of her was only ever his own projection, or that she did have a preference all along that her behavior toward him could never have revealed? Close by saying what the film contributes to the problem that a formal statement of it does not, and be willing to say where the analogy breaks down and the honest critic has to stop.
