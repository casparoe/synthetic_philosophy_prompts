An acquaintance of mine, a doctoral student in early-modern philosophy, recently gave me the following summary of Hobbes's relationship to geometry, and I should like you to audit it for accuracy. She said: 'Hobbes was a political philosopher who dabbled in mathematics; he became convinced that geometry was the model of all rigorous reasoning, and he spent his later years trying to solve the famous unsolved problems of the ancients — squaring the circle chief among them — in the belief that he had succeeded. His long-running feud with the Oxford mathematician John Wallis was a purely mathematical dispute, and when Wallis showed that his constructions were invalid, the matter was closed: Hobbes was simply not competent at mathematics, and the whole episode is a cautionary tale about philosophers straying into technical fields they do not understand. His underlying aim was the hope that if political philosophy could be made as rigorous as geometry, it might put an end to the civil and religious strife of his age.'

I suspect this is too tidy, and that it flattens whatever is genuinely interesting about Hobbes's engagement with geometry. Please take it claim by claim and tell me which parts a specialist in the period would accept, which they would wince at as simplifications, and which are plainly wrong. I am especially curious about what is supposed to be philosophically distinctive in Hobbes's attraction to geometry: I have a vague sense that it turned on the thought that we can demonstrate only what we ourselves construct, and that this bears on why he held civil philosophy to be capable of demonstration while natural philosophy was not — but I do not know whether that is a reading scholars agree on or one contested interpretation among several. Finally, does the moral she draws at the end — that the episode is a cautionary tale about philosophers wandering into technical fields — actually follow from the parts of her account that survive correction, or does it depend on the parts that need revising?

Throughout, please be careful to separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the period, and not to present a contested reading as though it were the consensus. Where you are offering an interpretation rather than reporting a fact, please say so.
