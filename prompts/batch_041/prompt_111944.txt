I run design review at a lab that builds autonomous software agents, and I have to sign off on whether each new system ships as a goal-directed agent — one that selects actions to maximize a stated objective — or an approval-directed one, where each action is chosen to maximize the approval of a human evaluator who has been shown the agent's situation and its reasoning. These decisions are hard to reverse once a system is live, and my reviewers are engineers with no background in the theory. Write me a decision procedure they can run in a forty-five-minute meeting: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in goal-directed-with-constraints, and where it stops and hands the decision to me because the question is what we are willing to ship rather than a fact about the system. Two wrinkles it has to absorb. One team proposes implementing the approval function by sampling uniformly from the top tenth of candidate actions ranked by predicted approval, rather than maximizing approval; say whether and how that changes the analysis. And in some deployments the approver is not a person but a model standing in for one, so the procedure must say when that substitution means the design is no longer approval-directed at all. Then run it on three proposals: a coding agent whose patches a human reviews before merge; a support agent authorized to issue refunds below a fixed limit without review; and an overnight research agent that designs and runs its own experiments and reports in the morning. Include the proposal I am rooting for and show the procedure rejecting it, and close by saying plainly what the procedure cannot decide.
