I chair the standing committee on religion and public life in our jurisdiction's assembly, and I have to circulate a written opinion before we vote a week from Tuesday. You will not know our arrangement, so here it is. We have no established church, and our constitution forbids the state to establish any religion or to prefer one religious society over another. But we inherited, from the 1870s, a system under which the revenue office collects a religious levy on behalf of the religious societies the assembly has recognized. The levy is a fixed percentage of a member's income-tax liability, withheld by employers alongside the income tax, so the money never passes through the society's hands; membership is whatever each society's own register says it is, and for the older societies that register is a baptismal roll, so most members joined as infants; the state returns the proceeds to the society, less its collection costs, as a per-capita grant. Separately, the state funds chaplains in prisons, hospitals, and the armed forces from general revenue, and by long custom those posts have gone to the recognized societies.

Three matters are before us. A man complains that he resigned from one of the recognized societies in 2016, but that his employer went on withholding the levy until 2023, because he sent his resignation to his congregation rather than to the registry office the society's internal rules designate; he wants eight years refunded. Our staff have drafted an amendment in three clauses: first, membership in a recognized society lapses unless the member files an affirmative declaration every ten years, with the state sending the reminder and the form; second, resignation takes effect when a single form is filed with any government office, with the levy stopped and refunded from the date of the member's first attempt to resign, whatever the society's internal rules provide; third, chaplaincy in prisons, hospitals, and the armed forces is funded from general revenue and open on equal terms to non-religious counselors, with the recognized societies competing for posts they now hold by custom. And an ethical-culture society that calls itself non-theistic has applied for recognition and for the levy, which the older societies oppose on the ground that a body with no God and no doctrine of membership cannot be a religious society for these purposes.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate. Lead with a bottom line I can defend in the vote: a recommendation on the amendment, clause by clause, including what you would change and why, together with a disposition of the complaint, a decision on the ethical society's application, and effective dates. Say who should bear the cost of any retroactive refunds, the state that collected them or the society that received them, and why. Treat honestly the two strongest objections on the losing side: that any state collection for religion is an establishment, so the levy and the chaplaincy should be abolished outright, and that the ten-year clause is the state legislating religious membership and therefore violates the very neutrality it claims to serve. Reason from our statute and from principle; several members keep invoking England's establishment, the German levy, French laïcité, and American disestablishment, and I do not want the opinion resting on any claim about foreign law or history that we cannot check from the papers in front of us. No citations or reading list, please; this goes into the record as an opinion, not a scholarly note. Write it in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a serious letter, in American English, in under a thousand words.
