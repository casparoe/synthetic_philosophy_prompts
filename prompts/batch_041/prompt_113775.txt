I teach a first-year metaphysics-of-time unit (I'm not a philosopher of physics by training — did a physics degree then drifted into philosophy, so I'm definately out of my depth on the relativity end) and I need a piece of teaching material that isn't another essay: a dialogue, something students can read and then pull apart in seminar. Set it in the hotel bar on the last night of a 'Time and Physics' conference, maybe 11pm, both characters a bit drunk and past being polite with each other. Character A is a block-universe person who treats the Rietdijk–Putnam argument as basically a proof: take the relativity of simultaneity, add the assumption that if event e is real for observer O and event f is real for observer O′, and O and O′ are suitably related, then e and f are both real simpliciter, and you get that every event in the manifold is equally real — no privileged present, end of story. She's a bit insufferable about it and thinks presentists are simply refusing to follow an argument. Character B is a presentist who actually knows the physics; she isn't confused about anything technical, she just thinks the argument loads the dice, and she's the one who has to do the work of locating exactly which premise she rejects and why. I want real back-and-forth: every time A makes a move, B responds to that specific move, and vice versa. Neither is a strawman, and B should get at least one genuinely good reply that A can't just wave away — and A should get one too, I don't want a rigged dialogue where the clever presentist wins at the end.

Some specific things I want them to fight about, because these are the bits my students get lost on and the textbook treatments gloss them: (i) whether the transitivity premise is really about 'being real for' or about 'being simultaneous with', and whether sliding between those is a cheat; (ii) the conventionality of simultaneity — if B says simultaneity is partly a matter of clock-synchronisation convention, A should push on whether that makes the whole argument merely verbal or actually dissolves it, and B has to commit to which; (iii) the 'here' analogy — 'here' is always somewhere and 'is here relative to' isn't transitive, so does the presentist get the block universe for free by that parallel or does the analogy break, and where exactly; (iv) whether the block view can explain the felt difference between remembering and anticipating, with A giving the entropy/records answer and then having to admit how much of that answer survives if you drop the block picture; and (v) A should at some point accuse B of a view in which the present moment does no explanatory work, and B should reply with something that isn't just 'it's primitive'.

Before you write, brainstorm in a few bullets the three or four load-bearing premises of the Rietdijk–Putnam argument and, for each, the strongest way to deny it — I want to see you've located the joints before you dramatise them. Then write the dialogue (600–900 words, stage directions no longer than a clause, no narrator telling us what they 'really mean'). End it unresolved but not vague: one of them should concede something real, and the last line should leave a specific question hanging that a student could take away and argue about. Don't have either of them cite literature or recite who said what historically — I want the arguments themselves in their own words, not a tour of the debate. You're a philosopher of physics writing for undergraduates who are smart but haven't read any of this yet; think hard about whether each reply is one the other character would actually have to take seriously.
