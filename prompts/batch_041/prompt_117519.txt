I am a doctoral student working on responsibility-sensitive egalitarianism, and I keep circling a pattern that I suspect is either a familiar problem under a name I have not encountered, or a problem that has not been isolated because it looks like two other problems at once.

The pattern concerns how we decide whether a disadvantage is one its bearer should bear. Take the standard apparatus: a disadvantage is unjust insofar as it traces to bad luck, and permissible insofar as it traces to the agent's own choices. What I notice is that in practice the verdict does not turn on the causal history. It turns on the comparison, and the comparison slides without any change in the causal facts.

Two cases I use in teaching. First, a worker takes a job in a mine knowing it carries a high risk of a specific injury, and is injured. Compare him with the counterpart who took the safer job at lower pay, and the shortfall looks like a stake he wagered and lost. Compare him with himself as he would have been had the injury not occurred, and the same shortfall looks like a bolt from the blue. Nothing about what he did or why changes between the two comparisons. Second, a student chooses to study a field with poor employment prospects because it is the only thing she has ever wanted to do. Compare her with the classmates who went to law school, and her prospects look like the price of a decision. Compare her with the person she would have been had her tastes been formed in a different household, and the same prospects look like the upshot of something she never chose. Again the causal facts are held fixed; only the term of comparison moves.

The second case suggests something the first does not: the choice of comparison is not innocent, because it selects how far back along the chain we treat the agent as the author of her situation, and hence how much of the situation counts as her doing. And in what I read, the comparison appears to be selected by the verdict the author wants rather than the reverse. Cases are set up already containing the preferred comparison, so the argument appears to run from causal history to verdict when it runs from verdict to comparison.

I know the standard regress objection, that choices trace back to unchosen tastes and circumstances, so that genuine option luck may be empty. That is not my pattern, since mine survives even if we stipulate the causal history and grant that some choices are genuinely the agent's. The question is which counterfactual does the normative work, and the theory seems not to say. I have also looked at the reference class problem in probability and at the analogous worry about baselines in the measurement of harm, but there the issue is which class or baseline yields a correct probability or a correct magnitude, whereas here the issue is which counterfactual fixes the loss at all, when the loss is meant to be the input to a verdict rather than a quantity to be estimated.

So: does this have a name? If it does, I want the name plus an account of what it covers that my cases do not, because I would rather learn that I am redescribing something established than coin a term for what already has one. If it does not, I want a name, a definition, and the edges — in particular, whether the trouble is a gap in the theory's specification, an illicit step in how the theory is applied, or evidence that the distinction between option luck and brute luck does no independent work at all. Those are three diagnoses and I doubt they can all be right. I would also like to know whether anything turns on the grain at which the choice is described, or whether that is a different problem wearing the same clothes.
