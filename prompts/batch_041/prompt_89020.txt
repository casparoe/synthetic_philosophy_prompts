I'm in a little reading group that's working through a popular book on free will, and last week we hit the chapter on Frankfurt cases. The author's summary goes roughly like this, and I want to know how much of it I should trust before I go repeating it to people.

"Frankfurt's classic paper demolished the principle of alternative possibilities, which said that you're only morally responsible for what you do if you could have done otherwise. His counterexample: Black wants Jones to perform some action, and has arranged things so that if Jones were about to decide against it, Black would step in and force him to do it anyway. As it turns out, Jones decides to do it on his own, for his own reasons, and Black never lifts a finger. Since Black stood ready to intervene, Jones could not have done otherwise -- yet we still blame him, and rightly so. So the principle is false. And with it goes the main reason anyone ever worried that determinism is incompatible with free will: even if determinism is true, and nobody could ever have done anything other than what they did, moral responsibility survives."

I'm pretty convinced by this, honestly - it seems like a clean arguement. But a friend in the group said the chapter was "doing something sneaky" and then wouldn't say what he meant. Can you walk me through it clause by clause and tell me what a philosopher who actually works on this stuff would nod along to, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong? In particular I want to know whether that last sentence -- the bit about determinism and responsibility surviving -- really follows from the counterexample, or whether the author is smuggling in a conclusion the case itself doesn't support. And if there's a standard reply from people who still want to defend the principle, tell me what it is and whether it actually works.
