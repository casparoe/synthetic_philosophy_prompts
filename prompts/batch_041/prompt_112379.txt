Last Friday I got into a two-hour argument with my friend Devi over drinks and it's still bugging me. The trigger was a guy we both know, Tom, who genuinely says he *prefers* working somewhere rigid and deferential -- he claims flat teams make him anxious and he likes knowing exactly where he stands. Devi's line was that Tom doesn't have a preference at all, he has internalized hierarchy, i.e. a bias, and that anyone who treats it as a real preference is doing apologetics for the status quo. I pushed back that "your preference was shaped by your circumstances, so it doesn't count" is a move that eats literally every preference anyone has ever had, including hers, and that it quietly hands whoever's doing the diagnosing an enormous amount of power to overrule the people they're diagnosing -- which is the exact power I'd want a story about before I hand it over. We never got anywhere and I'm still not sure which of us was being dumb.

Anyway I teach an upper-level seminar in moral psychology (mostly 3rd/4th years plus a couple of MA students, some background in ethics, none in empirical psych) and I want this to be the final. I don't want a question they can answer by reciting the adaptive-preference stuff or the implicit-bias stuff -- they've all read enough pop versions of that to bullshit for pages. What I want is the crux above, on the table. In class we ended up building a scrappy little framework: three tests you might run on a moral preference to decide whether it's authoritative -- (1) origin, where did it come from (socialization, disgust reaction, trauma, whatever); (2) counterfactual, would it survive under idealized conditions, full info, no coercion, no scarcity; (3) functional, what would it cost the person to give it up and what work does it do in their life (identity, belonging, self-respect). The students got weirdly attached to this thing, and I want the question to force them to notice that the three tests come apart -- there are cases where origin says bias, counterfactual says fine, functional says leave it alone -- and that each test has its own failure mode (genetic fallacy; whose idealization, exactly; and the functional test sliding into "does the preference make you sad", which is not the same question at all). Bonus if they have to say whether "bias" is even the right category here, or whether the real question is just which preferences we're permitted to discount, which is a different and harder question.

So: write me the essay question (one question, doable in a 60-minute closed-book exam, so no citations needed and the model answer shouldn't lean on any), then a model answer of roughly 800 words that shows what a strong response actually looks like, including at least one worked case where the three tests disagree and how the answer adjudicates between them, and finally a marking scheme out of 25 that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain, with one line per point on the mistake that usually costs marks.
