I write the little placards for a natural history museum -- the ones next to the specimens that say what a feature is "for". Visitors read them as settled fact, and once a label is printed we're stuck with it for years. My problem is the evolution-as-engineer framing: something looks built for a job, we write that it evolved to do that job, and then a consultant tells us we've overclaimed. I want a procedure my three writers can run on any "this evolved for X" claim before it goes to the printers, in about twenty minutes, with no access to the literature (so no citing papers -- they can't look anything up). Ground it in the adaptationism fight -- Gould and Lewontin on spandrels, exaptation, developmental and architectural constraint, drift -- but as steps a non-specialist can follow, not as a lecture. I need the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure just stops and says "don't claim selection here", and where it has to hand off to a curator because the call is about values or about how much hedging a label can carry. Then run it on three labels: the male nipple; the giraffe's neck, which our marketing people adore as "evolved to reach the high leaves"; and a human-origins panel claiming we lost our body hair to stay cool while running. At least one of those should come out in a way the exhibits team won't like -- say which and why. And be honest about where the procedure will still let a just-so story through.
