ok so this is a weird request. im in a grad seminar on non-classical logic (im the oldest one there, i do medical records review as a day job) and our last session is in two weeks, and everyone takes turns bringing something for the end. i want to bring a poem. but not a cute one.

heres the thing thats been eating at me. my grandmother died in february and her will has two clauses that say flatly opposite things about the house — one leaves it to my aunt, one leaves it to my mom — and theyre in the same document, same day, same notary. the lawyer says one of them has to give. my mom says obviously the later clause, my aunt says obviously the one that names her, and i keep saying, to everyones irritation, well, why does one of them have to give. which is not a thing you can say at a kitchen table.

and then i started reading for this seminar and realized theres a whole tradition that says my kitchen-table line isnt just me being difficult. so i want a poem that shows that, for an audience of people who already know the material, without being a lecture.

Form: a pantoum, 16 lines (four quatrains). You probably know it but just in case: the second and fourth lines of each stanza come back as the first and third lines of the next stanza, and the last stanza has to use the first and third lines of the very first stanza as its second and fourth lines. So every line gets said twice and the poem ends by bringing the opening back. I want that structure to be the argument, not decoration: a line that enters as a concession or a hedge should return later as an assertion, and the poem should close with the first line and the third line of stanza one sitting right next to each other in the final stanza, so the poem ends holding both and doesnt apologize for it. Loose meter, rhyme if it wants, dont force it.

What it needs to get right, because these people will wince:

- the speaker is NOT saying everything is true. thats the stupid version. only this one thing, this one contradiction, is both. the poem should make the difference felt, maybe in a line about how the poem isnt claiming the whole world is on fire, just this one page.
- rejecting the inference from a contradiction to absolutely anything is one move; actually believing some contradictions are true is a stronger, separate move. the speaker should make the stronger move, and should notice that they could have stopped at the weaker one (a database can be inconsistent without anyone believing anything, etc.) and that they didnt want to stop there.
- somewhere the poem should register that a very ordinary piece of reasoning — it's either A or B, it's not A, so it's B — is exactly whats not available here. not as a technical announcement, as a line someone would actually say.
- and i really want the negation thing in there, because its the part that made me sit down. theres a difference between denying a claim in the toggling sense, where if the claim is both true and false then its denial is also both, and denying it in the sense of just ruling it out, where saying it isnt true settles the matter. the speaker should want to say the contradiction is false and also not want that to be the end of the story, and a reader who knows the distinction should see the poem doing it on purpose. one or two lines, not a paragraph.

dont name any logicians and dont quote anyone, i dont want it to sound like a term paper and i dont want to be the person who misquotes someone at the last session. no footnotes. it should stand up as a poem for someone who doesnt know any of this — my mom might read it — while someone whos been in the seminar all term should find it correct and maybe a little uncomfortable.

title optional. thanks.
