I am preparing a seminar unit on the persistence of material objects, and I want to use the Ship of Theseus not as a riddle with a stock answer but as a stress test for candidate criteria of identity over time. I have therefore drawn up six criteria and a set of scenarios, and I would like you to rank the criteria against them.

The six criteria, understood as applying to artifacts such as ships:

C1. Material continuity: an object persists just so long as a majority of its original constituent parts are still assembled in it, and ceases when they are not.

C2. Causal continuity: an object persists just so long as each later stage is produced from the earlier stage by a continuous, non-branching causal process of the appropriate kind, however much matter is exchanged along the way.

C3. Pattern identity: an object persists just so long as its functional and structural organisation is preserved, irrespective of which particular parts realise it.

C4. Institutional designation: an object persists just so long as the relevant institution — museum, registry, or legal order — designates it, by its own rules, as the continuation.

C5. Four-dimensional worm identity: what we call persistence is simply the relation between temporal parts of a single four-dimensional whole, so there is no further question to answer.

C6. Criterion-relative conventionalism: identity claims are never true or false absolutely but only relative to a chosen criterion, so the dispute has no unique resolution.

The scenarios:

S1. Gradual replacement: over many years every plank and timber is replaced one at a time and the removed parts discarded; no single step involves more than one replacement.

S2. Reassembly: the discarded original parts are later recovered and reassembled into a ship structurally identical to the original, while the continuously repaired ship remains in service.

S3. Fission: the original parts are divided into two halves, each of which, together with new timber, is built into a ship structurally identical to the original; both are launched.

S4. Rotation: the ship is renewed by removing one plank at a time and reinstalling it elsewhere on the same ship, so that at every moment the ship is composed of exactly the original planks, yet no plank ends where it began and the arrangement is gradually permuted until every plank has occupied every position.

S5. Threshold probe: as in S1, but replacement proceeds in batches, so that at one point exactly half the original parts remain and at the next step fewer than half.

Please rank the six criteria from most to least adequate, grouping them into tiers. Judge each criterion by how well it satisfies two constraints at once: (i) it returns a verdict in every scenario and leaves none open; and (ii) its verdicts depend on no threshold, convention, or stipulation that could reasonably be set otherwise. For each criterion, give a score from 0 to 10 for overall adequacy, name the single scenario that is most diagnostic of its weaknesses, and explain in one or two sentences why it lands where it does. Finally, identify any two of the six that you take to be notational variants of one another, and say whether adopting your top-ranked criterion would commit the seminar to any particular view about the persistence of persons, or whether the ship can be settled while personal identity is left untouched.
