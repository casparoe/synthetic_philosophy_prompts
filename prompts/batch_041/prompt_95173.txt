I'm in an MA program (mostly philosophy of mind, undergrad was cog sci) and I have a seminar paper due on whether we should say that LLMs have beliefs. My cohort has split into two camps that both annoy me: the people who think it's a category error to attribute any mental state to a model, and the people who think the only real question is whether the attribution is useful for prediction, full stop. I've been arguing about this with a friend for weeks, mostly over beer, which probably explains my confidence; my supervisor read a draft and said it was either trivial or false and I should figure out which. So I've rewritten it and I'd like you to poke holes before I commit to the new version.

The argument, compressed. Start from Dennett's intentional stance and the real-patterns line: ascribing beliefs is a tool, licensed by predictive and explanatory payoff, and it's not fiction-talk because the patterns it picks out are really there. Fine. Now apply that to a chat model and the payoffs look decent — I can predict a lot of its behavior by treating it as an agent with views about the world, and it's a much better compression than trying to simulate the weights. So the Dennettian move seems to hand the pro-attribution camp the win for free. My paper's move is to say that belief-talk has always been doing two jobs that happen to travel together in animals and come apart here. One is the predictive job. The other is what I've been calling the accountability hook: ordinary belief ascriptions license demands — be consistent, own what you said, give a reason, answer for it — and they make the subject criticizable when it doesn't. I propose a rough diagnostic, the pushback test: tell the system it contradicted its earlier claim and see what it does. A believer is disposed to either defend the old claim, revise it, or at least register the tension. The model will smoothly agree that it was wrong, apologize, produce the correction, and then, in a fresh context with the framing reversed, assert the original claim with the same fluency — the response is fully explained by the prompt, not by anything the system is on the hook for. Second claim, which I'm less sure about: for animals you can usually compress the ascriptions into one more or less coherent belief set (noisy, but convergent), whereas for these systems the best compressions are prompt-indexed and mutually inconsistent, so there's no "the" belief set for the real-patterns defense to be about — I've been calling this pattern drift. Then I try to rescue something by replacing the binary question with a graded one: how much of the belief-role a state occupies, from merely predicting, to being treated as committed, to being accountable.

What I want from you is not a summary of the debate and definitely not a reading list — assume I've read the obvious stuff and can't look anything up anyway. I want the weakest link. Specifically: is my two-jobs split a fair reading of how the intentional stance is supposed to work, or am I building a strawman that any decent Dennettian would just dissolve by saying the hook is itself just more prediction (predicting how the system responds to social pressure)? If it does dissolve, does my paper have anything left? Is the pushback test actually a good test, or does it smuggle in something it shouldn't — a stable self-model, or caring about consistency, or phenomenology — such that it proves too much and would exclude plenty of things we happily call believers? Does pattern drift do any real work beyond the boring observation that behavior is context-sensitive, and if it doesn't, should I cut it? And tell me honestly whether the graded conclusion is a real position or just a way of not answering. If the whole thing is definately trivial, say so and tell me what the non-trivial version of the question would be.
