I am an undergraduate in a political theory seminar on Mill, liberalism, and empire, and I also have a background in clinical ethics from a bioethics minor and two years volunteering on a hospital ethics consult service. For my final paper I want to attempt something the seminar has not prepared me for, and that I suspect cannot be done by summarizing the secondary literature.

Restate Mill's defence of British rule in India in the vocabulary of clinical ethics: capacity and its assessment, informed consent, paternalism and its justifications, beneficence versus autonomy, surrogate decision-making and the best-interests standard, the least restrictive alternative, conflicts of interest between ward and guardian, review and reversibility of guardianship, and the requirement that incapacity be decision-specific and time-limited. Make the restatement as faithful as you can, using the structure of Mill's own argument rather than his biography: the claim that a people may be in a state of nonage, that a despotic or tutelary government can be legitimate while that condition lasts, that the aim must be the ward's improvement, and that the guardian's authority is therefore bounded by that aim. Mill held this alongside the harm principle and the sovereignty of the individual over himself, so the restatement has to say how those commitments were supposed to fit together, and whether the developmental premise is a narrow empirical exception or a load-bearing part of his liberalism.

Then give me the residue report, in both directions. Treat that as the real deliverable.

First, what does not survive the translation? I am especially interested in three readings of the developmental premise: (a) a regrettable concession to what Mill took to be facts, conceptually isolated from the rest of his liberalism; (b) a premise his liberalism actually needs, because liberty is valuable only to those capable of improvement; (c) an artifact of his employment at the India House, which he might have abandoned outside that role. Say which reading the clinical vocabulary can even express, and which it cannot.

Second, what does clinical ethics have that Mill's position cannot express? Consider and rule on each of these: capacity as decision-specific rather than global; the ward's own values as authoritative rather than the guardian's conception of the ward's good; the ban on the guardian profiting from the guardianship; the demand that guardianship be reviewed and reversible; the weight of the ward's dissent even when capacity is compromised; and the absence of any notion of a collective patient or of a people as ward. Then consider the reverse direction: where does Mill's position license something clinical ethics forbids, such as permanent wardship, a guardian who is simultaneously judge of capacity and beneficiary of the arrangement, and no exit? Does the clinical frame have the resources to call that a misuse of paternalism rather than a straightforward application of it?

Finally, adjudicate. Is Mill's position best understood as a coherent extension of a paternalist logic that clinical ethics already contains, or is it something the paternalist frame actively distorts, for instance a claim about the moral standing of a political community, or about historical time, that has no clinical analogue? Where the translation licenses a conclusion Mill would reject, say whether the fault lies with the translation, with Mill's inconsistency, or with the clinical framework's limits. Do not smuggle in the clinical framework's answers and call them translation, and do not dissolve the residue by declaring Mill simply insincere.

No citations are needed; I care about the structure of the argument and the residue, not the bibliography.
