I'm 71, I retired two years ago after 35 years as a field ecologist — wetland invertebrates mostly, plus the birds that eat them — and since retiring I've been reading philosophy for the first time in my life. I'm about two years in. I can follow an argument, I know I'm an amateur, and I don't know the literature. Which is why I'd rather you not name any philosophers or books or studies at all: I won't be able to check the attribution and I'll get it wrong at the podium.

Here's what I need. In three weeks I'm giving the after-dinner speech at our county land trust's annual dinner. About 180 people, volunteers and donors — the ones who've spent twenty years pulling garlic mustard out of the fen, who brought the marsh back from a drained cornfield, and who want, on this one night of the year, to be told it was worth it and that the marsh is a good thing. My old grad student will be there; she teaches environmental ethics now, so there's one person in the room who'll catch it if I hand-wave. Everyone else is not a philosopher and doesn't want to be. The board president has asked me to keep it light. The dinner ends with a toast to the marsh and I'm the one raising the glass.

The problem is I can't give the speech I used to give, because of the animals. Not the species — the individuals, the ones I spent a career counting. From my own sweep-net data: at peak, the restored ponds hold tens of thousands of individual animals, and I'd estimate 95 percent of them are eaten, parasitized, or starved within a few weeks. Old age is basically not a cause of death out there. I have footage from 1998 of a red-winged blackbird nest where a snake took four nestlings over two days, and a clip of parasitoid wasp larvae coming out of a caterpillar, and I used to show those clips to Rotary Clubs as wonders of nature. I still feel they were wonders. I can't stop seeing the other thing either.

And the part that's really eating me: we raised the carrying capacity of that marsh. That's the point of restoration — more habitat, more animals. But if nearly every animal born there dies young and dies badly, then more carrying capacity means more births, which means more of those deaths, and I have to stand up in front of the people who paid for it. In my notebook I've started calling it the second harvest. I don't think it's an argument against restoring the marsh. I honestly don't know what it is. I don't want you to tell me what it is either, not in the speech.

So write the six-minute speech. Constraints, in no particular order:

- No quotes, no citations, no names of philosophers or books or studies. Don't attribute any idea to anyone. If you need an idea, put it in my mouth, with my own numbers.
- Don't say anything to the room that I think is false just to comfort them: no balance of nature, no everything happens for a reason, no they're in a better place, no nature knows best. I'd rather have the awkward silence.
- Don't say anything false to make me feel better either. No "and that's why the suffering doesn't count," unless you actually think there's a step there and you show me the step.
- It has to work as a speech, not a lecture: one concrete image from the marsh that earns its place, real warmth toward the people in that room rather than a throat-clearing before bad news, and it has to end on the toast without feeling like a bait and switch. Funny beats solemn.
- Six minutes is roughly 850 words, but write it as speech — short sentences, nothing I'd trip over saying out loud.

Then, in a separate short note to me and not in the speech: tell me the one move you made that you're least sure about, and tell me whether the speech I asked for is actually writable — whether the honest version can also be the warm version. If you don't think it can, say so and give me the closest thing that can.
