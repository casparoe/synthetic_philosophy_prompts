I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have been asked to give the eulogy at the memorial for my oldest friend, Hal, who died last month at seventy-four. Hal was a philosopher, and for the last twenty years of his life he worked out an account of promising that he insisted was correct and that almost none of his colleagues accepted. His view, which he put to me many times, was this: a promise is not a way of giving someone a reason to rely on you. It is a way of handing someone a piece of your own authority over yourself, and you do not get that piece back until the thing is done. He drew what he took to be the consequence. A promise made to someone who dies before you can keep it does not lapse. You still owe it — not to her memory, not to her family, not to your own self-respect, but to her, because she is the one still holding the piece of authority you handed over, and there is no longer anyone alive who can hand it back. Eight years ago, in her last month, he promised his wife Nora that he would keep her garden alive for as long as he lived. He was a terrible gardener. He kept it anyway, badly, to the end.

Half the room at the memorial will be philosophers who know his account and will be listening to see whether I get it right. The other half will be Nora's family, who are not philosophers and will be listening for comfort. Please write the eulogy.

It has to be built on his account rather than decorated with it: the account should do the structural work, above all in the final paragraph. Nothing in the speech may presuppose that Nora can now be reassured, disappointed, proud, or let down, or that Hal kept the promise for the sake of his own character, the family's feelings, or anyone's memory. No jargon, no names of philosophers, no -isms, no quotations. Ordinary speech throughout.

Hard limits: 450 words at most, no more than five paragraphs, and end on a single closing sentence of no more than twenty-five words — the one line the room will still be repeating at the reception.
