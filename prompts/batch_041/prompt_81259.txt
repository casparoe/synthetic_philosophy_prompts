I chair the methods and standards committee of a general science journal. The editor has referred a manuscript to us under our policy on complexity claims. The authors compare two cosmological models, one a modified-gravity model and one a cold-dark-matter model. To argue that the modified-gravity model is simpler, and therefore deserves a higher prior, they gzip the source code implementing each model and report that the modified-gravity code compresses to 4,312 bytes while the cold-dark-matter code compresses to 4,847 bytes. They call the 535-byte difference an estimate of the difference in Kolmogorov complexity, and they frame the argument in terms of Solomonoff's universal prior: the model with the shorter description has the higher prior probability, and the gzip lengths are a practical proxy for the relevant description lengths. A referee has objected that gzip is not a universal computer, that the measured difference is an artifact of the compressor, the programming language, and the arbitrary choice of which parts of the model count as the model, and that the result would flip under a different compressor. The authors reply that the invariance theorem guarantees that any two universal machines differ by at most an additive constant, so their result is robust in principle. The referee replies that the constant is unknown and could easily exceed 535 bytes, and that in any case gzip is not universal. The committee is split: some members regard any compression-based complexity claim as pseudo-quantitative, while others note that the authors are using a heuristic common in practice and that rejecting it would require a line the journal can defend. The editor wants a decision memo she can act on, and she wants the committee to settle a policy question: may authors use off-the-shelf compression as a proxy for Kolmogorov complexity in this journal, and if so, under what conditions?

Please draft the committee's short written opinion. It should recommend a concrete disposition of the manuscript -- accept, reject, or revise with specified conditions -- and give the reasoning in a form the authors and the referee can both respond to. It should engage the strongest version of the authors' invariance argument rather than dismissing it, and it should distinguish what is defensible in their approach from what is not. It should also state a general policy for future submissions that invoke Occam's razor, Solomonoff induction, or minimum description length, including what authors must report and what claims they may not make. If the disposition is revise or reject, say what specific analysis would be required for a resubmission. The memo should be addressed to the editor, not to me, and should be written for a scientifically literate but non-specialist readership. Do not include citations, reading lists, or references; the editor needs the committee's reasoning, not a literature review.
