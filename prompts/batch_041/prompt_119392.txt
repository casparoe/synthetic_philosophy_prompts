I sit on the ethics committee of a mid-sized teaching hospital — I'm the non-clinician member — and I've spent enough years reading rationalist blogs and forums to get suspicious whenever someone says that hard cases simply have to be judged "case by case." Our consult notes go back about a decade, and read in a batch they have convinced me that we are in fact running a decision rule that nobody has ever written down. Our policy requires the committee to name the closest prior consult whenever it makes a recommendation, which is how I first noticed the pattern; the recommendations are advisory, so the attending sometimes does whatever he had already decided to do, and that is part of why I care more about getting the rule right than about winning this particular vote.

Our track record, as compactly as I can put it. (1) A 68-year-old woman with metastatic pancreatic cancer had a directive refusing "CPR, a breathing machine, and the ICU." She came in with pneumonia and respiratory failure, awake and frightened, and the committee recommended against intubation and against transfer to the ICU; she died on the ward two days later. (2) A 59-year-old man with severe COPD had a directive refusing "being kept on a machine when there's no getting off it." He came in with a pneumonia the team judged treatable, needed nine days of ventilation, was weaned, went home, and told us afterwards that he was glad we hadn't gone by the form; the committee had recommended treating him. (3) A 74-year-old woman with advanced dementia had a directive refusing "a feeding tube and antibiotics for infections once I no longer know my own family." She no longer recognized her son; when the aspiration pneumonia came she was moaning and pulling at her gown; her son demanded that everything be done; the committee recommended comfort care only, and she died. (4) A 51-year-old man with no serious diagnoses had filled out a form twelve years earlier at an employer health fair saying that he did not want "heroic measures in a terminal condition." He arrived with a severe but fully treatable sepsis, and his brother insisted that we honor the form and let him die; the committee recommended full treatment, and he walked out of the hospital three weeks later.

The new consult is a 63-year-old woman with a progressive motor neuron disease. Three years ago, while she was still working, she wrote: "If I reach the point where I cannot breathe on my own, I do not want to be kept alive on a ventilator." She is now in the ICU on a ventilator after a chest infection. The team's view is that with two to four weeks of support she has roughly even odds of being weaned and going home with worse lungs than before, and that if weaning fails she would need a tracheostomy and long-term ventilation. She is at most lightly sedated, oriented, and not depressed; asked directly, she says she wants to live, and she asks about her garden and her granddaughter's wedding. During her worst spells of breathlessness she says she has had enough. The psychiatric consultant says she can state a choice and give reasons, but that her capacity fluctuates with her oxygenation, and the team is split on whether she is currently able to revoke a directive she wrote while well. Her brother is her proxy and wants everything done; her daughter says her mother never wanted to be on a machine. What should we recommend if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which of the four prior consults is the closest analog, and which ones look closest without actually being so? I am less interested in the verdict than in the decision rule: give me the two or three rules that fit our four rulings equally well, say what each one predicts here, tell me which you would adopt and why, and — if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case — spell out what principle we would be committing ourselves to with each choice, and name the next case that would tell the rules apart. I don't need a literature review or citations; reason from the track record.
