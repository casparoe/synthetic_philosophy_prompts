One of my collegues in the epistemology reading group sent round a short precis of the knowledge account of assertion and asked whether it was fair, and I said I'd get back to them. I'd rather not wing it, so can you do the sentence-by-sentence audit for me? Their words, roughly:

The knowledge account says that you may assert a proposition only if you know it -- knowledge is the norm of assertion, the thing that makes an assertion warranted. This one norm explains why we don't assert lottery propositions (I can't tell you my ticket lost, even though it almost certainly did) and why sentences of the Moore-paradoxical shape -- saying that it's raining but that I don't know it is -- strike us as absurd. Since knowledge entails truth, the knowledge account is really just a strengthened version of the truth account, and it beats the belief account for the same reason: belief can't be what licenses assertion, because you can believe things that are false, whereas knowledge guarantees you're right. The rival truth account, meanwhile, can't handle the lottery -- if truth were enough, I could cheerfully announce my ticket lost. And all this matters practically, because knowledge is the thing that gets passed along in testimony: you're entitled to rely on my word only if I know. So knowledge is what assertion is answerable to, and if you know, you're in the clear to say so -- that's why we hold each other to it.
