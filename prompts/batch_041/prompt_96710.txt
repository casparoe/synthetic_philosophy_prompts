I've been running a small interview podcast for a few years now — long-form conversations with philosophers and social scientists, an audience that skews heavily toward people who came up through the rationalist blogosphere and will happily spend an evening arguing about decision theory in the comments but have no patience for throat-clearing — and next week's guest is a political philosopher who has spent most of her career defending a rather unfashionable line on state apologies for historical wrongs. Her position, as I understand it, runs roughly as follows. An apology is a speech act with two components: an acknowledgement that a wrong was done, and the placing of the apologiser under an obligation not to repeat it. A state, on her account, is a persisting corporate agent rather than a mere aggregate of citizens, so it can satisfy the first condition even for wrongs committed by generations now long dead; but it cannot satisfy what she calls the uptake condition, because the wronged parties are typically no longer alive and nobody living has standing to accept, release, or forgive on their behalf. From this she concludes that what we ordinarily call state apologies are not apologies at all but constitutive acts: they do not discharge a debt, they change what the polity is and what it will treat as authoritative going forward. That, she says, is why such apologies can be perfectly sincere even though no one is in a position to receive them, and equally why they are defective whenever they are offered as part of a settlement, a compensation package, or a diplomatic bargain — an apology tendered as the price of something else is no longer the kind of act it purports to be.

I'd like you to draft eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond getting her to restate this. I want at least one question each on: whether her account makes state apologies conceptually impossible rather than merely rare, and what work the distinction between 'impossible' and 'rare' is doing for her; how the constitutive reading is supposed to distinguish a genuine apology from a cynical declaration of a new official orthodoxy, given that both may change what the polity treats as authoritative; the non-identity problem — whether a polity that has since altered its constitution, borders, or membership is the same agent that committed the wrong, and what follows for her view if it is not; whether the account has anything to say about apologies for wrongs that are ongoing rather than historical, where the uptake condition could in principle be met; and the asymmetry between apologising and forgiving, specifically whether her view implies that the dead can be wronged but never released from the wrong, and whether that is a feature or a reductio. For every question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and press where the view is actually vulnerable rather than where it is comfortable; she is fond of deflecting objections by saying they assume a reparative model of apology, so I'd like at least one follow-up that makes that deflection harder to reach for. No questions about how she came to the topic, and none asking what she is working on next. Please write the whole thing in British English.
