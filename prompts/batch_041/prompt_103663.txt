I'm writing an essay on A. J. Ayer, and rather than just summarizing what he thought about life after death, I want to use his own near-death experience as a test case for those views. As I understand his position, he was a logical positivist who thought statements about surviving death are either ordinary empirical claims that happen to be false or, when they purport to describe some immaterial realm, not even meaningful; he also seems to have held that the idea of a person persisting without a body is barely intelligible, and that the supposed evidence for survival is worthless. Then in 1988, already in poor health, he choked on a piece of food, his heart stopped for about four minutes, and afterwards he reported a vivid episode — a red light, and two beings he felt were responsible for space and time — and said it left him slightly less certain that death would be the end of him, though he still expected it to be and did not treat the experience as proof of anything. What I'd like is a careful analysis of that episode through his own theory, and then an honest account of where the theory breaks. Specifically: does his verificationism even permit the experience to count as evidence about survival, given that he was not irreversibly dead and later recovered, and if not, what exactly is it evidence of? Can the emotivist side of his view accommodate the fact that a lifelong materialist's own attitude shifted at all, or does that shift sit awkwardly with the claim that such talk merely expresses feeling? And what does the case show about his theory in the end — a confirmation, an anomaly, or a situation his framework never anticipated? Please be disciplined about the mapping and say where the theory is simply silent rather than forcing it to speak.
