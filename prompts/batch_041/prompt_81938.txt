I'm a journalist — food, farming, and the odd bit of applied ethics for a weekend magazine — and thanks to a combination of flattery and a lost bet I've ended up sitting on the catering and ethics committee of my old university. We've got a decision to make before next term and I've been lumbered with writing the memo that goes out to the rest of the committee beforehand. I'm definately out of my depth, so could you draft it for me?

Here's the situation. Our catering contract is up for renewal and the sustainability office wants us to drop beef, keep chicken, and push the plant-based options hard. I was all for it until Priya, our animal-welfare person (she's a vet, annoyingly well read), made a couple of arguments I haven't been able to shake.

First, she says that if you count it out per calorie rather than per kilo, chicken is arguably worse than beef, not better — a chicken is a small animal, so you're killing far more individual birds than cows to put the same amount of food on the plate, and each of those birds has spent its whole short life in pretty grim conditions. A cow dies once; a tonne of chicken is hundreds of deaths. And she says the crops you have to grow to feed all those birds and pigs get harvested by machines that shred field mice, voles, nesting birds and heaven knows how many insects, so the "clean" chicken option is carrying a hidden body count that never makes it into the sustainability spreadsheet.

Second — and this is the bit that really got me — she says going fully plant-based might be the worst option of the lot if you take wild animals seriously and not just farmed ones. Her line is that crop farming is the single biggest thing humans do that kills wild animals: every field is a massacre of invertebrates and small mammals, and since there are astronomically more wild insects than farmed cows and chickens put together, a vegan menu could rack up more animal deaths than a beef one. She's honest that nobody knows whether insects feel anything, and that this is exactly the sort of question where the uncertainty is doing all the work. She also turns Marcus's land argument back on him: if you care about wild animals, she says, giving land back to forest or wetland doesn't save them — it just means more animals are born into a life that's mostly hunger, cold and being eaten alive, so "more habitat" isn't obviously kinder.

Our sustainability lead, Marcus, thinks this is sophistry. His position is that beef is obviously worst because it takes something like ten times the land per calorie of the alternatives, land that could be forest or wetland, and that "maybe insects have feelings" is no reason to keep serving burgers. He also points out we already buy higher-welfare chicken, and that the plant-based stuff is cheaper, which matters because we're broke.

So the committee is split three ways and I have to write something that actually helps us decide. What I need from you:

- A clear recommendation: which way should our default menu lean over the coming academic year — beef, chicken, or plant-based — and roughly what the rollout should look like (timeframe, what we do about the higher-welfare certification, whether we keep any beef at all for the formal dinners where it actually matters).
- The reasoning set out properly, including which facts we'd need before we could be confident, and which of Priya's and Marcus's points really do the work and which are red herrings.
- An honest treatment of the strongest argument against whatever you recommend. I have to read this out loud to people who'll disagree with it, so it can't just be a victory lap.
- A short section on what principle we'd be adopting if we go one way rather than the other, because I suspect we're really choosing between "cut the number of animals made to suffer" and "cut the total amount of suffering in the world," and those two don't obviously point in the same direction.

Keep it to something I can read aloud in ten minutes, and please write it in British English — I'll be filing a version of it to my editor as well. No need for a reading list or references; I can't check them anyway and this is a committee memo, not a term paper.
