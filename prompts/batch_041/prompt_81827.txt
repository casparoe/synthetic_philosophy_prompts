I have been collecting official apologies — statements in which a state, a church, or a university names a past wrong and declares itself sorry for it — and I keep running into a case I cannot classify. The statement is not evasive or hedged: it names the wrong plainly, uses the words "we apologize," and is received in the press as an apology. Yet it also assigns the wrong wholly to the past and to individuals who acted badly, and it accepts no present obligation — no reparation, no acknowledgment that anything is still ongoing. Critics call it a non-apology; defenders call it a genuine apology and say the critics want more than an apology can give. My own suspicion is that neither description fits, because what such a statement is doing is naming the wrong in order to date it rather than to own it: it establishes that something happened and that it is finished, and that is precisely the work it is meant to do. I have met the pattern in apologies for colonial violence, for institutional abuse, and for the internment of civilians in wartime. Is there an established term for this specific kind of official statement, as distinct from a non-apology, a qualified apology, and a bare expression of regret? And if there is no term, I would rather have a careful one than a catchy one: define it, say what it includes and excludes, and tell me where the line falls between it and an apology that simply failed.
