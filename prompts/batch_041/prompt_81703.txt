I run a monthly applied-ethics workshop for graduate students and a few interested outsiders, and I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for long enough that I would rather have a design with the mechanics spelled out than a warm description of the theme. Design me a single 90-minute session, 16 to 20 participants seated at tables of four or five, that makes the group feel the force of the claim that two parties with genuinely different values can both be made better off by trading across the issues they care about unequally — and that this is disturbing in a way that splitting the difference is not. The group should run into the phenomenon before anyone names it, and the discomfort should come from a deal that is obviously good for both sides and that some participants will refuse anyway.

The shape I have in mind, which you may improve but should not gut: four value blocs rather than political parties, six contested planks, and a private weighting of the planks that differs by bloc. Each bloc gets twenty chits and distributes them across the six planks in secret; the plank on which a bloc spends most is the plank it wins, and its score is the sum of the weights of the planks it wins. Round one is played with no communication, revealed and scored on the board so everyone sees the baseline. Then a negotiation round in which blocs may show their sheets, promise chits, and sign joint submissions, followed by a second reveal and a comparison against the baseline. One plank per bloc is marked as bedrock: that bloc must spend at least six chits on it and cannot accept any deal in which it fails to win it outright. Build in one overlap of the starred planks, so that one collision is unavoidable and pure waste.

I need you to actually design the planks, the weights, and the master matrix rather than gesture at them, and to check two properties of what you design. First, that at least three mutually beneficial bilateral trades exist, so the group has real choices rather than one obvious solution. Second, that the single largest gain lies between the two blocs with the least overlap in values, so the best deal available is also the most distasteful one, and the group has to watch itself hesitate. Tell me the baseline outcome and the best available trade as computed from your own matrix, so I can see how far the group falls short. Choose planks that are morally loaded without being identity-loaded, so the room argues about values rather than about politics, and say in a line or two why each plank earns its place.

Then give me: the materials list; a minute-by-minute timeline; what the facilitator says in the first five minutes and what the facilitator must not say until the debrief, including how to respond if someone names the phenomenon in minute ten or announces that this is utilitarianism with extra steps; the debrief itself, mapping specific moments in the session onto specific parts of the idea, including the moment a bloc discovers that a plank it values is free because nobody else wants it, the moment the rival blocs find the largest trade, the moment a bloc outside the deal loses, the bedrock constraint, and the refusals; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each; and a frank note on which features of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. I expect at least three such features, and I would rather you name the strongest one than the most convenient one — in particular, I want your honest account of the fact that the payoff numbers stipulate a common scale across blocs, which is precisely what the strongest objection to this whole way of thinking denies, and what the facilitator should say about that rather than papering over it.

Finally, tell me how to scale the thing down to twelve participants and up to thirty without turning the negotiation into a lecture, and whether the lottery variant — both blocs accepting a coin flip over the two competing platforms instead of a split — is worth adding, given that I have ninety minutes and no more. No lecture up front, no reading in advance, no real money; a trivial prize is fine.
