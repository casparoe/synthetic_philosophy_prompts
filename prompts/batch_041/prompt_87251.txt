I have just lost an argument about the Ellsberg urn, and I want to know whether I lost it on the merits or merely to a friend who argues faster than I do. The position he defended — and the one I want you to put on trial — is this: preferring the urn of known composition is not a bias to be explained away, but a defensible response to a real feature of the situation, namely that the description under-determines the prospect. His reasoning ran roughly as follows. A rational agent is guided by the description she actually has, not by a probability she has to invent in order to fill a gap in it; supplying the missing probability by symmetry, or by some principle of indifference, is not a neutral move but the manufacture of a fact she does not possess; and so the familiar charge that her preference violates Savage's sure-thing principle shows only that the principle presumes a state space more complete than the situation supplies. He then added a twist I had not met before. Imagine two agents with exactly the same information, differing only in what they have been told about how the urn was filled: one is told that it was filled by a process she would recognise as fair, the other that the filling process is unspecified. He says the second agent is entitled to treat these as different situations, because being filled by a known fair process and not being fixed by any process are different facts about the world, and probabilities are supposed to encode facts. I could not see at once what was wrong with that, and that is what worries me.

So: what would count against the position? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say of each what kind of thing it is — an argument that the position collapses into a rival, an empirical pattern it cannot accommodate, a coherence or money-pump result, or a case it cannot describe. I know the standard objections and I am not persuaded by all of them, so tell me which have teeth and which are cheap: which of the sure-thing-principle violations do real work, and which are artefacts of how the problem is posed. I am especially interested in three questions. First, can the position always be redescribed as a preference over second-order prospects, and if so does that make it vacuous, or merely reclassify it as an ordinary preference that happens to look strange? Second, does the aversion track the information an agent has, or only the way the urn is described to her? My impression is that ambiguity attitudes are not stable across domains — that they reverse between gains and losses — and that they vary with how competent people feel in the domain rather than with anything about the urn, both of which would sit badly with an account that is supposed to be about under-specification. Third, does the position as stated risk unfalsifiability? If 'the description under-determines the prospect' can be stretched to cover any case, what would it exclude? And what does the position predict that a plain subjective-expected-utility account does not?

Finally, tell me what my friend would himself have to concede, not merely what a critic would say. Does his position survive only in a narrowed form — say, as a claim about agency and design rather than about ambiguity, or about the incompleteness of the state space rather than about rational preference — and what does it lose when it is narrowed that far? I would be moved most by a demonstration that the aversion persists once every trace of an agent has been removed from the set-up, or by a coherence argument showing that the attitude cannot be represented as a preference over anything at all. I do not want a reading list or citations; I want the arguments, and an honest verdict on how exposed the position is.
