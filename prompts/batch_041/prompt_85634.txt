I'm in a second-year course on rationality and probability (cross-listed with phil of science) and we've just spent two weeks on van Fraassen's reflection principle. At reading group on Tuesday, Nadia and Owen got into it about it and I got voluntold to referee, and I honestly can't tell who's winning. Can you adjuducate it move by move?

The principle, as our TA put it: if you're certain that your future credence in some claim A will be x, then your credence in A now should be x. Then the TA gave us this case: Theo takes a drug -- call it Zedex, the name doesn't matter -- that for two hours after it kicks in makes him 95% confident in whatever proposition he's considering, no matter what the evidence is. He knows this about himself. Tonight he's 50/50 on whether his paper will be accepted; he's certain that tomorrow at 9am, post-dose, he'll be at 95%. Then the exchange:

(1) Owen: reflection says Theo's credence tonight should be 95%. It obviously shouldn't -- he knows the 9am credence is chemically manufactured, not a response to anything. So reflection is just false, and it's false for anyone who knows in advance they'll be impaired: drunk, sleep-deprived, hypnotized, whatever.

(2) Nadia: the case is out of scope. The point of the antecedent -- being sure what your future credence will be -- is that your future credence is supposed to be evidence about A, the way a trusted expert's opinion is. If you know it's coming from Zedex, it isn't evidence about the paper at all, so the principle never gets off the ground. It's like objecting to "defer to your doctor" by describing a doctor who's been bribed.

(3) Owen: that's either trivial or circular. Trivial, because if the principle only applies when the future credence responds to evidence, it just says "update on your evidence" in fancier clothes. Circular, because to decide whether my future credence is evidence-responsive I'd have to know what the evidence actually supports -- which is the thing I'm trying to work out. And van Fraassen never put that proviso in; it's being added specifically to dodge counterexamples.

(4) Nadia: it's not vacuous, it has real teeth in ordinary cases -- if I'm sure the doctor will call tomorrow with the result and I'm sure I'll believe her, it's irrational for me to insist today that my credence won't budge. And there's an independent argument: if Theo is at 0.5 now and certain to be at 0.95 tomorrow, a bookie can buy a $1-if-accepted bet off him today for 60 cents and sell him the same bet back tomorrow for 90, and Theo loses 30 cents no matter how the paper turns out. That's incoherence, drug or no drug. And the fix is that he should precommit -- take the dose after he's made the decision, or have a friend hold his wallet.

(5) Owen: the Dutch book shows he's exploitable, not that he's irrational -- the exploitability is a fact about the drug, and we don't call someone irrational for needing a medication. And the precommitment fix gives it away: if the rational response is to arrange not to have the future credence, or to ignore it, then the demand isn't that the credence itself match. Also, the two things in play here seperate. Suppose Theo thinks his 9am credence will be 0.95 with probability 1/2 and 0.05 with probability 1/2. His expected future credence is exactly his current 0.5, so the weaker condition -- your credence should equal your expected future credence -- is satisfied, but reflection still demands 0.95 in one branch and 0.05 in the other. So reflection isn't the weak expected-credence claim; it's the strong claim that your future credence is testimony about the truth, and Zedex is exactly a denial of that.
