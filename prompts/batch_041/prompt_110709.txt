I'm a second-year PhD student in political theory and I'm putting together a two-page proposal for a small summer research grant — three months, enough for conceptual work and nothing else. I've been circling a question for months and I keep failing to state it in a way that is both precise and answerable, which is probably why I need to see it written out. A friend who works on citizens' assemblies tells me I am overcomplicating something simple. My starting point is an irritation with how the sortition debate is normally framed. The defenders of selection by lot argue from fairness: everyone had an equal chance of being chosen, so the resulting body has some claim to speak for the rest of us. The defenders of elections argue from accountability: if we dislike what our representatives do, we can remove them. Each side is answering a different question, and neither seems to notice that the two answers come apart — and that term limits are the proof. A term-limited legislator has full electoral authorization and no prospect of retrospective punishment. A randomly selected citizen-legislator has no authorization of the ordinary kind and nothing to be punished for, since she will never stand again. The career politician has both, and that is precisely what people distrust about her.

The thesis I want the proposal to develop is that democratic authorization is doing the work of two quite different things — a prospective claim (you were selected by a procedure you had reason to accept before the fact) and a retrospective one (you can be held to account after the fact) — and that the case for any selection mechanism turns on whether these two are complements or substitutes. My hunch is that they are largely substitutes, at least in the range that matters: it is ambition that makes retrospective accountability necessary, so where there is no career to protect, prospective authorization alone may do the work, which would make sortition's most-criticized weakness far less serious than its critics suppose and would make term limits a far more radical reform than they are usually taken to be. But I can see the argument running the other way — that accountability is not only deterrence but also a channel by which information flows from the governed to the governor, and that a body with no future cannot be corrected mid-course — and I genuinely do not know which way it comes out. That uncertainty is the point.

Please write the two-page proposal. It needs a statement of the question that a reader could disagree with in a determinate way, rather than an invitation to explore the relationship between three ideas; an account of why the existing defenses of sortition, of elections, and of term limits do not settle it; the method, concrete enough that I could follow it week by week, and an argument for why three months of conceptual work is the right route here rather than a survey or a handful of case studies; what a successful result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and an honest scope, including what I will have to leave for the dissertation. Do not include a literature review or citations — I will add those myself — and please do not pad the proposal with the customary opening pages on classical Athens; I want the argument to stand on its own.
