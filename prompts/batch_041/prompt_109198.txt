I took up the history and philosophy of science in earnest after retiring from forty years in a physical chemistry laboratory, and I have spent the better part of a year trying to make peace with the charge of whiggishness. I have read Butterfield's short book of 1931 twice, and I take his central complaint to be the following: "It is part and parcel of the whig interpretation of history that it studies the past with reference to the present." My difficulty is that I cannot accept this as it stands, because much of the history of my own field that I find most valuable is written with reference to the present, and I do not believe its authors were sinning. I have tried to formulate a rule for myself — a history is whiggish just in case it evaluates past actors by how near they came to what we now believe — but it appears to me to prove too much in some cases and to miss the mark in others. What I want from you is the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that actually captures the verdicts I am about to give, and an honest account of which of those verdicts I ought to surrender.

Here are the cases as I judge them. (i) A chapter on the chemical revolution that first reconstructs Priestley's phlogiston chemistry in Priestley's own terms and only afterwards explains why Lavoisier's system displaced it: sound history, not whiggish, even though it ends by telling me who won. (ii) A history of geology that presents the acceptance of continental drift as the vindication of Wegener and treats the geophysicists who resisted him on physical grounds as men blinded by a commitment to a rigid earth: whiggish and bad. (iii) A history of genetics that says Mendel discovered the gene: whiggish and bad. (iv) A history of medicine that says Semmelweis's colleagues rejected handwashing because they were vain and obstinate: bad, but I do not think its fault is whiggishness; it is a different sin, and I cannot say precisely which. (v) A history of Chinese astronomy organised around the question of why it never produced a Kepler: whiggish and bad. (vi) A history of twentieth-century cosmology written by a working cosmologist, using the current model as its spine and discussing earlier proposals by what they contributed to it: not whiggish, and I would put it in a student's hands — it is a map, and a map is drawn from where the mapmaker stands. (vii) A historian's account of the research programme I myself spent my career on, now regarded as a sideline, which describes my generation's work as a holding pattern: I accept this as fair, and I do not experience it as whiggish, though it grades my whole career by present consensus. I am aware that (vi) and (vii) pull against (ii) and (v), and I suspect the real difference between them is not the one I first supposed.

Please state the principle, or the small set of candidates, precisely, clause by clause, naming for each clause the case that forces it; then tell me what it predicts for cases I have not listed — a museum exhibition on cold fusion, which the field has by and large written off; a history of phrenology that treats its failure as an ordinary empirical failure rather than a moral one; and a history of a field such as the amyloid hypothesis in Alzheimer's research, in which no consensus exists yet against which to grade anyone. Then say plainly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and must therefore overturn, and whether (iv) belongs to the same family as the others or to a different one. End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that gives the surviving principle in a single sentence, followed by a semicolon and the labels — for instance "(ii), (v)" — of any of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, or the word "none".
