You are to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two philosophers whose dispute has run for decades without resolution.

Party A is a defender of the doctrine of double effect. Party A holds that the distinction between harm an agent intends and harm the agent merely foresees marks a genuine and independent moral difference, and that this is what explains why, for example, redirecting a runaway trolley onto one person to save five can be permissible while carving up one healthy person to save five is not.

Party B is a critic. Party B does not deny that those two cases differ, but holds that the intended/foreseen distinction does no independent moral work: once you have fixed the facts about causal structure -- whether the harm is a means to the benefit or a side effect of it, how tight or loose the causal chain is, and so on -- and the facts about proportionality, nothing further is added by asking what the agent intended. On Party B's view, the notion of intention as it figures in the doctrine either restates causal structure or is a rhetorical flourish.

They have agreed to write one document together. Produce it.

It should contain, in order: (1) a numbered list of claims both parties can sign, stated so that neither would wince -- covering what the doctrine does and does not assert, what the classic cases do and do not establish, and what each side would count as a decisive objection to itself; (2) a table of the precise residual disagreements, with each side's position phrased in wording that side would accept as its own; (3) the cruxes -- the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side -- and for each crux at least one concrete case or argument that both parties agree would bear on it, together with each side's prediction about how it comes out, recorded in advance; and (4) an identification of any part of the apparent disagreement that is merely verbal, with an account of what the parties were talking past. I suspect one crux is whether Party B's preferred notions of causal structure can themselves be characterized without covert appeal to what the agent intended -- if they cannot, Party B's position may be circular -- but do not treat that as settled.

Use as a running example the following pair of cases, which I have constructed to pry the intended/foreseen distinction apart from the means/side-effect distinction. Case X: five people are trapped in a flooding tunnel; a bystander can save them by opening a lock, but the water that saves the five must first pass through a side chamber, drowning one person trapped there. The one's death is foreseen and lies on the causal path to saving the five. Case Y: the same five are trapped, and the bystander can save them by opening the same lock, but the water reaches the five through the main channel; a pressure valve that must be opened to operate the lock will, by a separate channel, drown one person trapped in a side chamber. The one's death is foreseen and does not lie on the causal path to saving the five. Say what each party predicts about each case, and whether the pair actually isolates the variable it is meant to isolate.

Be careful not to let the agreed-claims section become vacuous, and not to let the disagreement column smuggle in one side's framing. Where a formulation cannot be made neutral, say so and explain why.

Write your answer in American English.
