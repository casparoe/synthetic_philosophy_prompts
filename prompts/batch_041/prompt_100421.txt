In my ethics reading group we have been circling the same dispute for months, and I can now predict every move. The negative utilitarians hold that the badness of suffering is lexically prior to the goodness of pleasure: no amount of happiness, however large, can outweigh a fixed quantity of intense suffering. The classical utilitarians hold that this is incoherent: if suffering and pleasure are both welfare-relevant, they must be commensurable, and the only real question is the exchange rate. We have run the standard cases — a world that could be painlessly annihilated, the choice whether to create a happy person or a suffering person, and whether a single person's pinprick can outweigh any amount of happiness for others — and neither side has moved an inch. Both sides agree on the empirical facts and on the claim that suffering is bad; they disagree about what follows.

I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for a verdict on which side is right. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is it a substantive disagreement about the structure of value? A disagreement about the meaning of words like bad and worse than — one side using a comparative notion, the other a non-comparative one? Are the two camps answering different questions, one about what makes a state of affairs good and the other about what we have reason to do? Or is it a disagreement about which formal representation of value is correct?

I have a hypothesis I would like you to test rather than assume. I suspect the impasse is representational rather than moral. The negative utilitarian is implicitly using a lexicographic ordering, in which the suffering dimension must be settled before the pleasure dimension counts at all; the classical utilitarian is using an Archimedean ordering, in which any finite loss can be outweighed by a sufficiently large gain. These two orderings cannot be translated into one another without loss, and so each side experiences the other as simply denying an obvious value. If that diagnosis is wrong, say why. If it is right, say what follows.
