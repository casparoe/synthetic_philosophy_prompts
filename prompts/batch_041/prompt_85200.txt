A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about penal substitution, and the argument ended somewhere neither of us expected. He accepts the doctrine; I do not, and I pressed the usual objections — that guilt is not the kind of thing that can be transferred, that punishing an innocent volunteer is unjust even if he consents, that the debt model smuggles in assumptions the criminal law does not share. He kept answering with mundane analogies, and by the end I was no longer sure which of us was being naive. So I want to ask about the legal and financial practices those analogies lean on, rather than about the theology directly. In most jurisdictions a fine can be paid by someone other than the offender — a parent, a friend, an employer, a charity — and the offender is thereby clear; tort damages can be paid by an insurer or a relative on the wrongdoer's behalf; a guarantor can assume another's debt; a corporation can pay a penalty incurred by its officer. But the state will not let a volunteer serve your prison sentence, and in modern systems you cannot hire a substitute for military conscription or for most forms of compulsory personal service. Contract law shows the same split: a court will order damages for breach of a personal-services contract, but it will not order specific performance, and it will not let a third party perform the service in your stead and call the obligation discharged. The pattern, roughly, is that money-like penalties can be borne by another while personal and embodied ones cannot. What theory of punishment and responsibility does that pattern presuppose? Does it presuppose that the offender's desert is genuinely satisfied when someone else pays, so that what is transferred is the penalty itself and the offender is no longer liable? Or does it treat the fine as a debt attaching to the offender's estate, so that the person punished remains the offender and the payer is merely a source of funds? Does the distinction track a metaphysical difference between penalties essentially tied to a person and penalties merely tied to a person's holdings, or is it a policy choice about administrability, deterrence, and the expressive function of imprisonment? I would like the implicit theory reconstructed: what the practice strictly requires, what it merely fits, and where it is underdetermined among rival views. And I would like to know what would have to change if we took the monetary cases as the model and tried to extend their logic to imprisonment — by allowing a volunteer to serve the sentence, say, or by allowing the state to imprison someone else in the offender's place with the offender's consent. Would that be a coherent extension of the practice's own principles, or would it collapse the distinction between a penalty and a punishment? David Lewis's essay on penal substitution is in the background of my question, but I am less interested in what he concluded than in whether the legal practice I have described already commits us to an answer.
