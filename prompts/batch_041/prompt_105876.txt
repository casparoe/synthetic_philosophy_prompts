im not an academic, just someone who argues about this stuff with friends, and ive ended up holding a view I want you to stress-test. Call it the sunk costs arent always sunk view. The usual lesson is that honoring sunk costs is always irrational because bygones are bygones. My claim is that this is only true under a very thin description of a decision. In real cases the money or time already spent is usually bundled with things that arent sunk at all: skills youve built, information youve gained about the project or about yourself, relationships, reputation, a public commitment that changes other peoples expectations, and your own future credibility as someone who finishes what they start. So the standard charge often either attacks a strawman where the past has no causal trace except the expense, or smuggles in a normative assumption that only forward-looking expected value counts, which begs the question. I want you to act like a philosopher-economist who specializes in rational choice and take this seriously, not just tell me the textbook answer. Is there a clean way to restate my view so it isnt just a verbal trick about what counts as sunk? What does my view predict that the standard one doesnt? And is it in danger of becoming unfalsifiable, since I could always redescribe a continuation as preserving some nonsunk asset? Tell me which defeaters the defenders themselves should concede and which are merely awkward. Also say what you think I personally should find most persuasive if Im wrong.
