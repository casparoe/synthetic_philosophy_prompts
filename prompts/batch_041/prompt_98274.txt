I'm co-teaching a final-year seminar on Machiavelli and Renaissance political thought this term, and I've been handed the job of writing one of the exam questions. It's a 45-minute closed-book essay, and I'm trying to get away from the question we've recycled for years — something like "explain the relationship between virtù and fortuna" — because it gets answered every single time with the same tidy summary: fortune is luck, virtù is skill, the wise prince builds up his virtù so he doesn't have to depend on fortune, full marks, next script. What bugs me is that Machiavelli doesn't actually hand you that tidy picture. He gives you at least two images of fortune and they pull in different directions. In one, fortune is a violent river that flattens everything in its path but which men can partly tame in advance, in the calm season, by throwing up dikes and embankments — that's a story about foresight, preparation, institutions, patient accumulation. In the other, fortune is a woman who is won by the bold and the impetuous and not by the cautious — which is a story about temperament and seizing the moment, and which quietly makes fortune something that responds to how you act rather than something that merely happens to you. A colleague of mine thinks the tension is fake and that it dissolves as soon as you seperate two things the word "fortune" is covering: impersonal shocks that nobody controls (floods, plagues, the weather, a foreign army coming over the mountains) versus the unpredictable wills of other people (whether the nobles stay bought, whether the people stay loyal, whether your ally folds at the wrong moment). On that reading the dike image is about the first kind of uncertainty and the woman image is about the second. I'm not convinced that rescue works, and I'd like the exam question to make students actually adjudicate it rather than restate it.

So: please write the essay question, a model answer of roughly 650-750 words, and a marking scheme out of 20. The question needs to be unanswerable by reciting the fortune/virtù summary, and I'd like it to force students to (a) say what work each image is doing and why they sit awkwardly together, (b) say whether my colleague's impersonal-versus-strategic distinction actually rescues the unity of virtù or just relabels the problem, and (c) take a position on what happens to the whole fortune/virtù dichotomy if fortune turns out to be partly responsive to the agent's character. The marking scheme should name five or six discrete must-hit moves with marks attached and, for each one, a one-line note on how students typically muff it — generic rubrics are useless for marking consistently, so I want the specific moves. Please have the model answer work through at least one concrete case from the texts rather than staying at the level of abstractions.

Two last things. The model answer can't lean on any secondary literature or citations — it's closed book and I want something a strong student could actually produce from the texts alone, so no reading lists and no "as So-and-so argues". And I'd genuinely like you to flag where this question is unfair or where it rewards a clever-but-wrong answer. If you think the two images are actually perfectly consistent and I'm manufacturing a tension that isn't there, or if the "fortune responds to character" reading is a stretch of the text, say so straight instead of writing around it; I'd rather find out now than after marking forty scripts. And if the fault line I've set up is the wrong one, tell me what the better fault line would be.
