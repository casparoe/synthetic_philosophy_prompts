I'm taking a graduate seminar on the foundations of mathematics this term, and I've ended up holding a position I can't decide whether to keep. The position is that the inconsistency of our basic arithmetical and set-theoretic axioms is a live epistemic possibility — not a melodramatic possibility to be waved away, but something a rational working mathematician should assign real credence to and carry in the background when deciding what to trust. I'd like you to tell me what would count against it. Don't give me a reading list or citations; I want the shape of the case, argued out.

Please keep two things apart, since I suspect they have different defeaters. One is the weak reading: the axioms are not known to be consistent, and nothing we have rules the possibility out. The other is the strong reading: I assign the possibility non-negligible weight and let it affect what I do. And then there's a version a classmate holds, which he calls the proof-length version — that the question worth asking is not whether a contradiction exists but how long the shortest derivation of one is, and that if no such derivation is short enough for anyone to write down or check, then for us the axioms are consistent in the only sense that could matter. Tell me whether my position is genuinely distinct from his or just a vaguer way of stating it.

I want the defeaters ranked by how much they should actually move me, with the decisive separated from the merely awkward, and with attention to what a defender of the position would concede rather than only what critics would say. In particular: what would a consistency proof relative to principles I already accept do to the position, and does the fact that such proofs only push the question into a stronger theory leave it untouched or hollow it out? What would the discovery of an actual contradiction show — I suspect it would confirm rather than refute me, which strikes me as a problem for a position of this kind. And does the position predict anything a rival doesn't, or is it compatible with every possible course of experience?

Consider a case that worries me. Someone announces a derivation of a contradiction from the arithmetic axioms, but it is long enough that checking it takes years and it leans on formal verification software and on proof-theoretic principles I have no independent reason to trust. Does that count for the position or against it, or is it a case the position simply cannot describe?

Finally, I'd like an honest verdict on whether the position has been insulated by retreating into unfalsifiability — and if it has, whether that is a defect here or just what an honest claim about our epistemic situation looks like. Tell me which of these considerations I should find most persuasive, and where, if anywhere, the position earns its keep.
