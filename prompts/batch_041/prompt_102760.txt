I spent my working life as a field ecologist and have lately been reading the philosophy of biology; I am struck by how readily the heritability statistics I once reported are read as claims about causes, and I would like to know which of those readings survive scrutiny. To make the question concrete, take a study of clutch size in a songbird in which heritability was estimated in two populations of the same species: population A, in which every nest was subjected to the same manipulated thermal regime, and population B, in which thermal conditions varied naturally. The estimate for A came out roughly twice that for B. Please score each of the following propositions from 0 to 1 for how defensible it is as stated (0 = plainly confused, 1 = unobjectionable), rank them from most to least defensible, and give a sentence or two on each, noting especially any proposition that holds only under an assumption it leaves unstated and identifying that assumption.

1. Clutch size is more genetically determined in A than in B, since its heritability is higher in A.
2. A heritability of zero in a population would show that genes play no part in the development of the trait.
3. The gap between the two estimates is itself an estimate of gene–environment interaction for the trait.
4. The difference in heritability between the populations shows that the trait's genetic architecture differs between them.
5. A high heritability estimate warrants the inference that intervening on genes would change the trait.
6. The heritability estimated within A is the right quantity for predicting how the trait would respond to selection in B.
7. Heritable variation in the trait could arise entirely from heritable environmental differences among parents, with no genetic differences contributing.
8. Heritability, being a ratio of variances, has no application to a single individual.
