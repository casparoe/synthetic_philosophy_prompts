Last night I lost an argument to a friend who is finishing a dissertation on the philosophy of logic, and before I write the position up for a seminar I want to know whether I lost it fairly. Her charge, compressed, was that I have been quietly helping myself to two incompatible pictures of what a rule of inference is: one on which the rule is what confers meaning on a connective, and one on which the rule never actually obliges anyone to pass from premises to conclusion. I don't see the incompatibility myself, but I don't trust my own eye on this, so I'd rather hand you the whole package to audit than defend it piecemeal. The argument ran long and got heated in the usual way, so please take the claims below as my considered position rather than as offhand remarks.

Here is roughly where I've landed. (1) The meaning of a logical expression is constituted by the rules of inference that govern it: to grasp 'if' just is to have mastered modus ponens and its kin, so the rules do not describe a meaning that exists independently of them. (2) The tortoise in Carroll's dialogue is right, and not about a triviality — accepting P together with 'if P then Q' does not logically compel acceptance of Q, and no further premise, not even the conditionalized rule itself, closes the gap, since the very same gap reopens one level up. (3) Our entitlement to the most basic deductive rules is not something we could earn by deriving them from anything more basic without circularity or regress, and it does not need to be earned that way; it is default rather than the conclusion of an argument. (4) Someone who genuinely understands 'if', accepts P, accepts 'if P then Q', and still declines to accept Q is making a mistake — not merely behaving oddly, but failing rationally, falling short of what their own commitments already require of them. (5) The regress the tortoise opens is a regress about acts rather than about propositions, which is why the remedy, if there is one, must be to get the person to infer rather than to get them to believe one more thing.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture. Tell me which of these pull against one another and how badly, ranked from the genuinely inconsistent down to the merely awkward; tell me which apparent conflicts dissolve once the claims are stated precisely, since I suspect at least one of my worries is a confusion of my own and I'd like it named as such rather than treated as a live problem; and for each real tension, say what the cheapest repair is and what it costs me — which commitment I should weaken or abandon, and what I lose by doing so. Please be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who work on inference and logical normativity and which are genuinely contested, and don't let a contested reading pass as the consensus; I would much rather have the disagreement marked than smoothed over, even where that means telling me that a question I take to be deep is really just a verbal tangle. I genuinely can't tell whether the trouble is a real inconsistency or a failure on my part to state one of these claims carefully enough. End with a final line beginning Verdict: that names the single commitment I should give up first and gives the one-line repair.
