Write an internal onboarding memorandum, of roughly 800 words, from a career official of an invented government directorate to the magistrates newly appointed to it. The directorate's reason for existing — and the whole content of the memorandum — is to put into administrative practice the account of justice and force that Pascal works out in the Pensées: that justice without force is powerless and force without justice is tyrannical; that the two cannot be fused in principle, since force cannot be shown to be just and justice cannot be armed without contradiction; that they are therefore joined by custom, whose authority rests on nothing beyond its being received; and that the origins of an arrangement are consequently something the public is better off not examining. Invent the jurisdiction, the directorate, its officials, its ordinances and its incidents; the memorandum should read as a document of that world.

The comedy has to come from the administrative difficulties of holding this position and running an office on it, not from a cynical official venting cynicism. Keep the position whole. In particular, retain Pascal's concession that there are indeed natural laws but that our reason has corrupted our access to them; retain his asymmetry between the two orders — force is easy to recognise and hard to dispute, justice is the reverse; and retain his account of how, being unable to make justice strong, we made what was strong look just. A reader who has never read the Pensées should follow the piece without recognising a single allusion.

Give it at least four working sections, with flat civil-service headings and numbered paragraphs:

- Origin Management: what is recorded about when a rule began, how a recent and embarrassing origin is handled, and why an old origin is worth more than a good one.
- Separation of Ledgers: claims of rightness and capacities to compel are kept in separate registers and may not be cited in one another's documents. Show the rule in operation on one concrete invented rule — say an ordinance governing who may draw water from a shared canal in a dry year — whose real origin is a convenience of eleven years ago and whose force rests on nothing but the habit of compliance.
- Instruction of the Academy: what may be taught to magistrates in training, where the law is presented as ancient and its reception as the ground of its authority, and what may be said in the directorate's own seminars, where the same facts are discussed as facts. Include one invented incident in which a magistrate's after-dinner remarks obliged the directorate to act.
- Handling Appeals to Nature: how to receive a claim that a rule is contrary to natural law without conceding that any particular natural law is presently operative, and without conceding that none is.

The memorandum must be subject to its own rules: its document-control header should manage its own origin, and it should not state what it is for. The reader should be left able to see, without being told, what this costs — that a standing policy of concealing origins is itself an origin requiring concealment, and that a doctrine of received practice is poorly placed to be examined as a doctrine. Do not have the memorandum admit any of this, and keep the register deadpan throughout: no winking asides, no comic names, no jokes in the document's own voice, nothing to suggest the writer knows he is being funny. The satire is aimed at the position and at the administrative practice it licenses, not at any actual institution, country or person.

Close with a short final section headed Note on the Present Document, in the same register, that the reader will find either reassuring or not. Do not put words into the mouths of real historical figures, do not attribute invented quotations to real authors, and do not cite editions, fragment numbers or page references; no reading list is wanted.
