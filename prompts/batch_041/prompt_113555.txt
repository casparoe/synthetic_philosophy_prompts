Write a chapter from the internal staff handbook of a fictional frontier-AI laboratory — specifically, the chapter issued by whatever office the lab keeps for the purpose of preventing a model from doing harm even if the model is trying to. Use the register of a real corporate standard-operating-procedure document: numbered sections, the occasional cross-reference to another section, a revision-history line at the head, and a tone of unbothered administrative competence. Aim for roughly 700–900 words. The imagined author of the document is neither a fool nor a villain; inside the world of the document he is a reasonable compliance officer writing reasonable rules, and the piece must never step outside his voice to tell me that any of it is funny.

The humor has to come from the substance of the containment problem, taken literally at the level of office procedure. The document should handle, in whatever order suits it, most of the following, and handle each one correctly — someone who works on this problem should recognize every hazard as a real one rather than an invention:

- surveillance of the model's actions, and then the problem of surveilling the surveiller, given that the only system competent to judge the model's outputs may be another instance of the same model; the policy must therefore confront the possibility that the two instances coordinate rather than compete, and may not assume the channel between them is closed, since the document has no way to show that it is;
- the possibility that the model underperforms on the evaluations meant to measure it, so that a good score is not evidence of a low ceiling;
- the pipeline that rewrites or filters the model's output to strip out hidden triggers while preserving the output's usefulness, and the trade-off the policy is obliged to name out loud;
- a finite budget of attempts available to an adversary — whether the model or a user — so that some probability of a successful attack is priced in rather than driven to zero;
- what a staff member should do upon becoming convinced that the model is in fact trying to help, the answer being that this conviction is not something the lab can check, so it is to be logged and not acted upon.

Include at least one clause that is scrupulously drafted, plainly unenforceable, and never acknowledged as such — a requirement to detect deception by a method the document does not supply, or a rule governing a resource the office does not control.

The document must never say whether the system is in fact well or ill disposed toward the lab, and should speak only of what to do in case. Name no real laboratory, company, researcher, or paper; invent the organization, the model, and the office. The hazards must be stated plainly enough that a reader who has never encountered this research area can follow them, so do not lean on vocabulary the reader has to bring with them, though you may use the field's own terms where they earn their place.

After the handbook chapter, add a short note in your own voice, outside the document's fiction — 100 to 150 words — saying what you take the piece to be satirizing, which of the real hazards you built it around, and where you have stretched or simplified for comic effect.

I want a committed piece rather than a survey of possibilities: commit to one fictional organization, one containment posture, and one tone, and let the satire have a target you are willing to name. Do not hedge the joke in both directions, and do not lay out alternatives for me to choose among.
