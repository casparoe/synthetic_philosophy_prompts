My friend works on an alignment team and we had a long, dumb argument about RLHF at a party last week and neither of us won. Her line was basically: the human preference is a fact about the human, the pairwise comparisons are measurements of it, the reward model is an instrument for reading it off, and the policy is trained to hit the target. My line was that half the time I don't have a preference until someone asks me to click, and then I make one up, and its different the next day. She said fine, that's noise, we average over tens of thousands of comparisons and the noise cancels out. Anyway. What I actually want from you isn't a verdict on who won, it's this: the practice itself — the actual pipeline, where you take a prompt, show an annotator two responses, ask which is better, collect a few hundred thousand of those from many different annotators, fit a single scalar model that assigns a number to a prompt-response pair so that the preferred one scores higher (with the gap between scores setting the probability that one is preferred), then fine-tune a language model to maximize that number while keeping it close to the original, and evaluate by having humans do more pairwise comparisons and reporting a win rate — that whole thing makes a bunch of assumptions about what a preference is. I want those spelled out.

So: what does this practice presuppose about preferences? Does it need them to be complete and transitive? Does it need a single number to be the right representation of how much someone wants something, or is the scalar just a modeling convenience that a multi-dimensional, lexicographic, or genuinely incommensurable set of values could fit into just as well? Does pooling many annotators into one reward model presuppose that there's a meaningful group preference — and if so, is the practice quietly assuming away the whole problem of turning many people's rankings into one ranking that social choice theory is about? Does treating order effects, framing, and day-to-day flip-flopping as noise presuppose a stable true preference underneath, as opposed to preferences being constructed in the act of choosing? And does the whole thing presuppose that a human's approval is the right thing for the model to chase — some kind of preference-satisfaction view of what's good for us — or is that just an engineering shortcut that could be swapped for something else without changing the code?

I also noticed something that looks like a tension: the same people who build these pipelines are often the first to say that annotators disagree wildly, that the interface shapes the answers, and that reward models get gamed — but the pipeline still treats the pooled aggregate as the thing to be estimated and then hit. Is that an inconsistency, or can you be skeptical about stable preferences and still build the pipeline with a clear conscience? What I'm after is: reconstruct the implicit theory of preference and of welfare that this practice only makes sense under, tell me which parts are strictly presupposed and which are just convenient (so that rival theories of preference would license the same code), say where the pipeline is philosophically underdetermined, and then tell me what would concretely change if you took seriously the idea that preferences are made rather than found. Don't give me a survey of the philosophy of preference in general, and don't tell me about papers I should go read — just the presuppositions, the consistency check, and the counterfactual.
