Roughly: Bertrand's paradox isn't really a paradox about geometry. It's a symptom of asking a probability question before you've specified the physical process that generates the random object. "Pick a random chord" — fine, but random how? The three classical answers aren't three competing answers to one question; they're three answers to three different questions, and each is correct for the process it actually describes. So indifference doesn't hand you a unique prior, it hands you a family of priors indexed by which symmetry you've decided to respect. My punchline is that this is good news, not bad: the principle of indifference should be demoted from a principle of belief to a principle of experimental design. It tells you what counts as "the same setup," not what you should believe. It's a rule about how to build the experiment, and we've been misusing it as a rule for priors.
