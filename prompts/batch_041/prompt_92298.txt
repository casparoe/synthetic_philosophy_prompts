I retired from the bench about two years ago — thirty-four years of analytical chemistry, mostly method development and validation — and since then I've been making up for lost time on philosophy, badly and in no particular order. I've got stuck on the "it's raining but I don't believe it" business. I can feel that something is off with the sentence, and I can even gesture at why (you're telling me how the weather is while simultaneously telling me you don't think it's that way), but every time I try to turn the gesture into an actual account it either explains too much or too little. So before I read another paper about it, I want the spec, not the theories: what does any adequate account of the oddity have to be able to do? I spent my career writing acceptance criteria for instruments and the interesting part was always when you discovered two of the criteria couldn't both be met, so I'd like that bit flagged rather than smoothed over.

Here's the kind of thing I keep tripping over, and I'd like the list to cover it, each item with the case that motivates it. First, the two versions aren't the same animal: "it's raining but I don't believe it" can actually be true of me — the weather doesn't consult me — whereas "it's raining but I believe it isn't" can't be true of anybody, so whatever the account does, it can't be working by declaring the content contradictory or false. Second, the oddity evaporates when I shift person or tense: "it was raining but I didn't believe it" is an ordinary thing to say about last Tuesday, and "it's raining but she doesn't believe it" is just gossip, so the account has to be sensitive to first-person present-tense position rather than to the words alone. Third, there's apparently a cousin of the puzzle with "know" in it, and I don't know whether a single account is supposed to swallow both or whether that's a separate job. Fourth, a friend in my reading group insists the real phenomenon isn't the sentence at all — she says you can't even believe the conjunction, hold it as one belief, silently, which sounds like a much stronger and stranger claim than "you can't say it out loud," and I want the spec to say whether that's a requirement or a different question. Fifth, whatever the story is, it has to leave the sentence understandable — I understand it perfectly well, I just find it off — and it has to avoid sweeping up innocent cases, since "it's raining but I haven't checked the gauge" and "it's raining but I don't hope it's raining" aren't odd in the same way. And I'm curious about the machine case: if a parrot or a chatbot emits the sentence, is the oddity still there, or does it live in the speaker's mind? That seems to matter for whether we're giving an account of words or of a person.

So: give me the requirements as a numbered list, each with the case or consideration behind it and a line on whether it's a genuine requirement or just something one camp likes to insist on. Flag any subset you think can't be jointly satisfied — that's the part I most want, since my hunch is that the demand for one story covering both the saying and the believing is where it breaks. Then tell me which single requirement does the most to knock out candidate accounts, and briefly how the main families fare against the list (the it's-just-conversational-implicature line, the you'd-have-to-be-incoherent-to-believe-it line, the it's-about-the-norms-of-assertion line). I don't want names and dates and I'm not going to chase papers, so skip the bibliography — I just want the checklist I can hold theories up against. A table is fine if that's easier. Also, admittidly, I'd rather the list be longer and pickier than short and tidy; I can prune later.
