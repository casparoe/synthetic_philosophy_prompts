Ok so I've been following the expected-value fanaticism stuff for a few years now — the whole cluster of arguments that says if you're an EV maximizer then you have to take a gamble that with probability one in 10^60 gives you 10^90 units of good and otherwise gives you a stubbed toe, or whatever the case of the day is, and that you should take it no matter how bad the bad case is as long as the good case is big enough. And it feels totally stuck. The fanatics say: yes, thats what the theory says, your intuitions here are the thing to throw out, bite the bullet. The anti-fanatics say: no, any decision theory that tells you to do that is a theory of a creature that can't exist, or the utility function has to be bounded, or you should reject the Archimedean axiom, or one-in-grahams-number is morally equivalent to zero. Everyone knows everyone's moves and nobody has moved in years.

My hypothesis for why: the two sides are answering different questions under one name. "Is EV maximization the correct decision rule for an ideally rational agent?" and "what should we — finite, error-prone, one-shot people — do when we meet a tiny probability of a huge payoff?" The fanatics are answering the first (yes), the anti-fanatics the second (bound it, regulate it, don't let it run your life), and then they argue as if they'd contradicted each other. Is that the right diagnosis? Or once you seperate the two questions is there still a real substantive disagreement left — about whether the axioms of decision theory are up for grabs, about wether value can be non-Archimedean, about what an infinitesimal probability even means? And if it is substantive, what kind of disagreement is it: about the axioms, about the target (ideal agent vs. bounded agent), or about pre-theoretic intuitions neither side can argue for?

Second thing, and this is the part I actually want you to chew on: I've been wondering whether the real fork isn't pro/anti fanaticism at all but fanaticism about outcomes vs. fanaticism about actions — a view where in the limit only the probability-weighted outcome matters, vs. a view where the act of staking everything on a 10^-60 shot is itself something whose disvalue can't be washed out by a big enough payoff. Is that a real distinction or am I just reinventing something? If it's real, does it split the fanatic side, the anti-fanatic side, or cut across both?

I have a suspicion that people casually present things like "you just need a bounded utility function" or "the continuity axiom settles it" as if they were the consensus, and I don't think they are.
