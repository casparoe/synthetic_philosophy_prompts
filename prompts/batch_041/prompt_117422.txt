I am trying to isolate the principle behind my judgments about the legitimacy of the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals, and I would rather have the principle than a survey of positions. Treat the following as data. (1) Convicting the German leadership for waging aggressive war was legitimate, even though I accept that aggressive war was not a punishable offense under positive international law when they waged it. (2) Convicting a commander for atrocities committed by troops under his command, which he neither ordered nor knew of, was not legitimate. (3) Convicting a defendant for conduct that an unambiguous domestic law then in force commanded him to perform was not legitimate. (4) That the charter, the bench and the prosecution were supplied entirely by the victorious powers is not by itself a defect. (5) That the victors declined to examine their own conduct — the firebombing of cities, the killing of prisoners — while trying the defeated for theirs is a defect, and one that undermines the judgments on aggression without undermining the atrocity convictions.

Is there a single principle that yields all five, or must I surrender one of them? If I must, say which, and explain why abandoning that verdict is cheaper than the alternatives. State the principle precisely enough that it generates verdicts on its own, and then give me its verdicts on two cases I have not listed: (a) a tribunal of exclusively foreign judges trying the officials of a state that committed no aggression but tortured its own citizens under the authority of its own law; and (b) a defendant who, before the war, had himself publicly urged that aggressors be individually punished, and who is now tried under precisely that standard.

Do not lay out the range of positions and leave the choice to me. Commit to the principle you think is correct, and be explicit about what it costs you and where it is weakest.
