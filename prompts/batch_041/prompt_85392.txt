I've been kicking around a view about Zorn's lemma and I want to know what would count against it, seriously. The view is that Zorn is really a compactness theorem in disguise. The poset isn't the point; the point is that you have a bunch of partial structures, a chain is a directed union of approximations, an upper bound is a consistent union, and a maximal element is a maximal consistent completion. On this reading the order-theoretic statement is just one way of writing down the finite-approximation principle: if every finite piece of a structure can be realized, the whole thing can. I know Zorn is equivalent to AC over ZF, and I know the compactness theorem for first-order logic is equivalent to the Boolean prime ideal theorem, which is strictly weaker than AC. I also know there are Zorn-style principles, and that some are equivalent to AC while others are strictly weaker. So what I want is a map of the view's exposure. What would count against the finite-approximation reading? I mean the evidence and arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be. Does the strict strength gap between Zorn and compactness kill the reading, or can I say the extra strength is an artifact of the order-theoretic packaging? Are there applications of Zorn where there is no finite-approximation structure at all, and if so does that refute the reading or just show it is incomplete? What would a defender of the reading concede as counting against them, as opposed to what critics would say? What does the reading predict that a bare AC-equivalence story does not, and if the answer is nothing, say that. And be honest: has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so is that a problem for a view of this kind? I'd especially like to know whether there is a natural formal result that seperates the finite-approximation content from the choice content, and what it would look like. I'm not asking for a bibliography or reading list, just the arguments and the shape of the thing.
