I am a philosophy professor. In a recent departmental seminar on meaningful work, a colleague closed down a graduate student's question with a one-liner distilled from Moritz Schlick's essay On the Meaning of Life (1927): the meaning of life is play. The student had described her mother, who has spent two decades in repetitive, poorly paid care work, does not describe the work as joyful, and yet insists that her life is meaningful because the work is hers to do. The colleague replied that on Schlick's view this cannot be meaning at all, since meaning lies in play rather than work, and that the student was confusing meaning with duty. I doubt the one-liner can bear that weight, but I cannot yet say precisely what it does claim.

Please lay out the distinct theses that the slogan could be expressing. I want a menu rather than a defence of one reading: readings that are nearly tautological, readings that are substantive and defensible, readings that are probably false, and readings that are merely rhetorical. For each, say what it commits one to about the relation between play and work; about joy and pleasure; about the present and the future; about whether meaning is a property of an activity, a feeling of the agent, or a verdict on a life as a whole; and about whether youth is being used as a psychological description or as a normative ideal. Then say which of the readings, if any, would actually support my colleague's dismissal of the student's question, and which would leave the question untouched or even make it more urgent. Finally, say which reading my colleague probably has in mind, and whether that reading is one Schlick himself could have accepted or is a distortion of his position.

I should like the answer in British English. Please state how confident you are in the claims you make, and single out the point about which you are least sure.
