I'm in a small reading group that has spent the term on personal identity, and we've stalled on the neuron-replacement case in a way that I think is better handled as a probability question than as an interpretive one. Here is the scenario as precisely as I can state it. A subject undergoes a procedure in which, one at a time and at a rate of roughly one per second, each of her neurons is replaced by an artificial unit that is functionally isomorphic to the neuron it displaces — same input-output profile, same pattern of connections, same contribution to the rest of the system — so that at every stage her dispositions, memories, and first-person reports are indistinguishable from what they were before. The replacement happens in situ rather than by copying; she is briefly unconscious during each individual swap but awake and behaving normally between swaps; and at the end she reports feeling as she always has. Consider the proposition P: the subject who exists at the end of the procedure is numerically identical with the subject who began it. Please give me your credence in P, but I mainly want the derivation rather than the bare number, and I'd ask you not to stop at the observation that it depends on one's theory of personal identity — that dependence is exactly what I want laid out.

Decompose your credence at least into the following terms, with a rough probability and a line of justification for each: (a) your credence that some purely psychological-continuity account of personal identity — one on which a suitable causal continuity of mental life is both necessary and sufficient — is the correct account; (b) conditional on such an account being right, your credence that the procedure as specified preserves the relation it demands, where I am especially interested in whether the intermittent unconsciousness and the wholesale change of substrate matter, and whether your answer here is stable across the gradual and the instantaneous versions of the case; (c) your credence that personal identity is in some cases indeterminate or admits of degrees, in which case say what you take P to be asking once that is taken into account and give the number you would attach to the precisified version; (d) your credence that numerical identity is not what matters in survival, so that P concerns a distinction without a difference; and (e) whatever further terms you think belong. Then combine them and show the combination.

Finally, and this is the part my group keeps fighting about, give me your credence in the corresponding proposition for a variant in which all the replacements occur simultaneously rather than one at a time, and for a variant in which the procedure is later reversed, so that the artificial units are themselves replaced by fresh biological neurons and the subject ends up with an ordinary biological brain. I want to know whether your three numbers form a smooth gradient, and if they do not, exactly where the cliff falls and which term in the decomposition is doing the work of placing it there. Close by telling me which single term you are least confident in, and what argument or experimental result would move it most.
