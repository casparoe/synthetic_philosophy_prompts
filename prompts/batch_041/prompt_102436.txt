Ok, so this is a bit of a weird one. I've recently ended up on the ethics subcommittee of my local food co-op (don't ask), and there's this woman, Priya, who just seems to have thought through everything — animal products, fair trade, whether we stock a paper with dodgy labour practices, all of it. And I've noticed I basically vote however she votes and then tell myself I agree with her. Now I'm worried I don't actually know any of it myself, I'm just borrowing her conclusions. My flatmate says that's completely fine, moral testimony is just like any other testimony. My sister says no, you can't outsource your moral life like that, you have to understand it for yourself. I want to know what the two camps could actually agree on, so I'd like you to draft the joint statement that a committed member of each camp could sign after a proper good-faith adverserial collaboration.

Camp 1, the permissive view: moral testimony can transmit knowledge and/or justification, and deferring can be epistemically and morally fine — sometimes even the right thing to do. Camp 2, the demanding view: what matters morally is understanding, and testimony at best gives you a true belief without the thing that makes it properly yours.

No reading list, no citations — I can't look anything up right now, and I don't want a bibliography. Just the document. Please write it in British English.

I want:
1. A numbered list of agreed claims both sides can sign. Include what moral testimony can do, what the dispute is not about, and what each side would count as evidence against its own view.
2. A disagreements table. Columns: the precise disagreement in wording that the side it belongs to would accept as fair (no strawmen), the crux underneath it, a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on that crux, and what each side predicts about that case.
3. A short section on which parts of the disagreement might be merely verbal, and why.

Please make sure these get covered, because this is the bit I actually care about:
- The Aya-and-Bo case. Aya works out for herself that she should break a confidence to protect someone. Bo just trusts Priya's say-so on the same question. It later turns out Priya's reasoning had a hidden flaw, though her conclusion was still true. Does Bo's belief lose its warrant or justification in a way Aya's doesn't? What does each side predict?
- Whether deference is different for obligations, for supererogatory acts, and for judgements about what's admirable. Is it worse to defer on 'should I give to this charity?' than on 'is this person admirable?'.
- Whether acting on a borrowed moral belief makes the action less praiseworthy, and whether it affects your standing to blame other people who disagree with you.
- Whether there's a difference between deferring to someone whose reasoning you couldn't reconstruct and someone whose reasoning you could reconstruct but haven't bothered to.
- The recantation case. Priya later says she was wrong and changes her mind. Does Bo's belief survive at all? Does Aya's?
- What both sides think about the fact that we all start off as moral children and presumably have to borrow before we can understand anything.
- Whether the distinction between first-hand and second-hand warrant is even coherent, or whether it's doing any real work.

Both sides need to be equally competent — I don't want the permissive side to be a caricature of 'just trust your betters' or the demanding side to be a caricature of 'you must reinvent ethics from scratch'. And the cruxes should be real ones: settling one should actually move at least one side.
