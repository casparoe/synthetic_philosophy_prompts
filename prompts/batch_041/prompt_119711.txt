I would like a case constructed to specifications: a thought experiment that puts the paradox of deontology in its sharpest form. The specifications matter more than the scenario, so I will state them first.

The case must turn on a choice between two options, and the option that a deontological constraint forbids must be the option that is better in the constraint's own currency — by following the constraint, the agent must bring about more infringements of rights than she prevents. The several whose rights are infringed when she declines must be infringed by someone else rather than by her, so that the choice is between her own infringement of one person and other people's infringements of several. The setting must be ordinary — a clinic, a school, a small business, a landlord's office, a volunteer organisation — with ordinary people, no exotic technology, and a choice that someone actually in that role could face. No trolleys, no footbridges, no transplant surgeons, no ticking bombs, no organ lotteries, no hostage standoffs. The scenario itself must fit in a single paragraph. The numbers must be small, one person against a few, so that no threshold view of deontology would treat the constraint as overridden by the stakes.

Three further specifications do the real work, and each must be met. First, the person the agent would infringe must not be used as a means to anything: the infringement must be a foreseen side-effect of an act aimed at something else, so that the familiar reply that no one may be used merely as a means is unavailable. Second, the person she would infringe must be someone whose right will be infringed anyway if she declines, so that following the constraint spares no one, not even the person it forbids her to touch. Third, the constraint must be the best available formulation of that constraint rather than a defective rule the agent could simply abandon; the case must not work by having her obey something obviously bad.

Then do the following. State the verdict of each of four positions and show how each verdict is generated rather than stipulated: an absolutist constraint-based deontology, a threshold deontology, act consequentialism, and a view that grounds constraints in respect for persons — in the inviolability of each person — rather than in the integrity of the agent. Identify the single feature of the case that makes the paradox bite, and say what happens when it is removed: if following the constraint would spare the person it protects, does the paradox evaporate, and does the constraint then collapse into a rule for maximising protection? Give the strongest reply available to the deontologist, and say whether it answers the paradox or concedes it by relocating the justification of constraints from the persons protected to the agent who obeys them. Tell me also whether the verdict survives once I stop treating omissions as morally lighter than actions; if it does not, say what that shows about how much of the paradox rests on that asymmetry. If you judge that the specifications cannot be jointly met, say which one fails and why, rather than fudging it. I am not interested in who has said what; I want the case and the reasoning.

Write the whole answer as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — the way one would write a careful letter.
