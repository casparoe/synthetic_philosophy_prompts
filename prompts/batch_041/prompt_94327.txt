Most liberal jurisdictions will confine someone who has committed no crime if a psychiatrist judges that a mental disorder makes them dangerous to themselves or others or unable to meet their basic needs, yet will not confine someone who is merely wretched, reckless, or eccentric and no less likely to come to harm; and the very same diagnosis that grounds this preventive detention also grounds an excuse after the fact, in the insanity defense, while an offender whose violence is traceable to temperament, upbringing, or rage gets nothing of the kind. Reconstruct the conception of mental disorder this pair of practices presupposes. Does it require that mental illness name a real kind of condition — a fact about a person that holds independently of our disapproval of how they live — or only that some recognized class of experts be licensed to decide who may be confined and who excused? Which accounts of mental disorder does the practice rule out, and which does it merely accommodate? Is the asymmetry between confinement and excuse the expression of a single coherent view of mind and responsibility, or two unrelated administrative settlements that happen to share a vocabulary? Then take Szasz's claim that mental illness is not an illness at all but a metaphor for troubles in living that we have chosen to medicalize: say what would survive of these practices if that claim were true, and what would have to be put in their place. Commit to a verdict on whether the practices genuinely presuppose a realist conception of mental disorder, rather than laying out the options and leaving the choice to me.
