I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy training, and a few of us at work have a long-running argument over drinks about what people call "races to the bottom" — situations where everyone does the locally sensible thing and the group ends up somewhere none of them wanted. I've been keeping a mental list of cases along with my gut verdicts, and I'd like someone to tell me what principle, if any, actually separates the ones that bother me from the ones that don't. Here's the list. Two shops on the same block both start staying open an hour later, each to avoid losing evening customers to the other; neither owner wants the longer hours, and both would be happier if the city forced everyone to close at the same time — I call this a genuine trap that justifies a rule. Two delivery startups undercut each other on price until one folds; the survivor turns out to be genuinely leaner and customers get cheaper service — that's just competition working, and I wouldn't intervene. Farmers all dosing their herds with antibiotics because each gains a little and the resistant strains are a shared cost — a trap, and clearly one that needs a coordinated cap. Two rival states each building up weapons to avoid falling behind the other — a trap. A standards war, where two firms spend years and fortunes pushing competing formats before one wins — I don't think this is a trap at all, because somebody had to find out which format was actually better, even though both sides would have preferred to skip the fight and just flip a coin. Two students both studying an extra three hours a week to get ahead on a curve, learning nothing extra — a trap. And an arms race in ad-blocking, where sites pile on more ads, users install better blockers, and sites respond with anti-blockers — this one I can never settle: it looks structurally like the others, but users arguably do end up better off with the blockers, so I'm not even sure "everyone would be happier if they all stopped" is true here.

My tentative rule is that it's a trap whenever every party would prefer the outcome in which they all stop to the outcome in which they all keep going — but the standards-war case seems to have that feature and I still don't want to call it a trap, and the antibiotic case seems to be a trap even though the farmers who happen to avoid resistant infections come out ahead of the ones who don't. I'd like a precise principle, or a small set of competing candidates, that reproduces as many of these verdicts as possible, stated in a way that lets me tell in advance which side of the line a new case falls on. For each candidate, please tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon and why I should prefer keeping the principle over keeping the verdict. Then apply your preferred candidate to two cases I haven't listed: (a) a neighborhood where each household keeps its lawn slightly more manicured than the next purely because of an informal status norm, and (b) two open-source projects that keep racing to ship features in order to attract contributors, degrading both codebases in the process.

Finally, be explicit about where your reasoning is most likely to fail. I'd rather have a principle with a clearly flagged weak point than one presented as airtight — so tell me which single consideration or counterexample would most change your mind about the principle you land on, and what a case would have to look like for you to conclude that the whole approach was wrong.
