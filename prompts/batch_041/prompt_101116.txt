I belong to a political-theory reading group that has spent most of this year on the liberal-communitarian debate — the charge that the liberal self is unencumbered, the countercharge that identity is constituted by unchosen attachments, the quarrel over whether a polity can be neutral among conceptions of the good. Last week we reached a question we could not settle from the texts: whether the communitarian picture, taken seriously as a political program rather than as social ontology, can avoid becoming a homeowners' association with a metaphysics. I have decided to answer in the form of a document rather than an essay. Please write me a parody of the covenants, conditions, and restrictions of a residential community — call it The Commons at Unencumbered Acres, a name the Association insists is a coincidence — whose governing documents quietly take sides in that debate. I want the flat, procedural register of a real HOA handbook: a short preamble, eight to twelve numbered covenants, and a fine schedule. It should be funny to someone who has taught the debate and legible to someone who has only heard of it.

The covenants should not announce their philosophy; they should simply do it. I would like the Board to hold substantive authority over what counts as living well and to be genuinely wounded when anyone calls it illiberal. Give me a clause on exit and on what the Association maintains happens to your identity when you take it; a participation requirement with escalating fines for atomistic conduct; a right of first refusal on any sale, so that the community can keep out the wrong kind of buyer; and a deliberative forum whose minutes are the only record that counts for anything. Let the objections land on their own — who actually authors the shared ends, whether a thick community is possible without exclusion, whether a covenant you cannot revoke was ever chosen, whether the situated self is a discovery about persons or a policy imposed on them — without any covenant ever stating one. Around 700 words.

Because a parody has no verdict to deliver, close with a brief note, clearly separate from the document itself, on where the piece takes liberties with the debate or leans on a contested reading of it — for instance, whether it treats the situated self as a metaphysical thesis rather than a claim about how identity is formed, or simply assumes that communitarians must be hostile to rights-talk. Say how confident you are that each of these is a fair target, and single out the move you are least sure is fair.
