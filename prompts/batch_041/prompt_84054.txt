I am convening a reading group on moral psychology for non-specialists — a lawyer, two clinicians, a schoolteacher, and several graduate students from other departments — and the session I have to lead is the one on guilt and shame: whether either is the better moral emotion, and whether a culture can choose which one it runs on. I do not want you to explain guilt and shame to me; I know the material well enough. What I want is a plan for explaining it to people who have never read a word of moral philosophy, in ninety minutes.

The order matters more than anything else, and I would like you to justify it in terms of dependency: what has to be in place before each later step even makes sense to the group. My own inclination is that the load-bearing distinction is not the one everyone reaches for — guilt about what one did, shame about who one is — but rather an asymmetry of exit. Guilt comes with a repair script built into it: confess, apologize, make restitution, and the ledger is cleared. Shame has no such script, because no act of restitution undoes being the sort of person who did the thing. On that reading, shame's characteristic failure modes — concealment, withdrawal, sudden rage, the wish to be someone else entirely — follow from its structure rather than from its being the harsher of the two. Tell me whether that is the right spine to build the session on, or whether I have it backwards; and if I have it backwards, what the right spine is.

Then give me the single case I should carry the whole ninety minutes on. It has to be one in which the two emotions can be prised apart in front of the group without any theoretical vocabulary, and I want you to say what the case must have and what it must conspicuously lack. I had thought of a student who plagiarizes one paragraph of a term paper, but I suspect the case has to be one where the act is small and the self-implication is total, so that the group feels why the exit asymmetry bites. Say whether that is right, and whether the case should be invented or drawn from ordinary life.

I want the misconception map next, in the order the misconceptions will actually surface in the room, with the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. My guesses are these: that shame is simply the fear of other people's disapproval and therefore merely prudential; that guilt is internal and shame external, so guilt is the more autonomous and hence the better emotion; that shame is what collectivist societies run on and guilt what liberal ones run on, the latter being the grown-up stage; and that the question of which is better is settled by whichever emotion produces better behavior, as though that were an empirical matter with an answer. Tell me which of these the group will actually arrive with, which they will instead form during the session, and which of my four is a non-issue.

For the final stretch I want to turn to whether a culture can choose, given that an emotional repertoire is transmitted by institutions, rituals, and the ordinary practices of blame and forgiveness rather than by argument. Give me the one concrete contemporary practice I should put in front of them so that the question stops being abstract, and tell me what that practice must illustrate.

Finish with two things: a question I can put to the group in the last ten minutes that would show me whether they have grasped the distinction rather than merely learned to use the words, and an explicit list of what I should leave out at this level and say that I am leaving out.

One request about your own confidence. For everything you tell me to teach, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. If the claim that shame is about the whole self is itself disputed, or if the exit asymmetry is one camp's way of drawing the line rather than a neutral description of the emotions, I need to know that before I stand up in front of the room.
