I am presenting a paper at our department's political theory workshop next month and I need a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defence of the paper itself. The workshop is where I most expect to be dismantled, so I want to walk in having already lost the arguments on paper.

My thesis is this: the problem of dirty hands is real, but it has been mislocated. It is not, at bottom, a problem about guilt, remorse, or what gets called moral remainder. It is a problem about standing. When a politician does the wrong thing for the right reason, what she forfeits is not a clean conscience but the standing to make certain claims — to invoke the very rules she has just broken, to blame, to punish, to demand trust, to speak as one who keeps her word. On this view the stain is not a psychological cost lodged inside the agent but a relational fact about her position vis-à-vis the people she governs, and if it is discharged at all, it is discharged by her accepting constraints she could easily evade, not by her feeling terrible. I also want to defend a secondary and more contentious claim: that the hands dirtied by refusing to act — by letting the atrocity proceed in order to keep one's own record clean — are dirtier, not cleaner, than the hands dirtied by acting. I suspect this asymmetry is the part that will actually get me into trouble.

The audience, as precisely as I can put it: a majority are analytic moral philosophers, several with consequentialist sympathies, who will want to know why any of this is not just rule-consequentialism in a melancholy mood, or a confused way of reporting a conflict between prima facie duties that a decent decision procedure would resolve without residue. Two are Kantians who will press on whether the politician is treating anyone merely as a means, whether the maxim survives universalisation, and whether my talk of "standing" is doing covert work that a properly specified duty would do openly. One is a political realist who thinks the entire problem is a philosopher's artefact and will want to know what observable difference any of it makes to what politicians actually do on a Tuesday. And one works on moral responsibility and the blame/standing distinction in the Strawsonian vein — she is the most dangerous person in the room, because she knows the standing literature better than I do and will hear every place where I am quietly borrowing from it.

Build me the prep sheet. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room — not the ones I would get from a first-year ethics tutorial, and not the ones I would get from my fellow rationalists. Order them by how much damage they would do to the paper, not by how likely they are to be raised. Phrase each one the way the person who raises it would phrase it, in their own vocabulary, so that I can recognise it coming. For each, give me a two-minute spoken reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front before the first question is asked, what to hold under pressure, and which single reply has to land for the thesis to survive the session. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks, and say plainly why I am hoping that. If my secondary asymmetry claim is simply false rather than merely contentious, say so rather than defending it for me.

Begin with a one-sentence bottom line — which objection, if it comes, actually sinks the paper — and then give the reasoning and the sheet itself, with no preamble and no restating of my request. Write in British English. Do not give me reading lists, citations, or names of texts to consult; I want the arguments, not the bibliography.
