I am a first-year doctoral student in philosophy who has just been handed the teaching for the second half of an upper-level undergraduate course on set theory and its philosophy, and I have to write the final examination this week. The material has become unexpectedly personal. A friend in the mathematics department — whose logic is considerably better than his philosophy — insisted over dinner that Skolem's paradox shows set theory to be either inconsistent or vacuous: a countable model that "believes" it contains uncountable sets is simply a model that is wrong about itself, and this exposes the word "uncountable" as marking nothing. I gave the standard reply, that the enumeration witnessing the model's countability lives outside the model and therefore no contradiction follows, and he was unimpressed; so, candidly, was I, because I could not say clearly why that reply is supposed to be satisfying rather than a way of changing the subject. I want the exam to force students into exactly that fight rather than letting them recite the reply, and I want the top band to have to take a stand on how much the reply actually accomplishes.

What I need is a single essay question, worth 25 marks and intended for sixty minutes of writing, built around a short flawed argument that students must diagnose rather than merely comment on. I have drafted the argument myself — it is a composite of things last year's cohort wrote, not a quotation from anyone — and it runs as follows. Assume set theory has a model at all; then by the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem it has a countable model, M. Inside M, Cantor's theorem holds, so M proves that the set it calls the set of real numbers is uncountable. But M is countable, and every subset of a countable set is countable, so the very set that M calls the reals is countable. Hence, of one and the same object, we have it that the reals are both countable and uncountable. Either set theory is inconsistent, or "uncountable" marks no property at all. Please write the question so that it requires the students to state precisely what the paradox is and why it is not a contradiction; to locate the exact step at which this argument fails and to say what would have to be true of the model and the metatheory for it to succeed; and then to move past the diagnosis to the philosophical question — how strong a case the paradox gives for the view that cardinality is not absolute, what a defender of that view would have to establish that the diagnosis above does not establish, and what the most serious reply is. I would also like a final part, aimed at the top band, asking whether the difficulty is a defect of first-order axiomatization specifically, and whether reformulating set theory in a second-order idiom removes the problem or merely relocates it; I want that part to require a defended verdict, not a survey.

Please give me the question itself, a model answer of about seven hundred words, and a marking scheme that totals 25 marks and is built around the specific moves a strong answer must make, with the marks each is worth. The scheme should distinguish an A from a B: a B states the internal/external distinction correctly and stops there, treating the paradox as fully dissolved, whereas an A must do more — show precisely why the flawed argument's conclusion does not follow, explain what the non-absoluteness of countability and of the power-set operation does and does not show, and give an articulate account of the strongest relativist worry together with the best reply to it, and vice versa. For each must-hit point, add a line on how candidates typically get it wrong, and say which of the errors should cost only a mark or two and which should be treated as fatal to the essay's central claim. The cohort is mixed — about half philosophers, half mathematicians — so the question should be answerable by either without favouring the other. Since this is a closed examination, nothing in the question or the marking scheme may require students to cite literature, attribute views to named authors, or produce reading lists; marks should attach to the arguments and the technical moves, not to who said what, and any position the model answer mentions should be described in a way that stands on its own without the name.
