I'm preparing a handout for a decision theory seminar on the Pasadena game and the problem of undefined expected values. Please construct a taxonomy of the positions one might take on the question: when a prospect's expected value is undefined because its payoff series is conditionally convergent, what should a theory of rational choice say about whether to play? The taxonomy should be a decision tree whose branch nodes are diagnostic questions—each phrased as a concrete case or claim that a student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to—and whose leaves are named positions (or 'unnamed' if no one defends them), each with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Build the tree from the logical space of possible answers, not from a list of authors. The first branch should separate views on whether the expected value is genuinely undefined, whether it is defined by a canonical ordering (such as the limit of partial sums in the natural order), or whether the prospect has no expected value but still a determinate rational status. Further branches should separate whether rationality permits, requires, or forbids playing; whether the undefinedness is a defect in decision theory or in the prospect itself; and whether the problem generalizes to finite decisions or is an artifact of infinite games. Include the St. Petersburg game and variants with divergent or oscillating series as contrast cases if they help separate positions, but do not let the taxonomy collapse into a survey of named paradoxes. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and note which combinations of answers are unoccupied, with a brief diagnosis of whether the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. The deliverable should be usable as a sorting instrument: a reader who answers the branch questions should end up at a position that reflects what they actually think about the Pasadena case, not at a label they recognize.
