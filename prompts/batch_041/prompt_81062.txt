I'm a philosophy PhD student, and next month I have ninety minutes with an interdisciplinary graduate workshop: half philosophers of science who will argue about natural kinds all afternoon but freeze at a regression table, half quantitative sociologists who fit age–period–cohort models without thinking and regard the metaphysics as noise. The question on the table is whether "generations" — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z — are real kinds. Don't argue the question for me; plan how I should teach it.

I want the session to turn on the identification problem: because cohort is defined as period minus age, the three are linearly dependent, so no pattern in the data alone can separate a cohort effect from an age effect from a period effect, and every generational claim encodes a constraint the analyst chose to impose. They should leave feeling that as a limit on what could possibly be true, not as a technicality to be worked around.

So give me the order: what has to be in place before that lands, and where the two other candidates belong — the distinction between a cohort and a generation that shares a fate, and the thought that a kind can be real without an essence if its properties cluster and something holds the cluster together. Give me the one case that carries the session, and say what it must have and lack. Give me the misconceptions each half of the room will bring and the point in the sequence where I head each off — I predict "a generation is just an age group" from one side and "the identification problem shows generational talk is empty" from the other. Tell me the closing question that would show whether they have the constraint or only the vocabulary, and what I should openly refuse to settle in ninety minutes.
