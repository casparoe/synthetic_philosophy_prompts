Question 3 (25 points). Browning's Ordinary Men (1992) and Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners (1996) both deal with Reserve Police Battalion 101. Set out each author's explanation of why the men killed. What does each say about the role of antisemitism, and what about the role of situational pressure? Which explanation do you find more adequate, and why? Finally, is explaining why a perpetrator acted as he did the same thing as excusing him?

Student's answer:

Both authors are answering the same question: why did a battalion of middle-aged Hamburg reservists, most of them not committed Nazis, shoot Jewish men, women and children in occupied Poland in 1942-43? Both also work mainly from the same evidence, the postwar interrogations of the battalion's own men.

Browning's explanation is situational. The men weren't picked for fanaticism; they were older, working-class men who had been drafted and found unfit for front-line service. What turned them into killers was the situation. They were under officers who expected them to shoot, they didn't want to look like cowards in front of their comrades, and after the first massacre the killing became routine. His central piece of evidence is the offer Trapp made at Józefów, before the battalion's first massacre: any man who didn't feel up to it could step out. Only about a dozen of the roughly 500 did. For Browning this shows the men had a genuine choice, and that refusing wasn't dangerous, only awkward — which is what makes the case so disturbing.

Goldhagen's explanation is cultural. He argues that a specific, virulent "eliminationist" antisemitism had deep roots in German political culture, and that it made the murder of the Jews both thinkable and desirable to ordinary Germans. The men of the battalion were therefore not ordinary men at all but ordinary Germans, carriers of a view of Jews as a mortal threat that had to be removed. They killed voluntarily and often eagerly. For him the offer to step out isn't a puzzle at all: he stresses that no German was ever seriously punished for refusing to kill.

I think Browning's account has real problems. The men didn't just follow orders — they hunted Jews down, humiliated them, and often killed more than they were told to, and the Milgram story about obedience doesn't explain that. On his own account the men had a genuine choice, and the choice they kept making was to shoot, which is exactly what makes the case so disturbing. What the situational story really comes down to is that refusing would have meant being ostracized in the unit and losing any chance of promotion, and in a war that amounted to duress. Since both books are built on the same interrogations, the disagreement between them isn't really an empirical one anyway; it comes down to how much weight you give the cultural background. And once Browning concedes that antisemitism was pervasive in Germany, I don't see that his situational factors do any independent work — they collapse into Goldhagen's. On the last part of the question: explaining is not excusing. Goldhagen's account is actually the more damning of the two, because on his view the killers did exactly what they wanted to do.
