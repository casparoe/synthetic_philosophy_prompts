The exam question was:

Two distinctions we drew this term:

(i) A social kind is thin when its members are held together only by collective acceptance of a status function (as with money, or a legal border), and thick when membership is additionally sustained by causal feedback — expectations, self-understandings, institutional incentives — that shapes the members themselves (as many claim for race and gender).

(ii) Hacking's dynamic nominalism claims that classifications and the people classified interact; a merely nominalist claim about a category is only that we could have drawn the category differently.

Now consider this case. In 2031 a mid-sized city creates a voluntary certification, Night-Owl Resident (NOR), for people who habitually work or socialize between 1 and 5 am. NOR holders get a small tax credit and priority on 24-hour transit. Within two years insurers start pricing policies on NOR status, employers list it as a plus, a NOR subculture with its own slang and hangouts appears, and some residents report they began staying up later precisely because NOR status had become advantageous.

(a) Is NOR thin or thick on distinction (i)? Justify.
(b) Using (ii), explain what it would mean to say NOR is socially constructed, and distinguish that from the merely nominalist claim.
(c) Does the looping effect, if present, make NOR more or less real? Explain.

And here's what the student turned in:

(a) NOR is a thick social kind. The status function is clearly doing work — the city counts a person as NOR, and that unlocks the tax credit and the transit priority, so collective acceptance is part of the story. But it's thick and not thin because the label feeds back into behavior: insurers price on it, employers reward it, and people actually change their sleep schedules in order to become or stay NOR. Membership is sustained by that causal loop, not just by acceptance.

(b) Hacking's dynamic nominalism says our classifications and the people classified interact, and NOR is a good illustration: the category wasn't just applied to a pre-existing group of night people, it helped make one. The merely nominalist claim would be much weaker — it would only say that we picked the label NOR and could have picked a different one. On the dynamic nominalist picture the label isn't arbitrary in that thin sense, because once it's circulating it shapes what there is to classify. On Searle's account of institutional facts, though, it's the looping effect that makes something a social kind in the first place — take away the feedback and a status function like money wouldn't be real at all.

(c) The loop makes NOR more real, not less, since a classification that changes people's behavior has causal powers and that's about as real as social kinds get. Without the loop NOR would just be a fiction. That said, NOR being voluntary is a reason the loop can't really get going here: since nobody's forced into the category, anyone who dislikes it can opt out, so it never becomes the kind of thing that constrains people. That's basically why race counts as thick and NOR doesn't.
