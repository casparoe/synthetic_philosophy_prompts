I run the staff side of something my city calls the Civic Lab, and after four years I still can't give a clean answer when councillors ask me what we're actually for. Here's the machine. Twice a year we pull a stratified random sample of about sixty residents, pay them, and put them in a room for a weekend with briefings from city staff and outside experts; they deliberate and vote on recommendations about whatever council has sent us — fare-free transit, a supervised consumption site, rezoning near the river. Anything that clears two-thirds goes to council, which has to answer in writing. Two things about how we run it that I don't think anyone else does. First, halfway through the weekend we make the participants write down what they'd need to learn in order to change their minds, and staff have to answer those questions in writing before anyone votes — sometimes that's a pilot result, often it's just that nobody has ever measured the thing. Second, we keep the minority reports and publish them alongside the majority ones, unreconciled, on the theory that averaging the dissent away looses something. Then there's the research side, which is a seperate unit: randomized pilots on street lighting, on 311 scheduling, on how often we collect garbage. Our charter says that when a pilot contradicts an assembly recommendation we don't shelve the recommendation, we remand it — go back to the assembly, show them the numbers, let them revise or reaffirm, and if they reaffirm, council has to explain out loud why it's overruling them. And the permanent fight: our sensor corps, about two hundred residents with cheap air monitors in their yards, keeps feeding us data the health department won't touch, while the assembly keeps citing it in its reports.

What I want is the philosophical x-ray. What does this arrangement presuppose about democracy, and about inquiry — what has to be true about each for the thing to make sense — and is it the same picture in both halves, or are two incompatible ones welded together, one where the public supplies the ends and the experts supply the means, and one where collective knowing is itself the point? Which rivals does it rule out, and which would it be perfectly happy with (plain preference aggregation, technocracy, the public-as-customer model)? Is the remand rule even coherent, given that we also treat the vote as final-ish and the data as revisable — who's the authority when they disagree, and what does that rule implicitly say a pilot is for? And is the sensor corps a separate commitment or the same one showing up again in different clothes? Somebody told me Dewey would either love this or hate it and I never got a straight answer, so give me the version where you tell me which parts are strict presuppositions, which are just things we happen to do that several different theories would license, and what would have to change if we dropped the idea that a democratic decision can be better than an aggregate of private preferences.
