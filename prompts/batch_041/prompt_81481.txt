"OK so UDT in one go. The basic move is that you don't choose between actions, you choose between *policies* — entire functions from situations to actions — and you pick the policy whose expected utility is highest, where the expectation is over your prior, before you've observed anything at all. So the question isn't 'what should I do now' but 'what policy would I have picked if I'd had to commit before finding out which situation I'd wake up in'.

In Newcomb's problem this gets you one-boxing: the policy 'one-box' is the one that scores well in the worlds where the predictor is right, and the predictor's accuracy is already baked into the prior. Because the policy is fixed before any observation, an updateless agent doesn't really update on evidence the way we do — it works out its policy once and then just executes it, and new information can't talk it out of the policy, since the new information was already in the prior.

On the smoking lesion it says don't smoke, same as EDT: the smoking policy has a higher probability of the lesion, so it's out. Nice illustration that it's still evidential reasoning under the hood, just applied to policies instead of actions.

Counterfactual mugging: it pays. The intuition is that from the agent's point of view the coin comes up before the policy gets picked, so the payment is really a precommitment, and precommitment is the whole trick. Parfit's hitchhiker is the same story — it pays the driver, where a causal agent wouldn't, because it's committed in advance.

The formal setup is a Solomonoff prior over environments, where the environment is a program that might contain a copy of your own decision algorithm, so the agent can estimate the probability that the other player in a prisoner's dilemma is running the same code as it is. That's how it cooperates with a copy of itself.

Known weaknesses: the Solomonoff prior is uncomputable, so the whole thing is uncomputable; and it has no account of logical uncertainty.

His example at the end: you're going to play a two-armed bandit, and you have to pick your policy in advance, before you know which arm pays. He says UDT picks 'always pull the left arm' and then never learns anything, which he thinks is obviously stupid and shows the formalism is a solution in search of a problem.

And it says you should pay the blackmailer."
