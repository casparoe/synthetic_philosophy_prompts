I'm a PhD student in political theory and I've somehow been volunteered to moderate the Q&A after next week's visiting speaker's talk. He works on political obligation and defends a fairness/fair-play line — roughly the idea that we're bound to do our part in a cooperative scheme that delivers benefits to us, benefits we've either accepted or can't reasonably reject. He also has a paper claiming civil disobedience can be squared with that framework, and honestly that's the part I don't buy, so I want the Q&A to put pressure there.

Draft me 8-10 questions. They should be keyed to the actual content of the position, not warm-ups — no asking how he got into philosophy or to summarise his book. I'd like at least one each on the following, each with a follow-up that anticipates the answer he'll most likely give:

- the acceptance vs. mere receipt distinction, and whether the view quietly lets the comfortable opt out while binding people who had no real choice about taking the benefits (the self-sufficient outsider problem, basically — what does he say about someone who genuinely doesn't need the scheme?)
- the particularity problem: why obligations to *this* state and these co-citizens rather than to whatever cooperative scheme happens to benefit you, including transnational ones, and whether what we're obligated to is law as such or only reasonably just institutions
- what happens in a seriously unjust state. If fairness can't bind there, has the fairness grounding done any work that a natural duty of justice wasn't already doing?
- the free-rider tension in disobedience: if my obligation comes from fairness to others in the scheme, isn't refusing to comply just offloading my share onto them? I want the standard move (something like: the scheme is already failing to be fair, so I'm not bound, or disobedience is itself a way of pressing for fair terms) laid out and then a follow-up that makes him choose between biting a bullet and giving up the fairness grounding
- whether the view requires the disobedient to accept punishment, and if so why fairness would demand submission to a punishment that may itself be part of the unjust arrangement
- one on intergenerational and unchosen obligations — benefits received long before we could accept anything, institutions we never opted into — since that seems to strain the acceptance condition hardest

Finish with a question that forces him to say which of these he'd rather abandon: the acceptance condition, the particularity of the obligation, or the claim that disobedience can be justified on fairness grounds. Keep the follow-ups short and specific so I can actually deploy them in the room.
