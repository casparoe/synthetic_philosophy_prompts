ok so in my historiography seminar we spent a whole section on whether the Trojan War "actually happened" and two people in the class, Dana and Marcus, went back and forth until the prof cut them off, and i genuinly couldn't tell who was winning. Maybe you can.

Marcus: the digs at Hisarlik turned up a city layer that was destroyed right around the traditional date, and there are Hittite records naming a place that's almost certainly Wilusa and a people that's almost certainly the Ahhiyawa, so the war is basically historical — the Iliad is a poetic memory of something real.

Dana: none of that gets you a ten-year siege, a coalition of Greek kings, or a woman named Helen. A burnt city is compatible with an earthquake, an internal revolt, or some random raid. Marcus is sliding from "something happened near Troy" to "the Trojan War happened," and those aren't the same claim.

Marcus: we do this constantly for ancient events — we accept plenty on one narrative source plus archaeology. If we hold the Iliad to a bar we don't hold other ancient narratives to, we're just being snobs about poetry.

Dana: the difference is what the source is *for*. A poem is built to be memorable, not accurate, so when poem and dirt agree it's weaker evidence than when a chronicle and dirt agree. Genre changes the weight.

Then it got weird, because our prof had drawn a distinction in lecture between "thin" and "thick" historicity — thin meaning some real event is in there somewhere, thick meaning the story as told more or less happened — and both of them tried to claim it. Marcus said he'd only ever claimed thin and Dana was beating up a thick claim he never made. Dana said that's a dodge, because the claim people actually care about when they say "the war was real" is thick, and Marcus keeps retreating to thin the second he's pressed.

Also at one point Marcus said "well by your logic King Arthur might have been real too," and Dana said "maybe he was, that's not a counterexample," and they both acted like they'd scored.

Can you go through it move by move and say who's right on each one — the archaeology point, the double-standard point, the poem-vs-chronicle point, and the thin/thick retreat — and then say what both of them are getting wrong? Is there a reading where they're each right about different things, or does one of them actually win?
