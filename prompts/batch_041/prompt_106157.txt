I run a small risk-analysis unit — four analysts — inside a state agency whose decisions land on individuals: parole, child-welfare removals, and the licensing of high-hazard facilities. Our reports must end in a number. A statute requires each report to state "the probability" of the adverse outcome over a fixed horizon, and a hearing officer who is not a statistician signs off on it. We get roughly ninety minutes per case, we have no budget for new data collection, and we are not permitted to leave the probability box blank or to answer "it depends." In practice the arithmetic is never the hard part. The hard part is deciding what the case in front of us is a case of. A parolee belongs to a hundred overlapping populations — men; men convicted of a particular offense; men convicted of that offense with a prior supervision violation; men of that age in that county; men who scored a particular way on our instrument — and the observed frequencies differ wildly across them, so the number we report is largely an artifact of the class we chose. At present we use a rule of thumb a predecessor left behind: use the narrowest class for which you have at least a hundred prior cases. A colleague wants to replace it with: compute the frequency in every class you can defend and average them. I suspect both rules are bad, but I cannot articulate why in a way my analysts can actually apply, and the last time I tried, one of them asked me whether the "right" class is a fact we are failing to find or a choice we are making and hiding — and I did not have an answer.

Please turn whatever the philosophy of probability genuinely knows about this — the reference class problem and the proposals that have been made for coping with it — into a decision procedure my unit can run on a live case. I want the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure terminates in a defensible single number, where it terminates in a range or in a refusal to supply one, and which steps are irreducibly matters of judgment or of values that should be escalated rather than settled by an analyst. It has to survive the cases that actually break us: classes nested inside one another; classes that conflict without either being nested; classes defined after the fact by the very outcome we are predicting; and classes so small that the frequency is a fiction. Two things I especially want addressed. First, whether the narrowest-class rule can be saved by some restriction, given that the narrowest class of all is the single case itself, whose frequency is either zero or one and therefore tells us nothing. Second, whether averaging the frequencies is ever more than a way of laundering the choice of classes. Then run the procedure on three cases and tell me what it outputs, even where the output is one the agency will hate: a parolee with a long record in a class of four thousand priors and a shorter, more relevant record in a subclass of sixty; a one-off licensing decision for a facility of a type that has existed twelve times, three of which failed; and a case in which the only class that is clearly relevant is one we are legally forbidden to use as a basis for the decision. Finish by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do, and what a hearing officer should be told about the epistemic status of the number printed in the box.
