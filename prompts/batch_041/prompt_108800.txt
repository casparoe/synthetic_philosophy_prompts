OK so I keep running into the same move and it's starting to bother me. Whenever anekāntavāda comes up — in a seminar, at a conference, in some intro lecture — someone trots out the blind men and the elephant. You know the one: a bunch of blind guys feel different parts of an elephant, one says it's a wall, one says a rope, one says a tree trunk, one says a spear, and the moral is that each has a piece of the truth, nobody has the whole thing, so all the views are true in their own way. It's usually wheeled in to make anekāntavāda sound reasonable — see, it's just the insight that reality has many sides and every judgement is true from some standpoint. And a colleague of mine, who is otherwise sharp, uses it to argue that anekāntavāda is basically relativism with extra steps, a kind of polite perspectivalism where everyone gets a trophy.

I dont think that's right, but I want to see exactly where the analogy is carrying the argument versus where the doctrine is being smuggled in or distorted. So: lay out the correspondence explicitly — what in the parable is supposed to map onto a naya, onto the seven predications of syādvāda, onto the claim that an object has infinitely many aspects, onto the omniscient kevala standpoint if anything maps to that at all. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. I have a suspicion that the blind men are all just wrong in the ordinary sense, that "it's a wall" is false and not true-from-a-standpoint, and that this disanalogy matters — but argue it, don't just agree with me. Also the parable's closing moral, everybody's equally right and nobody's wrong, looks like straight relativism, which is exactly what anekāntavāda denies when it insists the elephant is really there and the aspects are really in it. Does the analogy import a perspectivalism the doctrine doesn't actually hold? And does the tolerant, everyone-gets-a-piece conclusion people want to draw survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the parable doing all the work the whole time?

Last thing: if this analogy is as bad as I think it is, what would be a better one to use in a lecture, given that my audience won't know any Jain technical vocabulary? Don't give me a bibliography, just the analysis.
