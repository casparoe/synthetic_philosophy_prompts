I commission popular history for a mid-sized trade publisher, and roughly once a month a proposal arrives claiming the accepted story is wrong — a civilization older than the last ice age, contact between continents the textbooks say never happened, a wholesale re-dating of the ancient world argued from statistics on texts. I have no in-house historian, three weeks to decide, and an editorial board that will ask me to defend the call. Publishing crankery costs us credibility; turning down real revisionism is its own failure, and I can't sort them by whether the author holds a doctorate. Write me a decision procedure I can actually run on a proposal: the questions to ask in order, what counts as an answer good enough to pass, where it stops with a no, and where it should hand the decision back to me because what is at stake is editorial values rather than truth. I have fragments of advice — does the thesis survive any evidence at all, are its lines of support independent or do they all hang from a single premise, do the citations bottom out in primary sources — but no way to order or weigh them, and I don't want the result collapsing into the slogan that science is falsifiable, which seems both to over- and under-reject. It must separate pseudohistory from honest-but-wrong scholarship, from ordinary speculative history, and from outright fraud. Then run it on three proposals — a lost civilization before the ice age, a book claiming the conventional chronology of the ancient world was fabricated during the Renaissance, and a book claiming a celebrated ancient artifact is a modern forgery — and tell me which one it decides against my instincts, which steps are judgment calls dressed up as tests, and what it cannot settle.
