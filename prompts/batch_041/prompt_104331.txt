I am preparing a short position paper on the identity of material objects for a metaphysics reading group and, before I circulate it, I want a consistency audit rather than a defence. Here is where I have ended up, set out as flatly as I can manage.

(1) Endurantism. A material object is wholly present at every moment at which it exists. It does not persist by having numerically distinct temporal parts at numerically distinct times; when I say the ship survives the replacement of its planks, I mean that one and the same object is present before and after.

(2) Realism about the ordinary. Ships, tables, tools and organisms are genuine, mind-independent items in the world's inventory. They are not fictions, not shorthand for patterns in our experience, and not artefacts of our interests.

(3) The causal-continuity criterion. What carries an object's identity through change is the continuity of its causal history, an unbroken causal process linking the earlier object to the later one, and not the persistence of any of its matter, nor mere similarity of shape or function. Applied to the familiar case: the ship that is repaired plank by plank over many years is the original ship, because the process of repair is causally continuous with the launching; the ship subsequently reassembled from the original planks is a replica, however faithful, because the disassembly broke its causal history. I intend this as a criterion for material objects generally, not only for artefacts.

(4) Determinacy of identity. Identity is never vague. For any object x and any later object y, it is determinately true or determinately false that x = y. There are no borderline cases in which x is neither the same as y nor distinct from it.

(5) No arbitrariness. Sharp boundaries are drawn by nature. Wherever there is a fact of the matter about where one thing stops and another begins, or about when a thing has ceased and a different thing has begun, that line is fixed by the world and not by our conventions, interests or descriptive convenience.

(6) Unrestricted composition. Whenever there are some objects, there is a sum of them. I see no principled restriction on what composes what, and I am untroubled by the odd objects this yields.

What I want is an audit, not a summary. Which of these are in genuine tension with which, which pairs only look as though they are, and how severe is each real conflict? Order the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad. For each, tell me what would have to give and what the least costly repair would be; then tell me which single commitment, if I abandoned it, would relieve the most pressure overall, and what abandoning it would cost me. I have no stake in which one turns out to be the weak link; I would simply like to know where it is. Finally, take the Ship of Theseus itself and tell me whether my verdict in (3) still stands once the other commitments are taken at face value.
