Last term in a comparative genocide seminar someone kept saying 'the Holocaust is unique' everytime anyone brought up the Armenian genocide or the Holodomor, and it was plainly meant to close the discussion rather than open it. I still can't tell whether that line states something trivially true, something substantive and defensible, or something false, and I want it pulled apart before I have to sit through it again.

Take the slogan and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making, as a menu rather than one defended reading. Start from the near-tautology — every historical event is unique in some respect — and work up to the strong claim that it shares no common measure with anything else. Along the way separate the ones that sound alike but aren't: unique vs unprecedented vs incomparable; the uniqueness of the event vs of its causes vs of the perpetrators' intent vs of the victims' experience; and the question of whether any of these is really a descriptive claim at all or a moral demand in disguise (that it must never be relativised or normalised).

For each reading say whether it's plausible, whether it's even the sort of thing that could be true, and crucially whether it would actually do the work he wanted — i.e. block the comparison. Flag any that are self-undermining, e.g. a uniqueness so strong that it stops you saying what the thing is a case of.

Then the Bauman-ish reading, that it's a product of modernity rather than a lapse back into barbarism: does that support the uniqueness claim or undercut it? It seems to do both depending on how you take it, and I'd like to know which.

Finish by saying which reading he most likely meant and whether any of them actually gets him his conclusion that the comparison is off the table.
