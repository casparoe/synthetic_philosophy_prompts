I'm on the investment comittee at the small foundation I used to run (about $40m, we pay out 5% a year, and under the current plan the whole thing gets spent down by 2032). Last night my friend Dana — also a trustee, ex-trader — and I argued for two hours about something that's on the agenda for Tuesday, and now I have to write the opinion I'll circulate before the vote.

Here's the thing itself. A fund has offered us a stake in a single binary outcome — a drug that either gets approved or it doesnt; the analysts we hired say 60% it hits. We can put in however much we want, up to the entire endowment. If it hits, each dollar we put in becomes three. If it misses, those dollars are just gone. Dana's math: $1.80 expected per dollar, so if we play small and it hits we've turned down an expected $32m of grants. His line is that our job is to maximize expected dollars for the people we exist to serve, and that flinching because we might end up at zero is being precious with other people's money. When I brought up the idea of only ever betting some fixed fraction of your bankroll, he said that rule is for people who'll be making bets forever, and we won't be — there's no next bet, the foundation winds up in a few years, so the long-run argument doesn't even apply to us.

My gut says if this misses we don't have a bad year, we're finished, and we can't raise more (no donors left). But I couldn't say why Dana's wrong, and I don't want the memo to just say it feels too risky.

Draft the written opinion for the board: recommend a concrete course of action — how much, if anything, to commit, and whether we should adopt a standing rule for offers like this in the future — with the reasoning spelled out, and with the strongest version of Dana's case stated fairly enough that he'd recognize it as his own. Don't give me generic stuff about diversification; it's one binary deal, either we take a piece of it or we don't.
