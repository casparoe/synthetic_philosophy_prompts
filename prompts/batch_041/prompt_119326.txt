In my History of Political Economy seminar we have just finished the section on the Wealth of Nations, and our lecturer ended the unit on Book IV with a short characterisation of Smith on colonies that I have since been repeating in my own essays more or less verbatim. It ran as follows.

"Smith's treatment of colonies is best understood as a sustained attack on empire. His central claim is that colonies are simply not worth having: the colonial trade brought Britain no real advantage, and the entire apparatus of monopoly, naval protection and colonial administration was a pure loss to the home country. On his account the interests of the colonies and of the mother country are naturally harmonious — free trade would serve both — so the political tie serves no purpose except to enrich a small class of merchants and planters at everyone else's expense. He therefore urged Britain to give the American colonies up, and predicted that they would separate anyway. His objection to the chartered companies, such as the East India Company, was simply that they were monopolies. In all of this he is the first systematic anti-imperialist in the modern sense, though the case he makes is economic through and through: he has nothing to say about the wrongs suffered by colonised peoples themselves."

I found this compelling at the time, partly because it fitted neatly with the lectures on the mercantile system, and I am aware that I have been treating it as settled. What I would like is an audit rather than a general essay on Smith. Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me, for each, whether a Smith specialist would accept it as stated, would regard it as a defensible simplification, or would say it is simply wrong; where the summary is inaccurate, say what Smith actually held and how the difference matters. I am particularly unsure about two things. First, whether the lecturer has correctly rendered Smith's practical proposal for the American colonies, since if he did not simply advocate letting them go, I would like to know what he did propose and why that bears on the label "anti-imperialist in the modern sense." Second, whether it is fair to say that Smith's case is economic through and through and silent on the treatment of colonised peoples. Finally, tell me which of the lecturer's concluding judgements survive once the inaccurate parts are corrected, and which are only defensible if one of the inaccurate parts is retained.
