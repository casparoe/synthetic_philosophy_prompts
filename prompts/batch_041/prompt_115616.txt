I teach a grad seminar in epistemology and we just wrapped the unit on the lottery and the preface, and I've come out of it realizing I can't actually state the principle behind my own verdicts, which is a little embarassing for someone who's been assigning this stuff for a decade. So: take the role of a philosopher who works on the norms of belief and knows the formal literature cold, think carefully before you commit to anything, and please start by brainstorming the space of candidate principles — I'd much rather see three or four live options sketched out then get the first threshold view that pops into your head. What I want at the end is a principle (or a small set of them) stated precisely enough to actually apply, the cases that motivate each clause, what it predicts for the cases below that I don't have a verdict on, and a straight answer about which of my verdicts it forces me to give up.

Here's my gut, case by case. Lottery with exactly one ticket in the drum and it's mine: sure, my ticket loses, I'd say I know it. Two tickets, one mine: I get squeamish, but I'll still say my ticket loses, though I wouldn't stake much on it. A hundred tickets, one mine: I won't say my ticket loses — not that it won't, but that I don't believe it — and yet I'll happily say that some ticket wins, and that not every ticket loses. A thousand tickets and I hold 999 of them: now I believe I win, and I also believe of any particular one of the other guy's tickets that it lost. So the willingness to believe flips somewhere in the middle and flips back when the odds flip, which is roughly what I'd expect, but I can't tell you what's doing the work. Now the book. I've just finished a monograph with a hundred independent claims in it, each of which I'd bet a kidney on, and I refuse to believe the conjunction of all hundred — I'll even write in the preface that I'm sure something in there is wrong. That feels like the sane thing to do, and it feels like exactly the same shape of case as the thousand-ticket lottery, except that there I believe the disjunction (that not all the tickets lost) while here I disbelieve the conjunction, and I have a nagging sense those two attitudes are two sides of one coin. I also can't tell whether the real unit is the belief or the assertion: in the book case I'd assert all hundred sentences in the same breath, one after another, and balk only at the one sentence that conjoins them. Here's a half-baked principle I've been toying with, so you have something to shoot at: you may believe P just in case the probability of error is below a threshold, and the threshold rises with the stakes and falls with how many things you're stacking up. Test it against everything above and repair or replace it as needed. And a few I genuinely don't have a verdict on, so tell me what your principle says and whether you'd endorse it: (a) a hundred-step proof where each step is 99.9% reliable and I'm asked whether I believe the theorem; (b) a book whose hundredth claim is that at least one claim in the book is false; (c) a case where I can win a prize only by asserting the conjunction of a hundred claims I'd each bet a kidney on; (d) two independent claims, each 99% likely — do I believe the conjunction, and does your answer change if the two claims are about the same subject matter instead of independent. Be concrete about where the threshold lives and why, and don't hedge at the end — if your principle costs me one of my verdicts, say so and defend the sacrifice.
