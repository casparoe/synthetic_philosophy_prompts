And here's what the student wrote:

(a) The utility monster is a being that gets vastly more utility out of every unit of resource than a normal person does, so whenever it takes something from someone else, or whenever you redirect anything its way, the total utility in the world goes up. A total utilitarian, who just wants to maximize the sum of utility, is therefore committed to handing everything over to the monster and letting it consume everyone else's welfare. Nozick's point is that this is monstrous, and that it shows the aggregative picture runs roughshod over the separateness of persons — it treats people as mere containers of utility who can be sacrificed whenever the sums come out right. This is basically the same problem as the repugnant conclusion, since in both cases the theory tells us to accept an intuitively awful outcome just because the numbers work out, so I'll treat the two as one objection.

(b) Now the inverted monster. Since it only ever adds suffering, you might think a suffering-minimizer has nothing to worry about, and just says get rid of it. But I think the parallel does hold. The negative utilitarian is committed to counting the monster's suffering in the total, and once you're counting totals you're in the same boat as the total utilitarian: you'll accept any amount of individual sacrifice as long as the total goes down. So the inverted monster is a mirror-image counterexample, and suffering-focused views end up in the same trouble as classical utilitarianism.

(c) For the original monster, I don't think this refutes utilitarianism on its own — it's a bullet a total utilitarian could try to bite, or use to motivate a fix like prioritarianism, so it's a strong counterintuitive implication rather than a proof of falsity. But for the negative utilitarian case, as I argued above, I think it really is fatal, because a view that makes you endorse pure suffering isn't a view anyone could hold.
