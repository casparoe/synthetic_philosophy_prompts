I'm a postdoc in a quantum information group, and I have been talked into running the opening session of a new reading group on the philosophy of quantum mechanics, which will be attended by eight or nine graduate students who treat the word interpretation roughly as a term of abuse and who will be sitting in a seminar room with one whiteboard and bad coffee for seventy-five minutes. The topic I have been handed is whether the wavefunction is real, meaning the disagreement between the views on which the quantum state describes the world and the views on which it describes somebody's knowledge or information. I don't want the debate explained to me; I know it well enough to be unsure how to teach it. I want a plan. Tell me the order the ideas have to come in and what depends on what. My own sense is that the session stands or falls on whether they first learn to keep three things apart — the state as a piece of mathematics inside a formalism, the state as a claim about how things are, and the state as a summary of what someone knows — and that the question only becomes well formed for them once they see that the classical analogy, in which an epistemic state is a distribution over definite underlying conditions, is a contestable model of quantum states rather than the default reading of the word knowledge. Tell me where the load-bearing step in that sequence is, and where I should be willing to lose time.

I would also like the whole session carried by one example rather than five, and I have a candidate I distrust. I have been using the case of two labs that prepare the same mixed state by different recipes, one by mixing equal numbers of a pair of orthogonal states and the other by mixing equal numbers of a different orthogonal pair that happens to yield the same density matrix, and it always teaches the wrong lesson: the students come away with the picture in which the state is ignorance about a definite underlying condition, which is exactly the reading I want them to see as a claim rather than as common sense. Should that case carry the session anyway, and if not, what should, and what does whatever carries it have to have and to lack in order not to smuggle in the answer? Then the misconceptions. I can already predict a few — that an epistemic view means the state is in somebody's head, or is just instrumentalism; that an ontic view means the wavefunction is a physical wave in space like a water wave; that a no-go result rules out epistemic readings period, rather than under an assumption about preparation independence that nobody has independently motivated; that the whole dispute is verbal and would be settled by a good dictionary. I want the rest of the list, in the order they will actually surface in that room, and for each one the exact point in the sequence where I should head it off rather than merely correct it afterwards, plus any that I should deliberately leave standing for ten minutes because defusing it early would cost me a dependency it happens to be sitting on.

Finally, tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and admit I am refusing: the full proof of the no-go theorem, the details of the ontological-models framework, the toy models that reproduce various quantum phenomena while treating states as epistemic, the agent-centred views. Tell me what the single closing question should be, something answerable in a sentence, that would show me whether they have understood the question rather than merely picked up the vocabulary, and say plainly what I should do with the remaining time if the answer to that question is the wrong one. I don't need citations, a reading list, or any suggestions for further reading; I have the papers, and what I cannot reconstruct from them is the teaching. Write the whole thing in continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italicised text and no tables, the way you would set it out in an email to a colleague who is about to teach the same session.
