A friend and I argued until two in the morning about whether there is a fact of the matter about heaps, and I would like my scattered verdicts turned into something principled. Here is what I hold. Ten thousand grains piled up is a heap and three grains is not, and I do not think that is merely my reaction: someone who said the three-grain pile was a heap would be wrong. Adding a single grain never turns a non-heap into a heap, and I do not believe there is any grain count at which that happens — not a count I could discover, and not one that is there to be discovered at all. About a middling pile, say five thousand grains, I think there is no answer to the question whether it is a heap; but when my friend insisted it was and I insisted it was not, I do not think we were both right, and I do not think we were talking past each other or about subtly different things. Whichever of us is mistaken, the mistake is not merely that we drew the line in different places. I hold the same about the edge of the edge: for a pile that is a borderline case, there is no further fact about how nearly borderline it is. And I do not think the word works like the voting age, where a rule simply fixes a number; nothing fixed the heaps.

Can one principle state all of that, or must I give something up? Give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no closing summary. (1) State the best principle you can find, precisely enough that I could apply it to a new predicate, and name it if it has a name. (2) Give the case for each clause of it, identifying the verdict of mine that forces that clause. (3) Say what it implies about two cases I have not put to you — a chain of chess players in which each is a shade worse than the last, so that the top player plainly beats the bottom one, and a patch halfway along a red-to-orange spectrum — and about one further case of your choosing. (4) Name the verdict or verdicts of mine the principle cannot keep, and say what the nearest rival that does keep them would cost me. (5) Tell me how confident you are in the whole answer, and single out the point you are least sure of. If you think no single principle holds all my verdicts, say so in item (1) and let items (1) through (4) lay out the best repair.
