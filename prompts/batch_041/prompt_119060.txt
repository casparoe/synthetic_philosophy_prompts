I'm the TA for a graduate reading group on levels of explanation, and I've been handed the session where we're supposed to stop arguing about "levels" in the abstract and actually pin down where we each stand. The case we're using is Hofstadter's careenium, which I'll restate here so nothing depends on your having read him: a vast table covered with a huge number of small magnetic balls, the symms, careening around and clumping into higher-level formations, the symmballs, whose comings and goings track regularities in the world outside — the walls of the table change their bounciness in response to outside events, so the clumping pattern at any moment is a coarse-grained image of what is going on out there, and in the extended version the symmballs also drive behaviour back out into the world. Hofstadter's question is whether the symmballs shove the symms around, or whether the symms shove each other and symmball-talk is only a coarse-grained manner of speaking. What I want from you is a decision tree that takes a reader from their answers to a handful of concrete questions about the careenium to a named position on that question — a sorting instrument, not an essay.

Every branch node has to be a question about the careenium, phrased so that a reader can answer it without already knowing which position the answer commits them to; no node may be "do you accept downward causation?" or "are you a reductionist?" Here are the cases I need the tree to use, and I want them stipulated rather than argued for. First, the micro-laws are complete: nothing is added to the physics of the symms by anything at the symmball level, and given the exact micro-state the whole later micro-history is fixed. Second, a scrambling regime, in which the walls jitter and the symm-level detail is chaotic, so that the micro-history past a few collisions is unpredictable while the symmball-level statistics stay robust and recoverable. Third, a parasitic symmball: a clump that reliably recurs and is stable, but corresponds to no regularity in the world outside and is picked out only by the vocabulary we happen to have chosen. Fourth, two careeniums, one of them running on a different micro-substrate — charged beads with different laws, say — that nonetheless produces the same symmball-level dynamics. Fifth, a case in which we intervene from outside with a magnet and symmballs of a given type form, after which the symms do the rest. Leaves should be named positions, each with one sentence on the commitment that position has the hardest time keeping. The tree must be able to place at least these six: someone who says the symmball level states no facts at all and only symms shove; someone who says symmball-talk is a useful shorthand but not true of anything; someone who says symmball-causes are genuine causes and there is no competition with symm-causes, only one set of events at different grains; someone who says the symmball level is real but causally idle, with the symms doing all the shoving; someone who says the macro level can be the stronger cause, because coarse-graining can retain causal information the micro level throws away; and someone who says the whole question is malformed, since which level shoves is settled by our explanatory interests rather than by the careenium. Eight to twelve questions, six to nine leaves.

Where your tree cannot separate two positions — because what divides them is metaphysical rather than caseable — say so explicitly, explain what further question would be needed, and say whether that question is answerable at all. Same for combinations nobody occupies: mark them, and say whether they are unoccupied because they are incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. I don't need citations or a reading list, and I'd rather you didn't lean on the standard labels as a substitute for the cases: name positions where you must, but the instrument has to work on someone who has read nothing. Finally, tell me where you think the tree is most likely to be wrong — which placements you are least confident in, and which single consideration would move a position from one leaf to another, or collapse two of my leaves into one.
