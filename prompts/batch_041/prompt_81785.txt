Okay so I work at a small company doing neuron-replacement therapy — the pitch is that for people with ALS or early Alzheimers you swap their biological neurons out for silicon chips that do the same job, a few at a time, over about eighteen months, and at the end you're still you, just running on different hardware. We're past the rodent work and our first human protocol goes to the review board in six weeks, and I'm the one who has to write the "is it still the same person" assessment for each candidate protocol. The board signs off on the procedure itself, not just the individual cases, so whatever I write has to be reusable across protocols we haven't designed yet.

Our ethics consultant gave us a rule to work from and I understand it in the abstract but I genuinely cannot figure out how to apply it. Here it is roughly as she put it: a replacement step is acceptable if it's a modification of one ongoing subject of experience rather than a death followed by a birth — meaning there's no moment where the lights go out and a new light comes on. Great, but "modification vs death-and-birth" isn't something you can read off a microscope slide, and I need something my review team — two engineers and a lawyer, no philosophers — can actually run.

So what I want from you is a procedure. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each before we move on, which ones are answerable from the engineering specs (swap rate, how much overlap there is between old and new cells, whether the chips read out the biological cells before those cells are shut down, anesthesia, etc.) and which ones are just values wearing a lab coat. Then run it on the three protocols we're actually looking at:

(a) 1% of neurons a day, one-for-one, patient awake and talking to us the whole time;
(b) everything at once, in one long surgery under general anesthesia, so there's never a moment where the patient is a half-and-half mixture;
(c) the cheap version, where we grow the silicon network alongside the biological one, train it on the biological one's outputs until it can take over, then switch the biological half off.

Two more things. One: the lawyer needs a threshold she can put in a contract — something like "identity is preserved up to X% replacement" — and our consultant keeps saying there is no threshold, it's not that kind of fact. If she's right, the procedure has to show how to get from "no threshold" to something a contract can actually use, without just lying. Two: some patients have told us they don't care whether it's them at the end, they just want the body to keep going, and others have said stop the moment it isn't them. Our procedure has to say when the original signed consent stops binding and who gets to decide after that.

I'm not expecting the procedure to be neutral or painless. I want it to spit out at least one answer I won't like — tell me which of (a)/(b)/(c) that is and why — and I want you to be straight with me about what the procedure *can't* do and what kind of mistake it's going to make over and over. If some step is just a judgment call dressed up as a checklist item, say so instead of pretending.
