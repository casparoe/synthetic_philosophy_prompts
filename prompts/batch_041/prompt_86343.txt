ok so I'm doing a big essay for my humanities class and I want to use The Grapes of Wrath (we just finished it) as a test case for the Marxist idea that the economy — the base — explains everything else, the laws and the religion and the art and the ideas and all of it, the superstructure. My older brother is a philosophy grad student and he complicated the idea for me in two ways that I think are actually the interesting part, but I can't figure out how to run them through the book. Heres what he gave me.

First, "the base determines the superstructure" can mean two really different things. The strong version: if you know how a society produces stuff, you can basically predict its politics, its religion, its art — the culture is an output of the economy. The weak version: the economy only sets the limits and pushes — it makes some cultures likely and rules others out, but you can't read the culture off it. He says people slide between the two whenever they're losing an argument.

Second, he says there's a trap marxists fall into: explaining a thing by how useful it is to whoever's on top. Schools look like they exist to make obedient workers, the family looks like it exists to reproduce labor, etc. He says explaining something by its consequences is only legit if you can name the mechanism doing the sorting — somebody with power deliberately choosing it, or competition wiping out the alternatives, or nothing at all and we're just telling the story backwards. He said there was a whole fight about this between two philosophers, some analytical marxism thing, and I don't need the names, I just want the point used properly.

So what I want is a reading of the novel with those two ideas doing real work, and honest both directions — don't turn the book into a poster for the theory, and don't water down the theory to make it fit. The stuff I keep getting stuck on:

- He has those chapters where he cuts away from the Joads and writes about the banks, the tractors, the used car lots, the camps. I want to say those are the base chapters and the Joads' chapters are the superstructure, but that feels too neat. Is it? What is the zooming out actually doing?
- The way the farmers talk about the bank like it's some huge thing they made and then can't control. If nobody decided anything, whose interests is it serving, and can a base "explain" anything when no one is steering?
- Jim Casy. Everything that changes him is stuff that happens to him — jail, the camps, watching how people treat each other. Is that the economy explaining his ideas, or is that just experience, which isn't economics at all?
- Tom at the end saying he'll be there wherever people are fighting. The book seems to think the same dispossession that makes him say that is also what makes the guy driving the tractor do it anyway knowing it's wrong, and what makes the migrants suspicious of each other and of organizers. If the base spits out both solidarity and the tractor driver, has it explained anything yet, or just described the range of things people do?
- The last scene with Rose of Sharon. Thats not economic, it's almost a religious image, and calling it "superstructure" feels like cheating.

So: where does the book actually back the strong version, where does it need the weak one, and where does it need a mechanism the theory doesn't have? And at the end — does the novel know something the argument by itself doesn't, or does it just dodge the hard question? I'd honestly rather have the messy parts than a clean answer, my teacher hates clean answers.
