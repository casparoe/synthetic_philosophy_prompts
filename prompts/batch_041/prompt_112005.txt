I write about science and ideas for a general readership, and I am currently drafting a piece on Pascal's mugging. A colleague of mine, a careful reader of philosophy but no specialist in decision theory, offered me the following summary, which I have set down as he gave it:

'Pascal's mugging is Pascal's wager with the theology stripped out. A stranger offers you an astronomically large reward if you hand over your wallet, and threatens an astronomically large punishment if you refuse; since you cannot entirely rule out that he is telling the truth, the expected value of complying is infinite, and so complying is rationally required. What is disturbing is that the same reasoning licenses any absurd demand whatsoever. The standard reply is that one should not believe a stranger who makes an obviously absurd threat, and that disposes of the puzzle. In any case, the whole difficulty only arises if you allow utilities to be literally infinite, which no working decision theorist does, since utilities are defined only up to arbitrary rescaling. It is a curiosity about the arithmetic of infinities, not a serious problem for expected utility theory.'

Something in this strikes me as too quick, but I cannot put my finger on where the trouble lies. Please go through it claim by claim and tell me what someone who works on this puzzle would accept as fair, what they would wince at as a conflation or an overstatement, and what is simply wrong. I am particularly interested in whether the two defusing moves he offers -- don't believe the mugger, and keep the utilities finite -- do the work he says they do, and whether his closing verdict survives once the details are straightened out. If the summary undersells the problem, I would like to know what the strongest statement of it actually is, since otherwise the piece I am writing will be wrong in the other direction. My own hunch, for whatever it is worth, is that the "just don't believe him" line is carrying more weight than it can bear.
