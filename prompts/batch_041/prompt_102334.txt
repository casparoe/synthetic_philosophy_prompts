I run a fortnightly study group on game theory for working software engineers. Few of us have formal training in the subject beyond a single undergraduate course, so the group doubles as a refresher and as an excuse to work through the parts that never properly stuck the first time. We are putting together a self-marked problem set on iterated elimination of dominated strategies, and I would like you to write one exercise for it, together with a model answer and a marking scheme, so that members can grade themselves honestly with no instructor in the room.

Context and constraints:

- Treat the intended solver as someone comfortable with basic vocabulary — strategies, payoff matrices, mixed strategies, best responses, Nash equilibrium — but prone to falling back on half-remembered textbook examples. The exercise should therefore not be answerable by reciting a familiar illustration; it must force the solver to work with the specific payoff tables given below.
- Pitch the exercise at the level of a mid-course problem set, with one part that reaches a little beyond that level.
- Do not ask for or include any reading list, bibliography, or citations. Everything required to answer must be contained in the question itself.
- Keep the model answer to roughly 700–900 words, written the way a strong student would write it, with the algebra shown wherever it does the work.
- The marking scheme should total 20 marks; for each part it should name the specific moves a top answer must contain, state what each is worth, and add one line on how weaker answers typically go wrong.

I have drafted the two payoff tables below. Every cell is a pair (Row player's payoff, Column player's payoff).

Game A. Row strategies T, M, B; Column strategies L, C, R.

        L        C        R
T     (3, 0)   (0, 3)   (3, 0)
M     (0, 3)   (3, 0)   (0, 0)
B     (1, 1)   (1, 1)   (1, 3)

Game B. Row strategies X, Y; Column strategies L, C, R.

        L        C        R
X     (1, 0)   (1, 1)   (1, 0)
Y     (0, 0)   (1, 1)   (1, 1)

The exercise should consist of four numbered parts:

1. Using Game A, identify every strategy that is strictly dominated by a pure strategy, then identify every strategy that is strictly dominated once mixed strategies are permitted, exhibiting the dominating mixture explicitly in each case. Carry iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies through to its conclusion and state the surviving set of strategy profiles. (7 marks)

2. Explain, at a level a first-course student can follow, why the order in which strictly dominated strategies are removed cannot affect the surviving set, and why the same guarantee fails once "strictly" is replaced by "weakly." (4 marks)

3. Using Game B, exhibit two different orders of iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies that terminate in different surviving sets, and explain which feature of the game is responsible for the discrepancy. (5 marks)

4. Briefly: what is the relationship between a strategy's being strictly dominated and its never being a best response to any belief about the other players? Why is the clean equivalence available in two-player games lost once there are three or more players? Keep this part self-contained; the answer should explain the idea in its own terms rather than gesture at a named theorem. (4 marks)

Please write the four parts as the exercise, then the model answer, then the marking scheme. The model answer should read as a worked solution rather than a sketch, and the marking scheme should make clear where a merely correct-looking answer would still fall short of full marks.
