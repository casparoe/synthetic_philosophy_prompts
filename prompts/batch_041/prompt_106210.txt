For the past year I've been running a monthly thinking evening in the village hall for adults with no academic background — about fourteen regulars, ninety minutes, a wide spread of ages, and a couple of people who visibly flinch when numbers come up. Up to now I've given a short talk each time; for the next one I want to scrap the talk and run an activity instead, and I'd like you to design it.

The thing I want them to feel in their hands, rather than hear me explain, is this. A set of figures can say one thing about each of two groups separately and the opposite thing about the two groups lumped together. I want them to hit that reversal themselves, ideally while trying to win something, and to notice that no amount of staring at the arithmetic tells you which way you're supposed to read it. The part I care about most is the sting in the tail: once people have seen one reversal they tend to conclude they've found a general trick for spotting misleading averages, and I want the activity itself to pull that rug — the same figures should be capable of flipping back the other way once a third thing they weren't looking at is brought in, so that the choice of how to slice the data turns out to be a judgement about what caused what, not a fact sitting in the numbers. I'd like them to leave feeling that the arithmetic is the easy part and the judgement is the hard part.

So: design the evening. I'd like it to include the following.

- The setting, the group and the time are as above; assume no statistical training and one or two people who will freeze if it looks like a maths class.
- Rules and roles. If it works better as teams, a market, a betting game or a set of competing advisers, choose whatever lets the reversal arise from their own choices rather than from numbers I hand out already arranged. The reversal should happen to them before anyone names it.
- A scoring or payoff scheme that doesn't need real money — points, tokens, a leaderboard, a tin of biscuits as the prize, whatever fits.
- Materials I can print or assemble at home, with a rough sense of what goes on each card or sheet, and enough structure that I'm not improvising in front of fourteen people.
- A timeline with rough timings for the ninety minutes, including where the second twist lands so it doesn't feel bolted on.
- The debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto the idea, especially the move from "which average is right?" to "which grouping reflects the causal story, and who gets to decide that?". They shouldn't hear a name for any of this until the very end.
- An honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how I should handle each of those failures in the room.
- And, just as honestly, which part of the idea the activity simply cannot reproduce — the piece I'll have to supply myself in words, because no arrangement of cards will make them feel it.

Write it as instructions I can follow cold, on a Tuesday, with a printer and an hour to set up.
