I do regulatory biostatistics — clinical trials, tox studies, that kind of thing — and I keep getting asked to apply the precautionary principle by people who clearly mean something by it and clearly can't say what. So here's the ask. Take the philosophical debate about the precautionary principle — the version thats written into environmental law and the version the philosophers argue about, where you're supposed to act on a threat of serious or irreversible harm without waiting for full scientific certainty — and restate it, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use: null and alternative hypotheses, type I and type II error rates, power, pre-specified stopping rules and alpha spending, equivalence versus non-inferiority designs, priors and posteriors, loss functions, expected value of information, and the distinction I care most about, sampling uncertainty versus structural or model uncertainty.

Then the part I actually want: the residue report. What survives the translation cleanly, what only translates if you distort it, and what has no counterpart at all. Specifically: is the serious-or-irreversible-harm trigger anything more than a loss ratio? Does scientific uncertainty split cleanly into sampling error plus model ambiguity, or does the principle need a kind of uncertainty error statistics doesn't have? Does the slogan about absence of evidence not being evidence of absence just collapse into a power calculation? Is the asymmetry in who carries the burden of proof expressible as a choice of which error is worse, or does it demand a refusal to trade off at all? And is reversibility just an absorbing state, a one-sided stopping barrier? Flag anywhere the translation is really just me assuming my framework is the correct one and calling that a translation.

At the end, say which piece of residue you think is a genuine gap in my framework versus the philosophers being vague, and then tell me what would change your mind about that verdict — which argument or consideration would flip it — and where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong.
