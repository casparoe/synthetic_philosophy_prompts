I sit on the risk panel of a small gaming operator; we vet new promotional games before launch, we meet monthly, individual decisions are worth up to a few hundred thousand pounds, and the panel includes a lawyer and a marketing director who will not follow a derivation, so whatever we adopt has to be something a junior analyst can execute and I can defend to the regulator. Our written policy has always been simply that we approve a game if our expected position is positive, and that has worked until now. Two proposals have broken it. In the first, a fair coin is tossed until the first head; if that occurs on toss n, our payout is a gain or loss of 2^n/n with alternating sign, so the probability-weighted terms we add are 1, −1/2, +1/3, −1/4 and so on. The second is the same game with the sign always positive and payout 2^n/n. In the first case the total depends on the order in which you add the terms, and by reordering them you can make it come out to any value you like; in the second the sum grows without bound.

Please turn expected-value reasoning into a procedure the panel can actually run on any proposal: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; where the procedure stops with a no; and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the issue is one of values rather than maths. I do not want a fudge that quietly changes the subject — if the honest verdict is that expected-value reasoning gives no answer here, the procedure should say so and then say what is to take its place. Then run it on both proposals, and on a third case of your choosing that it handles cleanly. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer the panel will not like, and state plainly what the procedure cannot do.
