I am a volunteer on the cultural affairs commission in the town where I live, and in three weeks the eight of us have to vote on how to spend a one-time bequest of about four hundred thousand dollars. Two proposals are in front of us. One would convert the disused armory on Water Street into a 250-seat theater with a resident company and a six-play season. The other would put the same money into a summer festival series in the park, together with small grants to local makers, on the plan that a motel tax takes over the cost after the first year. I run a hardware store and have no academic training, but for some months now I have been reading Rousseau on my own, and I cannot shake his long attack, written in 1758, on the proposal to open a theater in Geneva. As I understand him, his case is not that plays are indecent or actors dishonest. It is that a theater has to please in order to survive, so it ends by flattering its audience instead of correcting it; that even the wittiest French comedy, the one about the honest man, has the audience laughing on the side of the smooth talker; that the craft of the actor is precisely the talent for seeming to be what one is not, and that we should think twice before admiring it; and that the remedy is not more refined entertainment but public festivals where there is nothing to watch because everyone is taking part.

What I cannot settle by myself is how much of this survives the trip to a town like ours. He was defending a small city of tradesmen where everyone knew everyone; our theater would sell most of its tickets to commuters and weekend visitors, so the character of the town may not be what is at stake at all. I am also uneasy about the thought that a town ought to hold an official view of how its residents' characters should be formed, since that seems like exactly what we are meant to keep out of. And there is an awkward local fact: the summer festival that Rousseau's alternative would amount to reviving is not innocent here. A local historian has documented that in the 1920s the festival ran as an old settlers' pageant that celebrated certain families and left newer arrivals feeling unwelcome, and some people in town still remember it.

Please write the memo I will circulate to the other seven commissioners before the vote. Open it with a clear recommendation and a concrete course of action, whether that is funding one proposal, splitting the money, or funding one with conditions, and then give the reasoning in a form the others can actually argue with. Treat Rousseau's objection as a live argument rather than an antique curiosity, and say precisely where his case turns on claims about how people's habits are shaped that could in principle be checked, and what we would need to learn in order to check them. Give the strongest version of the case for whichever side I end up recommending against, without making it a straw man. Say plainly what the commission cannot know before the vote and what that should mean for how we decide. Finally, tell me what fact or argument, if it turned up, would change my recommendation. Keep it to two pages, and leave out citations and reading lists; I will add whatever references the commission asks for.
