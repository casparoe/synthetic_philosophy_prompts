I have been half-convinced, and half-horrified, by the quantum immortality argument for some months now, and what I want from you is not a summary of it but a map of its exposure. As I understand it, the argument runs: take the Everettian reading of quantum mechanics seriously, so that every outcome of a measurement with nonzero amplitude is realized in some branch; then any process that might kill me has, in some branch, an outcome in which I survive; and since I can only ever find myself in a branch in which I am still around to find myself, I should expect to keep surviving, in some branch, indefinitely -- the version of me that dies is never the version I get to be. Quantum suicide is the same reasoning with the experimenter as subject, and I take it that the conclusion is explicitly not supposed to be testable by that subject. What I want to know is what would count against this, ranked by how much damage each item does. Which premises could fail, and what would the world have to look like for each failure: the Everettian framework itself, the assumption that branching is total rather than a matter of degree, the assumption that a person persists along a branch at all, and the self-locating step that converts branch-counting into odds for me?

I have collected objections and cannot tell how to weigh them. One is that the argument needs a particular way of assigning probabilities to branches -- branch counting, or something like it -- that does not survive contact with the Born rule, so that the conclusion is an artifact of a probability measure nobody should accept. Another is that the survivor is a duplicate rather than me, which would make immortality a claim about a successor and not about my survival. A third, which a friend of mine insists is the real reductio, is that if the reasoning works it cannot be confined to quantum events: there is presumably some tiny amplitude of the world's wavefunction in which the bus misses me, so the same argument should make me immortal against ordinary classical risks, and a view that implies I can never die of anything at all looks like a reductio of the reasoning rather than a discovery about my life. A fourth, which I find harder to place, is that in realistic cases the amplitude in which a recognizable successor of me persists may be spread across an enormous number of branches each of vanishingly small measure, so the conclusion is not that I will be around but that an ever-diminishing tail of the wavefunction contains something descended from me -- and I cannot tell whether that is a substantive objection or a restatement. I would find it most persuasive if you could identify a premise the argument needs that Everettians have independent reason of their own to reject, rather than one they can simply refuse to grant.

Finally, and this is the part that troubles me most: this is a position whose central claim I could never observe to be false, because the branch in which it fails is not one in which I am present to observe anything. Is that a fatal defect, or is it simply what any self-locating claim looks like from the inside, and does it mean the argument is really a conditional claim about how to reason under Everettian branching rather than a claim about my life at all? I would like an honest verdict on whether the view risks anything, and if it does not, whether that is disqualifying for a view of this kind or merely a sign that I have been asking the wrong question.
