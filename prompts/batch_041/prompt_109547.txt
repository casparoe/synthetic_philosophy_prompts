I am a third-year undergraduate in a political theory seminar on Arendt and the Eichmann trial, and I have a problem with a phrase. "The banality of evil" is doing at least four different jobs in the discussions around me, and I suspect some of those jobs are ones it cannot do.

The four uses I keep meeting:

(i) In seminar, a classmate deployed it to deflate any talk of perpetrators as monsters: since Eichmann was an ordinary, unremarkable man, the capacity for atrocity must be evenly distributed, so we are all Eichmann. She used it to close a discussion about the complicity of ordinary Germans rather than to open one.

(ii) A friend in a history course uses it as a thesis about the deed rather than the man: modern atrocity is assembled out of small, individually unremarkable administrative steps, so whatever is banal belongs to the process, not the person, and the horror lives only in the aggregate.

(iii) Our TA uses it as a psychological diagnosis of Eichmann himself: a dull careerist who never grasped what he was doing.

(iv) In a discussion about moral education, someone used it to argue that the remedy for atrocity is thinking, because the root of evil is thoughtlessness. A classmate objected that Eichmann was in fact a convinced and boastful antisemite, pointing to the interviews he gave in Argentina before his capture, which Stangneth's book is built on. If the phrase fails as a description of the man, does anything in it survive?

Our lecturer also remarked in passing that Arendt had used the idea of radical evil in her earlier work on totalitarianism, and I cannot tell whether the slogan replaces that idea, renames it, or quietly denies it.

What I want is not a defence of the phrase, a summary of the trial, or a reading list. I want a disambiguation: the distinct precise theses the phrase could be asserting, laid out as a menu, so that I can see which one each of my interlocutors needed and whether they got it. Please organize each entry so that it says explicitly what the predicate attaches to (the person, the deed, the motive, the explanation of the deed, our moral vocabulary, or our perception of the evildoer), and whether the claim is about the world or about how the world appears to a spectator. Keep the readings apart where they are logically independent: "the deed is banal," "the doer is unremarkable," and "we are all capable of this" are three claims, not one, and I want to see where each stands on its own. Where a reading turns on a diagnosis of thoughtlessness, say whether that is meant as a cognitive deficit or a moral one, and whether the two can be pulled apart. I do not need citations or suggestions for further reading; I need the conceptual work.

Please write the response in these sections, in this order:
