Analyse the case of Oskar Schindler — the Sudeten German industrialist who ran a factory in occupied Kraków, employed some twelve hundred Jewish workers and thereby saved them from the camps, yet remained a profiteer, a Nazi Party member and a womaniser — through the lens of Susan Wolf's moral-saints argument, the claim that a life wholly given over to morality is not the life we should most want for ourselves or for those we love. I want the analysis to run in both directions. First, map the case onto the argument: which features of Schindler's conduct and character make him a candidate for moral sainthood and which disqualify him, and is he better understood as a moral hero than as a moral saint? Second, let the case test the argument: does Schindler confirm it, qualify it, or expose a gap in it? In particular, distinguish at least three things 'maximal moral goodness' might mean — how much good one does, how completely one is devoted to morality, and how morally clean one's own conduct is — and say which of these, if any, Wolf's argument is really directed against. Close by stating plainly what a moral-saints framing cannot settle about a case such as Schindler's, rather than obliging it to pronounce on everything.
