I have no academic background in philosophy. I read Brave New World in school and mostly forgot it, and then a few years ago I came across a set of ideas usually called the hedonistic imperative, and the two have been knocking against each other in my head ever since. I would like you to act as a philosopher who works on applied ethics and also reads novels closely, and to give me a serious reading of the novel through that lens. Please think this through before you answer, and begin with a short brainstorm: sketch three or four different readings you could give, say briefly what each would cost you, and then commit to the one you think is strongest rather than averaging them together.

Let me set out the position in my own words, because I do not want you assuming I mean something I have not said. The hedonistic imperative, as I understand it from David Pearce's manifesto of that name, written in the 1990s, says that suffering is not a permanent feature of life but a biological phenomenon: pain, fear, anxiety, grief and depression are produced by particular evolved machinery whose job was to motivate organisms to survive and reproduce. If it is machinery, it can be redesigned. With genetic engineering, nanotechnology and a much better understanding of the brain, Pearce thinks our descendants could remove the capacity for aversive experience altogether and put something else in its place — not a flat, dopey contentment, but a wide spectrum of positive states, what he calls gradients of well-being, in which a creature is still driven and ambitious, only never driven by pain. He is explicit that the project covers every sentient creature, not just us: the wild animals whose lives are mostly hunger, fear, cold, parasites and being eaten alive, and the global ecosystem itself, which would eventually be redesigned around that. And the familiar objections — that suffering deepens us, that it is the price of meaning, that a life without it would be worthless — he treats not as arguments but as a bias in favour of the world as it happens to be. He also insists there is no trade-off between happiness and achievement: a highly motivated, productive mind need not be a suffering one.

Now, Brave New World. What I want from you is a reading of the novel in the light of that position: where the book anticipates it, where it dramatizes objections to it, where it refutes it without meaning to, and where it simply misses it. My own hunch, which I would like you to test rather than adopt, is that the World State is not an attempt at the hedonistic imperative at all but a stability machine that uses pleasure as an instrument — soma, the feelies, compulsory promiscuity, conditioning to love one's allotted work — and whose real goal is that nothing should ever change. If that is right, then the novel is usually read against the wrong target. But soma is a hedonic technology, so please say precisely where the line falls between a society that manages feeling and a society that abolishes suffering.

Please address at least the following.

1. The scenes I should be looking at. I remember the hospital ward where Linda dies, with children being conditioned not to fear death; the Savage's insistence near the end that he wants the right to be unhappy, which the Controller seems to treat as a taste he can afford to grant; the castes conditioned to be content with lives of hard labour; and everything on the Savage Reservation, where people still age, fall ill, pray and grieve, and the World State does nothing about it. If I have misremembered any of this, say so. Tell me which scenes carry the weight and which are decoration.

2. Where the novel genuinely presses on the hedonistic imperative in a way that is not mere nostalgia for hardship — for instance, whether removing the aversive signal removes the very thing that lets a creature notice that its situation is bad and care about getting it right, and what that would mean for the Savage's Shakespeare, or for anyone's capacity to govern themselves.

3. Where Huxley stacks the deck. In the book, hedonic engineering is inseparable from caste, eugenics and a totalitarian state, and I suspect the reader is being invited to blame the pleasure for what is really the coercion.

4. The animals. The most radical part of the hedonistic imperative concerns wild animals, and the novel says nothing about them, even though its civilised world sits inside unimproved nature. What would the book have had to imagine for that part of the position to be on the table?

5. The ending. Does the Savage win the argument or only the scene, and what would a careful defender of the position say is the mistake in his case?

Finally, I want a verdict rather than a summary: what does the novel contribute to this debate that argument alone cannot? Is the reader's revulsion at the World State a moral datum, or a status quo bias in narrative form, and does the book have an argument against the abolition of suffering that survives once the totalitarianism is stripped away?

Two constraints. Do not quote from the novel or from Pearce — I am not asking for quotations and I have no way to check them — and do not give me a reading list, citations or page references; I would rather have your reasoning than a bibliography. Where you are unsure of a plot detail, flag it rather than inventing it. And keep the reading faithful in both directions: do not flatten the novel into an illustration of a thesis, and do not soften the thesis to make it fit the novel.
