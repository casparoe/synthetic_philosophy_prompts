Construct a thought experiment for an upper-level seminar on revealed preference and the interpretation of utility. The target is a decision-maker whose choices are fully consistent with utility maximization within any single fixed presentation of the options, but for whom no single preference ordering rationalizes the choices once two presentations that differ only in their labels are pooled. The intended result is a split between the operationalist reading of utility (utility as whatever ordering a consistent choice function encodes) and the mentalist reading (utility as a psychological quantity the agent is maximizing), with the operationalist — not the mentalist — forced to deny that the agent has any preferences at all. This is the reverse of the usual direction: consistency is normally the paradigm case of having a preference, and here it is precisely where the preference evaporates.

Constraints: ordinary life (a canteen, a shift rota, a shop, a scheduling habit), not science fiction; no lotteries, no risky prospects, no neuroscience, no reports of the agent's introspective states; the scenario itself no longer than one paragraph. The agent must genuinely trade options off within each presentation — she must not simply pick one fixed favorite every time — so that the rationalizing ordering is substantive rather than vacuous. Do not describe her as irrational, inconsistent, or mistaken: the presentation-sensitivity must fall out of the structure of the case, not be stipulated in the setup.

Then deliver the following. (i) The option set and the choice function over a small finite domain, given in two presentations P and P′ related by a relabeling. (ii) The check that the consistency condition you invoke — state it explicitly rather than assuming its form — holds within each presentation, and that each presentation admits a utility representation; say how many such representations there are and what that implies for the meaning of the utility numbers. (iii) The demonstration that the pooled data admit no ordering, or only a gerrymandered one. (iv) The verdict each reading gives and why. (v) Which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens if it is removed. (vi) The strongest counter available to a defender of revealed preference — in particular, the move that the relabeled options simply are different options — and whether that move saves the operationalist at an acceptable cost.

Finish with an honest note: the force of the case depends on the relabeling being genuinely irrelevant to anything the agent could care about, and that claim is not itself recoverable from choice data. Say where it is independently plausible, where it is contestable, and whether the case can be run at all without the constructor supplying that premise.
