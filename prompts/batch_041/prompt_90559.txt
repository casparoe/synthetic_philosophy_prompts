I'm in a small reading group on algorithmic fairness — mostly ML people, one philosopher who keeps us honest — and we keep bouncing off the same wall. Everyone in the room can recite that you can't have a score stay calibrated within groups and also have equalized error rates across groups once the base rates differ, and yet nobody can actually *feel* why, and the second someone asks "ok so what should the city actually do?" the conversation dies. So I volunteered to write a short story for the group that dramatizes the bind instead of stating it, and I'd like you to write it. Here's the seed; use it or reshape it, I'm not attached.

Setting: a mid-sized city health department, five minutes into the future, has a model that scores residents on how likely they are to have undiagnosed hypertension, and the top scorers get invited into a free screening-and-treatment program — there's a fixed number of slots, that's the whole point of the thing. The model is genuinely well calibrated inside each of the two big demographic groups the department tracks: hand someone a 0.3 and about 30% of people like them really do have it, in both groups. The wrinkle is that the underlying prevalence is different in the two groups — one group really does carry more undiagnosed hypertension, for reasons the story should let us glimpse without lecturing about them. A community org starts complaining that the program is unfair. I need the story to get us into the room where the data person shows, with actual numbers, that you cannot at the same time make the fraction of sick people who get flagged equal across groups and the fraction of healthy people who get flagged equal across groups while the score stays calibrated — and then someone has to work out which group ends up absorbing the extra false positives and why, and then someone else has to ask the question that actually matters, which is whether the fix lives in the model at all or whether the prevalence was the thing that should have been the target all along. No tidy ending where they swap in a fairer model; I want it to land on the discomfort that the "impossibility" was a fact about the world they were handed, not a fact about the code.

Maybe 1200–1800 words, third person, close on the data person if you can swing it. Please get the arithmetic right — if a reader redoes the numbers on a napkin they should check out — and definately don't let anyone dissolve the problem with a clever regularizer or a new loss function, thats cheating. Also the philosopher can mutter something about the labels only existing for people who already got screened, and you can decide whether to pick that thread up or leave it hanging. One more thing: no footnotes and no explaining the theorem in the narration, it has to come out of the characters mouths or not at all.
