Keynes's theory of probability is commonly summarized in two claims: that a probability is a logical relation of partial implication between a hypothesis and the evidence for it, so that the degree of probability is a degree of rational rather than actual belief, and that such relations are not always numerically measurable — some probabilities can be ranked but not measured, and some pairs cannot be ranked at all. Less frequently discussed is the companion notion he develops in the Treatise on Probability under the heading of the weight of an argument. Its point is that the balance of favorable against unfavorable evidence is not the only thing that matters about an evidential situation; one must also consider the absolute amount of relevant knowledge and relevant ignorance in play. On his account, adding new evidence always increases the weight of an argument, even in cases where it lowers the argument's probability.

Contemporary decision theory pursues a related worry with different machinery. Rather than a single probability function, an agent's state of opinion is represented by a set of probability functions — the set consistent with her information, or with the comparisons she is willing to endorse — and the extent of that set is taken to measure how much confidence she has, or how little her evidence settles. Decision rules such as maximin expected utility, E-admissibility, and maximality are then stated over the set rather than over a single distribution, and the framework is often motivated by aversion to gambles whose odds are unknown.

Compare Keynes's weight of argument with this credal-set treatment of confidence. Address at least the following:

1. Is weight a property of the evidential relation, as the logical theory of probability would suggest, or a property of the agent's epistemic state? Can the credal-set apparatus preserve that distinction, or does it collapse it?
2. Does weight correspond to any structural feature of a set of probability functions — its spread, its cardinality, the dimension of its convex hull — or is it a quantity of a different kind altogether?
3. How does each account handle the possibility that an argument's probability falls while its weight rises? Does one of the two make better sense of why that is not a contradiction?
4. Does either notion explain why a low-weight judgment is unsafe to act on? Does the explanation survive when the decision is one-off and irreversible rather than one that will be repeated many times?

Conclude with a verdict: is the credal-set framework best understood as a reconstruction of Keynes's notion, as a replacement for it, or as an answer to a different question?
