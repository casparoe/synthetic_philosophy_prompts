I run a decision-theory reading group for graduate students in philosophy and economics; about 18 attend, and I have a 90-minute session. I want to stage the Platonia dilemma and then the Luring Lottery as one continuous activity, so that the group runs into the superrationality problem in their own choices instead of hearing it described. They should not hear the names of either game, or the word for the reasoning, until the debrief.

Design the activity: rules, materials, a payoff scheme that needs no real money (points or tokens are fine), a timeline, and the debrief. Stage one should be the Platonia setup -- a fixed set of anonymous participants, each free to submit a single claim or not, the prize going to a sole claimant and to nobody if two or more claim -- arranged so that the group cannot simply nominate one person in advance and so that each decides alone and in secret. Stage two should escalate: each participant may submit any number of entries, the pot is divided by the total number of entries submitted, and the pot goes to a randomly selected entry. I want them to feel the moment when the individually best answer becomes matching or outbidding what everyone else submits, and to see where that reasoning leads once they all follow it.

In the debrief, map what happened onto the difference between maximizing expected payoff given others' behaviour and maximizing on the assumption that everyone solves the same problem the same way; onto why the recommended play in stage one is itself a lottery rather than a firm decision; and onto the objection that this is magical thinking, along with the reply that no causal influence is being posited. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time -- especially the ways a room of people who half-remember the puzzle will defect anyway -- and how to handle it. Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce, in particular the genuine anonymity and uniformity of reasoning that make the symmetric answer attractive, and the fact that the original contest's readers were far more numerous and never in one room. I will supply the historical details myself, so don't build the debrief around them.
