Over the last year or so I've drifted into the moral particularist camp — not the weak "principles have exceptions" version, but the strong one: there are no true exceptionless moral generalizations, reasons are holistic (a feature that counts in favour of an act here can count against it there), and no further principle tells you which way it'll go in the next case. I picture moral principles as lossy compression of a huge table of case judgments, and I suspect the loss is in principle rather than just in practice — moral competence looks more like a perceptual skill than like knowing a rulebook. It's basically the ethics version of all the LW-adjacent stuff about expert judgment resisting codification, which is probably why it appeals to me.

So: what would count against it? Not the easy stuff like lying to the axe murderer — I want the harder defeaters. Real or constructed cases where a feature's valence flips in a way I can't argue away, empirical results about how moral judgment is actually learned and structured, and the boring possibility that this is just generalism with very long principles, i.e. that it collapses into its rival. What does it predict that a generalist wouldn't? Which parts of the package are load-bearing — if holism goes but "no humanly usable principles" stays, what's left? And which of these defeaters would a particularist themselves concede rather than dismiss, since I dont want only what critics say.

Also tell me straight whether the view risks anything at all, and if it's unfalsifiable whether that's a fatal problem or just what you'd expect from a claim about what reasons there are. The thing that would move me most is a case where I honestly can't see the flip.
