I've been invited to give a talk at the undergraduate philosophy society of the university in my town. I have no academic background — I read on my own and was suggested by a student friend — but I've spent a long time thinking about the argument usually called Pascal's mugging, and I want the evening to go well. I'd like you to prepare my question-and-answer sheet.

Here is what I plan to argue. Pascal's mugging is normally treated either as a problem about infinity or as a problem about probabilities so small that they should not count. I think both diagnoses miss the point. The real trouble is that an offer with an unbounded payoff lets the person making the offer set the exchange rate between probability and value: whoever can name a larger number gets to decide what I should do. So the right response is not to bound my utility, and not to declare the probability negligible, but to say that the offer is not a well-formed bet at all. My proposed test: a bet is admissible only if it survives an outbidding auction. If a second offerer can always top the first by naming a larger payoff at the same price, then neither offer has a determinate value, and the correct move is to refuse the scale rather than compute on it. I'll illustrate with an example of my own — two muggers who each promise to outdo the other for the same five pounds — plus a variant in which the mugger raises his offer every time I hesitate.

The room will be mixed. Mostly second- and third-year philosophy undergraduates, two graduate students who work on formal epistemology and decision theory, a handful of students active in effective altruism, and one or two who are religious and have their own stake in Pascal's wager. None of them will have read anything on this specifically, but the graduate students will know the standard machinery and will use its vocabulary.

Give me the eight or so objections I am actually likely to get, ordered by how much damage they would do to my case. Phrase each one the way that particular person would put it — the undergraduate reaching for a textbook move, the formal epistemologist pressing on the axioms, the effective altruist who thinks strange expected values are simply the price of doing business, the religious student who is unsure whether I am attacking or defending him. For each objection, give me a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is weak. Tell me what I should concede early and cheerfully, and what I should hold. Finally, tell me the question I am hoping nobody asks.
