I retired from experimental physics about three years ago (materials, mostly neutron scattering) and I've spent the time since reading philosophy — epistemology, mostly, and mostly badly. Here's something I've been trying to write up for my reading group and I keep going in circles, so I'd rather have a straight audit.

The question is whether leaning on AI systems wrecks your understanding. What I actually believe, as far as I can tell, is this:

1. I'm a reliabilist about knowlege. A belief is knowledge if it came out of a process that reliably gets things right; I don't need to be able to reconstruct the justification from the inside, and I dont think I have any privileged access to my own reasons anyway. That was basically my attitude in the lab — I trusted the diffractometer, I never audited the fitting software, and I was right to.

2. Understanding is a different animal. I've started calling the thing I mean 'downstream sensitivity': to understand a result is for there to be other things in your head that would have had to be different if the result had been different, and for you to feel that pull. Somebody who can only produce the right answer, with nothing downstream of it that would have had to move, doesn't understand it — even if they know it.

3. Understanding and knowledge come apart, and that's fine. Lots of knowledge with no understanding is a real and common state. So is a fair bit of understanding without being able to state the facts cleanly.

4. Leaning on a chatbot is epistemically worse than leaning on a textbook, a colleague, or an instrument. It does something to you that the others don't.

5. But opacity can't be the reason. I used instruments whose guts I genuinely did not understand, I built error bars out of calibrations inherited from people I never met, and I cited whole literatures in fields where I couldn't have checked a single derivation. If the complaint about the chatbot is 'you can't see inside it', that complaint covers most of what I did for thirty-five years. And if the complaint is that I don't know its error rate — fine, somebody will calibrate the thing eventually, and then my unease should dissolve. I don't believe it would.

6. I don't think there's a principled line between reasoning I did myself and reasoning I absorbed from other people and now run without remembering where it came from. Every attempt I've seen to draw that line either puts most of science on the wrong side of it, or turns out to be a line between 'old' and 'new' with a nice name stuck on it.

7. My real worry is not about me, or about any one knower. It's about whether a field — or a whole culture — quietly loses the ability to do something. That's a claim about a collective, over decades.

Which of these can't all be held at once? And note that some of them are obviously meant to be pulling against each other when I think they're actually compatible — I want those flagged too, because if I've been defending a distinction that isn't doing any work I'd rather find out now. Rank the real collisions by how bad they are, and for each one tell me the cheapest thing to give up and what giving it up costs me. Please don't just tell me it's all one big tension — I already know that, that's why I'm asking.
