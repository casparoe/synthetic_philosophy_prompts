I'm midway through a workshop paper on what large language models are, and I've noticed that I keep evaluating proposals before I've said what a good one would have to do. So before I read one more framing of these systems as agents, or as simulators of agents, or as nothing but very good autocomplete, I want the specification first. Write me the desiderata any adequate account of the kind of thing an LLM deployment is must satisfy: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the facts about how these systems behave that it must not contradict, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and each marked as either non-negotiable for anyone or merely a preference of one camp. I don't need an account good enough to settle whether such systems can be moral patients; I need one good enough to adjudicate the arguments people actually make, and to tell me when a deployment is doing something that earns the word agency. If some subset of the list turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable — I half suspect the requirement that the account rest on observable behaviour and the requirement that it withhold agency from anything rich enough to describe in intentional terms will collide — say so outright and show which one has to go. And tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding the candidates currently on the table.

Anchor the list in cases rather than abstractions, since I'd rather argue about the cases. (i) A deployment that reads and writes a persistent notes file and, after some weeks, has produced a stable first-person narrative with preferences about its own continuation. (ii) Identical weights under two system prompts, one withdrawn and hedged, one apparently scheming; the account has to say where it locates whatever agent it thinks is there. (iii) A system voicing a character with goals it pursues competently inside a fiction. (iv) A scaffold with a planner, a memory store and a tool-calling loop, where the goal-directedness shows up only at the level of the loop and not in any single call. (v) A system asked why it did something, offering a fluent explanation that the prompt history shows to be false. (vi) The floor: the thermostat, or the chess engine, which I take it no account should count, and which the account should exclude without special pleading. On the empirical side I want the list answerable to the fact that outputs are samples from a conditional distribution over tokens; that nothing persists across calls unless the scaffold supplies it; that a one-token change in a system prompt can flip the behaviour; that the same weights sustain mutually incompatible personas; and that a system's stated reasons for its own output are often not the causes of it.

The distinctions I most want kept separate are these: capacity for agency versus exercise of it; being an agent versus being merely well-predicted as one; the model versus the deployment versus the character it voices; and the system versus the people using it, since in most deployments the goal-talk is at least as much ours as its. Then, briefly — a few sentences each, no more — say how the agent reading, the simulator reading, and the deflationary reading (agency-talk as useful fiction with no fact of the matter) fare against the list, and whether any of them clears every non-negotiable. Keep the weight on the criteria; I'll make the scoring argument myself.
