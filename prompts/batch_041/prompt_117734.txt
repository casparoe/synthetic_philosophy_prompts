so my friend is a lawyer and she has this move she pulls whenever I complain about someone. In court, she says, you cant just walk in and sue a person — you need standing. You have to be the one who was actually harmed; if its not your fight the court wont hear you, no matter how good your case is. And she says moral criticism runs on the same rule: if your'e a hypocrite about the very thing you're complaining about, you've got no standing, so the person you're criticizing can wave you off without ever answering the substance. She pushes it further too — since basically everyone is a hypocrite about something, most moral criticism is out of order, and the whole practice is shakier than we pretend. She has the political version as well: the columnist who's been hammering the minister over his offshore accounts turns out to have a few of her own, case dismissed, nothing to discuss.

I half buy it and half think the legal analogy is carrying all the weight and nobody has checked whether it can. Can you audit it?

What I want:

1. The correspondence, item by item — plaintiff, defendant, the injury, the court, the judge, who gets to decide the standing question, the remedy, and whatever else maps — with each one marked as holding, holding partly, or breaking.
2. The disanalogies that actually matter. Some things I know from her: standing is a threshold question decided before anyone looks at whether the claim is true, so losing on standing says nothing about being right; the court can't just waive it by agreement; sometimes a third party can sue on someone else's behalf; and the doctrine exists mostly to keep courts in their lane as institutions rather than because the plaintiff is a bad person. Which of those transfers to moral blame and which don't?
3. The three conclusions — the one-on-one dismissal, the political case, and the sweeping skepticism — and whether each survives once the mapping is on the table. If the sweeping one collapses but something narrower survives, say which and why.
4. Whether there's a better analogy for what hypocrisy actually does to a criticism. My hunch is it's closer to a credibility or weight problem than a gate you can't get past, but you tell me, and say where the replacement still breaks.

Please skip reading lists and citations — I can't look anything up and I don't really care who said it first, I care whether the analogy holds. Also don't seperate the "does it map" question from the "does the conclusion follow" question; I want to see the second depend on the first.
