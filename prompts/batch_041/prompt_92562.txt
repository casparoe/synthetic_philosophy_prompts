I write about science and ideas for a living, which means that once or twice a year I get invited to do something I'm not really qualified for, and this year it's a 90-minute session for a graduate reading group in the philosophy department near me, about 14 students, and the topic they've asked for is causation by omission. What I don't want is a lecture, partly because they know the literature better than I do and partly because I think the whole thing is easier to feel than to explain. What I want is a runnable activity where the group manages to cause a harm by doing nothing, and then has to fight about who did it, so that by the end they've been pulled in two directions at once. First pull: of course the people who sat there caused it, look at what they didn't do, you can't tell me the silence didn't kill the fish. Second pull, and this is the one I care about most: the instant you accept that, the list of causes explodes — everyone who could of acted, everyone who was asleep, the person who set the schedule, the committee that cut the budget, the guy who talked his colleague out of coming in that night — and there's no principled place to stop. I want them to feel that the only reason the list doesn't explode in ordinary life is that we're quietly leaning on norms (duties, roles, who was supposed to be doing what), and that those norms are doing work we usually credit to causation itself.

A few things I'd like baked in, though design around them however you think best. Stage it as some kind of operations room with real roles and real asymmetry of information — I keep picturing a water utility at 2 a.m., but pick whatever gives you the cleanest incentive structure. The rules have to make the harm come genuinely from inaction: waiting has to be the cheapest move for every individual player, and it has to be individually reasonable, not obviously cowardly or stupid, or the whole thing collapses into a morality tale. I want a physical artifact that survives into the debrief — some kind of log or ledger that records every act of restraint, including the restraints of people who aren't in the room at all, so that when we spread it across the table the list is already embarrassingly long before anyone has proposed a criterion for trimming it. And I want a last act where the ledger turns up someone who didn't refrain but who stopped somebody else from acting, a preventer of a preventer, so they have to say whether that person caused the harm more or less than the people who just sat on their hands, and whether their criterion can handle both without being gerrymandered.

So: roles and rules, materials (nothing that costs money or needs a screen), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments from the game onto the actual problem — the but-for test, the way we treat doing and refraining differently even when the outcomes are identical, and the fact that the law won't hold you liable for failing to act unless you had a duty, which is a norm smuggled in and dressed up as a limit on causation. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it — my guess is they'll moralize when I want them to analyze, and that one person will announce we're all responsible and try to end the exercise early, but I'd like your predictions, not just mine. And be straight with me about which part of the idea the activity simply can't reproduce and that I'll have to say out loud myself, because I'd rather know where the seams are than discover them in front of 14 grad students.
