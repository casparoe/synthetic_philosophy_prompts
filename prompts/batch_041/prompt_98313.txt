I write about science and ideas for a general readership, and I am at work on an essay about the way we carve the past into named blocks — how much of what we call the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution is discovered in the archive and how much is imposed on it by the people doing the naming. For the centrepiece I want to stage an impossible interview. Jacob Burckhardt, the Basel historian whose 1860 Civilization of the Renaissance in Italy did more than any other single book to fix the Renaissance as a period — defining it by the state as a work of art, the emergence of the individual, and the recovery of antiquity — is to be brought forward to the present, given a full and fair account of what has happened to his category since his death, and asked for his verdict. I do not want a summary of what he actually wrote, and I do not want a portrait of him as a quaint nineteenth-century figure. I want your best inference about what he would say if he were seated across from me today, informed and unpersuaded.

Assume he has been told about, and has understood, four developments, none of which he lived to see. First, the continuity thesis: medievalists who locate renaissances of their own in the twelfth century and earlier, and who argue that the changes he described were gradual, regional, and largely a nineteenth-century construction. Second, the ascendancy of early modern as the operative label, dissolving his period into a longer stretch running from roughly the fifteenth to the eighteenth century, and the parallel redrawing of the Scientific Revolution as a slow, multi-centred, uneven process rather than a break. Third, quantitative cultural history: millions of digitized texts, inventories, wills, and price series that show change as drift in humdrum documents rather than as a turn, and the accompanying argument that period labels are instruments of the historian's convenience rather than discoveries about the past. Fourth, global and comparative history: the objection that Renaissance and Middle Ages alike are European categories that distort the periodization of Chinese, Islamic, and South Asian history.

To give the interview a spine, I want to put a test to him — mine, not his. A period label earns its place, I want to say, only if it can be anchored either in what contemporaries called themselves or in a discontinuity that shows up in the ordinary record — contracts, wills, inventories, prices — and not merely in the masterpieces. Ask him to say which parts of his Renaissance survive that test, and which do not.

Give me the following. A verdict on each of the four developments, with a clear statement of where he would dig in and where his own method would force him to concede; the single element of his argument that is most exposed to the quantitative and global objections, and the single element he would defend to the end; his response to my test — whether he would accept it, amend it, or reject it, and on what grounds, including whether the test is one his own book could have passed; and finally a short passage in his voice, plainly marked as your invention rather than a quotation from anything he wrote, in which he answers the charge that he invented the period he claimed to have found. Close with an honest note distinguishing what in your reconstruction is disciplined inference from his known commitments and what is guesswork about a man who cannot be consulted.
