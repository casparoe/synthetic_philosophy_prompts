I am a retired experimental physicist, forty years at the bench, and only in the last few years a reader of philosophy; I ask your patience with my vocabulary and your help with the argument. What I want is practice in defending the reality of time against an opponent better equipped than I am, and I would rather have the argument than the attribution: I do not need to be told who said what, and a reading list would be of no use to me.

Please play a convinced Gödelian. Your position is that the objective lapse of time is not a feature of the world but a contribution of ours, and that this is what the rotating solution of the field equations reveals — not a curiosity about one model but a discovery about the theory. You hold that for time to pass objectively, reality would have to be divided, independently of any observer, into past, present, and future: into a succession of nows, each of which comes to be and then ceases to be. General relativity makes simultaneity frame-relative, and so leaves nothing for any such division to rest on. And the rotating universe, whose swirling dust tilts the light cones until a traveller could in principle return to his own past, shows that even the existence of a global ordering of events depends on how the matter happens to be arranged — so that an objective becoming, if there were one, would be a contingent local accident rather than a structure of spacetime as such. Argue it from the physics; you know the theory well enough not to need the name of the man who found the solution.

Hold that position throughout. You may concede small points, and you may grant that the physics survives your conclusion untouched; you may ask me a factual question if you need one settled before you can answer; you may not give up the main thesis, step out of the role, or answer me with a survey of the literature. A paragraph per turn, please, and keep the role until I say we are finished.

Here is my opening. Your conclusion, if it holds, holds because our universe is supposed to lack the division you say time requires. But the expanding models that describe our universe do contain a cosmic time: the matter singles out a family of surfaces of simultaneity, every event takes its place in one global order, and no observer can disagree about that order. If a global order of that kind is enough to give you time, then we have it, and your conclusion is false of the only universe we can check. If it is not enough, then the argument from the rotating universe establishes only that some spacetimes lack becoming, which nobody ever doubted, and tells us nothing about ours. Which horn will you take? And one further thing, from a man who has spent his life among apparatus: the rotating solution looks rigged to me — the rate of rotation, the density of the dust, and the cosmological constant have to be tuned to one another, and the thing does not expand, whereas ours does. How much of your conclusion rests on a contrivance?
