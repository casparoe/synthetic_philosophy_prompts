I am preparing a session for a final-year undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of mathematics, and I want to build it around Edwin Abbott's Flatland (1884) as a way into Benacerraf's dilemma: the tension between a semantics that requires mathematical terms to refer to mind-independent abstract objects and an epistemology that requires some causal or constructive connection between the knower and what is known. The readings I keep getting — from students and from the commentary I have seen — are thin: the book is said to show how hard it is to imagine higher dimensions, and there the matter rests. I want a reading that takes the novella seriously as philosophy of mathematics and takes the dilemma seriously as philosophy, rather than using one to decorate the other.

Work through the episodes that bear on the dilemma and say what each shows. A Square's education proceeds by the Sphere's failure with words and success with deeds: the dimensional analogies leave him where he was, and what moves him is being lifted, having a tablet moved through the third dimension, having the Sphere pass through his interior. That looks like an unqualified endorsement of the acquaintance requirement. Then, once he is in Spaceland, he demands a fourth dimension on the strength of an analogy — point, line, square, cube, next — and the Sphere, who has had contact with a higher space and cannot imagine a higher one still, refuses him. Compare that with his own failure to teach the monarch of Lineland, and say what the parallel does to the analogy argument: is it genuine mathematical reasoning, or extrapolation the book quietly discredits? Consider also the Pointland vision, where a being who is his own entire universe cannot receive a message at all.

I would then like you to test against the book a possibility that I have not seen discussed and that I suspect is the novella's real proposal: an epistemology of testimony, or revelation. The Sphere's practice of bringing a new apostle at each millennium, the decrees and the imprisonment of witnesses that follow, and the memoir itself — written in prison seven years on, addressed by a two-dimensional author to readers he takes to be inhabitants of Space — together suggest that mathematical knowledge of abstracta is transmitted by the word of someone who has been there, and is hostage to political conditions. Is that a genuine third option, or does it merely relocate the causal contact onto a third party? Does it matter that in Flatland the transmission is blocked by decree rather than by metaphysics?

Finally, attack a hypothesis of mine. The most natural escape from the dilemma is to say that meaning is use: the word for the third dimension is meaningful to A Square because he can do things with it. But the book ends with a man who can use the word and cannot teach it, whose brother in the next cell cannot be convinced by it, and whose testimony is suppressed. If that is right, the novella is a counterexample to the pragmatist dissolution of Benacerraf's problem, and the constructive and intuitionist routes deserve the same treatment.

Where does the book resist all of this — where is it chiefly a satire of Victorian caste, regularity, colour and women, whose epistemology is incidental? End by saying what the novella contributes to the debate that the argument alone does not, and where an honest critic has to concede that the reading I am after is imposed rather than found.
