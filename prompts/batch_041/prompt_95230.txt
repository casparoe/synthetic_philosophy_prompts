I'm a high school student, and for the extended essay attached to my philosophy course I've been working through Elizabeth Anscombe's attack on the decision to award Harry Truman an honorary degree at Oxford — the episode in which she argued that the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki were murder, that Truman was therefore a murderer, and that a university's decision to honour him was a scandal rather than a lapse of taste. What interests me is that she seems to be making a claim of a peculiar kind: not that the bombings had bad consequences, or fewer good ones than people suppose, but that intentionally killing the innocent is absolutely prohibited, and that this prohibition does not bend no matter how much good might come of bending it. Her way of defending that, as I understand it, leans on a distinction between what an agent intends, whether as his end or as his means, and what he merely foresees as a side effect of what he does — which is why she can condemn the deliberate terror-bombing of cities while allowing, in principle, a strike on a military target whose collateral deaths are foreseen but not aimed at. At lunch two friends and I argued about this until we were told to leave: one of them insists Truman saved hundreds of thousands of lives, the other says that is exactly the sort of accounting Anscombe was refusing to do, and neither could tell me what evidence would ever make the other change his mind. I have more or less come round to her position, and that worries me, because I cannot tell whether I have come round to an argument or to a wall.

So I want you to treat the position as mine and map its exposure. What would count against it — which arguments, intuitions, historical facts, or formal results should actually move me, sorted by how decisive they would be, from the ones that would be fatal to the ones that are merely awkward? I would like you to distinguish two things I suspect are tangled together: whether the absolute prohibition on intending the death of the innocent is a substantive moral thesis, in which case consequences and cases can bear on it, or a conceptual thesis about the description under which an action is intentional, in which case almost nothing empirical could touch it — and if it is the latter, whether that is a virtue or a way of buying immunity. Then tell me honestly whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. Please include, among the defeaters, at least the following, evaluated rather than merely listed: the objection that a rule whose whole point is to protect the innocent ends up forbidding the acts that would protect the most innocents, which looks self-defeating; the case of a drone operator who destroys a house in which a wanted commander is meeting, knowing that the commander's family is inside, and who insists that he does not intend their deaths; the charge that the category of the innocent, rather than the prohibition, is doing all the work, so that the view may be far narrower or far more demanding than it first appears; and the thought that her verdict on Truman does not change at all if the bombings turn out, unknowably, to have been pointless — which either shows the view is immune to consequences by design or shows that it never was a view about what to do. I also want to know what the position predicts that its rivals do not: if it is true, where should we expect to find a difference in kind between two acts with identical body counts, and is that prediction testable in anything but hindsight? For each defeater, say which ones a committed follower of Anscombe would concede as genuinely counting against her and which ones she would dismiss out of hand, and why the dismissal is or is not legitimate. What I would personally find most persuasive is a demonstration that the very same criterion that condemns Truman also condemns something I am certain was permissible, or that the criterion cannot be stated without circularity — so if you think that is the shape of the real problem, say so, and if you think the position survives that, tell me what it costs to hold it.
