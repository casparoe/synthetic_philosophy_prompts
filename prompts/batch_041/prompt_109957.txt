Construct a thought experiment for a graduate seminar on whether justice in exchange can be reduced to voluntariness and mutual advantage. The materials to be used are Aristotle's account of exchange as the restoration of a proportion grounded in need rather than in the parties' bargaining positions; his claim that money is a conventional measure of that need, and so depends on the very thing it is meant to measure; and the distinction he draws in the Politics between acquisition bounded by the needs of a household and acquisition that has no natural limit because its end is money itself. His familiar complaint about lending at interest is the best-known expression of the second, so you may not use it.

The case must be engineered so that these materials condemn an exchange that any voluntarist, mutual-advantage account of justice must endorse, and the condemnation must be generated by Aristotle's own apparatus rather than stipulated. Constraints: a present-day setting and ordinary institutions, with no imaginary economies, no state of nature and no exotic instruments; no coercion, deception, misinformation, unequal bargaining power, third-party harm or externalities, and no party left worse off by the transaction, so that the divergence cannot be explained away by hidden harm or defective consent; the parties' motives must be innocent and stay innocent; the case must not turn on a loan at interest, on gambling, or on the sale of something whose sale is independently wrongful; and the party who profits must be a private individual or a household rather than a firm or a state.

Deliver the scenario in a single paragraph; then the verdict of the mutual-advantage account and the verdict of Aristotle's, with the reasoning for each; then what the just terms would be on Aristotle's own standard and whether that standard determines them, and if it does not, whether the indeterminacy is an artefact of your case or a feature of the account; then the single feature of the case that does the work, and what becomes of each verdict when that feature is removed and everything else held fixed; then, keeping apart the two strands of Aristotle's objection, that this kind of acquisition is unbounded and that it deforms the agent, which strand the case actually needs, and, if it needs only the second, why that is not a stipulation; and finally how a defender of the mutual-advantage account would attack the case, and how a defender of Aristotle would say it is rigged. If the two verdicts cannot be pulled apart under these constraints, say which constraint blocks it and what the nearest case that works would look like.
