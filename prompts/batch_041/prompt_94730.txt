I have become convinced that the correct general theory of rational choice is geometric rather than arithmetic: an agent ought to maximize the expected logarithm of its outcome, or equivalently the long-run growth rate of its resources, and should regard the ordinary expected-value rule as a confusion between the average across an ensemble of possible worlds and the average across time within a single world. On this view the Kelly criterion is not a piece of gambling advice but the local instance of a fully general principle, and the apparent counterexamples -- positive-expected-value bets that any sensible person declines -- are not counterexamples at all but illustrations.

What I want from you is an honest map of what would count against this. Be concrete about which decisions the view is committed to getting wrong, and say whether any of them are real choices rather than artifacts of the formalism. Three specific pressure points. First, the one-shot case: an agent faces a single irreversible choice, with no repetition, no copies, and resources that are consumed rather than compounded, so that there is no growth rate to maximize. Does the principle fall silent there, and if so, is a theory of rationality that says nothing about a whole class of decisions acceptable -- or does the ensemble/time distinction quietly reappear in a form that is doing no work? Second, the choice of log specifically: is there an independent argument for the logarithm over any other concave function, or is geometric rationality a redescription of ordinary risk aversion with a growth-rate story bolted on, such that it predicts nothing an expected-utility maximizer with the same concave utility could not also predict? Third, the cases where the arithmetic rule looks defensible: a bet that risks only a small, replenishable stake for a large payoff, or a decision whose outcome you will not live to experience, or one where the typical (median) result under geometric rationality is worse for almost everyone even as the mean under arithmetic maximization is higher.

Then rank the objections. Distinguish those a committed defender would themselves concede as genuine costs from those they would dismiss as misunderstandings of the view, and separate the fatal from the merely awkward. Tell me whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if it has, whether that is a vice here or the honest price of a theory that is really a family of heuristics rather than a single principle. Finally, state what the view predicts that its rivals do not, and whether there is any observation or argument that should move someone who already accepts the ensemble/time distinction and merely disagrees about what follows from it.
