For the past month I have been the unwilling archivist of my grandfather's papers — he ran a small printing business that failed in the 1980s — and the work has turned into an unplanned tutorial in the three ways Nietzsche says one can write history. My uncle retells the shop's story as proof that our family is capable of great things; my mother keeps every ledger and receipt and would sooner lose the house than the files; I would like to indict the whole inheritance and begin again from nothing. Write me a joke built on the actual content of monumental, antiquarian, and critical history, one I could tell at the family dinner. It need not name Nietzsche or use his vocabulary, but all three attitudes should be recognizable in it, and the humour should turn on what the essay says about each mode's danger when it is pursued alone: that the monumental makes the past look imitable, that the antiquarian embalms what it preserves, and that the critical condemns an inheritance it cannot step out of. A guest who has read the essay should laugh because the mechanics are exactly right.
