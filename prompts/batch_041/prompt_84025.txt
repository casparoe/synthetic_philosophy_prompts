I'm teaching a session on Plato's ethics and I want to build it around a real mess rather than a thought experiment: the night in 415 BC when somebody went round Athens and smashed up the Herms — those square stone pillars with the bearded head of Hermes and a phallus on them — just as the Sicilian expedition was about to sail. The facts, since you can't look them up: it happened in one night, across the whole city; the same season people were accused of parodying the Eleusinian Mysteries at private drinking parties; Alcibiades got named, was allowed to sail anyway, was recalled, and jumped ship to Sparta; the investigation ran on informers and rewards, including a denunciation naming hundreds of men that then came apart, and one accused man who turned informer and saved himself; Thucydides leans toward well-born young men in their cups, while the city talked itself into a plot against the democracy; and the expedition went on to Syracuse with its general under a cloud and was destroyed.

The lens is Plato's own view of drinking, from the Laws: a drinking party is a cheap and more or less harmless test of a man's character, and also practice in self-control, as long as its run properly by a sober man; wine takes off the shame that hides what you're really like, shame for him being basically fear of what people will say; and he'd ban the stuff entirely for the under-eighteens, allow it in moderation to men under thirty, and hand it to the over-forties as a medicine against the sourness of old age. The party in the Symposium — Alcibiades crashing in drunk, Socrates outdrinking everyone and staying sober — is the literary version of the same idea, usually dated the year before, so use it if it helps.

Now run the affair through that, and then back the other way. Who exactly is the man being tested, and who's the sober president, when the drinking happens in a street at night with no rules, no host, and nobody who can even say afterwards who did it? Tell me where the theory earns its keep — does the shame-and-audacity psychology actually predict the crime? — and where it goes blind: collective anonymous action, the city's own panic as the thing that really got tested, the cheap harmless test turning out to be neither, and a psychology of shame that needs an audience in a case defined by there not being one. Then say what the episode does to the theory: confirm it, break it, or show that the sober president was doing all the work and the wine none of it. And if you can, say what Plato's legislator would have done with the culprits and with the panicking city — and where he'd be silent.
