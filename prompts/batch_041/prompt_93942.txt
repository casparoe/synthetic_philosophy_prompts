Ok so I've been reading about universal basic income and I keep getting stuck on one design choice that nobody seems to argue about directly. Almost every serious UBI proposal pays the money to individuals, not households — you get your check whether your single or married, and a couple gets two checks. But basically every existing welfare program does the opposite: it looks at household income, treats the couple as one economic unit, and cuts you off if your partner earns enough. Same with work: UBI has no work requirement, but most cash assistance does, or has time limits. So we've got this weird situation where the same political coalition is fine with an unconditional, individualized transfer in one spot and a conditional, household-based one everywhere else.

And theres a second thing that bugs me. If the UBI is funded by progressive taxes, then the net transfer ends up looking alot like a negative income tax — the rich pay it back, the poor come out ahead. So what is "universal" actually adding? Is the universality doing real work, or is it just a frame?

What I want to know is what philosophical commitments this UBI design pattern actually presupposes. Does paying individuals rather than households commit you to the view that the person, not the family, is the proper unit of distributive justice — or is that just an administrative convenience you could swap out? Does dropping the work requirement presuppose a view about whether people deserve their income, or about what freedom really requires, like that freedom needs an unconditional floor rather than just formal non-interference? And the universality defense — some people say pay everyone including millionaires not because the rich need it but because means-testing is stigmatizing and universal programs survive politically. Does that only make sense if you think dignity or non-stigma is itself a distributive good, or can it be defended on purely self-interested grounds?

I guess I'm asking: reconstruct the implicit theory of the person, of desert, and of the value of universality that this design assumes. Tell me which parts it strictly needs versus which parts it just happens to fit with, and whether the parts are even consistent with each other — because individualizing the unit and dropping the work requirement seem to pull in different directions about whether the point is to respect people or to protect them. And what would actually have to change in the program if you swapped the underlying view for the household-based, reciprocity-flavored view the rest of the welfare state seems to run on?

Please answer in American English.
