I'm in a metaphysics-of-science reading group and we've just wrapped the block on laws of nature — the Humean best-system picture on one side, and on the other the view that a law is a primitive relation of necessitation holding between universals, something over and above the regularities. I said I'd bring in an objection of my own to the Humean side, and I've ended up with an arguement that either lands cleanly or refutes itself, and I honestly can't tell which, because when I follow it through it seems to take out the anti-Humean as well. I'd like you to act as a philosopher of science who has taught this debate many times and knows what every move costs, and help me work out what's gone wrong.

Here it is, tidied up:
(1) Laws explain their instances. That this particular copper rod lengthened when heated is explained, at least partly, by the law of thermal expansion.
(2) Nothing can explain a fact that it isn't independent of. A fact can't be its own explanans, and you can't explain something by citing it.
(3) On the Humean picture, the law of thermal expansion just is the regularity that heated copper rods lengthen — a generalisation over the very instances we wanted explained.
(4) A generalisation over some facts can't be what explains those facts. Citing the fact that all heated rods lengthen to explain why this one lengthened just re-cites the thing to be explained, plus all the others.
(5) So Humean laws can't explain their instances.
(6) But they do (1). So Humeanism is false: the law must be something over and above the regularities, a relation of necessitation between universals, and that relation is what does the explaining.
(7) Now ask the same question about the necessitation relation. That N(F,G) obtains is a contingent fact — that's the whole point of it being a relation between universals rather than a logical truth. By (2) it needs an explanans it's independent of. Either there is none, in which case it's a brute fact, which is just the sort of thing (2) was supposed to rule out; or it's explained by some further necessitation relation, which is explained by a further one, and so on up.
(8) So the demand that killed the Humean kills the necessitarian too, and no account of lawhood survives it.
(9) Therefore either (1) or (2) is false: either laws don't explain their instances, or explaining something doesn't require being independent of it.

What I'd most like to keep is (1) — I definately think it's a datum that laws explain, and any view that gives it up has lost the plot.
