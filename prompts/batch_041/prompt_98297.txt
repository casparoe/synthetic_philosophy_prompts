I volunteer at a legal aid clinic that handles asylum claims, and I keep running into a shape of argument that I don't have a word for. The shape is this: an obligation seems to get weaker the more widely it applies, and the weakening is treated as too obvious to need defending.

Three instances, and I genuinely don't know whether they are one thing or three.

First: a country takes in a few thousand people from one war. When the next war displaces people, officials say they cannot take them, because they have already taken so many. The numbers are trivial next to the country's capacity, so this is not really a capacity claim. It is more that having done the thing once is being used as a reason not to do it again.

Second: a person is refused protection because the danger they are fleeing is general. Everyone in the region is at risk, so they are not being singled out. Had the same danger been aimed at them personally, they would qualify. So the fact that more people are threatened makes each of their claims weaker, and the claim would be at its strongest if they were the only person threatened.

Third: in arguments about who owes what to the displaced, the obligation is said to fall on whoever caused the displacement. A country that sold weapons or voted for the intervention is told it is not the cause in the relevant sense, because its contribution was one of many and no particular displacement can be traced back to it. But the same country then says that because it has accepted some refugees from that war, it has done its part and its slate is clean. Here the duty appears to shrink both when the contribution is shared and when the reception is shared.

I half-remember an eighteenth-century essay on perpetual peace which says that strangers have a right to be received as visitors but not as settlers, and that the right has to be thin precisely because it belongs to everyone. That feels close to what I am circling, but I don't know whether it is the same phenomenon or only its ancestor.

What I want is not a label. If there is an established name for this, tell me which of my three cases it actually covers and where it stops covering them. If there isn't, coin one and define it, and tell me what it rules out: how it differs from an ordinary limit of capacity, from the observation that some duties are borne by many hands at once, and from the idea that a duty becomes less demanding as its content becomes more general. I would also like to know whether the third case belongs with the first two or is a different animal wearing the same clothes.

Please take a definite position. Tell me what the phenomenon is, give it a name or tell me which existing one to use, and defend the carving rather than laying the options out for me to choose between.
