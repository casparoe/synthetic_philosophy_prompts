I work in the harm-reduction unit of a state public health agency — technically I'm in the benefit-cost and equity shop, but really I'm the person who gets the weird proposals. We get maybe three or four a month where the upside is preventing a small number of genuinely awful outcomes (a child dying of a preventable infection, a nursing-home resident getting a pressure ulcer that turns septic, someone losing a foot to undiagnosed diabetes) and the downside is a tiny, barely noticeable burden spread across a huge population (an extra twelve minutes at the clinic, five more questions on a form, a 0.3% chance of a mild headache from a new screening protocol). My director has read about the torture vs dust specks thing and now wants a decision procedure we can actually run. Not a seminar answer — a checklist my one analyst and I can use in a week, with the director signing off. I need it to say what to measure, in what order, what counts as a sufficient answer, where it stops with a no, where it stops with a yes, and where it has to hand the decision to a human because it's a values call rather than a fact. There's alot of pressure to just multiply tiny numbers by huge populations and call it evidence, and I want the procedure to be honest about where that move is legitimate and where it's a trick.

We've been told to build it around a draft framework from our sister agency, the Cumulative Micro-Harm Review (CMHR). It has four knobs and I don't fully understand how they fit together: (1) a severity floor — below a certain intensity, a burden isn't counted at all; (2) a reversibility multiplier — irreversible burdens count more than temporary ones; (3) a distributional discount — burdens falling on people who are already worse off count more; and (4) a catastrophic anchor — if the harm you're preventing is above some severity, you're allowed to aggregate micro-burdens up to a cap, but not beyond it. I don't know how to set the floor, what the multiplier should be, or whether the anchor is just a deontological cheat dressed up as a parameter. Turn this into a procedure a practitioner can follow. Keep whatever distinctions actually matter and drop the ones that don't survive contact with a real caseload. Mark the steps that are judgment calls. For each case, show the procedure's verdict, the step where it turns, and what would have to change for the verdict to flip.
