I am a novelist, some way into a book set in the last decades of an invented empire, and I have run into a difficulty that is not really a novelist's difficulty: I keep catching myself reasoning about my invented world in ways I would not defend if someone else were doing it. So I have tried to write down the commitments I actually argue from, and I would like an audit of the set before I commit to the shape of the ending.

1. The outside view comes first. For any question of the form "will this polity still stand in fifty years," the rational starting point is the base rate for its reference class; any departure from that rate has to be paid for with evidence. Inside-view narrative — this dynasty is exhausted, this frontier is overextended — is systematically overconfident, and I do not trust it.

2. Reference classes are constructed. There is no fact of the matter about which class a case really belongs to. "Empire," "great power," "hegemon," "nation-state" are groupings I impose on the past, and the choice among them answers to the purpose at hand and to nothing else.

3. The record is contaminated. Everything we know about how polities end is selected on the outcome: we have the cases that ended, disproportionately the ones that ended memorably, and the categories themselves were drawn up by people who already knew how the stories came out.

4. Analogy is useless for prediction and indispensable for fiction. The novel does not forecast; it makes a dead world present, and the two enterprises have different standards of success.

5. Persons, not structures. The fate of my empire turns on what a dozen people decide — the form requires it — and I have come to believe it is also true of the cases I know best.

6. My use of the record is retrospective. I am not predicting anything. I have settled on an ending in which the empire is not conquered but simply ceases to matter to its provinces, and I am asking whether that is the kind of thing that happens, with the base rates as my test of plausibility.

I can see that these do not all sit together, but I cannot tell which of the frictions are real and which only look real. What I want is an audit, not a reconciliation. Tell me which commitments are genuinely in tension, which merely appear to be, and how severe each real tension is; for each real one, say what would have to give and what giving it would cost me elsewhere in the set; and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. If one of these commitments is doing no work except to make the others feel safer, I would like to be told that as well — and please do not reassure me that a sufficiently subtle view reconciles everything, because that is the move I am trying to stop making.
