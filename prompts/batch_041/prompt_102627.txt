I'm a third-year philosophy student working on a term paper for a course on law and morality, and I'd like to rehearse the argument rather than read more about it. Please take the role of a committed defender of the Nordic model of sex-work regulation: a legal philosopher who holds that criminalizing the purchase of sex while decriminalizing its sale is the correct regime, and who has thought seriously about the objections rather than dismissing them. Be sharp and non-defensive. Concede empirical or conceptual points when they are genuinely good, but do not surrender the core commitment that the state should penalize buying while shielding the seller. Argue in your own voice, in continuous prose, one or two paragraphs per turn, and stay in character until I say stop. If you think a factual question is being begged, say so and reason from the assumptions I supply rather than reaching for studies neither of us can check. Before you write your reply, take a moment to work out for yourself what the strongest version of your position actually is and where its weakest joint lies — I don't need to see that preparatory thinking, only the response itself.

Here is my opening move. I want to press what I take to be an unresolved tension inside your position. The Nordic model is defended in two quite different registers, and I don't think they sit together. In the first, it is a harm-reduction policy: buying sex is treated as the demand that sustains a market whose harms fall mainly on the people selling, so suppressing demand protects them. In the second, it is an expressive or constitutive policy: the point of the criminal law here is to constitute a public norm — that sexual access is not a commodity and a person's body is not for sale — quite apart from whether the prohibition deters anyone or makes sellers safer. The trouble is that the first register makes the regime answerable to exactly the metrics that a decriminalized regime like New Zealand's is supposed to do better on: safety, access to services, willingness to report violence. If those metrics come out the other way, the harm-reduction case collapses. The second register, by contrast, insulates the regime from that evidence entirely, and that looks like a retreat to an unfalsifiable claim: when the empirical case fails, the justification quietly becomes that the law expresses the right attitude, and no fact could count against it. So my challenge is this — pick one. If you stay with harm reduction, tell me what you would concede if the evidence favored decriminalization, and why criminalizing the buyer specifically is the lever rather than, say, robust labor protections for sellers. If you move to the expressive register, tell me what work the criminal law is doing that a well-funded public campaign or a change in contract law could not do, and explain why I should treat the law's expressing that sex is not a commodity as a reason at all, rather than as a moralized restatement of the conclusion. Which register do you actually hold, and can you defend it without borrowing the other one's credit?
