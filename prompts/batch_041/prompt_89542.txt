I am a second-year archaeology student in a seminar on archaeological inference, and I keep colliding with a phrase my professor produces whenever anyone asks what past people actually thought or believed. Ask whether a burial assemblage licenses any claim about the mourners' beliefs regarding an afterlife, or whether a cache of standardized clay tokens licenses talk of a "bureaucracy" or an "office," and the answer arrives in the same six words: the past is a foreign country. It is delivered as though it settled the matter — as though it showed that attributing beliefs, concepts, or institutions to people who left us only objects is a category mistake or a piece of naive projection. I doubt that a single sentence can bear that weight, and I suspect it is doing several different jobs at once, some of them entirely compatible with ordinary archaeological inference and some of them, taken literally, corrosive to the whole discipline.

So what I want is a careful disambiguation rather than a defense or a refutation. What are the distinct precise theses that "the past is a foreign country" could be expressing? For each one, say what it commits you to, whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, a contentious claim that reasonable people reject, or simply false, and — most important for me — whether it actually supports my professor's use of it to shut down questions about past beliefs and institutions. Please spread the readings along the genuine fault lines rather than clustering them: how far concepts can be translated across contexts, and whether a wholly unintelligible alien scheme is even a coherent notion; whether the gap from objects to beliefs is a difference of degree (harder evidence) or of kind (no evidence at all); whether the real point concerns underdetermination, that the same material admits of incompatible interpretations; whether it is an ethnographic-analogy problem, that we can only read the past through categories borrowed from living societies; and whether it is in the end an ethical or political injunction against judging the past by our own standards, which would be a different claim entirely. Tell me which reading the phrase most likely carries in a seminar room, and which reading would have to be true for it to do the argumentative work my professor wants it to do.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — your verdict on whether the slogan, on any of its readings, actually supports using it to close off inference about past beliefs and institutions — and then lay out the menu of readings and the reasoning behind each verdict, with no preamble and without restating my question. Write in American English.
