I'm a junior in high school and I'm in this after-school game theory club, and my friend Maya dropped an argument on me that I can't get out of my head. We were doing iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, and our club leader said that sometimes a pure strategy is strictly dominated only by a mixed strategy, not by any pure strategy. Maya made up this little row-player game to test that: row has A, B, and C; column has X, Y, and Z. Ignore column's payoffs, they don't matter for row dominance. The row payoffs are:

|   | X | Y | Z |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 3 | 0 | 0 |
| B | 0 | 3 | 3 |
| C | 1 | 1 | 1 |

If row plays A half the time and B half the time, the mix gets 1.5 against X, 1.5 against Y, and 1.5 against Z. So C is strictly dominated by that mixed strategy. But A doesn't dominate C, because A gets 0 against Y while C gets 1, and B doesn't dominate C either, because B gets 0 against X while C gets 1. So the mixed strategy seems to do something no pure strategy does. Maya says that's impossible in general and wrote this out:

(1) By definition, a pure strategy s is strictly dominated if some mixed strategy does strictly better than s against every pure strategy the other player might play.
(2) Suppose m is a mixed strategy that strictly dominates s, and m is a weighted coin flip over pure strategies s1, ..., sk.
(3) Against any one opponent pure strategy t, the payoff of m is a weighted average of the payoffs of s1, ..., sk.
(4) If that weighted average is strictly bigger than s's payoff against t, then at least one of s1, ..., sk must be strictly bigger than s against t.
(5) So for each opponent pure strategy t, there is some pure strategy in the support of m that does strictly better than s against t.
(6) Therefore there is some pure strategy in the support of m that does strictly better than s against every opponent pure strategy.
(7) So if s is strictly dominated by any mixed strategy, it's strictly dominated by some pure strategy.
(8) Hence mixed strategies add nothing to iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies.

Maya thinks this proves mixed dominance is basically a myth. But (8) can't be right, because our club leader and the table above seem to show it doing real work, and if (8) were true then iterated elimination with mixed strategies would never eliminate anything that pure dominance wouldn't already get. I'm definately missing something. Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I'd rather keep (1) than (8), but I want to know what I'm paying. Also, if I reject the bad step, does that change how I should think about iterated elimination in games where mixed strategies matter?
