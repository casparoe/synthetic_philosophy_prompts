I am drafting a position paper on incommensurable values and hard choices and would like an audit of my own commitments before I commit to a line of argument. Here is where I have landed.

1. Value pluralism. There are several irreducible kinds of value — welfare, autonomy, fidelity, aesthetic worth, and others — and no common currency into which they can be converted. This is a claim about the structure of value, not about our ignorance of it.

2. Incomparability. For some pairs of options there is no fact of the matter about their relative value: it is not the case that one is better, nor that one is worse, nor that the two are equal. Again, a feature of the options rather than of our information about them.

3. Reasons track value. An option has a reason in its favour only in virtue of some value it realises; where two options stand in no value relation, neither has more reason in its favour than the other.

4. Determinacy of what to do. Even so, I think hard choices frequently have correct answers. Someone facing an incomparable choice can act as she ought, not merely permissibly, and someone who acts otherwise is open to rational criticism rather than merely to pity.

5. A verdict I want to keep. I have been working with this case. Nadia is a field biologist offered a three-year post at a research station on the far side of the world. Her mother has just been given a diagnosis of a degenerative illness and will need daily help. Nadia goes. I think she did wrong in going. I also think that going was not worse than staying, and that staying was not better than going.

6. Agent-regret. Someone who chooses well in such a case may fittingly regret that she could not also have had what she gave up, and that regret is neither a judgement that she erred nor a symptom of weakness of will.

7. Institutional commensuration. I think a regional health board dividing a fixed budget between a hospice-at-home service and a neonatal intensive care unit is entitled to rank the two on a single scale — cost per quality-adjusted life year, say — and to act on the result, and that its decision is not arbitrary but can be right or wrong. I do not regard this as a regrettable second-best forced on us by scarcity.

I suspect that some of these pull against one another and that at least one pair only appears to. What I want is an audit rather than a commentary: which pairs are genuinely in tension and which are not, how severe each genuine tension is, and, for each, the cheapest revision that would dissolve it and what that revision costs. I have no idea which of the commitments is doing the damage. If there is a single commitment whose abandonment would clear up the most, say which it is and what I would be giving up by abandoning it. I do not need references; I want the argument. Please write your answer in British English.
