I am a novelist by trade — three books, none of them philosophical — and I have somehow talked my way into giving a paper at a two-day workshop on value theory next month, on a problem I came to sideways while writing fiction and have since become mildly obsessed with. The problem is the spectrum argument against the transitivity of better than: arrange a sequence of worlds, or lives, or single life-plans, so that each adjacent pair differs by a change small enough that no one is willing to say the later item is worse than the earlier one, and you arrive at an endpoint that is plainly far worse than where you started. My two stock examples are a population spectrum, running from a single person at an extremely high level of well-being to a great many people at a level barely above nothing, and a personal spectrum, running from a novelist who gives none of her time to others to one who gives all of it, with each hour along the way costing what feels like nothing. Since at least as good as cannot be intransitive without collapsing into absurdity, something in the sequence has to give, and my paper argues that transitivity survives intact — but only if we stop treating better than as a two-place relation and let it take a third argument, the grain at which the comparison is drawn. My semi-formal story is that a grain is a set of admissible transformations of the value-bearing features of the items being compared, and a comparison holds at a grain just in case it is invariant under those transformations; each step in the spectrum, I claim, is true only relative to the grain at which it is uttered, so the argument is a sorites that slides the grain rather than a counterexample to transitivity. I lean throughout on a distinction of my own between the anchor of a comparison — the dimension along which two items are being lined up — and its margin, how far apart they sit along that dimension, and my diagnosis is that the spectrum steps feel compelling because they hold the margin fixed while quietly switching the anchor.

I know roughly who will be in the room, and it is a small, sharp audience of about twenty. There is a decision theorist who works on preference representation and will want to know what formal object I take better than to be, and whether I have not simply renamed incompleteness as a third argument place. There is a philosopher of language who works on comparatives and degree semantics, who will go straight to the embedding facts — what happens to my relativized relation under negation, inside conditionals, and inside comparatives embedded in comparatives — and who will take it as obvious that natural-language comparatives are transitive by construction, so that the burden falls entirely on me. There are two population ethicists who regard the spectrum argument as a proof that the relation really is intransitive and who are not in the market for a rescue, one of whom will press me on whether my view can still make sense of the thought that some outcomes are simply better than others, full stop. There is a Kantian-leaning moral philosopher who suspects the whole enterprise is a symptom of trying to rank everything, and a handful of graduate students who will ask the one naive question I cannot answer. Please write me a Q&A prep sheet for the session: the objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they would do rather than by how likely they are — likelihood times severity — each one phrased in the vocabulary of the particular person who will raise it, with a two-minute reply I can deliver out loud without notes and a candid note on exactly where that reply is soft. Tell me what to concede in the first thirty seconds so that the rest of the hour is not a fight about the wrong thing, tell me which of these people is most likely to end my paper for me and why, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks, along with a plain verdict on whether I should keep hoping that or raise it myself before someone else does. No references, reading lists, or citations, please; I know the terrain well enough, and what I need is the argument, not the bibliography.
