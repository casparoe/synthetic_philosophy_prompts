I spent forty years in a molecular biology lab and have lately been reading philosophy of science, mostly around reduction and emergence, so I'd like your help thinking through something that nags at me about my old field's habits.

Here's the practice. In my department, a paper that reports a solid correlation plus a tidy working model gets knocked down in review as "descriptive, not mechanistic" — the referee wants the molecules, the pathway, the parts doing the work. Yet those same referees, in the same breath, will happily accept "selection favored this trait" or "the system relaxes toward the free-energy minimum" as a real explanation, with no derivation from physics anywhere in sight. Grant panels behave the same way: they demand mechanism, then fund work that runs entirely on higher-level generalizations.

You're a philosopher of science specializing in reduction and emergence. Before you answer, take a bit of space to brainstorm the candidate positions, then give me your considered view. What does this practice actually presupose? Does the mechanism-demand commit my colleagues to a layered ontology with physics at the bottom, or only to something weaker — an epistemic norm about what counts as understanding? Which theories of reduction does the practice fit comfortably, which does it quietly rule out, and are the two halves of it — mechanism-worship and cheerful reliance on higher-level laws — even consistent with each other? Also, is the practice genuinely committed to a hierarchy of levels, or is that just a metaphor everyone repeats? And tell me what would have to change about how we referee papers and fund grants if the underlying picture were replaced by a more thoroughly pluralist or non-reductive one.
