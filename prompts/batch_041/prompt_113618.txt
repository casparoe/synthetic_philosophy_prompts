I want a credence, not a literature review. The proposition: among the causes of today's cross-country income differences, the institutional cluster — secure property rights, constraints on the executive, rule of law — carries more causal weight than geography (climate, disease environment, transport costs, soil quality) and culture (trust, time preference, cooperative norms) combined. I put myself at about 0.6. Give me your number and then the decomposition that produces it, with a rough probability and a one-line justification for each term: (i) that "institutions" names a cause rather than a restatement of prosperity, a de facto arrangement that rich societies can better afford, or a rating that partly encodes income; (ii) that the historical instrumental-variables strategies — settler mortality, colonial legal origin, and their kin — satisfy their exclusion restrictions; (iii) that geography acts on income mostly through institutions rather than directly; (iv) that culture is largely downstream of institutions rather than an independent cause, including the awkward cases where the arrow appears to run the other way, as in regions whose ancestors were heavily raided for slaves and which show low trust today; and (v) that the question is well-posed at all, given that we observe one history rather than an ensemble of counterfactual histories over which a variance share could be computed, and that countries are not independent draws.

Combine the terms, tell me which one the final number is most sensitive to, and name the single result — empirical or conceptual — that would move it most, saying whether (v) would make such a result moot. Open with the number.
