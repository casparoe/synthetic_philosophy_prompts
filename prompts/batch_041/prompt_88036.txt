I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last several years reading philosophy in a somewhat unsystematic way, and I have agreed to lead a two-hour continuing-education seminar for a group of retired scientists and engineers on whether there can be laws of history, using the arguments usually gathered under Popper's critique of historicism. I need a plan for how to teach the material, not a summary of the material and not an explanation for me. The audience is technically fluent—they can read a graph, follow a causal argument, and spot a bad extrapolation—but they have no philosophical training. They will arrive with a scientist's instinct that a robust trend is a law and with a suspicion that talk of laws of history is either platitudinous or mystical. I have a whiteboard and two hours, and I do not want to assign reading.

The session must do the following conceptual work, in an order you justify: first, make the distinction between a law and a trend feel necessary rather than verbal, including the difference between a trend projected under a ceteris paribus clause and a law that holds unconditionally; second, bring out why a law of history would have to range over a single, non-repeatable sequence in a way that a law about a repeatable class of events does not; third, explain the role of the unpredictable growth of knowledge in the argument; and fourth, say what remains of historical explanation and conditional prediction once the strong historicist claim is rejected. I want the distinction between a law over repeatable events and a law over a single historical sequence to be load-bearing, not a footnote, and I want the ceteris paribus problem to be more than a slogan.

For the carrying example, I do not want the usual political cases—inevitable stages of economic development or the cyclical rise and fall of civilizations. I want the alleged law that the history of life exhibits an inevitable trend toward greater complexity, or, if you judge that example unworkable, a closely analogous biological trend. Use it to generate the law/trend distinction and the single-sequence problem, and make explicit where the analogy to human history holds and where it breaks. Tell me what the example must include to do this work—for instance, a measurable proxy for complexity, a proposed mechanism, a time scale, a contrast between a local lineage and the whole biosphere—and what it must leave out or it will teach the wrong lesson.

My audience is scientifically literate, so I expect them to form specific misconceptions: that any robust trend is a law; that Popper is denying that history has patterns; that the argument depends on free will or on the unpredictability of individual human beings; that historicism is simply any attempt to explain history; and that if prediction is impossible, historical knowledge is worthless. Finally, list what I should deliberately omit at this level, with the reason for each omission, and give me one sentence I can use to tell them what I am omitting and why.
