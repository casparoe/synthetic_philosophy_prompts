I am a novelist at work on a book of historical fiction set in England in the 1870s. One of my characters is a young provincial reformer, invented, who has made himself a disciple of John Stuart Mill, and the set piece of the novel is a long night's argument in which his companions put this question to him: whether bringing a child into the world is a service or an injury, given what that child's life is likely to be. My readers will hear in the question a doctrine Mill never met — that the suffering of a life brought into being is a reason against bringing it into being, while the happiness of a life that would otherwise never have existed is no corresponding reason in its favour, so that we are not indifferent to making unhappy people but may be indifferent to making happy ones. I do not want my disciple to say whatever is convenient for my plot. I want the best-supported extrapolation of what Mill himself would have held, and I want to know exactly where the extrapolation turns into guesswork.

Treat yourself as a scholar of nineteenth-century moral philosophy who also has a working command of the present debate over population ethics. Before you answer, brainstorm on paper the live positions for yourself — aggregate welfare across every life that will ever exist; count only harms and benefits falling on determinate individuals; and the asymmetry's own defence — and then argue from Mill's actual commitments rather than from his reputation. Think slowly; the interesting part of this is where his doctrines pull against each other.

Please address at least the following:

1. Would the Greatest Happiness Principle, as Mill states it, count the happiness of people who will never exist? When he names the end of action he calls it "an existence exempt as far as possible from pain, and as rich as possible in enjoyments" — a description of how a life is to go, silent, so far as I can see, on how many such lives there ought to be. Is that silence significant or accidental? And when he says the standard is "not the agent's own happiness, but that of all concerned," who counts as concerned — everyone alive, everyone who ever lives, or only those who can be located?

2. His doctrine of the higher pleasures, and the value he sets on individuality and self-development: does that commit him to saying that a life can be a good to the person living it, and therefore that creating such a life is itself a good rather than merely permitted? Or is that claim only ever about lives already underway?

3. The Malthusian and the feminist strands in his writing — his advocacy of prudential restraint and his impatience with improvident multiplication among the poor, on the one hand, and his attack on the legal subjection of wives and on forced maternity, on the other. Is the first strand the asymmetry in disguise, or is it only a concern for the welfare of people who already exist? And given his hostility to coercion in this sphere, would he have held that whatever reason there is to refrain from procreating is a reason of prudence and morals only, never of law?

4. Non-identity: the child whose life goes badly because it was conceived would otherwise have been nobody. As a hedonist, could Mill make sense of weighing a life against the alternative of no life at all — treating not being born as a state of affairs to be ranked, rather than as nothing whatever?

I have given my character a phrase for the asymmetry: he calls it "an injury that has someone to fall on, and a benefit that has no one to fall on." Tell me whether that figure of speech tracks a real distinction in Mill's ethics or quietly conflates two.

Answer as a short prose memorandum, in continuous prose rather than bullet points, and show me your reasoning as you go. Do not give me citations, page references, or a reading list: I cannot check them and they are no use to me in a scene. What I do need is your honest calibration. Separate plainly what is settled among people who know both Mill's corpus and the population-ethics literature from what is genuinely contested, and do not let a contested reading pass as the consensus. Mark your inferences as inferences and give me your confidence in each main claim. If you judge one of my questions to be malformed, or think Mill would have been pulled in two directions and left the matter unresolved, say that rather than manufacturing a verdict — and tell me if my fictional disciple would have had to say something Mill himself would have found strange.
