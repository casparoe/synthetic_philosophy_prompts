I want to try a stipulated framework on a concrete episode and then hear what the framework gets wrong. The framework sorts the grounds on which wealthy countries might owe something to poor ones into three headings, and its ambition is that any defensible claim about such obligations can be assigned to exactly one of them, with different implications in each case for who owes, how much, and for how long. Reparative grounds run backward: an obligation owed by an agent to those it has wronged, discharged only by repair. Humanitarian grounds run forward: an obligation triggered by a serious harm that one can avert at a cost one need not bear, owed by whoever is positioned to avert it. Structural grounds attach to neither injury nor rescue but to participation in a standing scheme of cooperation — the rules of trade, patents, finance, migration — and require that the scheme's terms be justifiable to those bound by them.

Test the framework against the fight over access to HIV medicines in southern Africa at the turn of the millennium. Work with the following facts and resist adding others. In 1997 South Africa amended its medicines law to permit generic substitution, parallel importation, and price transparency, all aimed at lowering drug prices. In February 1998 the national pharmaceutical manufacturers' association and roughly forty multinational firms sued the government, contending that the law violated the country's intellectual-property commitments under the world trade regime; the United States and the European Communities backed the firms with threats of trade sanctions. Under intense international pressure — from treatment activists, NGOs, and the press — the companies withdrew the suit in April 2001. In November of that year the WTO ministerial conference at Doha affirmed that intellectual-property rules should not prevent members from protecting public health, including through compulsory licences. Yet the medicines reached most patients only years later, and much of the delay was the work not of foreign patent-holders but of the South African government itself, which was slow to procure and distribute treatment even after the legal and diplomatic obstacles had cleared. Generic competition drove the price of the drugs down sharply in the meantime.

Now run the analysis in both directions. First, the episode through the framework: sort the claims actually in play, identify the duty-bearer under each heading — the firms, the rich-country governments that supported them, the poor-country government that hesitated, or someone else — and say what each heading demanded that the others did not. Pay particular attention to whether the headings are genuinely independent, or whether the patent regime is what manufactures the emergency to which the humanitarian ground then responds. Second, the framework through the episode: does the case vindicate one heading at the expense of the others, or reveal that the rhetoric of rescue did the persuasive work while structural claims did the moral work? The episode ends in a legal victory, a diplomatic declaration, and a slow, partial rollout; say what the framework predicts about that outcome and where it falls silent. I especially want an honest account of what the framework cannot express: whether the activists' central demand was for aid, for repair, or for something the three headings have no room for — a demand to be treated as a party whose claims must be answered rather than as the recipient of others' generosity. Close by saying what, if anything, the episode teaches the framework rather than the reverse.
