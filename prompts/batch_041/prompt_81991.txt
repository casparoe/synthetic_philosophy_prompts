I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've ended up setting one of the exam questions for a six-week evening course on wild animal welfare that I help run for second- and third-year undergraduates. The question is worth 25 marks and they get 60 minutes; they can't look anything up, so it mustn't turn on names, dates or citations.

I want the difficulty to sit in a single crux rather than in recalling the state of the debate. My plan is to put a short numbered argument in front of them and make them locate exactly where it succeeds or fails. The argument: (1) the life of a typical wild animal contains more suffering than wellbeing; (2) bringing into existence a life that is on balance not worth living harms the animal whose life it is; (3) habitat destruction means fewer wild animals will come into existence; therefore (4) other things equal, destroying habitat is good. I also want a second, invented principle in the question — a colleague's coinage, the offset ban — according to which the suffering of an animal that already exists can never be cancelled, in the accounting, by the non-creation of a different animal. Students should have to say whether the step to (4) needs the offset ban, whether the ban is defensible, and what follows for (4) if it isn't.

Please write the question, a model answer of about 700 words that commits to a verdict rather than surveying the options, and a marking scheme that names the must-hit points with the marks for each and the specific mistakes that should cost marks — in particular the ones that distinguish a student who can handle the crux from one who merely recites that wild animal suffering matters and that nature is full of it.
