I'm in a discord that's half ML engineers and half econ-adjacent grad students, and we've been re-fighting the socialist calculation debate for about three months now and nobody has moved an inch. At this point I care less about who won than about what kind of disagreement this actually is, so what I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict.

The shape of it as I understand it: Mises's original point was that if you abolish the market for the means of production you get no prices for capital goods, and without prices you can't do economic calculation at all -- you can't compare the resource cost of two ways of making the same thing, so rational allocation isn't just hard, it's undefined. Lange's reply, roughly: fine, let a central planning board post prices and adjust them by trial and error in response to shortages and surpluses, and give plant managers two rules -- keep average cost at its minimum, and set output where marginal cost equals price. Then managers face parametric prices and the whole thing converges like a Walrasian auction. Hayek's counter is that this assumes the relevant knowledge is already gathered somewhere, when in reality its dispersed, largely tacit, and never present to any single mind, so the auctioneer is doing all the work. Then the later Austrians add that equilibrium was the wrong target to begin with -- markets aren't calculators, they're processes of discovery and rivalry.

So is that one argument, or four stacked on top of each other? My hunch, and I'd like you to steelman it before you knock it down, is that Mises's impossibility claim and Hayek's knowledge claim are actually different arguments that get run together, that the incentive problem is a third thing entirely, and that the ML people in my discord are answering a question nobody in the 30s was asking.

Two things I want out of this. First the diagnosis: which parts are a substantive disagreement about how economies work, which are a disagreement about what counts as an answer (does a convergent model count, or does the thing have to actually exist and get discovered by somebody?), and which are verbal -- one side meaning central planning by "socialism," the other meaning market socialism. Say which is which and why. Second, a test case we keep stalling on: a big tech company runs an internal market for compute, teams bid for GPU-hours, a scheduler posts shadow prices that move with demand, and there's no external market for the capital goods involved. The ML people say this is Lange's model running in production and it sinks Hayek. The econ people say it proves nothing at all. Who's right? And if the answer is "it depends whether the posted prices are a signal or a decree," unpack that, because I suspect thats the actual crux.

Give me the diagnosis and the reasoning behind it, not a history of the debate and not a reading list. And finish with what would count as progress here -- a formal result, an existence proof, a distinction -- or an argument that nothing will, and why.
