I teach a graduate seminar on historical method and the Gospels, and I am writing this year's final examination. The problem I keep running into is that my students can recite the standard criteria of authenticity — embarrassment, dissimilarity, multiple attestation, coherence, and the rest — and they can also recite the usual objections to each one, which means my old questions no longer separate students who have read the literature from students who understand why the literature reached an impasse. So this year I want a single essay question organized around a concrete proposal, one that cannot be assembled out of remembered summaries. The proposal comes from a student in the seminar, who argued over coffee that the whole criteria debate is a failure of informal reasoning. On his view, each criterion is really a claim about a likelihood ratio: the probability that a given saying or deed would have entered the tradition if it were authentic, set against the probability that it would have entered the tradition if it were not. State those ratios explicitly, he says, and combine them, and the apparent tensions among the criteria will either dissolve or turn out to be disagreements about stipulated numbers rather than about method. The tensions he has in mind are the familiar ones: dissimilarity purchases distinctiveness at the price of a Jesus who no longer belongs to Judaism; embarrassment looks like an argument from the church's willingness to embarrass itself, which is itself a historical thesis; coherence quietly reads the disputed theology back into the evidence as a test of authenticity; and every criterion seems to presuppose a background account of how traditions were transmitted, which the criteria were supposed to be independent of in the first place.

I want the exam to force students to say whether that probabilistic move rescues the criteria or merely relocates the difficulty — in particular, where the numbers are supposed to come from, given that the probability that the early church would have invented a saying is precisely what the criteria were introduced to estimate, and given that the criteria are applied one unit at a time to texts whose meaning may depend on contexts the criteria strip away. Please write the examination question itself, framed so that it demands a verdict rather than a survey and is answerable in ninety minutes by a well-prepared graduate student; then a model answer of roughly eight hundred words that actually commits to a position and defends it; then a marking scheme that distinguishes an A answer from a B answer. For the marking scheme, give five must-hit points worth four marks each, twenty in total, and for each point a one-line note on the specific error that typically costs candidates the mark. The question should not be answerable by listing the criteria or their standard objections, and the model answer should not turn on any particular scholar's book, so please avoid anything that would call for a bibliography or a citation. Write everything in American English.
