I am responsible for introducing the hinge of history hypothesis to a group of graduate students in history — the suggestion that we may be living at a point at which the expected influence of action upon the long-run future is unusually, perhaps uniquely, large. My audience is instinctively sceptical of anything that resembles presentism, Whig history, or a secularised eschatology, and will be quick to hear the claim as vanity dressed up as analysis. The session runs for ninety minutes and I have only a whiteboard. I do not want you to explain the hypothesis to me; I want a plan for how I should explain it to them.

Set out the order in which the components must be introduced and the dependencies that dictate that order; the single case or example that should carry the exposition, together with what it must contain and what it must not — it must be drawn from history rather than from artificial intelligence or nuclear weapons, since those are precisely the associations that will switch my audience off; the misconceptions they will arrive with or form — I expect them, for instance, to conflate the claim with the thesis that change is accelerating, or with the end-of-history thesis — each assigned to the precise point in the sequence at which it should be pre-empted; what I should deliberately leave out at this level, and how I should signal that omission; and a closing question or task that will reveal whether they have grasped the structure of the claim rather than merely been impressed by its stakes.

Two further constraints. My audience will suspect that this is Pascal's wager, or Pascal's mugging, in modern dress; the plan must meet that objection and decide candidly whether the resemblance should be conceded. And I want them to leave understanding the hypothesis as a comparative claim about leverage — about what fraction of the total influence over the long-run future is exercisable by those alive now — rather than as a claim about drama or significance; the sequence should make that distinction felt rather than merely stated. Please write the plan in British English.
