Two colleagues of mine have been arguing for months about a rule one of them has adopted. She is writing a dissertation and has resolved to believe every claim she can argue to a credence above 0.95, and also to believe, of the dissertation as a whole, that at least one of its claims is mistaken, since that is what the base rates about dissertations support. He says the two halves of that rule cannot both be satisfied: she believes each claim and believes that one of them is false, and those cannot all be true, so she is committed to an outright inconsistency and should either lower her confidence in every claim or give up the second belief. She answers that this is just the preface paradox, that the author of a preface is not thereby irrational, and that admitting the possibility of error is a mark of honesty rather than confusion. He replies that "everyone does it" is not an argument, and that her 0.95 is arbitrary: he can set the threshold at 0.6 and generate the same clash over a smaller bundle of claims, so she has merely relocated the problem into a magic number. She says the number is not magic, because believing is not the same as being certain, and a belief can be false without being irrational.

He then offers a repair: consistency is only required of beliefs she actually combines in a single deliberation, so she may keep all of them provided she never treats them as a package, since belief is a policy for action rather than a commitment to a global theory. She objects that this proves too much: if she is willing to assert each claim and act on each, she should be willing to act on their conjunction, and any threshold rule applied to a long enough list will eventually commit her to believing a contradiction.

They have asked me to settle it. Please go move by move: which of them is right at each step, what each is missing, and who is closer to the truth overall. Tie the verdict to the arguments as actually made rather than to the general literature.
