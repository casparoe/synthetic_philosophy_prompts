I'm a novelist, and I'm about a year out from drafting a book built around a priority dispute: two people arrive at the same insight at nearly the same time, and the story turns on what that coincidence does to them, their reputations, and everyone in their orbit. I've been reading around the Newton–Leibniz calculus quarrel and the near-simultaneous Darwin–Wallace papers, and I keep finding that the chronology is the least interesting part. What actually grips me is the conceptual machinery underneath it: what it means for an idea to be "in the air," whether a discovery of that kind was bound to be made by someone, and whether being first is even the thing worth caring about. I have roughly a year of research before I draft and I obviously can't read everything, so what I want from you is an agenda, not a survey and certainly not a reading list -- no bibliographies, please, since I'll be working largely offline.

Here are the candidate questions I've been circling, and I'd like you to rank them -- or tell me I've mis-framed the field entirely. First, the inevitability thesis: was the discovery bound to happen, and if so, does the mere existence of a near-simultaneous rival fatally undercut the "lone genius" story, or does it leave that story mostly intact? Second, the identity question, which I suspect is upstream of the first: are Newton's fluxions and Leibniz's differentials really one discovery or two, and are the Darwin and Wallace formulations genuinely equivalent, or does treating them as "the same discovery" beg a question? Third, the normative question: why do we care who was first, and should we -- is priority a genuine epistemic or moral category, or just an institutional convenience? Fourth, reception and neglect -- discoveries made and ignored, and the strange afterlife of an idea that has to be made twice. And fifth, the counterfactual-credit problem: if it would have happened anyway, on what basis does anyone deserve the credit?

I want the ordering defended on explicit criteria -- which questions are upstream of the others, which are tractable by argument rather than requiring empirical sociology I'd never be able to settle, which are neglected, and which would change how I write the characters if I got a real answer versus which are just decorative. Tell me what would move a question up or down the list, where the dependencies run, and which of my five you'd drop.
