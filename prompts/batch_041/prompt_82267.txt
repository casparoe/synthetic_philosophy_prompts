Our hospital's clinical ethics committee has drifted into a pattern on advance directives that nobody has ever written down, and I want to know what it commits us to before Thursday's meeting.

Four cases have shaped us. First, a woman in advanced dementia whose properly witnessed directive refused artificial nutrition and hydration; she showed no distress and no discernible wishes about anything. We honoured the directive and let her die. Second, a man who, while well, had ticked the box on a standard form demanding that everything possible be done for him whatever happened; after a devastating stroke he survived for months in a state of minimal awareness, without evident distress but in an existence he would once have called intolerable. We honoured the directive and carried on treating. Third, a woman who, within weeks of being diagnosed with depression, wrote that she would rather die than become a burden; years later, with moderate dementia but plainly content, engaged with her grandchildren and saying she wanted to live, we set the directive aside and treated her. Fourth, a woman with bipolar disorder who, while well, had signed a self-binding instruction of the kind usually called a Ulysses contract, refusing admission during future manic episodes; when she arrived in a manic episode and refused treatment, we treated her all the same.

Next week's case is a woman who, while competent, refused in advance all life-sustaining treatment once she could no longer recognise her family, adding an explicit self-binding clause: that even if she later seemed happy and asked to live, she wanted the earlier wish to win, because she knew she might change. She now fails to recognise her family, is cheerful, enjoys music and says she wants to live. She has a treatable infection which, left untreated, will kill her.

Tell me what we should decide if we are to remain faithful to our own track record. Say which competing rules fit the four cases and what each predicts here, which case is the nearest analog and why, and, if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the matter, which principle we would be taking on with either outcome. Then give me a single committed recommendation with your reasons, rather than a survey of options, and name the further case that would best separate the readings you have identified. Please write in British English.
