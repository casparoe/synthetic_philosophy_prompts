Alright, I've got to write a short position piece for a jurisprudence seminar and I want a consistency check before I commit to it. Here's where I've landed on the Hart–Devlin stuff.

(1) Millian about the criminal law: the only thing that can justify criminalising conduct is harm to other people. That it's immoral, that it offends people, or that it's bad for the person doing it — none of those count on their own. Small caveat I've always made: exposure can be restricted where the audience really can't get away from it. Nobody has to go into a sex shop, but everyone has to walk down the high street.

(2) Hart's distinction between positive and critical morality is right, and it's fatal to Devlin: what a society happens to disapprove of has no authority just because it's shared. Only the correct critical morality does.

(3) Still, I think there's something real in Devlin's worry. A shared moral code is a social asset — trust, fellow feeling, the sense that we're all playing the same game — and a society that holds nothing in common is genuinely worse off for it. So the state has a legitimate interest in keeping some of it alive.

(4) The criminal law also has an expressive job: it declares and it teaches. But the declaration has to be true — it should express the moral truth, not the majority's mood.

(5) A liberal state has to be neutral about the good life. No taking sides on contested questions about what makes a life worth living.

(6) Anything I endorse has to pass a reciprocity test: I have to be willing to live under it as a dissenter, in the minority, with the majority's morality not being mine.

Two things I'd defend in the seminar: I'd decriminalise drugs, sex work and assisted dying. But I'd keep adult sibling incest criminal even where no pregnancy is possible and nobody seems worse off. And I'm a retributivist about punishment — offenders deserve to suffer in proportion to what they did, and the state is right to denounce them for it.
