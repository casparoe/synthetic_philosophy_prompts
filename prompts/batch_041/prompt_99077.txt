For a graduate seminar in the philosophy of the social sciences that brings together demographers and social ontologists, I need a one-page classification instrument on the question: are generations real kinds? The participants already accept a common starting point — that people born in different decades differ systematically in attitudes, tastes, and life outcomes — and I want the tree to take that commitment as its root and resolve it, by successive diagnostic questions, into the named positions on offer.

Build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of authors: first the branch questions, then the positions that occupy the resulting combinations. Phrase every branch node as a question that someone could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and where a node turns on a contested case, give the case in one sentence.

At a minimum the tree must branch on the following, though you may add nodes:

1. Whether the birth-year correlation in the root is a genuine cohort effect or an artifact of age and period, given that age, period, and birth year stand in a three-way linear dependence that prevents their separate effects from being identified in standard models without further assumptions.
2. Whether a generation is only a location in the succession of birth cohorts, or becomes an actuality only when a set of cohorts responds self-consciously to a common historical problem.
3. Whether an actuality is one thing or divides into units that cut across it.
4. Whether generational categories are labels imposed from outside, categories whose members change their conduct because the category is applied to them, or kinds whose members share a cluster of properties held together by an underlying mechanism.
5. Whether a generation has causal power over its members' attitudes, merely compresses predictions of them, or is a retrospective narrative with no explanatory role.

The leaves must include at least: strong generational realism (generations are causally efficacious kinds); actuality realism (real only where a shared self-conscious response exists); cohort realism without generation realism (birth cohorts are real demographic kinds, generations are not); conventionalism (generations are useful coordinating fictions); performative or looping realism (the kind is real because its members take it up); property-cluster realism; real-pattern or instrumentalist realism (a generation is a real pattern that compresses prediction without a causal mechanism); and deflationary eliminativism (generational talk explains nothing). Each leaf should carry a name, a one-sentence statement of the view, its most awkward commitment, and a crux case that separates it from its nearest sibling.

At least two of the crux cases should turn on popular labels such as Boomers, Millennials, or Gen Z — for instance a person who identifies with a generation they were not born into, or a label in circulation before the cohort it names came of age.

Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and mark the combinations of answers that no position occupies, diagnosing each as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Do not cite or attribute any of this to named authors; name each position by its content. Keep the whole thing to a single page: roughly eight to twelve leaves and five to seven branch questions.
