I'm drafting an essay on the Hart–Devlin debate and would like detailed comments before I commit to the argument. As I understand the debate: Devlin holds that a society is constituted in part by a shared morality, that this shared morality is part of what holds the society together, and that the law may therefore criminalize conduct that threatens it even when the conduct wrongs no one who does not consent; Hart replies that the law may not enforce morality as such, and that the only defensible ground for criminalization is harm to others. My essay does not rehearse either side. It makes three moves.

First, it replaces Devlin's single premise — that a shared morality is necessary for social cohesion — with what I call a cohesion ledger. For any proposed legal enforcement of a moral norm, the ledger tallies the cohesion gained by making the practice uniform, the cohesion lost through resentment, concealment, and the creation of a class of offenders, and the availability of non-legal mechanisms (social sanction, exit, voluntary association) that would secure the same uniformity at lower cost. Devlin's premise is a claim about only the first entry.

Second, it introduces a case I call the moral mosaic: a society whose positive morality is not shared but is a patchwork of incompatible local moralities. I use a fictional coastal city-state with a settled native population and a large transient merchant population whose moralities conflict on one contested practice. In a mosaic, the question is whether cohesion requires a shared morality at all, or only agreement on how to resolve moral disagreement — what I call procedural cohesion.

Third, the thesis: once the ledger and the mosaic are on the table, Devlin's argument does not support legal moralism. It collapses either into the harm principle (because the cohesion costs are themselves harms and must be entered on the ledger) or into a procedural view on which the law's job is to secure coexistence among moralities rather than to enforce one of them. So Hart wins, but not for Hart's reason: Hart attacked Devlin's empirical premise about cohesion, whereas the deeper trouble is that Devlin's argument equivocates between substantive agreement and procedural agreement.

I'd like three kinds of comments. (1) Accuracy: is this a fair reconstruction of Devlin's actual argument, and does it misstate what Hart's reply actually was — in particular, am I wrong to characterize Hart's objection as empirical rather than conceptual? (2) Whether the cohesion ledger is a genuine addition or the harm principle in disguise: if every ledger entry can be redescribed as a harm, does my first move do any work? (3) Whether the thesis that Hart wins for the wrong reason is interesting or trivially true, and if it is interesting, which of the three moves carries it. Please be concrete about where the argument is weakest and what would fix it.
