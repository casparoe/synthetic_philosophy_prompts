I chair the grants committee of a mid-sized foundation whose charter commits us to reducing animal suffering. A donor has proposed what she calls a "moral trade": she will make a large unrestricted gift for farm-animal welfare, on the condition that the committee sign, in the foundation's name, a public statement she has drafted opposing a particular biomedical technology. That topic lies outside our charter; we have no settled view on it and no expertise. She is candid that the statement is not aimed at our mission — it advances a cause she cares about, and she is paying us to lend our name to it. Half the committee sees a clean win, since her money does our work and signing costs us nothing we value; the other half believes we would be selling the foundation's credibility, endorsing a claim we have not assessed, and establishing that our public positions are for hire. We meet Thursday. Draft the committee's decision memo. Structure it as: Recommendation (the specific course of action, in no more than three sentences, stating plainly whether we sign, decline, or sign with conditions); Why (roughly 250 words giving the affirmative case for your recommendation); The strongest objection (roughly 150 words presenting the best argument against your recommendation, in terms a dissenting member would accept as fair); and Reply to the objection (roughly 150 words answering it). Take seriously the possibility that our integrity as a funder is itself a good we hold in trust, and address whether endorsements are the kind of thing that can be traded at all.
