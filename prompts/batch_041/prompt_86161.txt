I'm a philosophy professor, and through a series of bad decisions I've ended up on our college's Faculty Hearing Panel, which is much less fun than it sounds. I also teach a jurisprudence seminar, so I've read all the usual material about whether interpreters should follow the words or the purpose, and I've now discovered that none of it tells me what to do when the text in question is an internal university rule and the "legislature" was a committee of my own colleagues. Here's the case, and I'm the one drafting the opinion we vote on Thursday, so I could use a hand.

Our Faculty Code, which no one outside this campus has ever heard of, has an Article IV reading roughly: no faculty member shall be disciplined for any statement made in the exercise of their professional duties, except where the statement constitutes harassment as defined in the University's Anti-Harassment Policy. A chemistry professor spent about ten minutes of a 300-level lecture arguing that a widely prescribed antidepressant doesn't work, that the trials behind it were manipulated, and he named our own psychiatry department's trial as one of the worst offenders. A psychiatrist in that department filed a complaint, and the dean opened a disciplinary proceeding under the Code's general unprofessional-conduct clause, on the theory that calling a colleague's research fraudulent in front of students is precisely what that clause covers. The chemist says Article IV flatly bars the proceeding: teaching is a professional duty, the statement was made while teaching, it isn't harassment as defined, and the one exception the drafters wrote is the only exception there is. The dean's office says Article IV can't possibly mean that, because its whole point was to protect scholarly inquiry from political pressure, and an unsubstantiated attack on a colleague's trial data doesn't protect inquiry, it poisons it. Our general counsel has circulated a page saying the plain text controls and we should stop overthinking, which I find definately too quick for reasons I can't quite articulate before Thursday.

What I need is the draft opinion I'll bring to the panel: a clear recommendation about whether the proceeding should go forward, be dismissed, or go forward only on some narrowed charge, plus the reasoning, plus an honest statement of the strongest case for whichever side I end up recommending against, since the dean's lawyer will be sitting right there and I don't want to look like I'm strawmanning her. Two things I want you to actually grapple with rather than gesture at. First, purposivism here isn't the same as guessing what the drafting committee wanted back in the seventies; I have no idea what they wanted as a group, and I'm not sure a committee has a single mind anyway, so if you're going to lean on purpose, say explicitly whether you're inferring it from the text itself or from the history, and what changes if it's the latter. Second, one colleague keeps insisting that the real argument for sticking to the words has nothing to do with words being clearer, it's that this panel didn't write the Code and shouldn't be in the business of supplying the goals the faculty senate forgot to write down, while another colleague says that argument simply doesn't transfer to us, since the panel is the faculty's own instrument rather than an outside agency imposing on it. I want the opinion to take a position on that, because whatever we say will get quoted back at us the next time a case comes up, and I'd rather choose our interpretive rule on purpose than let it fall out of however this one happens to land. Write it for people who aren't philosophers, the dean, the provost's office, the two student representatives, so keep the vocabulary civil, aim for something around 900 to 1200 words, and don't cite anything. I don't need a bibliography, I need a decision.
