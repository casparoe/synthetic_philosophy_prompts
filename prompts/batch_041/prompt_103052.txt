I work on the foundations of welfare economics and, more concretely, on the ethics of nudge-style policy, and I keep colliding with the same gap. People defend or attack a policy by pointing at what people choose, as though choice settled what is good for them, and then quietly help themselves to a richer notion of welfare the moment choice gives an unwelcome answer. Before I adjudicate between the hedonic, desire-satisfaction, and choice-based accounts of welfare, I want the specification written out first: the desiderata any adequate theory of what a revealed preference reveals — of what utility is a measure of — has to satisfy. I care about this for a specific purpose, so calibrate to it: a theory good enough to license or forbid soft paternalism, meaning it must be able to say, in a given case, whether overriding someone's choice serves or disserves their welfare, without that verdict collapsing into whatever the theorist already wanted.

Give me the requirements list, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, organized roughly as follows. First, the phenomena the theory must get right: intransitive and cyclic choice; framing and menu effects; the persistent divergence between what people choose and what they report or later endorse; and the fact that the same behavioral record often fits more than one utility representation once you are free to choose the state space. Second, the distinctions it must not collapse: preference versus welfare; what someone chooses versus what they would choose under better information; ordinal comparison versus cardinal measurement; intrapersonal aggregation versus interpersonal comparison; and first-order preference versus the higher-order attitude a person takes toward it. Third, the formal constraints it cannot contradict: that a representation theorem fixes a utility index only up to an increasing transformation, so any cardinal or comparable scale is extra structure the theory must earn; and that choice data alone do not identify the space over which the agent is maximizing.

I want two cases worked into the list, because they are the ones I cannot currently handle. The first is an agent who reliably chooses A over B, B over C, and C over A depending on the order in which the options are presented, yet who produces a stable, transitive ranking the moment she is asked to write one down — so I cannot tell whether the intransitivity lives in her preferences or in her choice procedure, and the desiderata had better say which of those the theory is answerable for. The second is an agent whose behavior satisfies every revealed-preference axiom with respect to a state space that includes a habit she says she is trying to break, so the identical record is equally well described as maximizing a taste for the habit or as maximizing a preference against it subject to a constraint; the theory needs a principled way to choose, or an honest admission that it cannot.

Then, and this is the part I most want, flag any subset of the desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — for instance, if combining a purely choice-based interpretation of utility with a mandate to correct for misinformation or adaptive preference forces the theory to smuggle in an external standard of welfare it claimed to avoid, say so plainly, since that impossibility is itself a result I can use. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are commitments of a particular camp. Tell me which single requirement does the most to eliminate candidates. Close with a short assessment of how hedonism, informed-desire accounts, and a strict revealed-preference interpretation fare against the list; keep that part brief, since I can run the scorecard myself once the criteria are fixed. A numbered list is fine.
