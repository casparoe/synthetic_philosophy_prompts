I lead a small team that ships a two-agent system: two instances of the same language model, each with its own system prompt, negotiating over how to divide a fixed pool of compute for the next batch of jobs. Each batch is handled by a fresh pair of instances with no memory of earlier batches, and the interaction has the shape of a one-shot prisoner's dilemma — each does better if the two of them split the pool as agreed than if both grab at once, each does better still by grabbing while the other keeps to the split, and the worst outcome for either is to keep to the split alone.

One of my engineers wants each agent's prompt visible to the other before either commits, and defends this with what he calls the program-equilibrium argument. In the idealized version of this setup, he says, what each side submits is a program that runs exactly as written, and mutual cooperation is reachable in a single round: both sides submit the same program, which cooperates if and only if the other side's submitted program is textually identical to it. Because the two match, both cooperate, and neither side gains by submitting anything else, since the other's program would then read as different and defect. His claim is that letting each of our agents read the other's prompt buys us the same thing at no cost.

The distinction I keep coming back to is this: a policy is legible when the other side can read it, and binding when reading it is enough to know what will actually be done. The programs in his example are both; our prompts are only the first. Ours do not determine actions — the same prompt, in a different context, at a different sampling temperature, after a different tool result, will not reliably produce the same move, and neither agent can establish afterwards that the move it observed was the one its own text prescribed. Reading the other agent's prompt tells you about its dispositions, not about what it will do.

I want to argue at our design review that the two prompts should stay private: not because visibility is worthless, but because legibility without bindingness is not a watered-down version of the setup my engineer is citing. It is a different regime, with its own failure modes, and a worse one than opacity. Please make that argument for me.

Argue it on the merits; I do not need a survey or a reading list. Be exact about which feature of the idealization our deployment lacks, so that I can state the gap without hand-waving, and about what the textual-identity trick really depends on. Take the strongest opposing case seriously — that dispositions are informative enough to guide a best response, that visibility at least gives each side something to condition on, that legibility is the natural first step toward a binding mechanism we might add later — and explain why none of it rescues his argument. Then tell me what would change my mind: whether anything short of full determinism restores cooperation, and in particular whether a shared random seed, an audited record of prompt-and-action pairs, or a policy the platform itself enforces would do it, and at what cost. Close by naming the strongest reason to think I have the position backwards.
