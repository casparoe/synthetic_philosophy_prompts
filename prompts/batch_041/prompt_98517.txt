I'm on the organizing committee for a small research program — roughly six funded fellows for two years, plus a workshop and a modest discretionary fund — devoted to causation by omission, and we have to commit to a thematic focus before we advertise the positions. The committee is split in a way I suspect is diagnostic. One faction treats the core puzzle as metaphysical: whether an omission can be a cause at all, given that an omission is not an event and, on some views, not any kind of entity that could stand in the causal relation; they want to know whether appeals to absences, negative facts, or disconnections in a standing process can do the work, or whether the whole idiom is a manner of speaking that a serious ontology should paraphrase away, and how any of this squares with the causal closure of physics. A second faction thinks the metaphysical question is either settled or sterile, and that the live problem is causal selection and its normative coloring: an agent who fails to act omits indefinitely many things at once, and we single one of them out as the cause, apparently by reference to norms, defaults, or expectations — so the interesting question is what governs that selection and whether it is doing metaphysical work or merely pragmatic work. A third group is applications-first: the but-for test in tort law, the killing/letting-die distinction in ethics, and — the reason I care — the attribution of causal responsibility to automated and semi-autonomous systems, where the omission may belong to no human at all. One fellow has floated a specific line I'd like you to assess: that the right unit of analysis is not an absence construed as a quasi-event but the agent's failure to occupy a causal role in a mechanism that was already running, which would relocate the selection problem into the question of which mechanisms count as in play, and thereby make the metaphysics and the normativity inseparable rather than separable.

What I want from you is an agenda, not a survey. If you had this program's resources, which two or three lines of work would you concentrate them on, and in what order? Defend the ordering against explicit criteria — which questions are upstream of which, how tractable each is with the tools philosophers actually have as opposed to requiring empirical work in psychology or law, how neglected each currently is, and how much downstream work would be opened or foreclosed depending on how it comes out. Tell me which of the three factions I described is closest to right about where the center of gravity should be, and be willing to say that one of these lines is a dead end or a distraction. I also want the reverse direction: for each line you rank highly, what result or argument over the next two years would knock it down the list, and what would move it up?

Finally, take the fellow's proposed reframing seriously enough to say whether it is a genuine advance or a relabeling that leaves the hard problems where they were. And of the applications in play — tort liability, the killing/letting-die asymmetry, and responsibility for automated systems — which would actually be changed by progress on the metaphysics, and which are trading on the metaphysics without needing it? Rank those too, and tell me where I would be wasting the workshop budget.
