For about a decade now I've watched "the whole is greater than the sum of the parts" do load-bearing work in arguments about whether social science can be reduced to psychology, and I have never been able to tell whether the people wielding it are asserting something trivially true, something substantive, or something false. The most recent occasion was a long forum thread on whether macroeconomic regularities are anything over and above aggregated individual behaviour; the slogan was produced, everyone nodded, and the person defending what amounted to methodological individualism was treated as refuted. I don't want a verdict on that thread so much as a menu I can carry back to it.

So give me the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, ordered from the nearly empty to the radical, and for each one say whether it is true, whether it is a platitude rather than a thesis, and what argumentative work it could actually do. I want the readings separated at least along these lines: (a) the additive reading, on which the point is only that the property in question is not a sum of the corresponding properties of the parts — and here I want to know what measure is being summed over, since for population, mass or income the sum is trivial, while for price level, cohesion or legitimacy it arguably is not defined at all; (b) the relational reading, on which the parts are not bare individuals but individuals plus their relations, so the claim is true by construction; (c) the explanatory reading, on which macro-level descriptions are indispensable in practice because the individual-level derivation is intractable or incompressible; (d) the semantic reading, on which social predicates are multiply realizable in individual-level facts, so no bridge laws connect the two vocabularies; (e) the ontological reading, which I would state as: there can be a difference in social facts without any difference in individual facts; (f) the causal reading, on which social properties have causal powers of their own. I also want a reading of my own placed on the list: that the macro-description is a compression of the micro-facts, and compression is not an additive notion, so a whole can be "greater than the sum" in the sense of admitting a much shorter accurate description. Tell me which reading the slogan's users almost certainly have in mind, which ones would actually have refuted the reductionist, which are in tension with each other, and whether any of them is merely the familiar warning about the fallacy of composition (or its mirror, division), which I take to be a claim about inference rather than about what exists.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
