In the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, English academic office was held under statutes requiring public subscription to the Thirty-Nine Articles and, for many fellowships and chairs, the taking of holy orders within a fixed term. Isaac Newton's career shows how such requirements were actually lived with. Elected a Fellow of Trinity College, Cambridge in 1667, he was bound to proceed to ordination within seven years of his MA; he had no intention of doing so, and in the 1670s a dispensation was arranged that released him from the obligation. His reluctance was not indifference to religion. He wrote enormously on theology — on the early history of the Church, on the interpretation of prophecy, on the textual history of the New Testament — and by his thirties he held beliefs that would not have passed as orthodox: he denied the Trinity. In 1690 he sent Locke a dissertation arguing that two verses long used in support of the Trinity, 1 John 5:7 and 1 Timothy 3:16, were later corruptions of the text; he then arranged for the work to be withdrawn, and it appeared in English only in 1754, twenty-seven years after his death. Almost all of his theological writing stayed in manuscript, and he is reported to have refused the sacrament at the end of his life. Yet he was not publicly silent about God. The General Scholium he added to the second edition of the Principia in 1713 describes a God who governs all things as Lord and Universal Ruler, and Newton allowed his disciple Samuel Clarke to conduct a public controversy with Leibniz in which Newton's own theology was plainly at stake. In 1710 his successor in the Lucasian chair, William Whiston, was deprived of the professorship and expelled from the university for avowing anti-Trinitarian views of much the same kind that Newton held privately.

I want to understand what this pattern of conduct presupposes. Begin with the institution's side: what conception of belief does a regime of subscription and conformity presuppose? Does it treat belief as a private state of assent that public profession need not match, or as something constituted and tested by profession itself — and can it be made sense of at all if belief is not under the will? Then take Newton's side. What does his combination of private heterodoxy, public conformity, selective publication, and a public theology of God's dominion but never of the Trinity presuppose about the relation between inquiry and its audience — about which claims may be published, and to whom a theological claim is addressed? Does he implicitly separate natural religion from revealed doctrine, and if so on what grounds? Is his silence merely prudent accommodation to a coercive institution, or does it carry a substantive view about the source of the Church's authority — a view his own historical-critical method would undercut, since he charged the Church with fraudulent tampering with the text while practising a discretion of his own? And what does the contrast with Whiston reveal about what the institution was actually punishing: belief, or avowal?

Reconstruct the implicit theory, and be careful to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with. Say which rival views of belief, sincerity, and institutional authority it rules out, and where it is underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license the same conduct. Finally, suppose the presupposed view of belief and authority were replaced — by the view that belief is a voluntary commitment enacted in profession, or by the view that a community's doctrines are its shared form of life rather than a set of propositions to be verified — and tell me what would have to change in the practice.
