I'm a second-year PhD student and I've been trying to write a qualifying paper on imprecise probabilities, and the problem is that every time I write down my own view it turns out I've signed up for something I don't want. So instead of a paper I'm going to lay out where I've landed and have you audit it. Here goes: (1) I'm a Bayesian — the probability axioms are constitutive of rational credence, and I don't think there are any doxastic states that aren't probabilistically representable. (2) Still, I don't think rationality requires sharp credences; some evidential situations are honestly represented by a convex set of probability functions rather than a single one, and having a set isn't a defect that gets repaired by picking a favorite member. (3) I think credence is a matter of betting disposition — tell me your credence in H and I should be able to say which of two bets on H you'll take, and if two agents' credences differ there's some bet they'll treat differently. (4) I'm ambiguity averse and I think that's rational rather than a bias: I'd rather bet on an urn I know is half black and half white than on an urn I only know contains some unknown mixture, even though I think the second urn is just as likely to be majority-black as majority-white. (5) I don't think a rational agent should be exploitable — a bookie who knows my dispositions shouldn't be able to string together a sequence of offers that leaves me worse off for sure. (6) I think the only legitimate update rule is generalized conditionalization (conditionalize every member of the set, take the resulting set), and I think learning can never leave me epistemically worse off than I was before. (7) For decisions I'm an E-admissibility person: an option is permissible if it maximizes expected value under at least one member of my credal set, and if that leaves me with a few options I don't think rationality picks between them. In practice, though, when I'm actually stuck I break the tie by taking whichever option is best under the midpoint, the average of all the functions in my set, and I honestly can't tell whether that's a harmless practical crutch or whether it commits me to something I just denied.

The case I keep coming back to — a friend tells me it's called dilation — goes like this. A machine flips a fair coin, and there's a little light on the machine. The only thing I know about the light is that it's either a tracker, coming on more often after heads than after tails, or an inverter, doing the opposite; I have credence 1/2 in each, and in each hypothesis the light comes on half the time overall. So the two hypotheses are mirror images of each other, and I have no grip whatsoever on which one is true. Before the light comes on, my credence in heads is exactly 1/2, and it's hard to see how the light could carry any information about the coin. But if I conditionalize each of my two functions on the light coming on, one gives me 0.6 and the other gives me 0.4, so my new credences in heads run from 0.4 to 0.6. Learning something apparently irrelevent made me less precise. My instinct is that nothing has gone wrong, this is just what conditionalization does here — but for all I know that instinct is exactly the thing that has to go.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture: which of these commitments actually pull against each other, which pairs only look like they're in tension and why, and how severe each real tension is, ordered from worst to mildest. For each genuine tension, tell me which one has to give and what I lose by giving it up, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most at once. I'd rather not be sent off to read anything, so skip citations and reading lists entirely — I just want the arguments. And please write it in American English.
