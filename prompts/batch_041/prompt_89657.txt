I am a backend software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and a reading group I belong to has pulled me into a puzzle I cannot resolve on my own. A friend there insisted that Mary Shelley's Frankenstein is, underneath the Gothic machinery, a story about a metaphysical question: whether a collection of corpse parts, once stitched together and brought to life, actually adds up to a single object at all, or whether it remains a heap of parts that everyone, including the creature itself, mistakenly treats as one thing. That claim sent me to what I now understand is called the special composition question — the question of when some things compose a further thing — and to the three familiar answers: that composition always occurs, so that any two things whatsoever make a larger thing; that it never occurs, so that only partless simples exist; and that it occurs only under some restricted condition, the best known being Peter van Inwagen's, on which a composite object exists just when its parts together constitute a life. I can follow the options on the page, but I cannot tell which one, if any, the novel actually dramatizes, and my friend's confident reading strikes me as too quick.

I would like a serious critical reading of Frankenstein through the lens of the special composition question, one that is faithful in both directions: it should show me where the novel genuinely stages the puzzle — the assembly of the creature, the creature's own account of itself, its demand that a companion be made, the way every other character regards it — and it should also tell me where the novel resists the reading, so that I have to concede that Shelley was not writing a treatise on parthood. There is one point I especially want pressed. Each of the standard answers is stated in terms of objective facts — life, contact, causation — but the novel seems to make the creature's status as a single thing depend on whether anyone will recognize it as a self; the villagers, Victor, and the creature himself disagree about what is there, and the disagreement does not look like a mere mistake about a fact. If that is right, is the novel gesturing at a view the literature has no settled name for, or is it simply confusing composition with personhood? And does the creature's request for a companion put pressure on van Inwagen's answer specifically — would Victor's second assembly bring a new composite object into being, or only another aggregate of borrowed parts?

Please write for someone who knows the novel but not the metaphysics, keep technical vocabulary to a minimum and explain it where it is unavoidable, and use American English throughout. End with a final line that begins Verdict: and names, in a few words, which answer to the special composition question — universalism, nihilism, a restricted view such as van Inwagen's, or none of them — the novel most supports, followed by a single sentence giving your reason.
