For the better part of a year I have been writing a novel whose protagonist is a young set theorist, and to keep her from saying things no philosopher would say I have been meeting a retired colleague of my brother's — call her T. — every few weeks. T. spent her career on the metaphysics of grounding, she is formidably consistent, and since my character is meant to inherit T.'s cast of mind I have been keeping a notebook of T.'s rulings on individual cases, hoping to reverse-engineer the pattern before I write the argument scene. The notebook so far reads: the fact that a swatch is entirely crimson grounds the fact that it is red; the fact that the swatch is red grounds the fact that it is red or square; the fact that it is red or square does not ground the fact that it is red; the fact that the left half of a mosaic is blue together with the fact that the right half is blue grounds the fact that the whole mosaic is blue; the fact that the whole mosaic is blue does not ground the fact that its left half is blue; and the fact that a triangle is equilateral neither grounds nor is grounded by the fact that it is equiangular. What the notebook will not tell me is whether T. conceives of grounding as a species of metaphysical explanation — the ground is what makes the grounded fact obtain, and the arrow points in the direction of "because" — or as something thinner, a one-way necessitation on which X grounds Y whenever X necessitates Y and Y does not necessitate X.

I am now drafting the chapter in which my protagonist has to rule on three fresh cases, and I would like to know what T. would say about each, and why, so that the character's verdicts ring true. First, the fact that a certain radioactive atom has the constitution it has grounds the fact that the chance of its decaying within the hour is one half. Second, the fact that a figure is a regular polygon grounds the fact that its interior angles are equal. Third, the fact that a set has exactly the members a, b, and c grounds the fact that it has exactly three members. For each of the three, give me T.'s verdict; then tell me which single rule best fits all six of the earlier rulings, and what that rule predicts for each of the new cases; then say which one of the three, if I could get T. to rule on only one of them, would most sharply expose the rule she is actually running, and name some further case I have not mentioned that would separate the explanatory reading from the necessitarian one beyond reasonable doubt. I am less interested in which verdict is correct than in which verdict is hers, so please keep the character's consistency in view, and where the earlier rulings genuinely underdetermine a case, say so and tell me what principle she would be committing to on each branch.
