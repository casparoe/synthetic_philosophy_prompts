I just spent an evening arguing with a friend about Skolem's paradox and came away unsure whether my own position even holds together, so what I want is a consistency audit, not an exposition. Here is roughly where I've landed. (1) There really are sets, and set-theoretic statements have determinate truth values that do not depend on any formal system we happen to write down. (2) First-order ZFC is the canonical axiomatization of set theory, and its theorems are true of the sets. (3) By the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem, if ZFC has a model at all it has a countable one. (4) The uncountability of the continuum is an absolute fact about the sets, not merely something true relative to a model. (5) What secures that determinacy is the intended interpretation, which we pin down by quantifying in second order over the full power set of the domain. (6) Second-order logic has no semantics that is not itself set-theoretic, so it cannot be used to fix the intended interpretation without going in a circle.

My friend kept pressing the point that a countable model of ZFC cannot contain uncountably many reals, so (3) and (4) cannot both be true, and that the whole business shows set theory has no determinate subject matter. I suspect that particular pair is only apparently in conflict, and that the real damage is somewhere else in the list — but I cannot tell which is which, and I genuinely do not know which of the six I would give up.

So: tell me which of these commitments actually pull against each other, and how badly, ordered by severity rather than presented as equally bad. For each genuine tension, say what would have to be weakened and what that costs. For the pairs that only look inconsistent, say briefly why they are not. Then tell me which single revision would resolve the most.
