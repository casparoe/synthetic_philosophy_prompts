Right, so I cover science for a paper — long features, mostly replication-crisis stuff — and I've landed in one of those arguments where everyone else is very sure of themselves and I'm not. I've spent the best part of three months on a piece about a big nutrition trial, and the whole thing hinges on whether the trial was properly blinded, which I've never been able to confirm from the published material. Yesterday a postdoc who used to work in the lab offered to send me the internal emails and the randomisation file. She's definately not after anything, it's all above board, she'll just hand it over. Trouble is I've a horrible feeling it'll show the blinding was a shambles, which sinks the piece, and I'd then have to tell my editor, who's already booked the photoshoot for next month's issue.

My editor thinks I'm being an idiot: 'you can't lose by looking, mate. If it's fine, brilliant. If it isn't, you'd rather know. Either way the information's free, and free information can only ever help you.' My partner says the exact opposite: 'don't open it. You'll spend a fortnight feeling sick, and you'll end up spiking a perfectly good piece out of paranoia.' I can't shake the feeling that my editor is onto something real but is also glossing over something, and that my partner's position sounds like superstition but might not be. What am I actually weighing up here, and how would you sort it out?
