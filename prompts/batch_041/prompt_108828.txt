In a metaphysics reading group, a friend presented the following argument, which she calls the inheritance argument. I have not been able to fault it, and I cannot accept its conclusion. She states its engine first:

(A) If y is an origin of x, then anything required for y to exist is required for x to exist.

The argument then runs:

(1) No individual could have come into existence from a different origin.
(2) The origins of a human being are the particular sperm cell and the particular egg cell whose fusion produced it. Hence you could not have come from a different sperm or egg.
(3) Gametes have origins too. The sperm cell that is one of your origins was produced by a particular meiotic division in a particular cell in your father's body; that division is part of what produced it, and the same holds of the egg.
(4) Applying (1) to that sperm cell and (A) to you: in any world in which you exist, that meiotic division occurs and your father exists.
(5) Iterate. Every actual ancestor of yours, and every meiotic event that produced a gamete in the chain leading to you, is required for your existence.
(6) That chain is fragile: any difference anywhere in the history of the universe would have altered the timing, the pairing, or the assortment of chromosomes somewhere along it, and would thereby have replaced one of the gametes in the chain. She insists this is not a thesis of determinism, only a claim about how easily the chain would have been disturbed.
(7) So no world contains you in which the history of the universe went differently. Every actual event is required for your existence.
(8) The same holds for everyone. So there is no possible world containing me but not you, and no world containing either of us in which the dinosaurs did not die out.

I would like to keep (1) and (2) if I can, since origin essentialism seems to me independently plausible. Where does the argument go wrong, and what does rejecting the offending step cost me? I would rather not pay a price that leaves (1) and (2) looking arbitrary.
