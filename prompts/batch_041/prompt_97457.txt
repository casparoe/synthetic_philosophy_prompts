Across psychology and much of the social sciences, researchers routinely build models around latent constructs — general intelligence, working memory capacity, a single "depression factor," a latent quality score assigned to schools — and use them to predict and explain observable behavior, while officially declining to say whether the construct names something real or is only a convenient summary of the observables. The evidence that would settle the matter sits behind inaccessible information (what the brain is actually doing, how the construct maps onto any underlying structure), so the practice carries on without a verdict. And yet the same researchers who describe the construct as a mere summary will, in the next paper, explain a subject's performance by invoking the construct as though it were doing the causing. What view about how a theory's ontology is to be identified does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit account, say whether it is coherent, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and explain what would have to change if the inaccessible information became available — would the practice then be compelled to choose between realism and instrumentalism about the construct, or could it remain agnostic, and at what cost?
