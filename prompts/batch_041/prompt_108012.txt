Please act as a philosopher who works on early modern Latin American thought and on the philosophy of education, and who is also experienced at writing speeches that have to survive a skeptical room. Think carefully before you write.

I'm a high school junior. Next month I'm giving a five-minute speech at an assembly to launch our school's new after-school study program for girls, and I want it built on Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz's defense of women's education rather than on modern slogans. The room is mixed: about a hundred students, several teachers, the principal, and one parent who runs the school's religious-studies circle and has already told me the money would be better spent on the boys' sports program because the girls will end up raising children anyway. She will be sitting in the front row, and she will object on religious grounds that scripture assigns women a silent and subordinate role.

Three things need to shape the speech, not decorate it. First, I don't want to answer her by calling the objection benighted. The letter that admonished Sor Juana — written by the bishop of Puebla under a woman's pen name — granted scripture its authority, and her reply grants it too and still argues for women's education; so concede the premise my objector will not give up, and show why conceding it costs her nothing. Second, Sor Juana defends learning both as something a woman needs in order to teach her own children and in a way that isn't instrumental at all — she says something to the effect that she studies in order to be less ignorant rather than to know more — and I want the speech to hold both, because my audience only really listens to the career argument while I think the non-instrumental one is the honest one. Third, mark the difference between being permitted to study and being permitted to speak with authority, and say why a school that keeps demanding competence from girls while denying them the means to acquire it is in a worse position than one that simply refuses.

Start by brainstorming the three or four moves the speech needs, with a line for each on which of her arguments it draws on. Then write the speech itself. Paraphrase her rather than quoting her: I don't want a quotation I can't check, and I'd rather the argument be right than the wording. Avoid "empowerment," "glass ceiling," and any sentence that would fit into a speech on any topic. It should be something I can actually say out loud in five minutes.
