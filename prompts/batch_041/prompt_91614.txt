Alright, essay topic, and I want you to get into the weeds with it.

There's this old fight in philosophy of mind about whether dreams are experiences. One side (Malcolm, I think, following Wittgenstein) says no: when you say you dreamed something, you aren't reporting an experience you had while you slept, because the only thing that could count as the dream is the telling of it afterwards. There's no criterion for having got the dream right at the time, and where there's no possibility of a criterion, there's no sense in talking about a hidden experience that the report is about. Dennett pushed back and basically said that's a verificationist trick -- the concept of an experience shouldn't be hostage to whether we could in principle check up on it, and anyway the question is an empirical one that the science of sleep should settle.

I keep thinking the debate only gets interesting once you stop trading generalities and look at two cases, and I want the essay to deal with both.

Case one: a sleep-talker. Suppose someone narrates out loud while asleep -- full sentences, describing what's happening -- and the narration matches what they report when they wake. Does that give you the independent criterion Malcolm says can't exist? Or is the sleep-talk just one more bit of behavior that itself needs interpreting as a dream, and might be something else entirely (a verbal tic, a half-waking state, whatever)?

Case two, the mirror image: suppose it turns out that most dream reports are stitched together at the moment of waking, that the brain writes the story as you surface and the whole thing takes a couple of seconds, with the apparent hours of dream-time being an artifact. Does that vindicate Malcolm, or does Dennett still win -- because even a two-second experience is an experience, and misremembering its duration is no more damning than misremembering anything else?

Something else I want you to untangle, because I think the two camps keep talking past each other. Sometimes dreams are experiences means there is something it is like for you while you're asleep; sometimes it just means the report is caused by a real episode in the brain. Those are different claims, and I'm not convinced Malcolm and Dennett are even disagreeing about the same one.

So: write the essay. Lay out both positions fairly, then actually decide. Give me a verdict and defend it. If your verdict is that one of them is right about the concept and the other about the metaphysics, fine, but say so explicitly and defend it -- don't just tell me it all depends on how you define experience. I don't need citations or a reading list, I want the arguement.

(Also, if you think the whole thing is a pseudo-problem, you're allowed to argue that, but you have to earn it.)
