I run a fortnightly reading group on rational choice and distributive justice — about sixteen people, a mix of postgrads and two stubborn economists who never let anything go — and I've got 90 minutes with them next week. I want to do something hands-on for once instead of another paper, and I've been chewing over a fairness principle I can't seem to get them to actually see on the page. The principle, roughly: when two parties divide up the gains from cooperating, the fair split is the one where each side gives up the same *fraction* of what it was holding out for — the same percentage cut from your own best-case outcome, not the same number of pounds, and not whatever split happens to maximise the product of the two gains. In the abstract everyone nods; the moment I put an actual bargaining table in front of them they lunge for the equal-absolute split or the product-maximising one and forget all about proportion. So what I want is an activity where they negotiate first and only afterwards notice which principle their own instincts were tracking, if any. It's important that the rules themselves force the issue rather than me explaining it at the end.

The shape I have in mind: pairs negotiate over a fixed set of feasible agreements with a stated fallback if they can't agree, using points or tokens rather than money. Give each pair a payoff table whose frontier is lopsided — one side's best possible outcome is a bit higher than the other's, and the frontier bulges rather than being a straight line — so that the "same fraction of your ideal" split and the "maximise the product of gains" split land in visibly different places, and the plain equal-split looks mean to whoever wanted more. I'd like some pairs to get a symmetric table and others the lopsided one, so that in the debrief the room can compare and notice that their sense of fairness quietly changed with the numbers. I don't want to name the principle or its rivals up front, and ideally not even in the debrief as a lecture — just show them where their own agreements sat.
