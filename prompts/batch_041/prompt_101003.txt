You are a philosopher of biology with deep expertise in the units-of-selection debate — you know the gene-centric and multilevel-selection literatures, and you are fluent in the conceptual machinery at issue: replicators and interactors, the distinction between selection of and selection for, the MLS1/MLS2 contrast, contextual analysis and the Price partition, and the long-running dispute over whether genic selectionism is a causal claim or merely a bookkeeping convenience.

I host a small podcast on philosophy of science, and next month's guest is a philosopher of biology who has staked out a strongly deflationary position: she argues that gene-level and group-level selection are not rival hypotheses about what actually does the selecting, but two descriptions of a single causal process — so the units-of-selection controversy, on her view, is largely verbal, and the only genuine disagreement is about which description is more tractable or explanatory. She is quick to say of any alleged group-level adaptation that it can be redescribed gene-wise, and equally quick to say of any gene-centric story that it can be redescribed at the level of interactors.

Please draft nine interview questions that go well beyond having her restate the position, and for each one supply a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I want the sequence to press exactly where the deflationary line is under the most strain, so that her signature move — redescribe the dispute as one process under two descriptions — cannot keep absorbing each new objection. I specifically need:

- one that forces her to say what, if anything, would count as evidence that the level of selection makes a causal difference rather than a merely descriptive one;
- one on the averaging fallacy, and why critics think the deflationist conflates selection at one level with a mere aggregation of selection at another;
- one on the MLS1/MLS2 distinction, and whether her position can accommodate it without collapsing into one or the other;
- one on the replicator/interactor distinction, asking whether her two-descriptions view leaves interactors any causal work to do at all;
- one that draws out the consequences for a contested case — the evolution of altruism and eusociality — where the choice of level is supposed to matter for explanation and prediction, not just for accounting;
- one on what she takes the disagreement between gene selectionism and multilevel selection to be about, if not about which level really does the selecting;
- one that asks whether her view is a form of pluralism that leaves the science unchanged, or a stronger claim that the whole debate has been a mistake;
- and a closing question about what a young researcher should do differently if she is right.

Begin by brainstorming for yourself, briefly, about where the deflationary position is strongest and where it is most vulnerable, then write the questions. Do not include generic questions about how she came to the topic, and do not ask her simply to summarize her book. Each question should be answerable only by someone holding this specific view, and each follow-up should anticipate the actual move she would make rather than a strawman. Write in American English, number the questions, and keep each to a few sentences.
