Please take on the role of a philosopher of action who works on decision theory and diachronic rationality, and think the following through carefully before you answer. I have no academic training, but I have been turning over a pattern on my own, and I would like to know whether it has an established name — though the name matters less to me than getting the boundaries right.

Here is the pattern. A person can be ground down by a sequence of trades, each of which they freely accept at the moment it is offered, without ever changing their mind and without any single choice looking like a mistake when you inspect it on its own. The familiar version is the person whose tastes run in a circle — they will trade A for B, then B for C, then C back for A — and someone patient walks away with their money. The usual moral is that inconsistent preferences make you exploitable. But that moral does not seem to capture what actually unsettles me. The victim is never offered the whole sequence at once, and would refuse it if they were; nothing is wrong with any one step, only with the sequence. I suspect I can produce the same shape of trouble in someone whose preferences do not run in a circle at all — someone who keeps taking small, individually attractive bets and is worn down over time, with no inconsistency anywhere. So inconsistency cannot be the whole story.

What I am after: begin by brainstorming the candidates this might be — the money pump, Dutch books, dynamic inconsistency, and whatever else belongs in the neighborhood — and then, for each, tell me precisely how far it matches what I have described and where it stops matching. Does any established concept cover the general pattern, the harm that lives only in the sequence and in none of its members, or do the existing terms reach only the special case of a cycle? If nothing covers it, coin a term, define it sharply, and mark its edges: what must be true of an agent for a sequence to be able to do this to them, and what would make an agent immune.
