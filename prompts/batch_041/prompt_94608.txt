You are a philosopher of mind with specialist knowledge of Advaita Vedanta and of the philosophical writings of Erwin Schrödinger, and you are practised at reading literary fiction in a way that is faithful both to the text and to the philosophy. I would like a critical reading of Andy Weir's short story "The Egg" (first published in 2009) through the lens of Schrödinger's Vedanta-inflected views on the unity of consciousness.

Some background, so that we are working from the same materials. In "The Egg", a forty-eight-year-old man dies in a car crash and meets a being who tells him that he has already lived many lives and will live many more; that every human being who has ever lived or will ever live is an incarnation of the same single subject; that the universe was made as an egg in which that subject is to grow; and that once the last human life has been lived, the subject will be born as a god. Schrödinger, in his late philosophical writing — the 1956 lecture "Mind and Matter" is the clearest case — argued, under an acknowledged debt to the Upanishads, that consciousness is never encountered in the plural: each of us can say "I" and mean something that is in no way multiple, and the apparent plurality of minds is a paradox to be dissolved rather than a datum to be explained. He takes the Upanishadic identification of the individual self with the ground of all being as a serious metaphysical proposal, not as devotional poetry.

I want the following, in this order.

First, a short brainstorming section in which you set out two or three rival readings of the story before committing to one. Say plainly which you are rejecting and why.

Second, the reading proper. Where does Weir dramatise Schrödinger's insight, and where does the story's machinery work against it? Press especially on the following. The story supplies a mechanism — one soul taking turns, with memory veiled between lives — where Schrödinger offers something closer to a claim about the grammar of first-person experience. Does the mechanism make the unity easier to imagine at the cost of making it false to the philosophy? Is a single subject running sequentially through many lives really the same claim as the Upanishadic one, or has the story quietly reinstated the very plurality it means to deny, merely relocating it to different times? Consider whether the veiling of memory is doing essential work or is an evasion.

Third, the ethical turn. The being's lesson is that in harming another you harm yourself, and in being kind to another you are kind to yourself. Is this a genuine consequence of non-dualism, or does it collapse into an unusually well-informed self-interest? What would the difference look like in the story's own terms, and does the story have the resources to mark it?

Fourth, the cosmology. The story ends with the protagonist becoming a god among other godlike beings, and the being itself is one of a plurality. What does this do to the monism the story has been trading on? Is the plurality of gods a leftover the story cannot absorb, or does it point to something the philosophy also has trouble stating?

Fifth, the counter-reading. Where does the story resist the Schrödinger reading, and what should the honest critic concede? Might it be closer to a different position — for instance the view that there is one numerically identical subject who is everyone at all times, or the more popular doctrine of rebirth, or, contrarily, a Buddhist no-self view for which the whole apparatus of one soul and many lives is a mistake? Which of these does the story actually enact, as distinct from what it says?

Finally, say what the story adds that the philosophy alone does not — what a narrative can show about the unity of consciousness that an argument cannot — and be honest if your conclusion is that it adds nothing and that this is a case where fiction diminishes the idea.

Use headings and write in continuous prose; avoid bullet-point lists except where a list is genuinely the clearest form. Do not quote the story verbatim: paraphrase scenes in your own words, since I want your reasoning rather than your recall. Do not supply citations, page references or a bibliography. Write in British English. Think carefully before you begin; the obvious reading here is probably too quick, and I would rather have a reading that strains against the story than one that flatters it.
