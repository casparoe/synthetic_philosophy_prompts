I'm hoping you can help me think through something that has been nagging at me for a week, and I'd rather have a careful, unhurried answer than a quick verdict. My closest friend and I have argued for years about how a person ought to spend their spare time and money. She is a tenant organizer and is convinced that the most urgent thing either of us could do with twenty hours a month is to knock on doors for a housing campaign in our city; she regards the animal-welfare charity I give to and volunteer for as a well-meaning distraction for people who would rather feel good than change anything. I hold roughly the mirror-image view: I think her campaign is, at best, a rounding error and, at worst, actively harmful to people I care about, while the animal work is the clearest case of real good available to either of us. We have never been able to talk each other out of these positions, and we have both stopped trying.

Last week she proposed something I cannot get out of my head. Instead of continuing to argue, she said, we should simply trade. For the next six months, I would take her Saturday canvassing shifts and she would take my Saturday shelter shifts. Her argument is that this is a pure improvement: each of us would be doing more of what the other already thinks matters most, so by each of our own lights the world comes out better than it does now, nobody has to pretend to have changed their mind, and nothing is lost. She has even sketched a version of it for our whole friend group, a sort of standing swap in which each of us periodically takes on someone else's least favourite form of do-gooding in exchange for someone taking on ours. Part of me thinks she is obviously right, and part of me recoils, and I can't put my finger on why. It isn't that anyone would be deceived — the campaign coordinator knows perfectly well that I am a body with a free Saturday, not a convert. It is more that I would be spending my one life's effort on something I believe is close to worthless and possibly worse than worthless, and that no one is making me do it. Yet when I ask what refusing would accomplish, I get nowhere: if I say no, she canvasses and I do shelter shifts, which is exactly the situation I am already in. So I want to know whether my reluctance is just sentimentality about my own clean hands, or whether there is a genuine reason to decline — and, if there is, whether it survives the fact that saying no changes nothing. Please tell me what, if anything, is wrong with her proposal, and what I should actually do.
