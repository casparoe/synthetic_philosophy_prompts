Right, so I'm putting together a session on infinity for a reading group and I need a hand sorting out the usual responses to Hilbert's hotel. The setup, in case it helps: an infinite hotel, every room already occupied, a new guest turns up, the manager shifts everyone up one room, room 1 is free. Then infinitely many new guests, then infinitely many buses each carrying infinitely many passengers, and so on. Nothing breaks, so the usual moral is that the whole-is-greater-than-the-part principle just fails for infinite collections and our size intuitions are parochial.

What I want from you is a tier list of the following six reactions to that puzzle, ranked by how much genuine philosophical work each one actually does. Not by whether you agree with it, but by how much it earns its keep as a response. Stick them in tiers (S/A/B/C/D, whatever you like) and give me a sentence or two on why each lands where it does. Also tell me which one you'd put dead last even though it's popular.

1. The finitist/ultrafinitist line: the hotel is a fiction, there are no completed infinite collections, so no conclusion follows about anything real.

2. The Aristotelian move: the hotel only ever gives you a procedure that can be carried out step by step, never a completed infinite totality, so the state where all rooms are full and yet there's a vacancy is never actually reached.

3. The structuralist move: room numbers are labels, not furniture. Reassigning them doesn't change the hotel, so the appearance of paradox is an artefact of treating the labels as part of the thing labelled.

4. The identity/haecceity move: each guest just is the occupant of a given room, so shifting everyone up one means they're now different guests. There's no fact of the matter about the same person having been moved.

5. The sizes-are-comparative-only move: cardinality isn't a measure of size, it's a measure of matchability. Euclid's principle that the whole exceeds the part is about measure, not about matching, and the two can coexist without conflict.

6. The deflationary move: there's no paradox here at all, just a bad intuition about what more means for infinite sets; the puzzle only has force for people who never did the maths.

A couple of those entries are my own framing and I'm not sure they map cleanly onto anything in the litrature, so don't worry about naming sources or giving me citations — I just want to know which of them pull their weight and which are just wordplay. Write it in British English please. Cheers.
