I'm a novelist — third book just went off to my editor, so naturally instead of starting the fourth I'm having a slow-motion philosophical crisis about the whole racket. I keep running into the old line that history gets written by whoever won, and I want you to do one very specific thing for me. Take that entire debate — whether the record of the past is just the winning side's story, whether there's any honest sense in which the losers' version of events is recoverable, all of it — and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually think in. I mean narratology and craft: focalization, who gets to be the narrating I, unreliable narration, free indirect style, the implied author, the difference between the story and the discourse that tells it, dramatic irony, the reader's reconstruction of what a character wont or can't say out loud. Dont water it down into a loose analogy — do a real point-by-point translation, and flag where you had to fudge.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue report. What in the historians' and philosophers' version of this argument has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? I have a hunch it's something to do with evidence — that a novel's narrator can be as biased or as blinkered as she likes because nothing outside the text pushes back, whereas a historian is stuck with an archive that will only let him tell certain stories no matter how good his prose is. Is that hunch right, or is it exactly the kind of thing a novelist tells himself so he doesn't have to take his own narrator problem seriously? And tell me straight whether the residue is a hole in my framework or a sign that the original debate is vaguer than it sounds. Definately be honest about the seams.
