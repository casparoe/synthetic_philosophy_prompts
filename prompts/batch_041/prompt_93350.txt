The principle, as it was put to me, is this. If I am now certain that at some later time my credence in A will be p, then my credence in A now should be p; or, conditionally, P(A | P_future(A) = p) = p. Its corollary is that my present credence should equal my present expectation of my future credence. It was presented as a requirement of rationality and motivated by something in the family of sure-loss arguments — that whoever violates it can be bet against in such a way as to lose whatever happens. The objection I was also given is that it fails whenever the future me is not to be trusted: I may know that I shall be swayed by a bad argument, or worn down, or drugged.

What I want from you is a systematic translation of the principle, its motivation, and the standard objection into my vocabulary — using the real machinery of conditional expectation where that fits and the machinery of prices, arbitrage and retraining where that fits — followed by the residue report. In particular:

1. Whether the principle, once translated, turns out to be a theorem rather than a requirement. My instinct is that the version saying my present price should equal my expected future price is just the tower property, hence automatic for anything that deserves the name of a credence, and that all the content therefore sits in the conditional version. If that is right, tell me what the conditional version does that the tower property does not, and whether my vocabulary can even mark the difference.
2. What becomes of the 'should'. In my world a price is either exploitable or it is not; the only failure mode I recognise is a sure loss, and 'irrational' means 'arbitrageable'. The principle looks as though it is making a claim about how a belief ought to answer to evidence rather than about what pays. Does my vocabulary have any counterpart to that claim, or does the normative force simply drop out?
3. What becomes of the self-doubt cases. Put in my terms: if I condition on my future self's forecast being p, and that forecast is itself evidence that my pipeline is broken or my judgement compromised, why should I adopt p? If the answer is that I should not, say whether that shows the principle was too strong or merely that it was never meant to cover cases where the future forecast is informative about the forecaster.
4. Whether 'my future self' survives at all. In my vocabulary there is no special relation between me and my later forecast beyond shared training history; deference to a later forecast looks like deference to a better-informed colleague, and is conditional on their competence, whereas the principle appears to demand it unconditionally. Tell me whether that is a defect of my vocabulary — it cannot express the continuity that makes the principle plausible — or a sign that the principle smuggles in an assumption.
