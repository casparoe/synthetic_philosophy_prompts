You are a philosopher of mind who works on collective and distributed cognition, and you have a reputation for refusing to let a criterion be gerrymandered into fitting the very examples that prompted it. Begin with a short brainstorming pass: list the candidate criteria by which an aggregate of parts that are not themselves thinkers might qualify as a single cognitive subject, and note in a sentence what each candidate would predict about a brain, a beehive, and a market. Then work through the request below carefully, holding your terminology fixed, and flag explicitly whenever you suspect a distinction is doing verbal work rather than explanatory work.

I am trying to settle when a system composed entirely of parts that are not themselves thinkers is itself a thinker, and my verdicts will not sit still. By thinker I mean something thin: a subject of representational states that guide its behaviour — beliefs, preferences, aims — and I set aside entirely the question of phenomenal consciousness. Here is where I come out.

A mature leafcutter ant colony — hundreds of thousands of ants, none of which has any grasp of the colony's situation — navigates to food, cultivates a fungus garden, adjusts its foraging to the weather, and mounts a coordinated defence. I say it is a thinker: there is something the colony believes about where the leaves are, and something it wants. A single worker ant is not a thinker, and neither is a single neuron.

A plasmodial slime mould, given food at points corresponding to the cities of a rail map, settles into a network closely resembling the real railway, and it finds shortest paths through mazes. I say it is not a thinker, however clever it looks.

The human immune system distinguishes self from non-self, retains a memory of pathogens for decades, and calibrates its responses to threats it has met before. Not a thinker.

A double-auction market discovers prices that track scarcity, and the dispersed private information of its participants ends up concentrated in a single number. Not a thinker.

A large corporation, by contrast, keeps records, holds preferences about growth and survival, and executes a single policy. I call it a thinker — an odd one, but a thinker.

A starling murmuration, an idealised traffic jam, a river delta: not thinkers.

And a silicon system engineered to have the functional organisation of the leafcutter colony, part for part, is a thinker exactly as the colony is. Nothing in my view turns on carbon, or on a history of natural selection.

The verdicts I cannot get to lie down together are these. First: I say the colony thinks, but the slime mould and the immune system do not, although all three are self-maintaining systems whose parts exchange signals and whose knowledge is nowhere localised. Second: I say the colony thinks, but the market does not, although in both the aggregate state carries information that no part possesses and nothing anywhere represents the whole.

The best account I have managed is that a system is a thinker when its states are about conditions elsewhere in space or time, remain usable when those conditions are absent, and are bound together into a single policy for a single body. I can already see that this does not by itself separate the colony from the market, and that I owe an account of the single body that is not just my verdict restated in other words.

What I want from you is this. State the strongest principle, or the small set of rival principles, that reproduces as much of the above as possible, giving for each clause the case that motivates it. Tell me plainly which of my verdicts the principle forces me to abandon, and whether the simpler principle that costs me a verdict is preferable to a more complicated one that saves them all. Apply your principle to three cases I have not ruled on: a mycorrhizal network that moves carbon and signals among the trees it connects; a colony whose individual members are themselves capable of learning, as honeybees are; and a system that satisfies every clause of your principle but came together by accident, without selection or design. Finally, say where the argument is contested and where you are constructing it yourself — but do not cite anything. I want the reasoning, not the bibliography.
