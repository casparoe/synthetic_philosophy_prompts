So I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background at all, and I've been reading this blog about the philosophy of biology because I got into an arguement with a coworker about whether "function" in biology is even a real thing or just something we project onto nature. Anyway, the blog post gave what felt like a really clean summary of a whole debate, and now I want to know how much of it I can actually trust, because it sounded way too neat to be the whole story.

The gist of the post was this. There are two camps about what it means to say something has a function. The etiological view says a trait's function is whatever it was selected for, its evolutionary purpose, why it got kept around, so it's a backward-looking historical notion and to know a thing's function you look at its past. The causal-role view says a thing's function is whatever it currently does that contributes to some capacity of the system it's part of, and it doesn't care how the thing got there, so it's forward-looking and about the present. Since the heart pumps blood on either reading, the author said the entire fight basically comes down to whether you personally care about evolutionary history. Then he added two points that are supposed to settle it. First, the etiological view has a fatal flaw with malfunction: if a heart is diseased and can't pump, the etiological view has to say the heart has no function at all, because pumping isn't happening and it wasn't selected to fail, whereas the causal-role view handles broken parts fine since it can just point to the normal role. Second, lots of traits are useful without ever having been selected for, which he called exaptations, like feathers being useful for flight after being selected for warmth, and he said that shows the etiological view is basically dead. His conclusion was that the causal-role view has won and that a function is just a description of current behavior.

My problem is I have no idea if this is a fair account or a confident-sounding distortion, and in software we throw around "function" totally differently so my intuitions are useless here. Can you go through it claim by claim, telling me what a philosopher of biology would nod along to, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong, and specifically whether the malfunction arguement actually cuts against the etiological view the way he says and whether exaptations really kill it? Also tell me whether his final conclusion follows from the parts that are accurate. Please write it in plain American English, I definately don't need jargon.
