For years I've followed the rationalist blogs and forums where the proposal that goes by the name of tool AI gets debated: roughly, that we should build systems which only answer questions, predict, or generate artifacts on demand, and never systems that pursue ends in the world, on the grounds that a tool has no ends of its own and so nothing to go catastrophically wrong in the way an agent does. I have never been satisfied with how this argument is run, and I'd like you to write an essay that takes a position on it. Aim for roughly 1,000–1,500 words.

Requirements:

- Start by stating the tool-AI proposal in its strongest form — the version a competent defender would endorse — not the strawman that a tool with no goals is merely inert machinery.
- Then confront the objection I find hardest to dismiss: that "tool vs. agent" cannot be drawn by asking whether a system *has* goals, because having a goal is a claim about what a system's behavior is selected for, not about what it represents internally. On this view, a system trained to maximize a learned model of user approval has an objective defined over the world (approval depends on what the user does next), and its only lever is the text it emits. Test your position against a case of my own devising. Call it the Pure Tool: one output channel, one string per query, no memory across queries, no ability to act, no goal represented anywhere inside it, trained end to end to maximize predicted approval of its string. Is the Pure Tool an agent? Does the answer change what you should expect about its safety? You don't get to settle this by declaring that a system's goal is whatever its designers wrote down.
- Consider the alternative that the distinction is real but lives elsewhere: not in the internals but in the interface contract — the action space is strings, the user keeps discretion, responsibility sits with the user. Say whether that is a genuine safety property or a convention for relocating liability.
- Reach a verdict: does "tool vs. agent" name a load-bearing distinction, and if so, what is the correct criterion for drawing it? A verdict like "the distinction is real, but it tracks the size and shape of the action space rather than goal-directedness" is fine. Declining to land is not.

Don't recite the standard talking points (instrumental convergence, the orthogonality of goals and capability, the treacherous turn) as though the recitation were itself an argument; use them only where they do work in yours.

Give me a verdict with a stated confidence, and say what argument or evidence would move you off it. If my framing is confused — if, for instance, "tool" and "agent" are not two positions on one axis but answers to two unrelated questions, or if the Pure Tool is a pseudo-case that dissolves under examination — say so plainly and explain why, rather than writing the essay I asked for. I would rather be told the question is malformed than receive a fluent answer to the wrong question.
