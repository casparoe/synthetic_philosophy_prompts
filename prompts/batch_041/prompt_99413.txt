I retired a few years back after a long career as a field ecologist, and since then I've been trying to teach myself philosophy, which is humbling but alot of fun. Some friends talked me into hosting a tiny interview podcast about big ideas, and the next guest is a scholar who's spent years arguing that Malthus got a raw deal — that the famous prediction about population outrunning the food supply wasnt wrong, just early, and that we should stop treating it as the textbook case of a refuted theory. I want to push him hard without being rude, and I want the questions to actually engage his position rather than let him recite the usual defense. Can you draft eight to ten questions, plus a follow-up for each thats keyed to the answer hes most likely to give?

A few things I specifically want covered. One question on what exactly "early" is supposed to mean here and how it differs from "unfalsifiable" — I want him to say what his theory forbids, and roughly when we'd be entitled to conclude that the forbidding never happened. One on the auxiliary-hypothesis move: once the industrial revolution and the demographic transition showed up, why is patching the theory respectable here when patching phlogiston or the ether wasnt, and does he think there's a principled difference or just a difference in how much we like where the patched theory lands. One that presses the point that Malthus himself had preventive checks built in, so falling fertility can look like the mechanism working rather than a refutation — ask him how a theory that can absorb both the prediction and its failure is supposed to be tested, then give me the follow-up for whatever he says. One on the counterfactual he probably leans on, something like "absent fossil fuels he'd have been right," and whether a counterfactual we can't run is doing any real evidential work or is just a way of keeping the theory in the game. And one from my old side of the fence: as an ecologist I always suspected the geometric-versus-arithmetic business was really just a claim about a population near its carrying capacity, so I'd like a question on whether his Malthus is a claim about ecological dynamics, or about a tendency that can be indefinitely held off, or about a conditional whose antecedent — no preventive check, no technological escape — may simply never obtain, and what the content of such a conditional even is.

Also, for at least two of the questions, tell me where I should expect him to equivocate or slide between a claim about a force and a claim about an outcome, and hand me the follow-up that would catch it. And if there's a spot where the honest answer is basically "thats a research program," flag it so I'm not sitting there flat-footed.
