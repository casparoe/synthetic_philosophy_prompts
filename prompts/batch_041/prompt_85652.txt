I have no philosophical training, and what follows comes from a few years of reading popular books about decision-making and from repeatedly failing to keep my own resolutions, so I would be grateful if you would check the package for consistency rather than summarise what other people have said about these questions. I am not after a reading list; I want to know what follows from what I have said.

Here is roughly where I have ended up. First, the rationality of a choice is settled at the moment of choosing, by the aims and the information the chooser has at that moment; if I accept a deal because I then want it more than what I am giving up, I do not believe there is any further fact about what I "really" want that a bystander could use to show that I made a mistake. Second, I think a person whose preferences run in a small circle — who will trade a cup of coffee for a cup of tea if you add a penny, tea for water with another penny, and water back for coffee with a third — is defective in a way that matters; I have seen this described as a money pump, and I do not think the loss is a mere curiosity. Third, I do not think a person's preferences have to be stable in order to be rational; that I wanted one thing at twenty and something else at forty, and will want something else again at sixty, does not by itself make me inconsistent or defective, because changing your mind is not a fault. Fourth, I think a resolution made in advance can bind me: if I give a friend fifty pounds and tell her to hand it back only once the work is finished, then when the deadline arrives and I would far rather have the money, I think finishing the work is what I ought to do, and that taking the money and abandoning the work would be irrational rather than merely weak. Fifth, I do not think there is any moment in my life that is the real me; my aims at each time count for what they are, and no time's aims count for more than any other time's. Sixth, I think a person who is led, one bearable step at a time, into a state they would never have chosen at the outset — where no single step is the step at which it goes wrong, because each step on its own is worth taking — has made a mistake, and that "every step was fine when I took it" is not a defence of having gone the whole way.

I suspect these cannot all be true together, but I cannot tell which of them are genuinely pulling against each other and which only look as though they are. Please tell me, in order of how serious the conflict is, which of my commitments cannot be held together, what would have to give in each case, and what the cheapest repair would be; and if a single change would clear up the most trouble, say which one. I would also like to know which of the apparent conflicts are not real conflicts at all, and why not. Please write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter — no headings, no numbered lists, no bold, and no tables.
