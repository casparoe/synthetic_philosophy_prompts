I've been having the same argument with my flatmate for about a year now and I can't work out what we're actaully disagreeing about, which is starting to bother me more than the disagreement itself. She's midway through an MA in philosophy, I did a conversion course and now work in local government, so neither of us is an expert and we've both read just enough to be dangerous. Her view is what she calls suffering-focused: suffering isn't merely weightier than pleasure, it's categorically prior to it — no amount of anyone's happiness can buy off a fixed quantity of agony, and she'll bite the bullets, e.g. that a world containing enormous numbers of lives barely worth living is worse than an empty world, and that if there were a button that instantly and painlessly ended all life, wiping out every future instance of suffering along with every future instance of joy, the fact that the suffering stops would be a reason to press it, even if other reasons might outweigh it. I hold the boring view: suffering is very bad, plausibly worse than pleasure is good, and that definately justifies taking animal suffering and the worst human deprivations far more seriously than we do, but none of it requires the lexical bit or the claim that suffering is the only intrinsic disvalue. We agree about the facts, we agree that factory farming is monstrous, and we've gone round the same loop about forty times.

My three ways of making sense of it are these. (1) It's partly verbal: she means by 'suffering' something like 'experience that is intrinsically bad for the one having it', so it follows trivially that it's the only thing that matters intrinsically — the axiology is packed into the vocabulary, and my 'pleasure' is the same kind of thing with the sign flipped, so we may not even be disagreeing about a subject matter. (2) It's a real but unresolvable clash about the shape of the value function — bounded versus unbounded disvalue, additive versus lexical priority — where neither of us can argue for our starting intuition, because every argument would appeal to the intuitions in dispute, so the stall is bedrock and I should stop expecting movement. (3) We're answering different questions under one label: she's making an axiological claim about what's intrinsically bad, I'm making a claim about where scarce money and attention should go, and 'negative utilitarianism' gets used for both, which would explain why all our empirical agreements shift nothing. There's a fourth possibility I keep half-seeing: she says things like 'a life with more suffering than happiness in it is a bad life, full stop', which sounds like a claim about lifetime wellbeing rather than about sums of moments, and I suspect that's doing more work in her position than either of us has noticed.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? I want a diagnosis built from what each of us is committed to, not a survey of the field, and an honest answer about what would count as progress — a distinction that dissolves it, a formal result about which value functions are coherent, a way of testing the intuitions against something other than each other, or just accepting that it's bedrock and saying why. And tell me whether the label itself is doing damage by dressing up a practical priority as an axiology. Please don't give me reading lists or citations — I'm not near a library and half of it wouldn't be online anyway — and write in British English.
