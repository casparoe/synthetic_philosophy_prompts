Write a short story of roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words for an anthology I am assembling on the moral grammar of speech. Each contributor has been asked to stage a single conceptual puzzle as fiction rather than to illustrate a familiar moral lesson, and yours is the alleged asymmetry between lying and misleading: the thought that a false assertion wrongs the person who trusts it in a way that a true but carefully engineered statement does not.

Set the story in a society whose public morality treats that asymmetry as bedrock. Its central prohibition, enforced not by courts but by a standing body of examiners, forbids uttering a false declarative sentence in any official proceeding, while saying nothing whatever about what a speaker may imply, suggest, or leave unsaid. In consequence a licensed guild, the Order of Plain Speech, has arisen, whose members train for years in the discipline of uttering only truths while steering their hearers toward false beliefs. They are not regarded as deceivers; they are regarded as the most scrupulous speakers in the society, and their scruple is precisely what makes them useful to clients who need a damaging fact to go unlearned without anyone being able to say that a lie was told.

Tell the story from inside that guild. Let the narrator be a junior member undergoing examination for advancement, or a senior member conducting an inquiry into a colleague. The story must contain three things: an exchange in which every sentence the speaker utters is, taken literally, true, and in which the hearer nonetheless comes away with a belief the speaker intended him to have; a rendering of that hearer's reliance vivid enough that the reader feels what he was owed, whether or not the speaker owed it; and a revelation, late in the story, that the guild's own founding charter was secured by a statement of exactly this kind.

The story must not settle the puzzle by having any character announce the distinction, and it must not resolve it by making the guild simply villainous or simply admirable. Leave the reader able to argue either that the guild's practice is a distinct wrong, a breach of a guarantee that assertion carries and implication does not, or that the distinction is a verbal trick that launders one betrayal under the name of another. Close on an image rather than a verdict. Do not refer to real philosophers or real legal cases; the society's own institutions should carry the whole weight of the argument.
