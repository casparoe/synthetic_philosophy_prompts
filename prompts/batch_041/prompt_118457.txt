A colleague in our law-and-philosophy reading group keeps using a photographic analogy to explain why vague terms leave borderline cases open, and I want to know whether the analogy can bear the weight she puts on it. Her line is this: a photograph taken slightly out of focus has no sharp edge anywhere in it, since the boundary between the subject and the background is a smear. That, she says, is what vagueness is like. There is no fact of the matter about exactly where the boundary lies, and it is not merely that we are badly placed to see a line that is really there. She adds that no admissible way of sharpening the term would be more correct than any other, and she draws a legal conclusion from all this: when a statute uses a vague predicate and a case falls in the smear, the judge who decides it is not applying a rule the law already contained but drawing a line the law left undrawn, and so is legislating. A friend who holds the rival view — epistemicism, on which a vague predicate has a sharp but unknowable cutoff, so that heap is either true or false of any given number of grains even though no one can say which — insists the photograph shows the opposite of what she thinks. The blur, he says, is itself perfectly definite: a blurred edge has an exact luminance profile, and the blur is a fact about the lens rather than about the world. He takes the analogy to be his.

Please audit the analogy rather than adjudicate between the two friends. Set out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the lens, the subject, the smear, the print, and the viewer. Then say where the mapping breaks, and in particular which features of photographic blur are quietly imported that vagueness may not have. Is a photograph even the right kind of thing to be vague in the first place, given that it is a representation with a definite content rather than a predicate with a use? Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the use she is making of the analogy from those that do not. Tell me how the analogy fares on each of the two views, and whether the legal conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If a better analogy is available for the point she is trying to make, give me that one too, and say what it buys and what it costs. Please write your answer in American English.
