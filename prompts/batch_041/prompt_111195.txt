For the past fourteen months I have chaired the allocations committee of a small charitable foundation, and I have gradually come to think that the way we make grants is not merely imprecise but confused. The procedure is uniform: for each proposal we write down a probability of success, a value conditional on success, and a cost, and we fund in descending order of value per dollar. Two months ago we had to choose between two proposals of nearly identical cost, and I argued for the second. The first was an intervention with good trial evidence whose effects we can measure within five years. The second was an attempt to change the long-run trajectory of a large and durable institution: its theory of change runs through decisions that will be made by people who are not yet born, and on reflection our estimate of its probability of success, and of its value if it succeeds, were not rough measurements of anything but numbers we produced because the form demanded a number. What I have come to believe is that the difficulty here is not our ignorance. The long-run consequences of everything we fund are so sensitive to downstream contingencies, and the relevant outcome space is so ill-defined, that it is doubtful whether a determinate expected value attaches to the second proposal at all, and doubtful in the same way, though less visibly, for the first.

Please set out the most important implications of this for how the committee should actually operate. Rank them by how much they should change our practice rather than by how interesting they are, make the reasoning explicit at each step, and tell me which of them I should decline to act on. Four questions matter most to me. Does the argument give us any reason to prefer the proposal whose effects we can measure, or does it bear equally on every option we could fund and so fail to guide us at all? Does it tell against the rubric itself, or only against the confidence with which we fill it in? If we fall back on our best guesses while labelling them as guesses, are we being honest about the limits of our knowledge, or smuggling the discredited rubric back in through the side door? And what is the strongest case that the whole difficulty dissolves once the decision problem is specified carefully, so that our ordinary procedure was never in trouble? The committee is practical and unsentimental and will want to know what changes on Monday morning.
