I am a science journalist at work on a long feature about the biodiversity crisis, and a philosopher I interviewed for it has left me with an argument I have not been able to shake, chiefly because I cannot find the loose joint. He put it as follows.

(1) A category of things in nature is real only if the world itself settles what falls under it — that is, only if there are conditions for membership that hold independently of any classifier's interests, purposes, or choices.
(2) For every criterion biologists have proposed for species membership — reproductive isolation, monophyly, a distinctive ecological niche, consistent diagnosability — there are organisms for which the criterion yields no verdict, or the wrong verdict, or a verdict that conflicts with the verdict another criterion gives: the ring species whose populations intergrade the whole way around, the asexual lineages that never recombine, the hybrid zones, the cryptic complexes that are reproductively separate yet nearly indistinguishable.
(3) So for every species concept there is some case it cannot handle.
(4) So there is no case of species classification that the world settles; the boundaries are drawn by us and not found by us.
(5) If the boundaries between species are drawn by us, then so are the boundaries of the higher taxa assembled out of them, and so is any count of how many kinds of living things there are.
(6) So there is no fact of the matter about how much biodiversity exists, and none about whether it is being lost — only facts about what we have chosen to count.

He regards (6) as the honest upshot of taking the species problem seriously, and thinks conservation biologists who flinch from it are in denial. I think (6) is false — that the world's variety of living things is shrinking seems to me as solid a fact as any in biology — but I cannot tell whether I am rejecting a premise or missing a step, and what unsettles me further is that nothing in the argument appears to be specific to species: if it works, it looks as though it would work against any category biology counts with. If I had to choose I would keep (6) and surrender (2), but I would like to see the bill before I pay it. Tell me which step to give up, why it looked sound, and what the alternatives cost.

Give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion.
