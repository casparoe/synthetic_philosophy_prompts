Right, I need a poem for a seminar opener and I'd rather it actually did some work than just gestured at the idea.

The idea is the boundary problem in democratic theory: before the people can decide anything, something has to decide who the people are, and whatever decides that seems to need a demos of its own to authorise it. I want the poem to make that feel like a loop you're standing inside, not a paradox you can solve with a footnote.

Here's the form I want: a pantoum, four or five stanzas. You know the drill — the second and fourth lines of each stanza come back as the first and third lines of the next, and the last stanza folds into the first so the whole thing is a closed circuit. That repetition-with-shift is the point: the same words return but the 'we' saying them has quietly changed size, so the poem keeps restating its own ground rule and keeps failing to have stated it.

Content-wise, let it pass through three of the standard answers without ever labelling them: (a) anyone whose interests are affected by a decision should get a say; (b) only those actually bound by the coercion of the law should get to make it; (c) sod it, the boundry is drawn by whoever already has the power to draw it, and that's the only warrant there is. The pantoum should make the first two feel like they're answering a question that already presupposes the very demos they're trying to constitute — and (c) feel like the honest but ugly exit, not a punchline.

A few constraints. Don't name any of the positions or any philosopher. No preamble-style grandstanding about the people. Someone who teaches this stuff should be able to tell which stanza is which answer and why they can't be squared; someone who doesn't should still get a decent poem out of it. And leave it unresolved — the loop should close, but it shouldn't click shut. Length: whatever the form needs, but keep it tight enough to read aloud in about a minute.
