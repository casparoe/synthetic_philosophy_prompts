I teach in a PPE programme and came to philosophy from a doctorate in economics, so the apparatus I actually think in is mechanism design and contract theory: principals and agents, incentive compatibility, the revelation principle, renegotiation-proofness, subgame-perfect equilibrium, the time-inconsistency of optimal policy. I am fluent enough in that vocabulary to be dangerous, but I am not a working theorist, so do not assume I will catch a loose use of it.

There is a debate in constitutional theory that I have never seen done properly in my own terms. On one side, a constitution is defended as a Ulysses contract: the sovereign people, foreseeing that future majorities will be tempted to do things the founding generation regards as catastrophic — default on the debt, persecute a minority, dismantle the republic — tie their own hands in advance, and the entrenchment is justified precisely because it is hard to undo. On the other side stands the dead-hand objection and the democratic objection: a generation cannot bind its successors, and a document that does so substitutes the will of the dead for the will of the living. Threaded through both is the older puzzle of whether a collective can be a single agent across time at all, or whether the people at one moment and the people at a later moment are better modelled as distinct parties.

Restate this debate in the vocabulary of mechanism design and principal-agent theory as faithfully as you can. Then give me the residue report.

Structure the response in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. Framing (~150 words). State which objects in the constitutional debate you are treating as the principal, which as the agent, what plays the role of the contract, and what the objective function is. Flag any modelling choice you had to make before you could begin.

2. The Translation (~700–900 words). Restate the pro-entrenchment position and the dead-hand objection in mechanism-design terms. Use the actual machinery: the time-inconsistency of preferences and the value of commitment, incentive compatibility and self-enforcement, the revelation principle if it bears on whether a constitution can simply command, renegotiation-proofness, incomplete contracts, and the difference between modelling the polity as one long-lived agent with time-inconsistent preferences and modelling it as a sequence of distinct short-lived agents. Do not soften the translation to make the philosophy look nicer than it is.

3. Clean Transfers. What in the original debate maps onto the apparatus without loss. Be brief and concrete; one line per item.

4. Distorted Transfers. What can be carried over only by fudging, and what the fudge costs. Say explicitly where you were tempted to let the apparatus's assumptions do the work of an argument.

5. Untranslatable Residue. What has no counterpart at all in the vocabulary — claims, distinctions, or worries that simply cannot be stated in it. Include the ones that are not obviously about preferences or incentives.

6. Verdict on the Residue (~300 words). For the residue as a whole: is it a defect of the target vocabulary — something mechanism design is missing and ought to be able to express — or a sign that the original claim was less clear than it looked, and dissolves once you try to say it precisely? Where the residue is genuine, say what it is about the constitutional case that the apparatus structurally cannot represent. Where it is not, say which philosophical formulation was doing no work.

One constraint throughout: do not smuggle in the target framework's assumptions and call the result a translation. If a constitutional claim holds only because you have assumed a single long-lived principal with stable meta-preferences, say so at the point where you assume it.
