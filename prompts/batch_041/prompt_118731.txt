I teach an upper-level seminar on evidence and confirmation, and I am setting the take-home final. I want a single essay question on the raven paradox, and I need it to be unusable by a student who has merely memorized the standard discussion — who can recite the equivalence condition, the satisfaction criterion, and the line that a green apple barely matters, without being able to think. So I am anchoring the question to a case that is not in the literature, and I want the case to carry the weight rather than illustrate a conclusion the student already knows.

Act as a philosopher of science with expertise in confirmation theory and formal epistemology and with experience writing assessment at the advanced level. I want you to reason about the case before you write anything for students: in a scratchpad, set out the strongest Hempelian (instance-based) answer, the strongest likelihood-based answer, and the precise point at which they come apart on this case. Only then produce the deliverables. Do not let the standard green-apple or grue material be the centerpiece; those may appear only as contrasts.

Here is the case I want the question built around. You may sharpen or add detail, but keep its structure.

A regional ornithological atlas recruits volunteers to photograph birds over a single season. Each volunteer is assigned to one of two protocols and may not mix them. Protocol A, the black-bird count: submit every bird you judge to be black, with your best species identification. Protocol B, the non-black register: submit every bird you judge to be non-black, with your best species identification. The hypothesis of interest is that all ravens are black.

Three features of the case are meant to do work.

First, on the satisfaction criterion every entry in the non-black register is an instance of the contrapositive of the hypothesis and so counts as confirming it; the register therefore looks like a confirmation-generating machine. The project coordinator accordingly proposes to strengthen support for the hypothesis by adding ten thousand further non-black register entries.

Second, one Protocol B volunteer photographs an albino raven, judges it non-black, and, unsure of the species, files it as a large white corvid of unidentified species. The register thus contains a record that, described one way, is a confirming instance and, described another way, is a counterexample.

Third, Protocol B is in one respect a more sensitive test than Protocol A: a non-black raven is by definition non-black, so it would be collected by Protocol B and could not be collected by Protocol A. Protocol B, however, is structurally incapable of collecting positive instances, since no black raven will ever enter the register.

The question should force students to decide what exactly the non-black register confirms and how much; whether the coordinator's proposal is coherent; whether the case shows that confirmation attaches to descriptions of evidence rather than to objects; and whether the paradox is best treated as a defect in the satisfaction criterion, as a symptom of treating confirmation as a binary relation when it should be comparative, or as an artifact of an unstated sampling assumption. A good answer should also say what the strength of any confirmation depends on — the prior plausibility of the counterexample class and the coverage of the sampling design — rather than on the sheer number of instances.

Now write the following.

1. The exam question itself, exactly as I would hand it to students: one essay prompt with clearly lettered parts, answerable in about ninety minutes, stating the case in full, since students will not have seen it before. It must not ask for citations, reading, or historical attribution, and must be answerable from the case plus general philosophical competence.

2. A model answer of 700 to 900 words of the kind a very strong student would produce. It must be self-contained and must not depend on knowing particular works, titles, or dates. It should make the crux visible: the difference between an instance-based reading and a likelihood-based reading, and the way the protocol fixes which observations were even possible. It should treat the albino raven carefully rather than gesturing at it.

3. A marking scheme worth 25 marks, broken into must-hit points with the marks each is worth, and, for each point, a one-line note on how students typically get it wrong. The scheme should separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain. Include the characteristic errors of students who have memorized the raven literature but cannot use it — for instance, announcing that a Bayesian treatment dissolves the paradox without saying what the likelihoods are conditional on, or treating the size of the register as a measure of evidential weight.

Before finalizing, check that a student who can only recite the standard literature would lose most of the marks, and that nothing in the question or the model answer requires a bibliography, a reading list, or an attribution to be answered. Write in formal prose, addressed to me.
