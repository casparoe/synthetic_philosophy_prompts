My reading group is doing a session on the cluelessness problem for consequentialism — the worry that even if you do the moral arithmetic right, the causal ripples of anything you do run out past any horizon anyone could see, so "maximise the good consequences" stops being a guide and becomes a kind of gesture. I'm meant to kick the session off on Wednesday and I really don't want to just summarise the paper, so I'd like to open with a poem instead, and I have a fairly specific thing in mind.

The form is terza rima — aba bcb cdc and so on, so the rhyme scheme is itself a chain, every stanza hooked to the next by a sound that keeps getting handed on. Six to eight tercets, then break the form deliberately at the end: normally you close a terza rima chain by letting the last line rhyme back into the stanza before it (thats what Dante does at the end of each canto), but I want the opposite, one final line with no rhyme partner at all, so the chain is just left hanging there. Content-wise: the speaker is looking back at one small, unglamorous thing they did as a teenager — putting in a word for somebody at a summer job, co-signing something they shouldn't have, driving a stranger to a bus station — and the poem walks that act forward, one stanza per decade, each stanza further out and more speculative than the last, the rhyme sound carried along like a baton. The important part is that the consequences have to keep flipping sign: in one stanza it evidently saved somebody, in the next it clearly wrecked something else, and the speaker can't settle it either way. Set it somewhere in the late seventies in a mid-sized town, and let the decades be marked by small period details you actually know (a payphone, a plant closing, a particular kind of weather) rather than by any invented research or statistics. And by the final tercet the speaker should have started to enjoy the speculation a little too much — treating the unknowable as scenery — which is its own small horror. Then the unpaired last line should land without resolving anything.

Couple of constraints. Don't use the vocabulary of the debate anywhere in the poem — no "consequentialism", no "expected value", no "utility", no "cluelessness" — somebody who's never read any of it should still get the vertigo, and somebody who's read all of it should recognise exactly which move the poem is making and wince at how accurate it is. No consoling ending either, no "so i do what i can", no circle-of-life business, and no moral about humility. Metre can be loose, blank-verse-ish, whatever sounds natural to you, I'm not going to scan it. Somewhere between 25 and 35 lines.
