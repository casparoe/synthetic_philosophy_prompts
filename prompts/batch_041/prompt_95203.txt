Hi, I'm a second-year PhD student working on free will and moral resposibility, and I've been fiddling about with some toy Frankfurt-style cases for the second chapter of my thesis. My supervisor has vanished for a month and I'm stuck on how the cases rank against one another, so I'd like you to just do it for me.

All six below are my own inventions, not from the literature, so please take them at face value and don't worry about what anyone's said about them:

1. The Understudy. An accountant has a chip in her head that will fire and generate the decision to embezzle if she shows any hesitation at the crucial moment. As it happens she deliberates, decides on her own, and embezzles; the chip never fires. Had she been about to decide against, the chip would have made her decide in favour.

2. The Weatherman. Same setup, except the backup is a fallible predictor who reads people's decisions correctly 90% of the time, and the world is genuinely indeterministic. He would have intervened had she been about to refrain. She decides on her own to embezzle. So there's a real chance that, had she begun to decide otherwise, he'd have misread her and not intervened, in which case she would have got away with refraining.

3. The Friend. The backup isn't a machine at all but a colleague who would have talked her out of it, or physically stopped her. My question is whether a personal, reason-responsive backup does any different work from a chip.

4. The Flinch. No counterfactual intervener at all. What makes it Frankfurt-ish is only that a half-second before her decision she felt a genuine but causally idle twinge of reluctance — a 'maybe not' that went nowhere and made no difference to anything downstream.

5. The Bought Vote. Again no intervener. She was bribed in a way that made refraining psychologically impossible; she deliberated, and the deliberation itself was the product of the manipulation. Structurally she could not have done otherwise, but there's no counterfactual backup anywhere in the story.

6. The Understudy's Understudy. The chip would have fired, but a second chip in the neurosurgeon would have stopped him activating the first one. So arguably she could have done otherwise — or arguably not, depending on whether we say the first chip already settled it.

Could you put these six into tiers — S, A, B, C, D — from 'this genuinely threatens the principle that moral responsibility requires the ability to do otherwise' down to 'this isn't a Frankfurt case at all, it's a case about something else'. A sentence or two each on why it lands where it does, and please flag any of them that only seem to work because of an ambiguity in what counts as the same act.

Then, seperately, give me two numbers between 0 and 1 for how much the principle of alternative possibilities survives: one for my best case in the list and one for the worst. 1 means it comes through completely untouched, 0 means it's finished. Just the two numbers plus your reasoning for each.
