I am preparing a seminar on psychiatric classification, and I want to interrogate an analogy that I think is doing far more work in the field than anyone admits. It is common to hear that a diagnostic manual such as the DSM is "a map, not the territory" — that its categories are useful coordinates or convenient fictions, and that any failure of the map to correspond to a natural boundary in the phenomena is simply what maps are like. The conclusion this is used to license is deflationary: worries about whether our categories are "real," or whether they carve nature at its joints, are confused, because a map need not carve anything — it need only be useful. I have seen this move used to quiet both clinicians anxious about the validity of a diagnosis and critics who suspect that some categories are artefacts of the classifying process itself.

Hacking's account of looping effects is typically introduced at exactly this point, as though it supplemented the map picture. My suspicion is that it cuts against it. I would like you to audit the analogy rather than merely adjudicate the conclusion.

Please do the following. First, set out the intended correspondence explicitly, as a table of correspondences if that is clearest: what plays the role of the map, the territory, the mapmaker, the user, the projection or scale, and the legend, and what is supposed to follow from the fact that maps are representations rather than copies. Second, identify where the mapping breaks — which features of maps are quietly imported into the picture of classification, and which features of classification have no counterpart on the map side. Third, separate the disanalogies that matter for the specific deflationary conclusion from those that are merely decorative: which breaks, if any, actually threaten the inference from "classification is a map" to "validity worries are confused," and which can be conceded without cost. Fourth, explain how looping effects bear on the analogy — in what sense a classification might redraw the territory it is supposed to represent, and whether that makes the map metaphor false, merely incomplete, or simply a special and unusual case of mapping. Finally, if the analogy cannot carry the conclusion it is being asked to carry, propose a better one and say what it would need to preserve.

Reason from the concepts themselves; the analysis should stand on its own rather than rest on appeals to authority.
