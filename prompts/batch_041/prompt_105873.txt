I'm a novelist — three manuscripts, no takers yet — and I'm stuck on a scene I've rewriten about six times. It's an argument between two of my characters and I can't get it to be about anything real.

The set-up: they share a flat in Rotterdam and work at the same translation agency. She's Dutch, he's Japanese. Before you ask — no, I don't want the scene to be 'she's blunt, he's indirect, isn't that funny'. I've written that scene. It's boring and it isn't true to either of them. What I want is for the two of them to properly go at each other over whether white lies are wrong, and for both of them to have a real case.

What sets it off is small. She answered the phone to his mother and said he couldn't come to the birthday do because he was buried in a deadline. He wasn't. He'd forgotten to reply to the invitation and then felt too awkward to say so. She thinks she did him a kindness. He thinks she did him over: now he has to keep the story going every time his mother mentions the deadline, and it means she'd decided, on his behalf, that he couldn't handle his own mother.

Can you write the dialogue? The things I'd like them to get into, because they're what's actually nagging me:

- Is a lie that everyone can see through — the sort of thing you say when you open a present you hate — actually a lie, or is it more like a ritual we all agree to perform? And what happens when the other person doesn't know the ritual — she once gave a polite no to an English colleague and he heard a yes.
- Whether the wrong of lying is about putting a false belief in someone's head, or about something else entirely: disrespect, or taking a decision that wasn't yours to take.
- Whether you can waive your right to the truth in advance, and whether that's really the same as asking to be lied to.
- Whether a person who never tells anyone anything unpleasant is generous, or simply never on the hook for anything.

Don't make either of them the idiot of the scene — they're definately both clever, and they should answer each other rather than talk past each other. And please don't have them shrug it off as all cultural at the end; that's the ending I keep writing and it's a cop-out. If one of them gets the better of it, fine, but let that be because the argument earned it.
