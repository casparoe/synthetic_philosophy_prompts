I teach a third-year seminar on memory, guilt, and political responsibility, and I am writing the final examination for it now. The course has become unexpectedly personal for me this term: I have spent several weeks going through family papers from the late 1940s and have concluded that a business my great-grandfather's family lived off for two generations was built on property confiscated during the war from people who never got it back. I do not know what, if anything, follows from that for me, and I have noticed that my students are drawn to the same question in a looser way — they want to know whether guilt, or something guilt-shaped, can reach people who were not there. That is the question I want the exam to be about, and I want it to be about the difficult seam in Jaspers's account rather than about his taxonomy.

The trouble with the essay question I set last year is that a student could do well on it by reciting the four kinds of guilt from the lectures published as The Question of German Guilt — criminal, political, moral, metaphysical — and never notice where the framework strains. In class I have given them a four-way disambiguation of what "collective" can mean when it is attached to guilt: guilt distributed across the members of a group, each answerable for the same wrong; guilt jointly incurred, for something that could only have been done together; guilt predicated of the group itself as a single agent; and collective liability that carries no guilt at all, only an obligation to bear costs or make repair. I want the exam question to force them to use that disambiguation rather than the four labels. It should make them say what licenses Jaspers's asymmetry — political guilt falling on every citizen simply in virtue of membership in the political community, moral guilt attaching only to an individual before his own conscience — and then test that asymmetry against a case he was not writing about: someone born after the events, who did nothing, could not have dissented, and yet inherits a benefit that traces to the wrong. They should have to decide whether that person falls under one of the four categories, whether the framework needs a fifth (and if so, what kind of thing it would have to be), and whether the phrase "responsibility without guilt" names a stable position or is a way of smuggling desert back in under another name. The question should be answerable in fifty minutes of handwriting, closed book, with no notes.

Please write the examination question itself; then a model answer of roughly 700 words, pitched at a strong third-year student rather than at a professional; then a marking scheme out of 20 that lists the moves a top answer must make, the marks each is worth, and, for each, the characteristic mistake that costs students the mark — I want to be able to see at a glance which misunderstanding a given loss of marks represents. Also tell me which single move in the model answer is the one that most reliably separates an A from a B. The students sit the exam with nothing in front of them and no internet, so please keep the question and the model answer free of citations, bibliographies, and quotations from the text: paraphrase Jaspers's position in your own words, and if some point cannot be made without quoting, make it without the quote.
