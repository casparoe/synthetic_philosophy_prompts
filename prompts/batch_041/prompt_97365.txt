I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about when breaking the law is justified, and my verdicts do not sit well together. A group that sits in at a segregated lunch counter, knowing they will be arrested: justified, even though litigation was already dismantling such laws. Quietly underpaying the portion of my taxes that funds a war I regard as unjust, by arranging my affairs so that the underpayment is hard to detect: not justified. Publicly announcing that I am withholding that portion and inviting the penalty: justified, though I cannot tell whether the publicity is doing the work or something else is. An official who leaks classified documents revealing that the government lied the country into a war, while concealing her identity to avoid prosecution: justified. Blocking a highway at rush hour to demand action on climate change: not justified, even though it is public, deliberate, and aimed at a genuine catastrophe. A shopkeeper who ignores a licensing rule he considers pointless because compliance costs him money: not justified. A doctor who performs an illegal procedure because she judges it medically necessary for her patient: justified. Someone who commits an act I would call justified and then flees the jurisdiction to escape the sentence: the flight is not justified, whatever we say about the act.

The rough principle I have been working with is that lawbreaking is justified when it resists a serious injustice and its costs fall on those responsible for the injustice or on the lawbreaker herself. Please test that against the cases, repair it where it fails, and tell me which of my verdicts I should abandon. I especially want to know whether the publicity of the act matters, whether it matters that the lawbreaker stands to gain or to sacrifice by breaking the law, and what the resulting principle implies for a case I have not listed: a public official who refuses, on conscientious grounds, to issue a license she is legally required to issue, where the refusal blocks a third party's lawful application.
