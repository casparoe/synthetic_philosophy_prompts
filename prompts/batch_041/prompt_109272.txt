I'm a novelist, three books in, and I've gotten myself into a philosophical hole I'm hoping you can pull me out of.

Six months ago a friend handed me Rorty's Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity and I haven't written a decent paragraph since. The problem isn't that I disagree with him. It's that I mostly agree. No mind-independent facts sitting out there for my sentences to line up against, vocabularies as tools for coping rather than mirrors of anything, truth as less a relation between my sentence and a chunk of the world than a way of saying the sentence is doing its job, progress as getting a better description rather than getting closer to the thing itself. Sure. But my whole job is inventing sentences about people who never existed and trying to make strangers care about them, and I can't tell anymore what I think I'm doing when I do it well.

So here's what I want from you: the most important implications of that view for the actual practice of writing fiction. Not a summary of Rorty, I've read the book twice. I want the consequences, ordered, concrete enough that I could act on them at the desk, and I want you to flag which ones are his and which your extending on his behalf.

A few things I keep banging into, in case they help you aim.

The book I'm stuck on is about a plant closing in a town my family's from. I'm writing it in a flat, almost clinical voice and I keep feeling like I'm betraying the people in it. Under his view, what would even be the content of that feeling? If accuracy is internal to the vocabulary I've picked, my flatness can't be inaccurate. So is the betrayal feeling just sentimentality, or is it tracking something real that antirepresentationalism has no room to name?

Redescription. He says the interesting move is to describe things in a new vocabulary, and the strong poet's project is self-creation. Fine, except my craft is the redescription of other people's lives. He also seems to think redescription can be a form of cruelty, which is what he's circling in the Nabokov and Orwell chapters near the end and in the whole private/public split. Give me a usable test for when I'm handing someone a better vocabulary for their own life versus using their life as raw material for mine. I need something I can feel from inside a draft, not a rule.

Orwell. My reading is that he thinks the cruelty descriptions there do work the argument can't do, that the book moves you rather than proves anything to you, and that this is fine. If that's right, what is the moral force of my novel made of? A technique I can practice on purpose, a con that happens to work, or something that only works if I don't know I'm doing it?

Solidarity. He wants us to aim at solidarity instead of objectivity, a bigger we. But a good propagandist wants a bigger we too. What is the actual difference between a novel that widens the we and one that just flatters it? If there's no criterion, say so. If there is one, I want it.

And the honest part: what should I not take from him? Where does this view make me a worse novelist, lazier about facts or colder about people? I think somewhere he says the novel took over from the sermon and the treatise as the main engine of moral change, and I want to know whether that's a compliment to my trade or a warning about it.

Keep it plain. No cites, no reading list, alot of this I just need in my own words anyway. I'm trying to finish a book, not write a paper.
