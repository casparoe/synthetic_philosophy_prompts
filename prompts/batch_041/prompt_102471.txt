Im putting together an episode for the science-and-ideas podcast I host, and next weeks guest is a developmental psycholinguist who's been on the nativist side of the language-acquisition fight for about thirty years. What I want the episode to hang on is her own reframing, which she insists the field keeps getting wrong: the poverty of the stimulus was never really a claim about how little language kids hear. It's a claim about search — the space of grammars a child could in principle entertain is so large that no domain-general learner could find the right one in the few years it has. So the poverty is in the hypothesis space, not the input. I want questions that force her to actually defend that rather than just restate it, and I want it to sound like I've read her stuff.

Eight to ten questions, and I need at least one each on:

- what the actual input looks like. We now have big corpora of child-directed speech, and the constructions that are supposed to flip the relevant parameters turn out to be vanishingly rare in it. Does the sparseness story survive the corpus counts, or does she quietly retreat to the hypothesis-space version?
- the distributional-learning reply — kids are demonstrably good at tracking transitional probabilities and chunking — and how she thinks "learned a pattern" differs from "learned a grammar." I suspect that's the line she'll want to draw, so make her draw it.
- the ideal-learner move: hand a general-purpose Bayesian learner the right priors and it gets the grammar out, and then the whole fight is just about where the priors come from. Is that a concession, or a redescription of nativism in fancier clothes?
- falsifiability. What would actually count against "the hypothesis space is too big"? If nothing does, she owes me a story about why this is still a scientific claim and not a slogan.
- the linking problem: even granting innate constraints, how do they hook onto the specific grammatical facts — parameters, features, whatever — without smuggling the grammar back in whole?

For each of these, give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, especially the deflections I can see coming — "thats a claim at the computational level, not the algorithmic one," or "you're conflating the data with the trigger." And end on the one I actually want answered: is there any child, any language, any experiment that could make her give up the framing?
