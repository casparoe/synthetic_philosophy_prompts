I sit on a working group — two roboticists, an insurance actuary, a regulator, and two philosophers — that has been arguing for the better part of two years about the responsibility gap for autonomous systems, and we have not moved an inch. I would like a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another round of the argument.

The positions, as they actually present themselves in our meetings and in the literature we keep throwing at each other, are roughly these. The first camp holds that when a system whose behaviour was neither specified nor specifiable in advance causes serious harm, no one satisfies the conditions for deserved blame: the designer did not choose the token act, the operator could not have foreseen it, and the system is not a candidate for blame at all. On this view the gap names a genuine moral loss, and the point of the phrase is that it ought to be closed by new institutions rather than papered over. The second camp holds that the gap is an artifact of an inflated picture of responsibility: some human or corporate body always had a duty, could have imposed a control, or occupies a role whose content includes answering for such outcomes, so that once forward-looking and role-based responsibility are admitted, along with liability regimes and audit duties, there is nothing left to close. A third position concedes that no one is blameworthy but denies that this is a defect, on the grounds that blame is neither the only nor the central response to harm. A fourth treats the whole thing as the familiar problem of many hands in new clothes.

My hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, not a conclusion — is that we are not disagreeing about the facts of any case, and that the impasse has at least three separable sources: (a) "responsibility" is doing duty for at least four things — causal authorship, answerability, liability to sanction, and deserved blame — and the camps are each defending a different one; (b) the camps disagree about what would count as closing a gap, so that institutional substitutes are treated by one side as a solution and by the other as a change of subject; and (c) beneath both lies an unargued premise about whether blameworthiness is necessary for a practice of accountability to be legitimate.

There is one further tool I want you to use, since it seems to cut across all four camps. I distinguish a coverage gap — no party is answerable for the outcome at all — from a fit gap, in which some party is answerable but not for the reason that makes the answer appropriate: the sanction lands on a party whose fault, if any, is not the fault that produced the harm, so the accounting is formally complete and substantively wrong. Coverage gaps are supposedly solved by assigning a responsible party; fit gaps cannot be solved that way, because assigning a party is precisely what produces them. I suspect that much of the apparent disagreement about whether the gap has been closed is really a disagreement about which of the two is at issue.

So tell me what kind of disagreement this is. Is it verbal, a clash of unargued starting points, a hidden empirical question about whether some human could always in principle have controlled or foreseen the outcome, or two camps answering different questions under one name — who is on the hook, who deserves blame, who should bear the cost? Engage the positions as I have stated them, say where my hypothesis is right and where it misdescribes the parties, and say what would actually count as progress here: a distinction, a reformulated question, a formal result about the limits of attribution, an institutional design both camps could accept — or an argument that no progress should be expected, and why.
