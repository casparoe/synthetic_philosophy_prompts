I host a small philosophy interview podcast — a couple hundred listeners, mostly people from my old grad seminar — and next month's episode is with a philosopher who defends a hard line on the rescue case. She's invented, by the way, not a real person, so dont worry about matching anything an actual living philosopher has said or written; I'm essentially building her position myself, and I need the questions to be fair to it rather than to some other version of it. Her line: in the choice between saving one stranger and saving five, the numbers give you no reason at all to save the five; that it's five is, as she puts it, not a reason for anything, and if you can't save everyone you should give everyone an equal chance. Two things make her worth an hour. First, she separates the levels — the numbers don't count for what you're permitted to do, but they do count for how much criticism you deserve afterwards: save the one and you've done nothing wrong, but you are, she says, the sort of person who leaves five people to die for no reason, and it's legitimate for people to hold that against you. Second, she refuses the label 'lottery view' even though she thinks the chooser ought to randomize, and she gets irritated when people say her position makes the one and the five interchangeable. She's also extended the view to triage — allocating a scarce drug, an ICU bed, who gets the ventilator — and I think that's where the cracks will show.

You're a moral philosopher who works on the ethics of saving lives and on non-consequentialist theory, and also someone who's actually good at interviewing: think carefully about where the real pressure is before you write anything. Start with a short brainstorm — maybe five or six bullets — naming the strongest objections to her position and the replies she's most likely to reach for, so I can see you're aiming, and then give me ten to twelve questions. I want at least one question each on: (a) exactly what she's denying — that the numbers give any reason at all, or that they give a decisive one — and whether her thesis is about reasons or about permissibility; (b) her argument that there's no standpoint from which five deaths are worse than one, since the 'we' who prefer the five isn't a person, and whether that argument proves too much: does it also say the numbers don't count against one versus a thousand, or against saving five people from a mild illness rather than one from death, and does her principle generalize into the absurd claim that two headaches aren't worse than one? (c) the randomization itself — walk her through the arithmetic of giving everyone an equal chance, where the one gets a half and each of the five gets a tenth, and ask how that isn't a weighing of claims in disguise, or a refusal to weigh them that just relocates the decision; (d) the partiality wrinkle, since views like hers are famous for letting you save your own child over five strangers, and I want to know whether the numbers-don't-count claim is doing any work in that case or whether it's a different argument wearing the same coat; (e) her two-level story, where I suspect the trouble is — press on whether criticism can be apt if the numbers genuinely give no reason, and whether the levels stay consistent when the person who saves the one is also the person who has to live with it; (f) the triage extension, including the case where you can save the five but only one at a time over the next year, which looks like aggregation smuggled in through the back door; and (g) the separateness of persons — what work it's actually doing in her argument, and whether she needs it or just likes it. One question should also be about the ex ante angle: if you didn't know which of the six you'd turn out to be, would her verdict about what to do change, and if so what does that show about the numbers counting somewhere. For every question, give me a one-line follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, and where there's an obvious second answer, the follow-up for that too — I don't want to be stuck nodding if she dodges.

Keep every question self-contained: a listener who knows the one-versus-five case and nothing else should be able to follow it, but a specialist should hear it land on something real. No 'what drew you to this problem' filler, and nothing that depends on her knowing the secondary literature by name — I can't have her citing people she hasn't read, and I'd rather the pressure come from the structure of her view than from a bibliography. Flag the two questions I should cut if we run long, tell me which one she's most likely to filibuster, and give me one closing question that's hard to answer with a restatement.
