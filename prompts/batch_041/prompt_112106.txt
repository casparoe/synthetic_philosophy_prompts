I have just sent my editor the manuscript of a book — twelve essays, each of which I would defend in a seminar — and she has asked me for a preface. The preface is meant to do the usual thing: say that I stand behind every essay, and concede that in a book this size some claim somewhere is probably wrong. I have read that sentence a hundred times without blinking. Writing it, I cannot tell what I have just committed myself to.

It is the same sensation I had last month in the lottery for the department's eight sabbatical offices, two hundred entrants. I was certain my name would not come up and certain that someone's name would, and both certainties felt like beliefs rather than bets. I had always filed the lottery paradox and the preface paradox as curiosities for other people. I now suspect that I have been living inside both of them for a fortnight and that they are not, in fact, the same puzzle.

What follows is the draft of a short essay. I would like your comments — on whether the argument is right, but just as much on whether it is interesting, and on whether the distinction I am drawing is doing real work or merely relabeling. I would rather have your reasoning than a reading list.

The standard treatment runs roughly as follows. In each case I hold a set of attitudes that cannot all be accurate together: every ticket will lose, yet someone will win; every essay is right, yet some claim is wrong. Since I am not prepared to give up any individual item, something else must give — deductive closure (I do not believe the conjunction), the aggregation of individual certainties, or the threshold picture of belief on which high probability suffices for belief.

My claim is that the two cases come apart exactly where the threshold picture is asked to do its work, and that the difference is not one of degree but of what the offending attitude is about.

In the lottery, the claim that strains against my ticket-by-ticket certainty is a claim about the setup: the draw is fair, someone must win, I have no special information. In the preface, the claim that strains against my essay-by-essay certainty is a claim about me: that a process this long and this human produces error at some rate. Neither is a claim about the content of the items I am aggregating. But they behave very differently. The lottery claim is entailed by the description of the case; I cannot drop it without changing the subject. The preface claim is a prediction about my own error rate — and it is itself one of the claims in the book. I have written a preface; the preface is an essay in the volume; the sentence "some claim here is false" is a claim here. If I believe it, then the one thing in the book I am most confident is wrong is the sentence I have just written to be honest about my fallibility. If I exempt it, I have carved out an exception for precisely the sentence that claims no exceptions are available.

That asymmetry, I think, is where the two paradoxes stop being one paradox. The lottery has no self-referential ticket unless you rig one; the preface cannot avoid having one.

Two things I cannot settle, and would like you to push on.

First: does this dissolve the paradox or merely relocate it? If the preface's offending claim is about my reliability rather than about the conjunction of the essays, then perhaps what I am entitled to there is not a belief at all but something weaker — a standing policy of expecting correction, held in place by nothing more than the base rate of people like me. I am suspicious that this is the relabeling I said I wanted to avoid, and I would like to be told plainly if it is.

Second: I keep wondering whether the preface sentence is a proposition at all. It may be a speech act — an insurance policy, a display of humility that purchases the reader's trust, something closer to a promise than to a claim. If so, I do not know whether the paradox survives being moved out of the domain of truth and falsity, or whether that move is just the oldest evasion in the book.

Please give me comments of perhaps four hundred words, addressed to: (a) whether the asymmetry is real or an artifact of how I have set up the cases; (b) whether the policy-instead-of-belief move is more than a redescription; (c) what the familiar escape routes — giving up closure, giving up aggregation, giving up the threshold — each do to my argument; and (d) any place where I have the technical details of either paradox wrong. Throughout, tell me how confident you are in each substantive claim you make, and single out the one you are least sure of. A hedged and accurate diagnosis is worth more to me here than a decisive one.
