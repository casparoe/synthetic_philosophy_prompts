I am the research director of a small institute that has just been given a three-year grant for foundational work on uncertainty, and the board has asked me to write a memo committing us to a short list of priorities. We have decided to concentrate on imprecise probabilities — credal states represented by sets of probability functions, or by something in that neighborhood — rather than on applied forecasting or on Bayesian methods as usually practiced, and the grant is enough to support roughly six to eight researchers. The difficulty is that the area is not a single research program but a cluster of partially independent ones, and I am not confident that they can be compared on a single scale. There is the foundational question of what an imprecise credal state represents: genuine indeterminacy in the world, incompleteness in the agent's evidence, a set of precise credences the agent has not introspected, or something else again. There is the dynamic question of how such states should be updated, where the familiar rule of conditionalization has several non-equivalent generalizations that come apart in cases of dilation and in cases involving independence. There is the decision-theoretic question of which rule a user of imprecise probabilities should adopt, given that maximin, maximax, α-maxmin, and various Hurwicz-style rules can disagree even when the credal set is held fixed. And there is a more technical cluster: the status of independence and conditional independence for sets of probabilities, the relation between comparative and quantitative imprecision, the coherence and Dutch-book arguments that are supposed to discipline the framework, and the prospects for extending it to group or social choice.

I would like you to rank these lines of work — adding any you think I have omitted — by how much they should matter to us over the next three years, and to defend the ranking rather than merely state it. Assume that our researchers are a mixed group of philosophers, mathematicians, and mathematically minded economists, that we care about foundational clarity more than about immediate applications, but that we would like the work eventually to be usable by people building systems that must be robust to ambiguity and by economists modeling disagreement. For each item, tell me how far upstream it is of the others, how tractable it is for the kind of people we can hire, how neglected it currently is, and what sort of result over the next few years would move it up or down the list. If you think two of the items are not really separable, or that one of them is a pseudo-question, say so and explain why.
