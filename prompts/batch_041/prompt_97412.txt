Hey, so I'm in a final-year seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and my prof has drafted a few of us to run the weekly discussion section next week. It's 50 minutes, about 16 students, and honestly if I just ask them whether we'd still blame people if determinism were true, I'll get the same three takes they've had since first year. I want an activity where they actually feel the thing in their body instead of arguing about it.

Here's the angle I'm after. The idea I want them to bump into: our practices of holding each other responsible -- the anger, the gratitude, the hurt, the forgiving -- aren't conclusions we draw from some prior fact about whether a person could have done otherwise. They're the responses of creatures who are in it with each other, face to face, second person. And there's a seperate stance always available, where you stop relating to someone as a someone and start relating to them as a something: a case to be managed, predicted, treated, fixed. We slip into it with small kids, with people who are very ill, sometimes with people we've quietly given up on. I want the activity to make them feel both stances from the inside and feel that the second one is (a) always available, (b) hard to actually hold onto, and (c) costs something real when you do hold it.

So: design me a runnable activity for this. Rough plan I have, but fix it however you think works best --

Set up a case conference about one person, let's call her Dara, who did something seriously wrong (a hit-and-run, or skimming from a community fund -- pick whatever). One participant plays Dara. The rest are a panel, but each panelist is given a specific relationship to Dara and a specific stake in the outcome, so nobody is neutral.

The core mechanic I want: two kinds of moves. Address moves -- speaking to Dara in the second person, asking her for reasons, expressing anger or disappointment or hope, demanding an account. And manage moves -- describing Dara in the third person as a case, citing risk factors, prescribing treatment, recommending a disposition. Give me a way to track which moves people make (tokens, a tally, whatever's light) and a small payoff structure so that both kinds of moves feel like they cost the mover something.

The twist that I think makes it land: Dara is instructed to only actually engage -- to give a real account, to genuinely reconsider anything -- in response to address moves. Manage moves get her to shut down and produce flat, compliant, agreeable-sounding answers that change nothing. So a panel that goes fully objective will reach a tidy, defensible decision that is completely hollow, and they'll feel the hollowness. Also, before the conference, hand the panelists a short dossier on Dara's background (deprivation, a head injury, whatever) that makes the objective stance feel like the responsible, mature choice -- then see whether they can still address her as a person once they've read it. I'm curious whether the dossier quietly does the work of switching them over.

I need: rules, materials, roles, the payoff/token scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 50 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the underlying picture -- the naturalness of resentment and gratitude, the objective stance as something we adopt for particular purposes rather than a neutral default, the fact that you can't hold it toward everyone at once, and that it seems to lean on the interpersonal stance for its very meaning. Don't name the philosopher or the term for the attitudes until the debrief, and don't lecture up front -- I want them to arrive at it.

Three more things. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: my guesses are someone hamming up the objective stance as a cartoon villain, someone refusing to play the objective role because it feels gross, the Dara player going off-script and getting genuinely hurt, and running out of time. How do I handle each, and which round should I cut if I have to?

Second, be honest about what this activity can't reproduce. I already suspect it'll make the interpersonal stance look good because it instrumentally works -- Dara only opens up to address moves -- whereas the real point is that these attitudes aren't justified by anything at all, they're just what being a person among persons consists in. If that's right, say so, and tell me how to hand them that extra step in the debrief without wrecking the mood.

Third, a quick note on how to scale it down if only eight people show up.
