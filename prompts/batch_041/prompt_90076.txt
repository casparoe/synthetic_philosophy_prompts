ok so I keep teaching the fitting-attitude analysis and I have an argument in my notes that I'm now pretty sure proves too much, and every time I sit down to figure out which step to cut I loose my nerve. its short, so here it is, more or less as I wrote it:

(1) For something to be valuable is just for it to be a fitting object of a pro-attitude -- admiration, approval, desiring-it-for-its-own-sake, pick your flavor.
(2) An attitude is fitting exactly when there are reasons for it.
(3) A demon credibly tells you it will torture a stranger unless you admire it. (Take the threat seriously and take the stranger to be someone you have no prior connection to.)
(4) So you now have a reason to admire the demon -- a real reason for the attitude itself, not merely a reason to wish you admired it or to act as if you did.
(5) By (2), admiration is the fitting attitude here. By (1), the demon is valuable.
(6) Nothing turned on the demon. Whenever there is any reason at all for a pro-attitude toward X, X comes out valuable.
(7) So value just tracks incentives: whatever anyone anywhere has some reason to admire is valuable. That's absurd. The demon is not valuable, and a threat -- or a bribe, or a pill that makes you admire something -- doesn't make anything valuable.

So (1) or (2) has to go, or something in between them does.

I really don't want to give up (1); I find it too elegant to lose, so I'd rather pay somewhere else. Tell me which step to reject, and why that step looked fine to me until now. Then price the options, because I want to know what I'm buying: if I keep (1) by narrowing (2) to reasons of the "right kind," what am I now on the hook for, and does the narrowing end up circular or else so tight that the analysis goes trivial? If instead I go hierarchical and say the right kind of reason is one that bears on the attitude in the right way, what does that buy and what does it cost? And what happens to someone who just bites the bullet and says there's no principled right/wrong kind distinction at all -- do they get to keep (1), or does (1) collapse into (7) and stop doing any work?

No reading list please, I know where to look. I just want the diagnosis and the bill.
