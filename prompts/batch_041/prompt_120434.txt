I write about science and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and I'm deep into a long piece on introspection — whether the mind's access to itself is anything like what the philosophical tradition promised, and what the cognitive science of interoception and metacognition has to say about it. The piece is mostly reporting, but I have one expository paragraph in the middle where I bring in Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument, and before it goes to my editor — who reads carefully and will ask — I want to be sure I haven't done violence to the argument. Here is the paragraph as it currently stands:

"Williamson's anti-luminosity argument is the most-cited proof that the KK principle is false — the principle that if you know something, you know that you know it. The argument is beautifully simple. Sensations fade in and out by imperceptible degrees: you feel cold at dawn and warm by noon, and at no point can you name the moment when the cold gave way to warmth, or be certain which of the two you feel. Williamson calls a state 'luminous' if being in it guarantees that you can know you're in it, and he shows that no mental state is luminous: if you can't be certain, you can't know. The consequence is that introspection, which Descartes made the foundation of all knowledge, is no more reliable than eyesight — the mind is as opaque to itself as the world is to the mind."

What I'd like from you is the audit I'd commission before publication: go through it claim by claim and tell me what a philosopher working on self-knowledge and the epistemology of justification would accept as written, what would make them wince, and what is simply not what the argument says. I'm most interested in the places where I've collapsed a distinction the argument depends on keeping apart, and in whether the last sentence — the Descartes flourish and the opacity line — follows from anything that survives correction, or whether I have to replace it with something more modest. I'd also like a view on my first sentence: is the KK framing the right way to bring this in for readers who have never heard of the principle, or is it a distraction that invites exactly the sort of conflation I'm worried about?

Assume my readers are intelligent generalists with no epistemology background and no patience for jargon, and pitch the correction at that level — if a technical term has to appear, tell me how to gloss it in a clause. I don't need citations, sources, or a reading list; I'm not going to name anyone in the piece except Williamson, and my editor will cut anything that reads like a syllabus. What I do need is a clear verdict on what I can keep as is, what has to be qualified, and what has to go.
