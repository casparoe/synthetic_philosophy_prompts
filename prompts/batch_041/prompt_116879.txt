I've been trying to get my head round that bit in Plato's Meno where Socrates explains why knowledge is meant to be worth more than true belief, and I'm stuck on what the explanation actually amounts to. I haven't got a translation to hand, so here's my garbled recollection of the passage: Socrates compares true beliefs to the statues of Daedalus, which are lovely and worth having while they stay in place but will scarper and run off unless someone ties them down. What ties them down, he says, is working out the reason why. Knowledge, then, is true belief that's been tethered, and he says that's the difference between the two, and why one is much more valuable than the other.

Two things bother me.

First, what kind of difference is this supposed to be? If it's just psychological staying-power — the knower won't be talked out of it, the mere true believer might be — then it looks like the extra value is prudential rather than epistemic. A man with a true belief that happens to be permanently unshakeable, say because he's stubborn or because nobody will ever challenge him, gets all the practical benefit of knowledge without, on the face of it, knowing anything. Is Socrates happy with that? Or is the tethering meant to be something about the belief's relation to the world — its being properly connected to what makes it true — so that the stability is a symptom rather than the point?

Second, what exactly counts as the tether? If the reason why is something the believer has to be able to give, then someone reciting a justification they've memorised and don't understand would seem to qualify, which feels wrong. But if it's meant to be a real explanatory connection between the belief and its cause, then the image of a chain or a knot is misleading, since the believer's own grasp of the reason drops out of the picture. I can't work out which reading the statue image is supposed to support, or whether Plato even needs to choose.

Could you help me understand what the tethering is, and whether the value knowledge gets from it is epistemic or just a matter of the belief sticking around? If there are competing readings of the passage, I'd rather know which one the Daedalus image actually favours than get a general survey of the literature.
