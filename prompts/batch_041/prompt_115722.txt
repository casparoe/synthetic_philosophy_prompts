I am a senior at our city's public high school and the youth member of the city Public Safety Advisory Commission, the body that reviews police policy before it reaches the city council. I was appointed in my sophomore year, and I have read the minutes back to the commission's founding, because I would rather vote on a principle I can state than on whatever the loudest person in the room happens to want. Next month we take up a proposal I cannot sort out, and I want to work out what our own record commits us to before the meeting.

The decisions we have made, in order:

We recommended body-worn cameras for all patrol officers, with automatic activation and release of footage to the public within fourteen days.

We recommended an unarmed civilian response team for mental-health and welfare-check calls, funded by leaving vacant sworn positions unfilled.

We recommended a ban on the department's use of facial recognition and on its sharing of data with federal immigration enforcement.

We rejected armed officers stationed inside the high school with authority to search lockers and bags at random.

We rejected a pay increase and a new training program, because the department would investigate its own complaints and would not release complaint data or any evaluation of the training.

We recommended a community-ambassador pilot in the neighborhood whose residents petitioned for it, and rejected the identical pilot in a neighboring area whose residents organized against it.

I have tried and failed to divide the commission into a reform camp and an abolition camp: the same members appear on both sides depending on the proposal.

The proposal now before us would add thirty officers assigned permanently to the four parts of the city with the most shootings, with their patrol assignments set from reported-crime data. The same package would create a civilian oversight board with subpoena power and its own investigators, require publication of stop, search, and use-of-force data at the level of the individual officer, and pay for an independent annual evaluation. The department's preliminary figures show shootings down in the pilot areas and stops of young men up sharply, with nearly all of the increase falling on Black and Latino residents; the full evaluation is unfinished. The mayor has said the thirty officers are not negotiable and the package will be voted up or down as a whole.

What should I recommend if I want to be consistent with our own record? Tell me which earlier decision is the closest precedent and why. Set out the rules that fit everything we have done, what each predicts about this proposal, and which one you would bet on, with your reasons. If the record genuinely underdetermines the vote, say what principle we would be adopting by approving and what we would be adopting by rejecting. Finally, tell me what proposal I should put on next year's agenda to separate whichever rules are still standing.
