The question I set was:

*Compromise between two value systems is sometimes modelled as a species of trade. Each side concedes on a dimension it ranks lower in order to secure a dimension it ranks higher, and the settlements that leave both parties better off than their disagreement point are said to lie on a 'contract curve'. (a) Explain what the terms-of-trade model is meant to capture, and how it distinguishes concessive from integrative compromise. (b) Assess the objection that the model collapses when the values at stake are incommensurable. (c) Say, on balance, whether the economic analogy illuminates moral compromise or misleads, and defend your answer.*

And here's what the student wrote:

"The terms-of-trade model begins from the thought that compromise is not the same as splitting the difference. When two parties with different value systems settle, each usually gives ground on a dimension it cares about less in order to get its way on a dimension it cares about more. This is structurally like trade between the butcher and the baker: both gain, not because they agree about what is valuable, but because they rank the goods on offer differently. The 'price' of a compromise is the rate at which one side's concessions convert into the other's, and the set of settlements that leave both parties strictly better off than their disagreement point is the contract curve. The model's real payoff is the distinction between concessive and integrative compromise. A concessive compromise is one in which each side simply lowers its demand, so that both end up with less than they wanted; an integrative compromise is one in which the parties restructure the dispute so that each gets more of what it actually values. The model predicts that integrative compromise is available whenever the parties' rankings differ, even when the values themselves are wholly different in kind.

The obvious objection is incommensurability. If two values share no common measure — if there is no unit in which both can be expressed — then there is no rate of exchange, and trade of the kind described seems impossible. This looks worse than a technicality. When we say that two values are incommensurable we mean that neither is worth more than the other, and that the two cannot be ranked at all; and where nothing can be ranked, no concession can be 'worth it', because the very notion of worth presupposes a common scale. So the model threatens to collapse precisely in the cases that matter most, namely conflicts between deeply different value systems rather than quarrels between people who want different quantities of the same good.

I think the model survives, but not by denying incommensurability. It survives because trade does not actually require a common currency; barter works, and barter needs only that each party be able to rank the bundles on offer. Any agent with a consistent preference ordering can do that, and nothing in the idea of a value system forbids it. So the analogy holds even between incommensurable value systems, provided we read 'incommensurable' as ruling out a common measure rather than ruling out ranking altogether.

The voluntarists are right that mutual benefit is what does the normative work here. As they insist, the disagreement point is the crucial variable: a settlement is defensible only if the baseline against which it counts as an improvement is itself just. Where the fallback of one party is starvation and the fallback of the other is a slightly worse deal, a settlement can be voluntary, mutually beneficial, and still exploitative. Gains from trade are therefore a necessary condition for a legitimate compromise, not a sufficient one.

On balance the analogy illuminates more than it misleads. Its deepest insight is that compromise need not be a zero-sum loss of integrity; it can be a positive-sum discovery of what each side actually values. The incommensurability objection is real but overstated, and the contract curve is a far better guide to compromise than the metaphor of 'splitting the difference'."
