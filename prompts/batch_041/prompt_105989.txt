Write a sonnet in the voice of Avicenna's flying man. In the thought experiment a human being is brought into existence in a single instant, fully formed and adult, suspended in empty space with his limbs spread apart so that he cannot touch any part of himself and his eyes veiled so that nothing from the world reaches him; Avicenna asks whether such a man would affirm his own existence, and answers that he would. Your poem should be spoken by that man, and it should render the argument accurately enough that a reader who knows the thought experiment recognises what is genuinely at issue: not a proof that the soul exists as a separate substance, but the claim that the self is present to itself with no contribution from the body, and then the further and far more contestable claim that whatever is thus present to itself must be immaterial. Let the octave build the void he is in, and let the volta be the moment he tries to doubt himself and discovers that the doubting already requires the doubter.

Impose on the poem a constraint that does the work of the argument: it may never name a body part, a sense, or an act of perception — no eye, hand or skin, no seeing, hearing, touching or feeling — and it must nevertheless leave the reader certain that someone is speaking. The body's absence from the poem's vocabulary should mirror the body's absence from the man's awareness. Take care not to let the poem slide into the cogito as though the two were the same move: Avicenna's man is not doubting the world and recovering himself from the doubt; he simply finds himself there, and the poem should let the reader feel that the step from being present to oneself to being no body at all is a further step, taken rather than found. A specialist should find nothing to wince at, and a reader who has never met the flying man should still find a poem.
