I am writing a novel whose central conceit is a message in a bottle: a sealed text washes ashore, and the plot turns on the assumption that its meaning is inside the bottle, waiting to be let out — that reading is retrieval, that the right code would settle what it says, and that characters who disagree about it are simply, some of them, wrong. My editor, who has read more cognitive science than I have, tells me this is the naive picture and that Hofstadter's three-way split in the chapter on the location of meaning in Gödel, Escher, Bach — frame message, outer message, inner message — is what dissolves it. Audit the analogy for me, and give me a committed verdict rather than a survey of options. Begin with a correspondence table: what in the bottle picture plays the role of the frame message, the outer message, and the inner message, and where the bottle, the sea, the sender, and the reader each land. Then lay out where the mapping breaks, and separate the breaks that matter for my conceit from the ones that are merely decorative. Say definitely whether the retrieval picture survives in any form; if it does not, tell me what the honest version of the conceit is, the one I can still write, and where in a novel the three levels would have to become visible for a reader to feel the difference. If the analogy is carrying the conclusion rather than supporting it, say so plainly and say which way the argument actually runs.
