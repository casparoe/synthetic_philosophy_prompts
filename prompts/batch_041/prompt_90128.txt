I'm a retired biologist -- thirty-odd years of it, mostly on freshwater snails, which I realize sounds like a joke but wasn't -- and since I packed in the lab work I've been reading philosophy in the evenings. A friend in my little reading group who actually knows the subject layed out an arguement for me last week and I've been chewing on it ever since because I can't find the loose brick.

Here it is, as he set it out:

1. A general term names a real kind of thing in nature only if there's some fact of the matter about which things fall under it -- a fact that doesn't just reflect which grouping happens to be handy for us.
2. There's no such fact for "species." We have at least three criteria in circulation -- can they interbreed, do they share a distinctive ancestry, do they form a recognizable morphological and ecological cluster -- and they give different answers. Ring species: neighbors interbreed, the two ends don't. Asexual organisms: the interbreeding test has nothing to bite on. Bacteria: genes hop sideways straight across the groups we want to call species.
3. So there's no fact of the matter about which organisms fall under "species."
4. So "species" doesn't name a real kind.
5. If "species" doesn't name a real kind, then when I say the tiger is a species, or that Homo sapiens is one species among others, I'm not reporting anything about nature -- I'm reporting my filing preferences.
6. But the recipe doesn't stop at species. Run it on "gene" (overlapping reading frames, regulatory stretches that don't code for anything, a "gene for" X that turns out to be five things), on "organ," on "individual organism" (is a Portuguese man o' war one animal or a committee? what about the mitochondria inside my cells?). None of them has a single criterion either.
7. So no biological category names a real kind, and biology is a filing system we impose rather than a description of joints in nature.
8. But 7 is bonkers. When the same antibiotic stops working, or a fish stock collapses, something real happened, and it wasn't my filing preferences.

He says 8 is just sentimentality, the argument is sound, and if I want to keep 8 I have to give up 1. I'd rather keep 8, obviously. But I can't tell weather the trouble is really in 1, or in the step from 2 to 3, or somewhere I'm not looking -- and I want to know what it costs me either way. Someone also told me there's a camp that says species aren't kinds at all but individuals, like particular historical lineages, and another that says a kind can be perfectly real without any single criterion, so long as the properties cluster and something underneath makes them cluster. Do either of those actually get me out of this, and what do they cost? If I have to drop one premise, which one, and what am I paying for it?
