I'm in a reading group thats been running about a year and a half, and it's split pretty evenly between ML people (two of them work on alignment-ish stuff, one does evals) and econ/public-policy people (one development economist, one who spent years on school accountability systems). We keep having the same fight about Goodhart's law and specification gaming, and I'm now fairly convinced we're talking past each other rather than disagreeing. The ML side treats it as close to a law of nature: apply enough optimization pressure to a proxy and it decouples from what you actually cared about, and this is structural, not a matter of picking better metrics, and it's the core difficulty for aligning anything powerful. The econ side thinks that's mystification: what's being described is measurement error, or the Lucas critique, or just the fact that people respond to incentives, and if you could optimize the true objective there'd be nothing left over to explain — no "law," just the truism that a proxy isn't the thing. They also suspect the ML framing smuggles in an adversary, someone deliberately gaming the number, into cases where there plainly isn't one (a model that reward-hacks in a way nobody intended, a school that teaches to the test without anyone deciding to).

Part of what keeps this going is that people are working with different cuts. One person in the group insists the real distinction is between the metric having always been a bad proxy and the metric having been fine until we started optimizing against it, and says lumping those under one name is the whole source of the fight. Someone else says the useful cut is fourfold: (a) the proxy just decoheres out at the extremes where we have no data, (b) the proxy is causally downstream of the goal, so pushing it doesn't push the goal, (c) optimizing the proxy causally changes the target itself, (d) there's an agent actively gaming you — and that the "is it a law?" question is really a question about which of those you have in mind. I don't know how to adjudicate any of that.

Could you write the document these two camps should have produced instead of argueing for a year — an adversarial collaboration they could both sign? I want (1) a numbered list of agreed claims: what the phenomenon is, where it shows up and where it doesn't, what it does and doesn't require (does it need an intentional agent? does the optimizer need to represent the metric at all? does it survive when the objective is exactly the thing you care about?), and which parts of the dispute are purely definitional. (2) The precise disagreements, each stated in wording that the side holding it would accept as fair rather than a strawman. (3) The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side; I'd guess one is whether degradation survives when you optimize the true value itself (say, because optimization moves you into regions where your estimate was never checked, or the system modifies itself), and another is whether the bad-proxy-always vs. fine-until-optimized distinction marks two genuinely different phenomena. If those cuts are real, the statement should separate them and say which one each side's argument is actually about; if they're not, say why. (4) For each crux, a concrete case, toy model, historical episode, or runnable experiment that both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. And at the end, tell me which parts of this fight are verbal, and whether the "law of nature" framing survives once the verbal parts are stripped out.
