I'm a science journalist who covers AI and philosophy of mind, and through a series of bad desicions I'm now on my newsroom's standards and ethics committee — the people who write the style rules everyone ignores until there's a scandal. Next Thursday we're voting on a proposed policy for how our reporters can describe what AI systems think, want, believe, or intend, and I've been asked to draft the committee's decision memo. The trigger is a mess from last month: a reporter quoted a chatbot saying "I'm afraid of being shut down" and we ran it straight, no context; separately, a tech reporter wants to write that a model "intends to deceive" when it fabricates a citation, and another wants to say an agent "broke a promise" when it abandoned a task. The copy desk is split, and I keep going back and forth on Dennett's intentional stance — is adopting it just a useful predictive shortcut we can legitimately use in quotes, or does it quietly smuggle in a claim about inner states these systems don't have?

I'd like you to write the memo. It should recommend a concrete, actionable policy — what language is allowed, what's banned outright, what needs a disclaimer or a follow-up sentence — with reasons my colleagues can actually push back on. And please take seriously the objection that refusing to say an AI "believes" anything is itself a controversial metaphysical position (eliminativism, or hard instrumentalism, or whatever you want to call it) that could make our reporting worse by stripping out the vocabulary readers use to make sense of these systems. A couple of committee members keep invoking the distinction between original and derived intentionality as if it settles the question, and I'm not convinced it does, so maybe address that too. Aim for something I can circulate Monday, around 800–1200 words, formal enough for a memo but not stiff.
