I am in a graduate seminar on philosophy of history, and for the past two weeks we have been working through the contrastive account of why-questions—the idea that any why-question has a topic, a contrast class, and a relevance relation, and that what counts as an explanation depends on how those are set. Over dinner a friend who studies early modern China claimed that the famous Needham question about why modern science emerged in Europe rather than China, and the related Great Divergence question about why industrialization took off in Britain rather than China, are not real questions at all but parochial ways of projecting a European outcome onto the rest of the world; I want to know whether that charge can be made precise. Please take the historical episode itself as the case: around 1750, parts of the Yangtze delta and England had roughly comparable levels of commercialization, agricultural productivity, and in some measures living standards; by 1850, Britain had undergone an industrial revolution and China had not. Historians have offered coal, colonies, institutions, property rights, the unification of the Chinese empire, the timing of the divergence, and the choice of units of analysis as candidate explanations. Analyze this episode through the lens of contrastive explanation. I want a disciplined mapping—what is the explanandum, what are the competing contrast classes (Britain versus China, England versus the Yangtze delta, Europe versus Asia, Britain versus the rest of Europe), what counts as a cause under a difference-making account versus a production account, and what work the relevance relation is doing—and then an honest account of where the theory runs out. Does the contrastive framework resolve the debate, or does it merely relocate the dispute into the choice of contrast class? Are there features of the episode—macro-historical singular causation, absent causes, equifinality, the lumping problem of whether Britain and China are even the right units to compare—that the theory misdescribes or cannot handle? And run it the other way: what does the Great Divergence debate show about the contrastive theory of explanation—confirmation, anomaly, or a case it never anticipated?

I am not asking for a bibliography or citations; I want the analysis, with the specific moves named. Be explicit about what is settled among historians and philosophers of explanation and what is genuinely contested; do not present a contested reading as the consensus. If you conclude that the question of why Britain and not China is not well posed, say so directly and explain what would have to be fixed for it to become well posed.
