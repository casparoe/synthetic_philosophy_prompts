I am in a metaethics reading group this term, and one of the members — an economist who works on monetary policy and is a fairly committed anti-realist about morality — keeps returning to a comparison that I can neither accept nor confidently resist. His line is that moral facts are like the value of money. Money is real enough, he says: it structures almost everything we do, statements about it are straightforwardly true or false, and it is not a matter of anyone's private opinion what a thing costs. And yet it exists only because we collectively treat it as existing, and its standing is not backed by anything that is valuable in itself. On his account the moral realist is in the position of a goldbug who cannot see that even gold's monetary standing was a convention, and who therefore mistakes a particularly stable social arrangement for a mind-independent fact. He uses the comparison to argue that the realism debate is a hangover from a discredited picture of value, that when we call moral judgments objective we can mean no more than we mean when we call an exchange rate objective, and that anti-realism therefore costs us nothing in seriousness or authority.

What I want from you is an audit of the comparison, not an adjudication of the underlying metaethics. Lay out explicitly what is supposed to map onto what, then tell me where the mapping breaks. I would like the disanalogies separated into the ones that actually bear on the argumentative work he is doing with the analogy and the ones that are merely picturesque, since I suspect most of the ones I can generate on my own are of the second kind and I want to know whether the first kind exists. Tell me what features of the money case the comparison quietly imports into the moral case without arguing for them; tell me what the analogy presupposes rather than establishes — I have a suspicion that whether there is anything playing the role of a gold standard is close to the whole question, and that the comparison answers it by fiat rather than by argument. Show me the ways the comparison can be run in reverse, and say who gets to run each of them and how much damage it does: in particular, whether the fact that money is mind-dependent yet supports objective truths about prices tells in favour of a constructivist middle position rather than against realism as such, in which case the analogy would support something considerably weaker than his conclusion. Then give me a verdict on whether his conclusion survives once the load-bearing disanalogies and the smuggled imports are on the table, and if it does not, what a better analogy would be and what that replacement costs. You can assume I know the standard taxonomy — non-cognitivism, error theory, response-dependence, constructivism — so please do not spend space introducing it.

Return the whole thing as a single JSON object with exactly these fields. analogy_as_used: the comparison stated in one sentence, in the form in which it is doing argumentative work. intended_conclusion: what he is using it to establish. correspondences: an array of objects, each with source_term (the money side), target_term (the metaethics side), fit (one of exact, close, partial, forced), and note (one sentence of justification). load_bearing_disanalogies: an array of objects, each with feature, on_the_money_side, on_the_morality_side, and effect_on_the_argument (one or two sentences). incidental_disanalogies: an array of strings, each a disanalogy that does not affect his argument, with a clause explaining why it does not. smuggled_imports: an array of strings, each naming a feature of the money case that gets carried over without argument. question_begged: a short string naming what the analogy assumes rather than shows. reverse_runs: an array of objects, each with run (the reversed use of the analogy), who_can_make_it (realist or anti-realist), and damage (one sentence). verdict: an object with conclusion_status (one of survives, survives_in_weakened_form, does_not_survive) and one_paragraph (at most 150 words). better_analogy: an object with candidate, why_better, and new_costs.
