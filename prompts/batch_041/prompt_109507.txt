I retired from the biology department about six years ago and, to keep busy, I've been volunteering at our small county natural history museum, mostly typing the insect collection into a new database. We've got about 40,000 pinned specimens and a lot of them came in from old collections with inconsistent labels, so several times a day one of us hits the same question: is this one specimen that got catalogued twice, or whose label got recopied at some point, or is it two seperate specimens that just look identical on paper? The whole point of the digitization is that these records go out to a public database and get used for range maps and loan records, so a wrong merge looses the provenance and a wrong split invents a specimen that never existed. The volunteers are retirees and grad students with about five minutes per record, and the curator has to sign off on anything that would touch a type specimen.

What I want from you is a procedure we can actually run, not a lecture. The questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, where it stops and says "these are two records of one specimen" or "these are two specimens," and where it stops and hands the thing to the curator because the question turned out to be a judgment call rather than something you can look up.

Here's what got me thinking about it this way. I've been reading a little philosophy since I retired, and I ran into Leibniz's principle that there can't be two things differing only in which thing they are, no numerical difference without some other difference, and then Max Black's 1952 paper in Mind where he imagines a universe with nothing in it but two identical iron spheres. I gather the principle isn't obviously safe, and that much of the argument is over whether things like "being this particular one" or "sitting in this drawer" count as properties, and whether the principle is meant to cover only a thing's intrinsic properties or all of them including its relations. I also half remember from quantum mechanics that identical particles are treated as genuinely indistinguishable, so that swapping two electrons doesn't give you a new state, and I'd like you to say whether that's actually relevant here or whether I'm just showing off something I don't remember properly. Use whatever of this is really doing work and drop the rest; don't pad it with name-dropping.

Two things I want you to be honest about. First, where the procedure is quietly assuming something metaphysical that might be false. I suspect a rule like "if every field matches, merge them" is exactly that, and I'd like to know what it's assuming, since our records only ever list a handful of properties out of all the properties a specimen has. Second, where the procedure will misfire even if that assumption is fine, and which distinctions have to survive the simplification for the volunteers.

Then run it on three cases:

1. Two beetles, same species, same collector, same date, same locality, same field number; one card has "dupl." penciled in the corner, and they're filed in different drawers.
2. Two cards identical in every field we record, same drawer, no distinguishing marks, same handwriting throughout.
3. A cluster: card A matches B on collector and date, B matches C on locality and number, but A and C disagree on species and date.

Include the case where your procedure tells me something I don't want to hear. My hunch is that the answer on case 2 will come out as "it depends on something you can't read off the label," and if so, say so plainly, tell me what the default should be, and say who ought to decide.

Finish with a single last line in exactly this form, giving your verdict on case 2: \answer{MERGE} or \answer{SPLIT} or \answer{ESCALATE}.

Please write in American English.
