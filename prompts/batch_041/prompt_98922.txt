I am a doctoral candidate working on the philosophy of biology, and for several months now a reading group I belong to has been going round the question of how to define life — or whether it can be defined at all — without anyone shifting position. The same cases surface every week and the same moves are made. One camp treats life as a genuine natural kind and tries to specify the conditions: a system that metabolises, maintains itself far from equilibrium, reproduces with heritable variation, and so evolves; on this view viruses and plasmids fall outside because they lack autonomous metabolism, while a minimal synthetic cell or an obligate intracellular parasite can be argued either way. A second camp holds that 'life' is a cluster or family-resemblance concept with no single essence, so that the hunt for necessary and sufficient conditions is simply a mistake — the operational formula astrobiologists tend to favour (roughly, a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution) is on this view a sorting device rather than a definition. A third and more radical camp wants to retire the category altogether, on the grounds that it does no explanatory work and merely marks where our chemistry-based biology happens to stop. And then there are the genuinely awkward cases — viruses, viroids, prions, transposons, so-called xenobots, and the more speculative suggestion that a sufficiently autonomous artificial or purely informational system ought to count — which each side recruits as evidence rather than treating as a difficulty.

My own suspicion, which I would like you to test rather than simply endorse, is that the impasse is not really a disagreement about biology at all. It looks to me as though the participants are answering different questions under a single heading: the origin-of-life researcher wants a criterion that carves the transition from non-living to living chemistry at its causal joint; the astrobiologist wants a detection rule that will not embarrass her when applied to an exoplanet or a subsurface ocean; and the philosopher of biology wants to know whether 'life' names a natural kind that figures in explanations. If that is right, the dispute may be closer to the stalled arguments over 'species' or 'planet' than to an ordinary empirical disagreement — a matter of divergent purposes and divergent standards for what a definition is for. What I want from you is a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement: whether it is verbal, substantive, or several questions wearing one word; which of the contending positions, if any, is really answering a different question from the others; and whether the awkward cases are doing genuine evidential work or merely rhetorical work. I am especially interested in whether it helps to split the single question into independent axes — metabolism, self-maintenance, heritable replication, evolution by natural selection, and goal-directed autonomy — which need not covary, and then to ask of each axis separately whether it is a matter of degree. Having done that, tell me what would actually count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a principled decision to stop asking, or a demonstration that some particular version of the question is well-posed and answerable. Please reason from the positions as I have set them out rather than surveying the literature, and write your answer in British English.
