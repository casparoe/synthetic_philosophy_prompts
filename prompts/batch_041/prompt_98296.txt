I'm a novelist, about two-thirds of the way through a book whose plot turns on the slow unraveling of a family holding company: four generations of a New England family whose fortune has come down intact, and a fifth generation now asking what it is owed and by whom. I'm writing the scene I have been avoiding for months, a long argument at a wedding, on a stone terrace after midnight, between two guests who have been circling each other for years and who are both a little drunk and entirely serious. One is a trusts-and-estates lawyer who has spent her career advising families like the one in my novel and who has come to believe that the only inheritance tax worth defending is a duty of the kind the Italian philosopher Eugenio Rignano proposed in the 1920s, one that rises with the number of times a given holding has already passed by inheritance or gift, so that wealth the dead person actually built is treated far more leniently than wealth that has now been inherited a second, third, or fourth time; she is drawn to it precisely because it promises to dissolve dynastic fortunes gradually rather than confiscate them at a stroke, leaving the bequest motive intact for anyone who is still building. The other is a political philosopher who thinks the generational-age idea is a category mistake and that the right unit of taxation is the recipient, not the donor: a lifetime cumulative tax on everything a person receives by gift or bequest, paired with a universal endowment paid to everyone at majority, so that what gets equalized is the starting position of persons rather than the duration of families.

Please draft that dialogue. Both characters must be very good: the lawyer as morally serious as the philosopher, the philosopher as comfortable with the mechanics as the lawyer, and neither permitted to win on charm or to be handed the best lines. I want them to press each other on the questions I keep getting stuck on. Is the number of prior transmissions itself a morally relevant fact, or only a rough proxy for something else, and if it is a proxy, why this one rather than the size of the fortune, the closeness of the relation, or the recipient's own means? Is the proper object of concern the dead donor's estate, the living recipient's windfall, or the family as an institution persisting across generations, since each answer points to a different tax and a different theory of what equal opportunity is for? How does the bequest motive actually behave when the charge falls on the third or fourth transfer rather than the first, and does the lawyer's scheme really preserve the saving and building it claims to protect, or does it simply relocate the moment at which a family begins to plan around the state? And how does either scheme survive the familiar machinery of avoidance, generation-skipping trusts, lifetime gifts, insurance, the corporate form, with the lawyer conceding what her scheme cannot do and the philosopher then having to say whether his does any better. I would like at least one exchange in which the philosopher lands a real hit and the lawyer visibly modifies her position, and at least one in which she does the same to him, so that by the end each has given ground on something and neither has prevailed by invoking an authority the other would not accept.

Do not resolve it. The scene should end with the disagreement sharper than when it began and the two of them still on speaking terms, since I need both to remain sympathetic to the reader. Somewhere between twelve hundred and eighteen hundred words, written as prose rather than a transcript, though they may interrupt and talk over each other, and you may name them or leave them unnamed as you prefer. The one thing I would rather you avoid is the standard tour of the philosophical literature on inheritance: I don't need the labels of the positions, I need two people who have thought about this for thirty years and can hear the weak joint in each other's argument.
