Ok so I'm in this seminar on science and technology policy and we keep going back and forth about nuclear power, and I want to work something out that isn't just the usual pro/con recycling. Take the two episodes: Germany deciding after Fukushima to shut its reactors down (and actually finishing the job in 2023) versus France keeping its fleet running and now talking about building more. What I want is for you to analyze both through one specific concept, the risk/uncertainty distinction -- the Knightian one, where "risk" means you can put probabilities on the outcomes and "uncertainty" means you genuinely can't, and where one of the possible outcomes is catastrophic and possibly irreversible. My working hypothesis, which I'd like you to stress-test rather than flatter: the German position only hangs together if nuclear is treated as uncertainty (unknown failure modes, unknown waste futures, proliferation chains nobody can model, and above all irreversibility), while the French position only hangs together if it's treated as a risk with a rough distribution you can price and insure against. If that's right, then alot of what looks like a disagreement about facts is really a disagreement about which box the technology goes in, and no amount of new data settles it. Tell me where that's too neat.

Second half of what I'm after, and it's the part I'm least sure about: I want the analysis to run backwards too, from the cases back onto the concept. So which features of the two stories does the risk/uncertainty frame actually capture (I'd guess: the irreversibility, the way both sides invoke the future, the asymmetry in who has to carry the burden of proof), and which does it flatten or misdescribe? My suspicion is that it flattens the cultural and identity stuff -- the German anti-nuclear movement wasn't doing decision theory, it had a whole picture of what kind of society it wanted -- and that Chernobyl and Fukushima function less as evidence than as salience shocks that moved a debate that was already settled emotionally. And one of my friends keeps saying Germany just did "risk laundering": traded a risk it found dread-inducing for a bunch of diffuse risks it found easier to live with (import dependence, more coal in the interim, whatever the grid does in a bad winter). Is that a real category or is he just being clever? I want to know whether these two cases show the risk/uncertainty line is doing genuine explanatory work, or whether it's one of those distinctions that looks razor-sharp until you actually apply it. Don't hand me a summary of the debate -- I know the standard talking points -- I want the mapping, and then an honest account of where it breaks.
