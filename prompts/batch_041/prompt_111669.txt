Alright, so I retired a couple years ago after 35 years in experimental particle physics, and I've been filling the time by reading philosophy, which is how I ended up stuck on Hume's chapter on miracles. What gets me is that his case isn't really philosophical at the bottom — it's a pile of empirical claims about how testimony actually behaves. Reports get embroidered as they pass from person to person. People love a wonder and will shade a story to keep an audience. Miracle stories cluster where record-keeping is lousy. Rival religions' miracles cancel each other out. Alot of that machinery is running on assumptions about the world that dont hold anymore, and I want to know what he'd do with the upgrade.

So: drop Hume into today. Give him a modern scientific education and full access to our instruments, but leave his 18th-century temperament and his own arguments intact, and tell me what he'd conclude about miracle reports now. I don't want a generic rationalist answer, I want a prediction about that specific guy, warts and all — he was vain, and he liked winning arguments. Three things I especially want you to commit on:

1. His test is comparative, not absolute: a report stands if believing the testimony false would be more miraculous than the event reported. Modern physics hands you competing hypotheses with astronomically small probabilities — a five-sigma threshold, a detector malfunction that would require twenty independent things to fail at once. Does he take his own test seriously and accept the well-documented anomaly, or does he quietly downgrade the test to a rule of thumb once somebody attaches a number to the competing hypothesis? My money's on the second, but tell me if I'm wrong.

2. Under quantum mechanics a single event can't violate a statistical law, so there's nothing left for a violation of the laws of nature to be. Does he insist that laws be exceptionless regularities, in which case physics has dissolved his category out from under him, or does he relocate miracles to the level of improbable runs and thereby make them boring?

3. His second-half arguments are mostly empirical generalizations about credulity and rumor, and the world they describe is gone: copying is lossless so nothing degrades as it spreads, everybody carries a camera so the witnesses now generate the primary record, and we can fabricate convincing video of anything. Which of those arguments does he keep, which does he drop, and does anything survive the transplant?

And the thing I actually care about: does Hume come out of this as the patron saint of the replication crisis, or does he end up embarrassed about what people do with him?
