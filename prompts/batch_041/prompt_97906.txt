I am a doctoral candidate working on material constitution, and I want to press you on one question rather than invite a general survey. Please give me a single credence, and then the decomposition that produces it.

The proposition: in the canonical statue-and-clay case — a sculptor shapes a lump of clay into a statue, and the statue is later destroyed (squashed, say) while the clay persists — the statue and the lump are numerically distinct material objects, both wholly material and exactly co-located for as long as the statue exists.

Give me your number, then break it into the sub-questions it actually depends on, each with a rough probability and a one-line justification, followed by an explicit statement of how those sub-probabilities combine into your final figure. I expect the following terms to do most of the work, and I would like each addressed:

1. Whether a question of numerical identity between material objects is well-formed independently of a sortal, or whether identity is always sortal-relative — and, relatedly, whether the lump is best understood as a countable thing at all rather than as a quantity of stuff; if identity is sortal-relative, whether any sortal is privileged enough to settle the case.

2. Whether the statue and the lump genuinely differ in their de re modal properties — the lump but not the statue can survive being squashed, the statue but not the lump could not have been formed from a different portion of clay — and whether any such differences are intrinsic to the objects rather than imposed by how we describe them.

3. Whether a difference in de re modal properties is sufficient for numerical distinctness, or whether modal predicates attach to descriptions rather than to objects.

4. Whether the principle that two material objects cannot wholly coincide is a substantive metaphysical constraint or an artifact of our counting practices.

5. Whether a four-dimensionalist or stage-theoretic treatment of persistence answers the puzzle or merely relocates it.

I am also testing a hypothesis of my own, and I would like you to treat it as an additional sub-question rather than as a premise. Call it modal deflationism: the thesis that every apparent de re modal difference between the statue and the lump is a difference in the descriptions under which we consider a single object, and that once the descriptions are held fixed the modal differences disappear. I want to know how much of your credence in the proposition rides on modal deflationism being false.

For context, my own credence sits near 0.6, though I suspect it is inflated by attachment to the deflationist program.

Finally, tell me which single sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and name the argument or consideration that would move that sub-question enough to shift your overall credence by roughly 0.1 or more.
