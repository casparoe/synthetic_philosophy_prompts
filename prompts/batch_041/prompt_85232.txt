I run the cost-benefit analysis unit in the central regulatory-review office of a national government. My analysts and I write the benefit-cost analysis for roughly fifty major rules a year — appliance safety standards, disclosure requirements for industrial chemicals, efficiency mandates, that sort of thing. The authorizing statute obliges us to publish, for each rule, a best estimate of net benefits over a thirty-year window, and the commission that signs off will not accept an analysis it cannot defend with a number; the whole office has six to eight weeks per rule, the analyst doing the work holds a master's in economics and no philosophical training, and I have been told to write the standing methodology note that the unit will follow from here on. The note has to fit on a few pages and be something a competent analyst can actually run.

The fight I need to settle in writing is about what to do when the thirty-year window is a fiction. At last month's meeting our vice-chair argued that for a whole class of rules the consequences that matter are not merely uncertain but unavailable to us — the effects on innovation, on the direction of technological development, on institutional trust, on the far future — and that since those effects may well swamp the ones we can measure, publishing a point estimate is not analysis but rhetoric with error bars. The other commissioners say this is an excuse for paralysis, and they have a point: a rule whose measurable effects are strongly negative and whose unmeasurable effects are anybody's guess is still a rule somebody has to decide on. I have seen this problem called "cluelessness" in philosophy I have skimmed, though I cannot tell whether the people writing about it mean what our vice-chair means. What I want from you is a procedure, not a position paper: the questions an analyst asks, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; a workable test for when the long-run effects are unknowable in the strong sense (my own suspicion is that the distinction that matters is between not knowing an effect's magnitude and not knowing its sign, and I want that suspicion either vindicated or refuted); and then, when the test fires, what the office is entitled to do instead — report a range, declare the comparison indeterminate, or fall back on some other criterion such as robustness across scenarios, precaution, avoiding irreversible harms, or preserving future options. Since I do not want the unit smuggling in a moral theory under the heading of method, the procedure should say explicitly which fallbacks follow from the statute's own logic and which are substantive value choices that only the commission can make, and it should mark the point at which the analyst stops and hands the decision upward.

Run the finished procedure on three rules and show me where each outcome was fixed. First, mandatory child-resistant packaging on a household product: compliance costs a few dollars per unit, injuries prevented are measurable inside the window, and I can think of no plausible mechanism by which the rule changes anything after year thirty. Second, a requirement that manufacturers disclose the composition of a widely used industrial chemical: compliance costs are estimable, but the rule plausibly redirects research in ways no one can model, and I genuinely do not know whether the net effect on welfare is positive or negative. Third, a rule restricting advertising to children that I happen to favor on grounds I would rather not examine too closely, and that I suspect the procedure will tell me to report as indeterminate or to decide on a proxy — I would like to see the procedure return that verdict anyway and defend it against me. For each case, give the output and flag the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as method. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do, and answer one larger question honestly: is the difficulty a defect in our methods that better economics would fix, or is it a feature of the world that would afflict any decision procedure that cares about consequences — including, for instance, a rights-based or deontological analysis that we might adopt instead?
