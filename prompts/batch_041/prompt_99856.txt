I'm a novelist — historical fiction, mostly 19th century, the kind of stuff where a revolution happens in the background and my characters argue about it. Next month I've got a slot on a panel at a history conference (the panel is called something like "Causation and the Writing of History," I forget the exact title). I'm the token novelist. The room will be maybe forty people: mostly working social and economic historians of the period, a couple of lit-crit people, and one analytic philosopher of history who's known for rolling his eyes at "theory" imports but who will absolutely pounce if I say something sloppy. They're polite but they think novelists are tourists.

I want you to act as a philosopher of history and a metaphysician of causation who also understands how these panels actually go. Think carefully, and before you write anything, spend a beat working out the strongest version of my position so your objections hit the real thing and not a straw man. Then give me a Q&A prep sheet.

Here's my thesis, roughly. Big historical events — take the 1848 revolutions, or the outbreak of any war you like — are usually overdetermined in a strong sense: there were several causal chains, and more than one of them was *independently sufficient*. So the standard counterfactual test ("if we'd removed X, would it still have happened?") misfires, because removing one sufficient chain still leaves the others. I want to insist this is NOT the same as joint causation, where you need both causes together; historians constantly run these two together and it wrecks the whole inevitability-versus-contingency debate. And I want to claim the novelist's multi-threaded narrative is actually a better fit to this structure than the historian's single-thread story, which I think is doing moral accounting and rhetoric, not causation.

So: what will this room actually throw at me, ordered by how much damage it does? Phrase each objection the way that particular person would phrase it — the metaphysician's version, the archival historian's version, the contingency-loving political historian's version, the lit-crit person's version. For each, give me a two-minute spoken reply and a note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should just concede up front to buy goodwill, and what I absolutely have to hold. And include the one question I'm definately hoping nobody asks. Keep it practical — I have to say all of this out loud, in a room, with no slides and maybe a glass of wine in my hand.
