In January 1976 the shop stewards' combine at Lucas Aerospace, a British defence and aerospace firm facing mass redundancies, published an Alternative Plan for their company. Rather than fight the new technology, the combine accepted it and demanded a hand in shaping it: the plan set out over 150 designs for alternative products — wind turbines, heat pumps, hybrid cars, medical equipment — to be built by converting military capacity to socially useful output, and insisted that workers, not management, should decide what technology was developed and to what end. Management was hostile, and the plan was never implemented. Analyze the episode through Marx's views on technology and automation.

Begin from what that account commits him to: the Capital argument that machinery is an instrument of capital against labour — displacing and deskilling workers and breaking their organisation — together with his view that machine-breaking mistook the machine for the social relation that set it to work; and the Grundrisse claim that as large industry develops, the creation of wealth comes to depend less on labour time than on the general state of science, with social knowledge itself becoming "a direct force of production". Then say what Marx's framework would say the combine faced and what it implies they should have done, and whether his distinction between machinery as a use-value and machinery as fixed capital — between the hardware and the social form that employs it — licenses the combine's wager that the same machines, differently designed and owned, could serve need, or undercuts it. Finally, run the analysis the other way: does Lucas confirm the theory, register as an anomaly for the claim that developing the productive forces drives emancipation, or present a case Marx never anticipated — workers taking over capital's own planning and losing on terrain the theory does not map?
