I'm the editor of a mid-sized quantitative social-science journal and I've got a paper on my desk that has turned into a genuine fight on the editorial board, so I need to write an actual memo instead of just ruling on it myself. The paper reports a strong association the authors found by mining a big administrative dataset; it replicates in two other independent datasets, the effect size is large, and our referee — a statistician who tried hard to kill it — says he can't find a flaw and believes the association is real. But nobody, including the authors, has the foggiest idea why it holds, and their mechanism section is, in the referee's judgment, not even wrong. He recommends publishing with the mechanism cut. My co-editor Dev wants to go further and invent a standing article type for cases like this, which he's calling "oracle papers" — his line is that if a claim is true and it replicates, the journal has done its job, and explanation is somebody else's problem that will sort itself out eventually. I half agree and half think he's missing something, and what I keep coming back to is the Meno, where true belief gets compared to one of Daedalus's statues: a fine thing while it stays put, but it runs off unless you fasten it down by working out the reason, and that's supposed to be the whole difference between knowledge and right opinion even though both of them get you to Larissa. The trouble is that when I try to say what the fastening actually adds — over and above our taste for tidy stories — I end up with either a platitude or with the annoying thought that if a true belief already gets you where you're going, then bolting a justification onto it adds nothing at all, since the value of the good process just gets swallowed up by the value of the good outcome. So I need help turning a vague intuition into an argument I can actually defend in front of people who outrank me.

Act as someone who knows the epistemology of knowledge and understanding cold but who has also sat on an editorial board and knows what a decision memo looks like. Before you write anything, brainstorm for yourself: lay out the strongest case for Dev's position in its most persuasive form, because I have to hand him something he can't just wave away, and then tell me where it breaks. Then write the memo I'll put in front of the board on Monday. It should recommend something concrete and votable — on this paper specifically (publish as is, publish with the mechanism stripped, revise and resubmit, reject) and on whether we adopt his oracle-paper category at all, and if so with what guardrails, e.g. whether we'd insist the authors strip every sentence of interpretation. Keep it to a page or so, let the reasoning do the work rather than the tone, and please don't just tell me that the Meno is about the value of knowledge — I already know that. What I need to know is what it implies for a journal that has to decide by Monday, and in particular whether there's any principled reason to care that these authors don't understand their own result, or whether I'm just being precious about a paper that is, by every check we have, correct. There's alot of pressure to publish it and move on, and no reading lists or citations please, I wont have time to look anything up.
