I'm a backend engineer (payments infra, nothing to do with any of this) and I've been messing around with Bongard problems for a few weeks as a hobby. You know the ones — twelve little figures, six in a left box and six in a right box, and you have to say what separates them. I got into them through Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, then found out they go back to a Soviet pattern-recognition book from the sixties, and that there's a board game version called Zendo that's the same idea with physical pieces. Hofstadter says somewhere that solving them sits close to the core of pure intelligence, which is the sort of thing that either means alot or means nothing. Anyway I wrote a blog post draft about them and I want you to tear into it. I have no formal philosophy training, so some of the framing is probably amateur hour, and I'd rather you be blunt about that than polite.

Here's the draft:

Everyone treats Bongard problems as a warm-up before the real work, and I think that's backwards. The whole point of the puzzle is that the data is compatible with infinitely many rules. You can always find one — the left figures are the six I've circled in red; or a rule that just names the six left figures one by one; or a rule that says "left if it's one of these six, otherwise follow some other rule." So the task isn't to find a rule that fits, it's to guess the one the designer had in mind. That's Hume's problem of induction in a nutshell: no finite run of examples pins down the generalisation, and nothing inside the examples can tell you which generalisation is the right one.

The fix people usually reach for is "pick the simplest rule," and I think that works better than philosophers give it credit for. Simplicity here isn't a vague aesthetic thing, it's description length, and description length is objective once you've fixed an encoding. So there really is a fact of the matter about which rule is the intended one — the puzzle has a determinate answer — it's just that our brains are running the compressor, and the encoding they use is the one they were trained on by a lifetime of looking at the world. I assume the current multimodal models mostly crush these now, since they seem to crush everything visual.

I also think Bongard problems are a problem for the Wittgensteinian family-resemblance story. The claim there is that real concepts have no necessary and sufficient conditions, just overlapping similarities. But every Bongard problem has a crisp necessary-and-sufficient condition, that's the entire format, and humans solve them by finding it. So at least some concepts really are definitions, and family resemblance is a story about words, not about concepts.

Relatedly, I think the hard part of these puzzles is perception, not reasoning. Once you've pulled out the features — holes, convexity, straight versus curved, symmetry, inside/outside relations, how many separate blobs — the search over candidate rules is a small combinatorial problem, and machines are great at small combinatorial problems. So Bongard problems are a vision benchmark wearing a reasoning costume, and if they're still hard for models that's a fact about segmentation, not about concepts.

Which brings me to the thing that actually bugs me. People say Bongard problems are a pure test of intelligence because they're language-free and culture-free — no trivia, no vocabulary, just shapes. But when I tried to write my own set I couldn't produce a single one where I was confident there was only one natural rule. I'd settle on "the left ones are all concave," and my wife would immediately say "no, the left ones all have a straight edge on the bottom." Zendo was worse: people kept proposing rules the master hadn't thought of, and the running joke was that the real rule was just "whatever the master says yes to." So the intended rule is doing an enormous amount of work for something nobody can point to, and I suspect the puzzles measure how well you've absorbed a particular culture's habit of treating certain descriptions as the obvious ones. Which is a wierd thing to call culture-free.

My conclusion is that Bongard problems are a test of whether a system shares our priors, which is a stranger thing to test than intelligence, and probably not what Bongard was after when he was doing pattern recognition in the sixties.
