There is a long-running disagreement over the raven paradox, and I want the joint statement that its two camps could sign. Three claims each have independent pull:

(i) The instance criterion: a universal generalization is confirmed by its positive instances, that is, by objects satisfying both the antecedent and the consequent — so a black raven confirms that all ravens are black.
(ii) The equivalence condition: logically equivalent hypotheses are confirmed by exactly the same evidence — so anything that confirms that all ravens are black confirms that all non-black things are non-ravens, and conversely.
(iii) Non-black non-ravens — green chairs, white shoes — do not confirm that all ravens are black.

The three cannot all be true, and the two camps divide over which to surrender. The probabilistic camp keeps (i) and (ii) and rejects (iii): it takes confirmation to be, at bottom, the raising of a probability, so that a green chair does confirm the raven hypothesis, only by a vanishing amount, and it treats the pull of (iii) as a confusion between a genuine but negligible increment and the increments that matter in inquiry. The testing camp keeps (iii) and therefore restricts or rejects one of the first two: it holds that evidence confirms a hypothesis only when it issues from a procedure under which the hypothesis's falsity would have made the observed outcome less likely, and it concludes either that the instance criterion must be limited to relevant instances or that the equivalence condition must be qualified so that equivalent propositions are not thereby equivalent hypotheses under test.

Both camps grant that the probability calculus is not in question as mathematics. They differ over what 'confirms' should mean, over how much of the sampling procedure belongs to the evidence, and over whether the equivalence condition constrains the confirmation relation itself or only the contents of hypotheses.

Write the joint statement. It should contain:

1. A numbered list of claims both camps can sign — about the raven case itself, about the relations among (i), (ii) and (iii), about what the probability calculus does and does not settle, and about what would count as progress. No item may be vacuous, and none may be worded so as to smuggle in one camp's position; where a claim is acceptable to a camp only with a qualification, state the qualification.
2. The precise points of residual disagreement, each first in the wording the camp holding it would accept as its own, then in wording the other camp accepts as a fair rendering of the opposing view.
3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp — and, for each crux, a concrete case or argument both camps agree bears on it, together with each camp's prediction about how that case comes out, stated in advance and in checkable form.
4. For each camp, what it would count as a reason to abandon its own position.
5. A verdict on how much of the dispute is verbal: if some of it dissolves once 'confirms' is disambiguated, say which part and why; if the residue is substantive, say exactly what turns on it.

Where they sharpen matters, use cases of this kind, and add others: an object drawn uniformly at random from the whole universe proves to be a non-black non-raven; a non-raven known in advance to be a non-raven proves to be non-black; a non-black object proves to be a non-raven; a raven proves to be black; a background that already entails that there are no ravens; and a hypothesis stated with a 'grue'-style predicate — a thing is grue if it is first examined before a certain future date and green, or otherwise blue — rather than with a colour predicate. For each case you use, say what each camp predicts about the direction and the size of any change in probability, and whether that camp regards the case as a test of the hypothesis at all.

Format the result as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a table of disagreements, with the cruxes, their test cases and the camps' predictions placed after the table. The document must stand on its own: no citations, no attributions to named authors, no reading list.
