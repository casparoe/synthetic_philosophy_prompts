I got dragged into a two-hour argument at dinner last week that I cant stop thinking about. My partner announced she's fine eating oysters but not shrimp, on the grounds that shrimp are somewhere on the dial and oysters aren't anywhere on it at all, and then our friend Dave — insufferable, but not stupid — said that by that logic his smart thermostat has a setpoint it "wants" to hit, so it's somewhere on the dial too, and the whole thing is just a dial we crank up until it lands wherever we already wanted to eat. I'm doing a philosophy MA and I've got a seminar on animal minds this term, so I volunteered to bring in something that actually sorts the views apart instead of us yelling "well where do *you* draw the line" at each other for another two hours.

So: build me a decision tree. Give it back as exactly five numbered items, no preamble and nothing tacked on at the end. Each item is one split: the question in plain language, two or three possible answers, and next to each answer the position it commits you to. The questions have to be answerable by someone who doesn't already know the labels, so make them concrete — a thermostat with a setpoint, a shrimp, a bee, a whole ant colony (is it the colony that has status, or the ants?), a plant that mounts a chemical defence when it's chewed on, a chatbot with a reward signal. I don't care whose position is whose: no names, no citations, I'd rather have the view than the person. And definitely don't let it collapse into utilitarian-vs-Kantian, which is what every handout on this already does.

The first split I want is the one that keeps eating the argument: whether sentience is even the kind of thing you can have to a degree, or whether the "degrees" are really just degrees of our confidence about a binary fact. From there I want the tree to reach, as leaves, at least these: the threshold view, where you're either in the moral club or out; the graded view, where moral weight scales with capacity for welfare; the view that what matters isn't sentience but having desires, or a self that persists; the rational-agency view, where only rational agents count directly and everything else is a matter of our own character; the functionalist view where the thermostat's setpoint really is a tiny interest, just a very weak one; and the precautionary view, where the line gets drawn by what we can rule out rather than by what's true. If two of those come out indistinguishable on the five questions, flag it in the item where they meet rather than smuggling in a sixth question, and if some combination of answers lands nowhere — nobody actually holds it — say so and tell me whether that's because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's bothered to explore it.
