I am a high-school student, and a question that began as an idle argument with a friend has turned into something of a preoccupation: when we call a mug fragile, or sugar soluble, or a candle flammable, are we describing something genuinely present in the object, or are we only saying something about how the object would behave if certain things happened to it? The second answer has come to seem too cheap to me, because I can always construct a case in which an object's behavior and its apparent disposition pull apart. Here are three mugs I worked out for myself. The first has been treated with a substance that, at the very instant anything strikes it, briefly hardens it to the strength of diamond, so that it never chips and never breaks — though the ceramic underneath is, in itself, exactly as brittle as any other ceramic. The second is genuinely brittle, but spends its whole life packed in foam, so that it too never breaks. The third shatters the instant anything strikes it, but only because a small charge in its base detonates on impact; the material itself is as tough as a hammer. In the first case the mug seems fragile in itself yet never behaves as a fragile thing; in the second it seems fragile yet is never allowed to show it; in the third it behaves as a fragile thing would without being fragile. (I gather that cases of the first two kinds are sometimes called finks and masks, but I would rather the dialogue earn its conclusions than rest on the labels.)

I would like you to write a dialogue in which two philosophers — both of them sharp, both well informed, neither one a strawman — try to settle what these three mugs show about the nature of dispositions. Let the first hold that a disposition just is a matter of how the object would behave under the relevant conditions, or else is nothing over and above the object's underlying structure, and let that philosopher be allowed to repair the account rather than abandon it at the first difficulty: they should say precisely which refinement they need, why it is not ad hoc, and what it costs them. Let the second hold that dispositions are irreducible real properties — genuine powers — that no conditional, however hedged, can capture, and that the three mugs are the proof. They should answer each other's actual points rather than trade monologues: when one proposes a refinement, the other should say what it fails to explain and what is conceded in turn, and when either gives ground, they should be explicit about how much. I would especially like them to reach the question of whether the first philosopher's account can distinguish the mug that is merely prevented from breaking from the mug that is not fragile at all, and whether that distinction survives every refinement on offer. Let the dialogue end without a forced victor, but with each of them naming the single consideration that would most trouble their own position, and with a clear statement of what each side would need in order to win.
