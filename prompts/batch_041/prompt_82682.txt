I'm prepping a class on the "evo psych: science or just-so stories?" fight, and I want to open with something older than the usual adaptationism/spandrels complaint. Here's William James, The Principles of Psychology (1890), from the chapter on instinct:

"Instinct is usually defined as the faculty of acting in such a way as to produce certain ends, without foresight of the ends, and without previous education in the performance. ... The actions we call instinctive all conform to the general reflex type; they are called forth by determinate sensory stimuli in contact with the animal's body, or at a distance in his environment. ... His nervous system is to a great extent a preorganized bundle of such reactions -- they are as fatal as sneezing. ... At first this view astounds us by the enormous number of special adjustments it supposes animals to possess ready-made in anticipation of the outer things among which they are to dwell. ... The older writings on instinct are ineffectual wastes of words, because their authors never came down to this definite and simple point of view, but smothered everything in vague wonder at the clairvoyant and prophetic power of the animals."

I cant quite tell what he's actually claiming when he says instinctive actions "conform to the general reflex type" and that the cat chases the mouse without any notion of life or self-preservation. Is that a physiological claim (the mechanism really is a reflex chain), a methodological one (purpose-words explain nothing), or something subtler -- deflating teleology while still treating the behaviour as functional? And the part I care about for the class: is his jab at the "older writings" the same objection people now make to evolutionary psychology, when they say "cheater detection" or "mate guarding" is a purpose-name pasted onto behaviour? Or does James's own picture actually license adaptationist explanation, since he says the nervous system is preorganized "in anticipation of" specific outer things -- i.e. he's an adaptationist about the machinery while refusing to put purposes in the animal's head? In other words, is he the first just-so-story critic or the first evolutionary psychologist? Tell me where my framing is most likely wrong, and what would change your verdict -- e.g. if the reflex-arc talk is doing something else entirely, or if I'm smuggling in a modern debate that his terms don't support.
