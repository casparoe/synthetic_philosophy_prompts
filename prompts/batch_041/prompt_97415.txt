I'm a second-year PhD student working on the epistemology of testimony, and I've ended up in a long arguement with my flatmate about conspiracy theories that has basically eaten my whole week. He reckons every conspiracy theory is just a failure of Bayesian updating and that's the end of it, which I think is far too quick, so I've been trying to build a small framework of my own to make the disagreement precise. I'd like you to act as a philosopher who works on conspiracy theories and social epistemology — someone who knows the literature well but isn't precious about it — and to start by brainstorming out loud and thinking carefully before you commit to anything, because I'd much rather see you find the cracks in my framework than defend it for me. Here's the framework, which I've stitched together from three or four places and probably mangled. First, a theory can be self-sealing: any piece of evidence that seems to tell against the conspiracy gets reinterpreted as further proof of it, so nothing ever counts as a disconfirmation. Second, a theory can preempt the evidence: it arrives with a built-in story about what the official account will say and why, so that by the time you actually hear the debunking you've already been told it was coming and told what it means, and it can't land the way a debunking normally would. Third, and this is the bit I care most about, there's the question of what work the label 'conspiracy theory' is doing when someone applies it — whether it's picking out a feature of the theory itself or just doing the job of a dismissal, so that calling something a conspiracy theory becomes a way of not having to engage with it at all. (I realise that last one is more particularist than the rest, I'm just not sure how far it stretches.)

Now take the satanic ritual abuse panic of the 1980s — the daycare cases, McMartin Preschool being the one everyone knows, allegations of a hidden nationwide network of abusers, children's testimony that we now think was shaped by the way they were questioned, and the alleged secret tunnels under the site that I'm fairly sure investigators went digging for and never found. Run that episode through the framework. I want the mapping done properly: what exactly was being alleged, who was supposed to be in on it, what would have counted as evidence for or against, and then which of my three elements actually show up and which don't. Does my diagnosis match what we think went wrong there, or does it misdescribe it — is this really a conspiracy theory at all, or is it closer to a moral panic or a contagion of testimony that nobody actually reasoned their way into, in which case the framework might be aimed at the wrong kind of thing entirely. Then reverse it, which is the part I'm most worried about: the self-sealing and preemption moves seem to show up just as much in conspiracy theories that turned out to be true (the Tuskegee business, Watergate, that sort of thing), so can my framework actually tell us anything ex ante, or is it just a fancy way of redescribing what hindsight has already handed us? And please be honest about where the framework goes silent rather than forcing it to speak — I'd far rather know the gaps than get a tidy answer.

Write it up in British English (organise, colour, that sort of thing), and don't bother with reading lists or citations, because I'm not going to look anything up — I just want the arguement itself.
