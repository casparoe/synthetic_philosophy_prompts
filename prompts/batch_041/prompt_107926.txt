For some time I have been keeping a list of my gut verdicts about a family of cases that all turn on one question: how much my present evidence tells me about which of several possible situations I am in, when those situations differ in how many instances of me they contain. In every case a fair coin is flipped before anything else happens, I know the protocol in advance, and the coin fixes how many instances of me there will be. By an instance of me I mean a being with my memories and my stream of experience up to the flip — someone who, from the inside, is me. I want to know what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to. The figure after each case is the credence I actually have that the coin landed heads.

1. Nights. Heads: I sleep through and wake once. Tails: I am woken, drugged back to sleep so that the awakening is erased, and woken again, so that each awakening is subjectively my first. I am awake now. 1/3.

2. Copies. Heads: one instance of me. Tails: two instances of me, made at the same moment with identical memories, in identical rooms, unable to communicate, both awake. I am one of them. 1/3.

3. Almost-copies. Heads: one instance of me. Tails: two instances, except that the second lacks a single trivial memory of mine — it does not remember my breakfast, though I do. I remember breakfast. 1/3.

4. Extras. Heads: I am the only instance of me. Tails: I am still the only instance of me, but the coin also creates a trillion ants, each with a thin stream of experience; the ants are not derived from me and share none of my memories. 1/2.

5. Birth rank. Long before I was born, a coin fixed how many humans would ever live: 100 billion if heads, 100 trillion if tails. I have since learned that I am roughly the 50-billionth human. 1/2.

6. Universe size. Before the universe began, a coin fixed how many observers it would contain: 100 billion if heads, 100 trillion if tails. The two hypotheses predict the evidence I actually have equally well, and differ in nothing else. 1/2.

The pair I cannot get to sit together is (3) and (4), or more generally (1)–(3) against (4)–(6). In (1)–(3) the coin changes how many instances of me there are, and I move; in (4)–(6) the coin changes how many minds there are, and I do not. But the near-copy in (3) differs from me by a breakfast, and the ants in (4) differ from me by a great deal; if anything the smaller difference should be the one that fails to count, and it is the one that counts. Every way I draw the line feels like special pleading.

I am also torn about this one.

7. Blueprint. Heads: one instance of me. Tails: two instances, except that the second is assembled from a complete blueprint of me rather than copied from me — no matter and no causal path connects it to me; it merely ends up with my memories and my present state. I cannot choose between 1/3 (it is an instance of me, so it counts as the copy in (2) does) and 1/2 (nothing that ever happened to it happened to me, so it is a different person who merely resembles me).

And here are two cases I have not thought through, on which I would like the principle's verdicts.

8. Long day. Heads: I am awake and aware for one day, then it ends. Tails: I am awake and aware for a hundred days with ordinary memory, then it ends. I am asked on the first day, and I know it is the first day: I have no memory of an earlier day, and I know that would be so on either branch. (My hunch is 1/2, but I want the principle's answer.)

9. Conditional copy. Heads: one instance of me. Tails: a second instance of me is created only if a second, independent fair coin lands heads. (I have no idea what to say.)

My own tentative principle, which I suspect is wrong: count how many instances of me each hypothesis says there are, and weight the hypotheses by that count. Everything then turns on what makes something an instance of me rather than merely another mind that resembles me, and that is precisely where (3), (4) and (7) pull apart. "Shares my memories" fails (3); "is an observer" drives (6) to 1/1000; "is causally continuous with me" leaves (8) obscure. I cannot state the criterion.

What I want: the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, precise enough that I could apply it to a new case without asking you again; for each clause, the case that forces it; then your verdicts on (7), (8) and (9); and an honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and which I should give up. If the principle that saves every verdict is baroque, say so, and tell me which single verdict you would sacrifice for a cleaner one.
