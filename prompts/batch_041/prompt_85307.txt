I teach a graduate unit on future contingents, and I end it with Ted Chiang's Story of Your Life. The unit runs through the sea battle in Aristotle's De Interpretatione 9; Peirce's refusal to let future contingents be true or false at all; the Ockhamist reply that they are true or false but not thereby necessary; the supervaluationist successor, on which a prediction is true only if true along every branch, and the relativist one, on which it is true only when assessed from a later context; and the awkward standing of what is usually called the thin red line, the single branch that has to be privileged if a claim about what will happen is to be true while its rivals remain open. What I want from you is the reading of the story I have never managed to give. My students know it mostly through the film adaptation, and discussion always collapses into a free-will question that is not the interesting one. The interesting question is what Louise's cognitive situation commits the story to. Her knowledge of her own future is not inferred from evidence; it is nearer to memory or perception of events that have not yet happened, which seems to require that all times be equally real. Is that the same as the future's being determinate, or as its propositions having truth values, or is the story quietly conflating the three? The story also leans on a least-time principle, in which a ray of light is said to take the path it takes because of where it ends up; is that a serious model of a future-directed reality, or a metaphor trading on the confusion of a variational description with backwards causation? And where does the story resist the reading? What can grief and deliberation be for someone who knows the ending in advance, and does her case show us something the arguments cannot about whether a timeless view of one's own life can be inhabited at all? Be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the story to an illustration of a position in the debate, and do not bend the debate to fit the story. Close by saying what the story adds to the problem that argument alone does not.
