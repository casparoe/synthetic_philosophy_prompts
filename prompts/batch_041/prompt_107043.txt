So I'm taking this seminar on punishment — it's basically retributivists vs. consequentialists for fourteen weeks, and we keep going in circles about whether it matters *who* suffers, as long as the amount of suffering is proportionate and the deterrent actually works. My friend came up with a thought experiment that broke my brain alot: imagine a company offering what she calls penance substitution. You get sentenced, and instead of serving it you pay a licensed substitute who serves the sentence for you — real prison, real hard labour, real years, just not yours. The consequentialists in the room shrugged. If the deterrent effect and the public denunciation come out identical, where's the objection? The retributivists got wierdly angry, and I couldnt tell whether their objection was deep or just aesthetic. The people I found most interesting were the ones who think crime is basically taking an unfair advantage by skipping the restraints everyone else accepts — for them, paying someone else to carry your burden stops looking like punishment and starts looking like a fee, which seems like a real objection and not just a vibe.

Anyway, I want a short story that lives inside this mess. Setting: a mid-sized port city, twenty or thirty years out, where substitution is legal, tightly regulated, and quietly class-stratified — wealthy offenders buy back their years from substitutes who are mostly people with nothing else to sell. Follow one substitute serving someone else's sentence. I want at least one moment where the retributivist intuition becomes obvious and urgent to the reader, a scene where it's suddenly clear that the wrong person is being made to suffer and that this somehow matters. But I dont want the story to be a brief for one side — give me a scene where the consequentialist case also looks genuinely decent, maybe even kind. No neat verdict at the end, and nobody should explain the theories out loud; let the plot carry it.

A few details I'd love in there, rearrange as you like: the substitute signs a document acknowledging that the punishment is being credited to the other person, that what's being transferred is the moral ledger and not just the time; the substitute is in some ways a better person than the offender, doing it for love or for guilt about something else entirely, so the reader has to sit with the fact that the suffering landed on the more deserving of the two; and some mundane texture — the paperwork, the going rate per year, what the substitute's sister says about it. Around 2000 words is fine, longer if the story wants it.
