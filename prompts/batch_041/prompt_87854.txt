I am a novelist at work on a book about a nineteenth-century connoisseur who keeps a ledger of his pleasures—the taste of a particular oyster, the resolution of a difficult fugue, the memory of a humiliating victory, the smell of a childhood kitchen. He ranks them, trades one against another, and defends his life as a pursuit of pleasure, but when I try to write the ledger's rationale I find that the entries have nothing in common except that he wants them. The word 'pleasure' seems to name no single felt quality, only a role in his economy of desire; yet he treats it as a single currency. I know philosophers have long asked what pleasures have in common, but I want the precise name and shape of the problem, not a reading list. Is there an established term for this—the fact that a theory can call all good things by one name while the things so named share no common measure—and if the nearest term is not exact, please say what it misses, distinguish it from the problem of comparing unlike goods and from the idea that pleasures differ in kind, and tell me what the best carving is. If nothing fits, coin a term and define it, with boundary cases. Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as you would in a letter.
