I've been asked to give the toast at my niece's twenty-first birthday dinner next month, and I'm the only person in the family who both knows the whole story and is willing to stand up in front of everyone. The short version: my sister has a thyroid condition managed with a medication that carries a modest risk of a mild but permanent developmental effect if taken in early pregnancy, and the specialist recommended waiting six months while she switched drugs. She and her husband had already been trying for two years, she was thirty-eight, and they decided not to wait. My niece was born with the effect. It is mild, she knows exactly what it is, she is entirely fine, and she has strong opinions about the whole subject. Her younger brother was conceived after the switch and does not have it. About thirty people will be at the dinner: roughly ten of my niece's friends from the philosophy department where she is finishing her degree, who will recognise the shape of the puzzle the instant I start hedging, and twenty relatives and neighbours for whom any hedging will sound like evasion or, worse, like an insult to the girl. My niece's parents will be at the head of the table, her brother will be beside her, and I am an actuary by trade, so hedging is my native tongue.

Please write the toast. I want it to be honest — the decision really was a risk, and a reasonable person could have waited — while never, at any point, suggesting to my niece that she is a consolation prize, or that her life is being weighed against some hypothetical better-off version of herself who might have existed instead, or that her brother is the child who was meant to happen. The logic of the problem should be doing real structural work here: it should be shaping which reassurances I am allowed to offer and which I have to withhold, rather than being name-dropped as decoration. The relatives should come away thinking they heard a warm, slightly funny toast about a beloved niece; the philosophers should come away noticing that the warmth was only available because I got the logic right, and that I resisted the obvious sentimental shortcut. Do not resolve the puzzle in the closing line and do not turn any part of it into a lecture. Under five minutes, no jargon, and do not put words in my niece's mouth about how she feels about any of it — she will be sitting three feet away from me.
