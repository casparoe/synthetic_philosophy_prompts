After thirty-five years running experiments at the bench, I have spent my retirement reading philosophy of science and would now like to write something of my own. I am applying for a one-year visiting fellowship in a philosophy department and need a short research proposal — no more than about 900 words, and please open with the question in a single sentence. My half-formed idea is this: the replication crisis is almost always staged as frequentists versus Bayesians, but I suspect that framing answers a question the crisis never actually asked. What failed to replicate in the notorious cases was neither a decision rule's long-run error rate nor an agent's posterior; it was a claim about a population, advanced without the scope conditions under which it was meant to hold. I want to argue that the unit of replication — the thing that can genuinely succeed or fail — is a claim together with an explicit scope condition, and that neither paradigm, as ordinarily practiced, forces the researcher to state that condition, which is why the same data can count as a successful replication under one analysis and dissolve under another. Please turn this into a proposal: state the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, explain why the existing frequentist and Bayesian positions leave it open, set out what I would actually do and in what order across the year, say what a genuine result would look like and what would count as the project failing, and give an honest assessment of whether the scope fits the time. I will supply the citations and the literature myself, so leave those out.
