I have no academic training in this area, but I have been an amateur forecaster for years — prediction markets, calibration drills, that sort of thing — and I have read a fair amount of the philosophy of probability on my own, enough to follow the arguments though not enough to know which side is respectable. There is one dispute I keep running into that I cannot get past, and I would like you to tell me what kind of dispute it actually is rather than which side is right.

On one side are people who insist that a rational credence is always a single number: you may be uncertain, but if you are rational there is a definite probability you assign, and if you cannot produce one you are either ignorant of your own mind or dodging the question. On the other side are people who say a rational state of belief can be a set or an interval — that being somewhere between 0.3 and 0.6 on something can be the whole truth about your doxastic state, and not merely a confession that you have not finished thinking. The first camp calls the second's intervals an evasion and a refusal to commit; the second calls the first's single numbers a pretence of precision that the evidence cannot underwrite.

I have watched this go nowhere for as long as I have been paying attention. The same handful of exhibits gets traded back and forth. There is the urn case where a fair coin decides which of two urns you draw from and the urns' compositions are complementary unknowns: unconditionally you are exactly 0.5 on drawing a red ball, and then learning which urn was used — a fact you would have thought irrelevant to the colour of the ball — widens you to the whole unit interval. The precise camp treats that widening as a reductio of imprecision; the imprecise camp treats it as a genuine discovery about what learning can do. Then there is the reflection principle and what it may legitimately demand of your earlier self, the question whether an interval is just a second-order probability in disguise or whether the whole point is that it is not, and the question of which decision rule you are permitted to use once your credences are a set — maximin, something less timid, or nothing coherent at all.

My hypothesis, and I would like you to test it rather than assume it, is that the two sides are not really disagreeing about anything, or at least not about the thing they think they are disagreeing about. The precise camp looks to me to be answering "what is it rational to believe?", while the imprecise camp looks to be answering "what can a finite and honest enquirer actually commit to, and what should they do in the meantime?". If that is right, they are answering different questions under a single word, and no further urn cases will ever settle it. But it may be wrong. It may be that a substantive disagreement is hiding underneath — about whether rationality requires a unique act of judgement, or about what a credence is for — or that this is a verbal quarrel about the words "probability" and "belief" that would evaporate under a decent stipulation, or that the impasse is a sign the question is ill-posed rather than merely hard.

Give me a diagnosis, not a survey. Say concretely which parts of the standoff are genuine and which are artefacts of wording or of conflated questions, using what each side actually does with the dilation case, the reflection principle and the choice of decision rule as your evidence. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a theorem, a change of question, a stipulated definition — and be honest if you think nothing will move it, and why. If you think my hypothesis is wrong, say precisely where it breaks. Please write your answer in British English.
