Ok so I need help diagnosing something, and I'd rather you reason it out than tell me who said what, cause I already know the cast of characters.

There's a fight in aesthetics about whether you can get an aesthetic belief, or even aesthetic knowledge, from someone else's word. One camp says no. If you tell me a painting is beautiful, I can end up believing you, but I haven't judged it; a judgment of taste has to come from my own encounter with the thing. The other camp says that's mystery-mongering: testimony transmits knowledge in every other domain (I know it's raining, I know when the battle was, I know a proof checks out, all on your say-so), so why would beauty be the one place where belief by report doesn't count? And then the exchange just recycles. The optimists offer mechanisms -- deference to expertise, reliable testimony, justification -- and the pessimists say sure, but that's knowledge of a verdict, not an aesthetic judgment. The pessimists point out how strange it sounds to say you think something is beautiful when you've never seen it and couldn't say what to look for, and the optimists say that's a quirk of our psychology or of the word. This has been going on for decades and nobody has moved an inch.

My hypothosis, and I want you to actually test it rather than just agree: the two sides aren't disagreeing about the epistemology at all. The pessimists are making a constitutive or genetic claim -- about what it takes to have an aesthetic judgment at all, whether a state whose etiology is someone else's testimony even counts as one -- while the optimists are making an evidential claim about when a belief about aesthetic properties is justified. If that's right, each side's arguments are non-responsive to the other's question, and the whole thing is two questions wearing one name.

But I want to push past that too. Distinguish two things testimony might do: hand you a verdict, versus give you standing -- licence to judge in your own voice, to defend it, to be answerable for it. My hunch is the pessimist's real point lives in the second, and that standing isn't transmissable the way verdicts are. If that split is right, does the debate dissolve as verbal, or does each side win half of it?

So: give me a diagnosis, with reasons drawn from the actual moves each side makes, tell me where my hypothesis is wrong or only half right, say whether some hidden empirical or constitutive question is doing the work, or whether it just bottoms out in a starting intuition about the autonomy of taste that neither side can argue for, and finish with what would count as progress here -- a distinction, a change of question, a case that breaks the stalemate -- or an honest argument that nothing will, and why.
