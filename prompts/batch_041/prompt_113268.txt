I'm not an academic — I dropped out of a psychology degree about fifteen years ago — but I run a small monthly discussion group at my local library, mostly retired teachers plus one retired GP who argues with everything, and next month I've promised them a session on Daniel Dennett's heterophenomenology: the idea that you take what someone says about their own experience seriously as data, but as data about what they believe and say, not as a direct window onto what the brain is actually doing. I don't want you to explain heterophenomenology to me, I've read enough to have a rough grip on it. I want you to plan how I explain it. What has to be in place before the term even makes sense to that room — my instinct is they first need to feel the squeeze (we each only have first-person access to our own minds, but a science needs third-person data) before the method can land as a solution rather than a dodge, though I'm not sure that's the right first move or whether it just makes the whole thing look like a fudge. Then the order the pieces have to come in, and what depends on what.

The other thing I'm definately going back and forth on is which example carries the whole forty-five minutes. My plan is the blind spot: everyone does the thumb-and-card trick at their tables, they all sincerely report a seamless visual field, and then the reveal is that there's no signal at all in that patch — so the report is honest and true about what they say they see, and at the same time tells us nothing about whether anything got filled in. But I worry it's the example everyone has already half-heard. The other candidates are people feeling their phone buzz in a pocket with no phone in it, or the duller one, change blindness. Tell me which should carry it, and what the carrying example has to have and has to lack for the rest of the session to hang off it. And then the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually arrive rather than the order that's tidy: I'm betting a chunk of the room will decide this is just behaviourism with a new coat of paint, another chunk will hear it as Dennett calling people liars about their own experience, and the GP will want to know what it does with a patient who confabulates — insists he saw something he plainly couldn't have — since he'll say the patient is still the one authority on what it was like. I need to know where in the sequence to head each of those off instead of bolting them on at the end.

Finally, the one question I should put to the room at the close to see whether it's landed rather than just been politely absorbed, and what I should openly tell them we're skipping at this level and why.
