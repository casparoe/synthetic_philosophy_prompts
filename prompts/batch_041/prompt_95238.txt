I am at work on a novel whose central figure is the Victorian utilitarian Henry Sidgwick, and I have reached a chapter in which he is confronted with a piece of reasoning that postdates him. I cannot write the scene until I know how he would really have handled it. The reasoning is this. Take a long series of possible outcomes. The first holds one person in an hour of agony. Each later outcome doubles the number of people suffering and lowers the intensity of what each of them feels by a sliver, until the last holds an astronomical number of people each feeling something so faint that it barely registers. Step by step the series looks innocuous: the reduction is too slight to matter, and no one who exists in the earlier outcome is any worse off in the later one. Yet the first outcome is plainly better than the last. If better-than is transitive, one of those judgments has to go. Tell me which one Sidgwick would have surrendered, or whether he would have refused the choice altogether; where in his own system he would have felt the cost; what he would have held on to even at the price of absurdity; and how the argument would have struck him in his own idiom. I want his reasoning, not a generic utilitarian's, so that the chapter is his and not mine.
