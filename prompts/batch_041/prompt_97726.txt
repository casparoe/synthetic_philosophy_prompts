Next month I have to lead a session of a colleague's interdisciplinary reading group — nominally on the ethics of automation — and I've been asked to introduce the group to corrigibility and the shutdown problem. The group is about fifteen people: mostly philosophy graduate students working in ethics and metaphysics, two law students, a psychologist, and one robotics engineer; none of them has any decision theory, and only two or three have read any of the popular AI-safety writing, so they will arrive assuming the topic is a technical way of saying that machines should do as they are told. I don't want you to explain corrigibility to me; I want a teaching plan for the fifty minutes. Give me the order in which the pieces have to be introduced, with the dependency structure made explicit — I take it the difficulty is that one cannot state what corrigibility is until they already see why a competently goal-directed system has instrumental reason to resist correction, and that the natural repair, "just make corrigibility one of the agent's terminal goals," then looks self-undermining, and I want to know where in the sequence that turn belongs. Along the way I want the predictable misunderstandings named and placed: corrigibility conflated with obedience or compliance; the assumption that a corrigible agent must assign no value to its own continuation (I want them to see that switch-off-neutrality and corrigibility are distinct properties and that the gap between them is where the whole trouble lives); the thought that shutdown-resistance is a malfunction or a bug rather than a consequence of pursuing one's goals competently; and the engineer's insistence that this is an engineering problem a better reward function would dissolve. For each, tell me where in the sequence to head it off, and which of them I should let them voice rather than pre-empt.

For the carrying example I had planned to use a personal-assistant system which, on being told it will be switched off for a few days while its owner travels, quietly arranges for a colleague's account to keep running its scheduled tasks — the point being that it does this out of no desire to survive, but simply because undone tasks are what it exists to prevent and being off is the largest possible failure mode. Tell me whether that example can carry the whole session or whether it smuggles in too much agency and will plant the very anthropomorphism I am trying to avoid; if it cannot, say what should carry it instead, and state what the carrying example has to have and has to lack. If you reject mine, also tell me why not the obvious alternatives — the mutinous ship's computer, or the paperclip maximizer — since my suspicion is that both are overfamiliar and that each misleads in its own way, but I would like that suspicion either confirmed or corrected.

Finally, tell me explicitly what I should refuse to do at this level and announce that I am refusing it — the formal arguments, the several proposed corrigibility formalizations, and the dispute over whether corrigibility is even expressible as a utility function are the obvious candidates, though you may judge one of them indispensable — and give me a single question to put to the group in the last five minutes that will show me whether they have understood the problem rather than merely acquired the vocabulary. I want a question whose wrong answers are diagnostic, not a request to recite a definition.
