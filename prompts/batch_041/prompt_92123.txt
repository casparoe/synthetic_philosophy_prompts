I am a third-year graduate student in algebra, and there is a pattern in my own proofs that I have never found a name for. It appears whenever I use Zorn's lemma and the whole argument turns on the step where every chain in my partially ordered set is supposed to have an upper bound.

Three cases will make the pattern clear.

First, I want a maximal linearly independent set in a vector space. My collection is the linearly independent sets, ordered by inclusion, and I take the union of a chain. Any purported linear dependence in the union involves only finitely many vectors, each of which already appears at some stage of the chain, so the dependence would already have been visible inside a single member of the chain, which is impossible. The union is an upper bound, and I am done.

Second, I want a maximal proper ideal in a commutative ring. Again I take the union of a chain of proper ideals. If the union contained 1, then 1 would already belong to some ideal in the chain, and that ideal would have been improper. So the union is an upper bound, and I am done.

Third, I try the same move on the finite subsets of an infinite set, ordered by inclusion, and it collapses. The chain of initial segments has an infinite union, so it has no upper bound among the finite subsets at all, and the collection has no maximal element.

My first guess was that the difference is simply whether the union of a chain stays inside the collection, but that is circular, and it tells me nothing in advance about which collections are safe. My second guess, which I like better, is that the working cases are exactly those in which a violation of the defining condition would already be exhibited by finitely many elements: a linear dependence is finite, a stray 1 is a single element, whereas finiteness itself is a condition that no finite piece of data can refute, since every finite piece of an infinite set looks finite. On that reading the failure has nothing to do with the order I happen to put on the collection and everything to do with the condition that defines it. But I do not trust the guess, and I suspect my two guesses are not the same condition.

What I want is the concept, not the reading list. Is there a name for the property that my first two collections have and my third lacks? Is it a property of the collection, of the condition that defines membership in it, or of the ordering relation? If it is a property of the defining condition, does it have an exact statement, is it equivalent to anything more familiar, and is the equivalence with the union-of-a-chain step as tight as it looks, or does the union step require strictly less? If strictly less, give me the sharpest example you can and say why it is the sharpest. I also want to know whether the repair has a name: when the union escapes the collection, I have sometimes got the proof to go through by changing what I collect, replacing the objects I originally wanted with objects whose defining condition finitely many elements can settle — a maximal subgraph with no cycle where I had wanted a maximal finite subgraph, a maximal proper ideal where I had wanted a maximal finitely generated one — and then the union step works again. Is that a recognised move with a name, or is it folklore, and does it always work or only sometimes?

And I want the boundaries, not just the label. State precisely what the property is; what it is not, in particular how it differs from the weaker condition that the union step literally requires and from the compactness-flavoured conditions on ordered structures that I keep half-remembering; and where its edges are — which conditions on a collection of sets have it, which conspicuously fail to, and what happens in the degenerate cases.

Finish with a single last line of the form \answer{...} containing only the best name for the property, or, if you judge that there is no established name, the short label you would give it.
