**What survives of luminosity?** The standard argument, as I understand it, goes like this. Take a condition that comes in degrees and can change by steps too small to notice — the water getting slowly warmer, the lamp slowly dimming, whatever. Line up a series of cases running from clearly-not-C to clearly-C. If C were luminous, i.e. if whenever you're in C you're in a position to know you're in C, then at the first moment in the series where C obtains you'd be in a position to know it. But that moment is by construction indistinguishable, from the inside, from the moment just before, when C didn't obtain. Since knowledge requires that you not be wrong in nearby cases — some safety or margin-of-error condition — you can't know it there. So no condition of that shape is luminous, and nothing interesting about our inner lives is luminous. Fine, I accept all that. My claim is that the argument needs the sorites-style series. It works for comparative or degree-theoretic conditions, which are really relations between your state and a threshold on some dimension. But not every phenomenal state is like that. Some are categorical and binary: being in pain versus not, seeing the figure as a duck rather than a rabbit, having a certain occurrent thought, the sudden click of a gestalt shift. For these there's no imperceptible-steps series to build, because there are no intermediate states — you're either in it or you're not, and I want to say the state announces itself. So I defend a restricted thesis: categorical phenomenal states are luminous, and the KK principle holds for our beliefs about them, so if you know you're in one you know that you know. Call it *categorical luminosity*. The anti-luminosity argument leaves it untouched, because it only ever gets going on graded conditions. Two things I say to head off objections. First, someone will say the restricted thesis is boring, since the graded conditions are the ones we care about — whether I'm in pain *enough* to stop working, whether the light is bright enough to read. I reply that plenty of the epistemology of self-knowledge hangs on the categorical cases, so it isn't boring. Second, someone will say the KK principle is doing no work here and that luminosity and KK are just the same claim in different notation, since knowing you're in C and being in a position to know that you know are the same thing once knowledge is factive. I want to say they come apart — luminosity is about a state being self-intimating, KK is about the iterability of knowledge — but I notice that in the middle section I slide between the two without flagging it, and I suspect a referee would pounce on that. I also wave away the objection that there are borderline cases even for categorical states (a twinge, a barely-there ache) by just insisting on the word categorical, which I'm fairly sure is a fudge, because it seems like you could build a continuum from a clear pain down to no pain and rerun the whole thing.
