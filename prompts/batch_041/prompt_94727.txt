I am assembling a teaching instrument for a graduate seminar on the ethics of war, and I want it to do more than lay out the usual pro-and-con. The dispute over the moral equality of combatants keeps stalling because the disputants are not disagreeing about a single thing; the phrase hides several claims that can come apart, and a good part of the literature's heat comes from people answering different questions while using the same words. Please write a decision tree that forces the reader to commit to a claim before being told what that claim is called, and that distinguishes genuine disagreement from people talking past one another.

Entry point. The reader begins by endorsing something modest: that a soldier fighting for an unjust cause is not, merely in virtue of that, a murderer, so that there is at least something to be said for treating combatants on both sides alike. Every substantive branch should be reachable from that starting point. A reader who rejects even the modest claim should be routed to a terminal node of its own; I want to see what the tree does with the person for whom the starting point already concedes too much.

First stage, disambiguation. Before any substantive question, the tree must make the reader say which of the following they mean by equality: (a) equal permissions — each is permitted to kill the other; (b) equal liability — each is a legitimate target of the other, whether or not either is permitted to kill; (c) equal accountability — neither is blameworthy for killing the other, even if one of them was wrong to do it; (d) equal standing under the code of war — both are answerable to the same rules, and neither is punished for what those rules permit. These come apart, and readers who pick different letters should be sent down different branches, with a note at the fork stating what, if anything, a reader who chose (a) and one who chose (d) are actually disagreeing about.

Second stage, under equal permissions. The operative node is not whether the war is just, but whether, when a combatant on the unjust side kills, what he does is permitted or merely excused; take the two readings separately. If permitted, the next question is what the permission rests on: the enemy's liability to be killed, or something independent of liability, such as the lesser evil, military necessity, or the scale of what is at stake. If excused, the next question is what does the excusing: duress, invincible ignorance about the justice of his cause, or diminished responsibility for what a state does in his name. Then the sub-question I care most about: on the liability route, is liability grounded in the objective fact that the enemy is prosecuting an unjust war, or in his culpability for the threat he poses? Those answers come apart precisely where a soldier is objectively on the unjust side yet not culpable for it, and the tree should reach a distinct leaf on each side of that fork.

Third stage, under the remaining readings. For liability: is the symmetry of vulnerability a consequence of ordinary self-defence morality, or an artifact of the convention? For accountability: is the unjust combatant blameless because he did nothing wrong, or because he did something wrong under conditions that defeat blame? For standing: is the combatant's privilege a legal fiction, a convention justified by its effects, or a fair scheme of mutual restraint that binds those who comply with it?

Nodes. Every node must be answerable by a reader who has never heard a name for any of the positions. Build the nodes at least out of these cases: a conscript in a war he believes to be just but that is not; a volunteer who knows his side's war is unjust; a soldier on the just side who commits an atrocity in the course of fighting it; and a war in which each side is sincerely convinced of its own justice and neither is right. If a branch cannot be separated using any of these, replace the branch.

Leaves. Each terminal node should carry the name of the position, or "unnamed" if no one defends it, together with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. A dozen or so leaves is the right size; do not pad the tree to reach that number. If they can be located in the space, I want the following to appear: a view on which the symmetry is a consequence of what each side can know about its own cause; a view on which it is a legal fiction maintained because both sides need it; and a view on which the appearance of symmetry is really equal wrongdoing.

Finally, mark any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate and say whether they are two positions needing one more question or one position under two names; and list the combinations of answers that no position occupies, with a one-line diagnosis of each — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
