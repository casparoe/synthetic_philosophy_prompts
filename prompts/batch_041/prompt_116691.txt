I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on decision theory, and I want a single ninety-minute session, with about twenty students, built around a casino. The target is the St. Petersburg game, but I want to get past the version everyone has already met — the one where you compute the divergent sum, shrug, and reach for log utility — and put the students somewhere they cannot coast on that. My plan is that they play before they calculate, and that the calculation they eventually do is not the familiar one.

Here is what I want them to come away having felt, in this order. First, that the price they are willing to pay and the expected value of the game come apart by an enormous margin, and that this is not a failure of nerve on their part. Second, that the expected value cannot be rescued by the thought that repeated play will vindicate it: I want them to run the game often enough to watch the running average refuse to settle, because the rare enormous payoffs keep arriving and dragging it upward, so the law of large numbers they have all been taught does not apply. Third, that once the game is given a cap, the expected value stops being a property of the game and becomes a property of their prior over caps — I intend to auction tickets for a version with a cap I do not disclose, and to let them discover where the paradox has migrated. Fourth, that the classical utility fix has a price, which I want them to find by re-running the auction under a stated utility schedule and then noticing what that schedule commits them to elsewhere.

Please design the session so that the sequence above happens in the rules and the payoffs rather than in anything I say. I will not name the paradox, the tradition it comes from, or expected utility until the debrief. Give me the exact form of the game as it is played in each phase and how the phases are timed across the ninety minutes; the materials and how I set them up; the payoff scheme, with no real money — tokens, candy, or points are fine, but the stakes must be real enough that people care; the auction mechanism, spelled out precisely enough that I can run it without improvising, including what to do when there are too few bidders; what the students write down, and when; and how the running-average demonstration should be organized so that twenty people can see it and it takes under fifteen minutes. Then give me the debrief, mapping each thing that happened onto the part of the problem it corresponds to, and say what the correct diagnosis of each phase is, so that I can explain at the end which of the moves they made was the right one and why.

I also want your honest account of what will go wrong the first time I run this: the predictable failure modes — where the auction will stall or overshoot, what misconception will survive the running-average demonstration, which objection someone will raise as decisive when it is not one, which phase will overrun — and how to handle each without giving the game away early. And tell me plainly what the activity cannot do: which part of the problem it will leave untouched, and what I will have to supply in speech because the activity cannot make them feel it.

Decide, don't survey. Pick the specific variant of the game, the cap structure, the utility schedule, the number of rounds, and the auction format, and tell me why those and not the alternatives. I would rather have a design you are prepared to defend than a menu of options. Write in British English, in continuous prose: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Write it as a letter to me; I am running this in three weeks and I need to be able to follow your instructions without translating them.
