Ok so I need help writing a final exam question and I have to explain why this is personal now, sorry in advance.

I teach an upper-level course called Consciousness and the Self — mostly phil of mind, with a unit at the end on non-Western stuff — and for years I treated Schrödinger's Vedanta thing as a curiosity. You know the move: famous physicist reads the Upanishads, decides the seperate selves are an illusion, one consciousness wearing many masks, everyone nods, we move on. I don't think I ever really believed it and I don't think the students did either.

Then in the spring I sat a 10-day silent retreat (yes, I know) and around day six something happened that I still can't describe well — not bliss, not lights, just this sudden thinning of the line between me and the room, and a strange sense that the "me" doing the watching was the same thing as whatever was doing the watching in everyone else. Written down it sounds like a bad poem. But I came back and reread the later Schrödinger and it stopped being a curiosity for me.

Now I have to write the final, and I want the essay question to actually put the screws to the view instead of letting them do the "quantum physics meets Eastern mysticism, how profound" routine. Here's the problem I keep running into, and I want the question built around it.

His claim only seems to work if there are at least two minds to be unified. You can't say "your consciousness and mine are the same consciousness" without already counting them — the counting is baked into the sentence. So either the unity only shows up when you stop counting (in which case it's a report about a state of experience and not a claim about minds at all), or it's a real claim about a single subject who appears many times (and then I don't see where the counting problem went — you've just asserted the thing). And then there's the flat-footed objection: I can't feel your toothache, your memories aren't mine, and we get anesthetized seperately. If it's really one consciousness, why is it so easy to slice?

In class I've been labelling three readings, and I made the labels up so they're probably not standard, but the students know them: (1) the one-seat reading — there is literally one subject of all experience and the plurality is perspectival; (2) the one-substrate reading — there's a single underlying something, but it isn't itself a subject, so it's not a claim about consciousness at all, just about where consciousness comes from; (3) the report reading — the whole thing is a description of what introspection finds at its limits, not a metaphysical thesis.

What I want from you:

(1) One essay question for a 90-minute final, closed book, answer around 700 words. It has to make them reconstruct the strongest version of the view rather than the strawman, make them confront the counting problem head-on, make them say what the access/isolation objection is actually supposed to prove (a lot of them just throw it out like a trump card and stop), and make them commit to one of the three readings and defend it against at least one of the others. Bonus if the question also makes them say whether the claim could be false — what would have to be the case for it to be wrong — and whether unfalsifiability is a defect here or just what you'd expect from a claim about the subject of experience.

(2) A model answer, about 700 words, written the way a strong student would write it, not a professor.

(3) A marking scheme out of 20. Name the specific moves a top answer has to make, say how many marks each is worth, and give me one line per move on how students typically get it wrong. Please don't hand me a scheme full of "shows good understanding of the material" — I need it to definately separate an A from a B, so tell me which move the B student will flub.

One more thing: they won't have read Schrödinger directly, just my lectures, so the question has to be self-contained enough that someone who was awake in class can answer it with no texts in front of them. And obviously no bibliography or anything like that.
