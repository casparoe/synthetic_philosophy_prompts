I run a small reading group — mostly political theory grad students plus a couple of philosophers, none of them with any real probability background — and next week it's my turn to lead the session on the Condorcet jury theorem. Don't explain the theorem to me, I know it. What I need is a plan for how to put it across in ninety minutes, because every time I've seen it taught it collapses into "more voters, more truth, yay democracy" and everyone nods and nothing actually lands. So what has to come before what? My instinct is that the conditional-independence assumption has to be introduced before the theorem itself, or it just reads as a technical footnote that people skip, and that the whole session should be built so the students feel that assumption doing load-bearing work rather than being told it matters. But you tell me the actual order and why, and tell me where my instinct is wrong.

The example I want to carry it: a jury where each member independently draws a signal that matches the truth with probability p (with p above one half), and we watch the majority's accuracy climb toward certainty as the jury grows. That's the clean case. The part I actually care about, and the reason I'm asking, is the second act: I want to spring on them the version where the signals aren't independent — say they're all partly reading off the same source, or some chunk of the jury is effectively a coin flip at one half — and show the clean result evaporating. Is that the right example to carry the whole session, or is there something better that has exactly the features it needs and lacks the distracting ones (a three-person jury, maybe, or an urn)? What must the carrying example have, and what should it not have? Then the misconceptions: I expect "the theorem proves democracy is reliable", "it works for any group of individually decent people", "independent just means they didn't collude", and probably someone conflating the competence threshold with the conclusion. Where in the sequence does each of those need to be headed off — before the theorem, right after it, or in the second act — and how do I head it off without turning it into a lecture? And at the end, one question I can put to the room that will tell me whether they've actually got the machinery rather than the slogan. Also, what should I openly refuse to do at this level and say so, since I'd rather cut something and flag it than bluff through it.
