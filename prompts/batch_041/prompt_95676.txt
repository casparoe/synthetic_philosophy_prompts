Ok, so I'm rebuilding the church-and-state week in my political theory seminar and I want to stop doing the usual "what would the founders have thought about prayer in schools" routine. Here's the case I'd rather use.

Germany. If you're a registered member of the Catholic church, one of the Protestant regional churches, or one of a handful of other recognised religious bodies, the state tax office adds a surcharge to your income tax — 8 per cent of the bill in Bavaria and Baden-Württemberg, 9 per cent in the other Länder — and hands the money to your church, keeping a small cut for doing the collecting. Only communities with the legal form of a "corporation under public law" can do this, and their right to levy the tax comes from an article of the 1949 constitution that imports, wholesale, an article of the 1919 Weimar constitution. You get out by going to a civil office and formally declaring that you're leaving the church — its a state procedure, nothing to do with canon law — and the tax stops. But several of the churches then treat you as someone who can't recieve the sacraments, can't stand as a godparent, and can't have a church funeral. Meanwhile the exit numbers run into the hundreds of thousands a year, and the tax is one of the things people cite when they go.

So: what would Madison have made of this? I want more than "he'd be against it." Specifically:

- Madison's case in the Memorial and Remonstrance against the Virginia assessment turns on a principle that doesn't care how big the burden is — the three pence line: "the same authority which can force a citizen to contribute three pence only of his property for the support of any one establishment, may force him to conform to any other establishment in all cases whatsoever." Does that argument actually reach a system where nobody is taxed for a church they don't belong to? Or does the fact that the state is the one doing the collecting, and keeping the membership rolls, put the same problem back in play in a new place?

- The opt-out. Does the escape hatch fix the conscience problem, or does it make things worse on Madisonian terms — religion turned into a line on a tax return, membership priced, and the state left policing the boundary of who counts as a member?

- His equality argument: that the Virginia bill both laid peculiar burdens on some and handed peculiar exemptions to others. How does that run against an arrangement where the Catholic and Protestant churches get a tax apparatus, the free churches get nothing, and the unchurched get nothing?

- Longer run: what would he predict about German religiosity, and would the falling numbers count as confirmation of the establishment-corrupts-religion thesis, or is that just secularisation with a receipt? Say which way he'd lean and how much weight the evidence can bear.

- And be honest about where you're extrapolating. He's arguing about a state that taxed everyone for teachers of the Christian religion, in a Virginia that had an established church until a few years before; Germany is a plural, voluntary-membership arrangement with the state as bookkeeper. Tell me which parts of his view are load-bearing and which parts are him thinking out loud.

No citations needed, I've got the reading list. I just want the argument laid out so I can put it on one side of a handout and let the students fight about it.
