A mate of mine and I have been going round in circles about the identity of indiscernibles — the principle that if two things share every property then there can't really be two of them, or at least that no world could contain two distinct things differing in nothing at all. I've ended up on the side that says it's true, and not just as a contingent fact about our world: I can't make sense of a difference between two objects that isn't a difference in how they are. The old two-spheres thought experiment doesn't move me — a universe containing just two exactly similar iron spheres, same size, same mass, each the mirror image of the other, with nothing else around to break the symmetry. I want to say either there's really only one sphere there or the description is somehow incoherent.

But I know I'm on contested ground, so I want the honest map of what would count against me — not just the two-sphere case again, but the version of it that's supposed to be sharpest, plus the moves that are meant to save my view, and whether those moves are legitimate or just rescuing a favourite. There are two things I keep hearing that I can't properly assess. The first is that two things can be told apart without differing in any intrinsic respect, simply by bearing an irreflexive relation to each other: each sphere is, say, exactly a metre from the other, and nothing is a metre from itself, so they come out 'discernible' after all despite sharing every intrinsic feature. Is that a real rescue or a trick of letting relations do work they can't do? The second is that a thing's identity might be partly constituted by its relations to other things, so that in the two-sphere world each sphere is essentially the one standing in such-and-such relation to the other — again, substantive answer or relabelling? I'd also like to know how this bears on the version of the problem where the indiscernibles aren't objects but the points of a space, and on the line of thought that treats two possible worlds which are qualitatively identical throughout as nonetheless two worlds.

So: rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, tell me which ones a defender of the principle would themselves concede and which they'd dismiss (and on what grounds), and say honestly whether the view has been qualified into something that can't be wrong about anything — and if so, whether that's a problem for a principle of this kind or just what you'd expect from something closer to a constraint on what counts as a thing at all. I'd be most moved by anything showing the principle collapsing into a triviality, or by a case where I have to admit two things really are distinct and there is genuinely nothing to tell them apart by. And if any of this is live in physics rather than only in philosophy seminars, I'd like to hear about that too.
