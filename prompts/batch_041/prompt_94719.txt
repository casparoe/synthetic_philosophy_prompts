I am a novelist at work on a book set in fourth-century Athens, in which a money-changer is one of the principal characters. My reading has left me with the impression that Aristotle condemned the taking of interest as unnatural, on the grounds that money is by nature a sterile thing and so cannot legitimately breed, and I have taken him to mean that anyone who lives by lending at interest, or indeed by buying cheap to sell dear, plies a degraded trade that a decent city would hold in contempt. I have accordingly written my man as a pariah — shunned in the agora, his shame doing much of the novel's moral work. Before I commit to the whole draft, I should like you to tell me whether I have got Aristotle right, or whether the picture is more complicated than that: whether his objection attaches to something narrower than profit-seeking as such, and whether the argument I have put in his mouth is really his. If I am mistaken, I should be glad to know what the correct picture would imply for how such a man might have been regarded in his own city, so far as that can be inferred from the texts. Please write back in British English and in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text or tables — as one might write a letter.
