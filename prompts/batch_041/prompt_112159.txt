hey, I'm a junior on our department's AI & Society review board (I'm in the AI safety seminar this term and somehow got roped onto this thing). There's a proposal on the table from a lab here that wants us to run all our hard adjudication cases through what they call a "factored deliberation" pipeline. The idea: take one messy judgment call — say, whether a flagged thesis chapter is misconduct or just sloppy citation — chop it into small sub-questions, hand each sub-question to a different reviewer who sees only that piece and none of the others' context, then have a coordinator stitch the answers into a verdict. The kicker is they want the coordinator's job factored the same way, so in principle it recurses down and down. They argue it's the cleanest idealization of how careful deliberation *should* work, and that it'll cut individual bias and give us an audit trail.

Half the board loves it. The other half thinks it's a machine for laundering responsibility, and that you can't carve up a judgment without losing whatever made it a judgment in the first place. I have to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting, and I keep going back and forth. Can you draft it for me: a concrete recommendation (adopt it, reject it, or pilot it with limits and a time frame), the reasoning behind it, and a real engagement with the strongest case against whatever you land on. Also spell out what this proposal quietly assumes about what deliberation is and whether those assumptions actually hold. American English, please, and don't hand me a reading list — just the memo.
