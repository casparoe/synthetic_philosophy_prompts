I've been reading rationalist blogs and adjacent forums for the better part of fifteen years, and the habit that stuck hardest is treating a disagreement as a hunt for the crux: the one sub-question that, if it went the other way, would flip the whole thing. Lately I've been spending my evenings on the White Horse dialogue attributed to Gongsun Long — the argument that a white horse is not a horse, which turns on the observation that a request for a horse can be met with a yellow or black one while a request for a white horse cannot — together with the surrounding material usually filed under the School of Names: the theses attributed to Hui Shi in the Zhuangzi, the hard-and-white paradoxes, and the Mohist Canons, which seem to be arguing with the same people. The received one-line dismissal, that the whole thing trades on an equivocation between the "is" of identity and the "is" of predication, now strikes me as a way of not reading the text. So I have been collecting rival reconstructions. On one, the claim is about kinds and scope: "horse" names a kind and "white horse" names a narrower one, and names with different scope cannot be substituted for each other everywhere — or, on a variant I find intriguing, "horse" is doing something more like the work of a mass term or a shape-marker than of a count noun, so the two expressions are not even of the same grammatical type. On a second, the point is about what will count as satisfying a request, i.e., about the criteria a name sets rather than about the set of things it picks out. On a third, it is about composition: what a white horse is includes whiteness as well as horseness, so the thing named is not simply horse. On a fourth, it is a trick about the copula and nothing more. I cannot tell which of these is doing the real work, and I suspect that the question of which is upstream of the others is itself the interesting one.

Here is what I want. I have roughly six months of part-time attention — a couple of evenings a week, no formal training in Sinology, enough college Chinese to look up a character but not enough to do textual criticism — and I want to end with a single essay I would be willing to put in front of people who actually know the material. I do not need a survey, and I certainly do not need a reading list; I cannot check citations anyway and would rather have your reasoning. Tell me which open questions in this area most deserve that time, and in what order: which, if settled, would settle or dissolve the others; which are answerable by argument from the text in translation plus ordinary philosophy of language, given my limits, and which genuinely require the philology I cannot do; which are neglected relative to their payoff; and which are traps — questions that look central and would eat the six months without changing anything. Where the agenda that is best for me comes apart from the agenda the area most needs, say so and explain the divergence, since I would rather learn that my comparative advantage lies in the wrong place than have it quietly smoothed over. If a fifth reconstruction of the dialogue strikes you as better than my four, lead with that instead of ranking my list.

Structure the reply as follows. "Bottom line" — one paragraph, under 150 words, naming the single question you would have me work on and the reason, in a sentence. "The candidate questions" — roughly 300 words, laying out the open questions as you see them, each with one clause on what turns on it. "The shortlist, ranked" — roughly 500 words, the two or three worth my six months, in order, each with what makes it upstream, how tractable it is for someone in my position, how neglected it is, and what a good outcome would even look like. "Field agenda versus my agenda" — roughly 200 words on where the two diverge and why. "What would move the ranking" — roughly 150 words on the argument or piece of evidence that would push a question up or down the list. Lead with the bottom line, and keep the whole thing to roughly 1,300 words.
