I have been reading Cicero's dialogue On Old Age — the one he wrote in the voice of Cato the Elder, addressed to his friend Atticus — and I have been trying to work out what Cato is actually claiming. I do not read Latin and I have no training in classics, so I am working from a translation and from my own notes. I have written a short essay, and I would like you to comment on it. In particular, I would like to know whether I have the structure of the dialogue right, whether my central reading is defensible or whether it distorts what Cicero is doing, and whether the point I make at the end about Cato the Elder's relationship to Greek learning is worth pursuing or is based on a misunderstanding.

Here is the essay.

Most summaries of the dialogue say that Cato is defending old age against four complaints: that it takes us out of public affairs, that it weakens the body, that it takes away sensual pleasure, and that it puts us near death. On this reading, Cato's reply is a set of consolations, and the work is a cheerful apology for the last stage of life.

I think this reading misses what is most interesting about the dialogue. Cato does not really deny the four complaints. He concedes that old men are slower and weaker, that they no longer enjoy the pleasures of the table and the bed, and that they are closer to death. What he does instead is redescribe each of these facts so that it changes its value. The loss of sensual pleasure becomes a liberation from a master; the loss of public office becomes the freedom to do work that does not depend on strength or speed; bodily weakness becomes an occasion for moderation; and death becomes either nothing at all or a passage to something better. In each case the fact stays the same and the meaning is reversed.

Once you see this, the dialogue looks less like a defence of old age and more like a demonstration that the fear of old age is the real problem. Cato's opening move is to say that the fault lies in character and not in age — that old men who are difficult and discontented were difficult and discontented young men too. That is a strong claim, and it shifts the whole burden onto the individual. If old age is miserable, it is because you are the kind of person who makes it miserable.

I am not sure this is a good argument, but I am fairly sure it is the argument. And I think it explains why the last part of the dialogue, about death, takes up so much space. If the point is that our attitudes are the problem, then the hardest attitude to fix is the fear of death, and that is where Cato spends his energy.

I am also puzzled by the way Cato uses examples. He appeals to Sophocles, to Themistocles, to Cyrus, and to old Roman generals. It sometimes feels less like an argument and more like a parade of impressive old men. Does that weaken the case, or is that the point — that old age is bearable if you have the right character and the right examples?

I also notice that Cicero gives this speech to Cato the Elder, who in real life was famous for disliking Greek learning and for thinking it would soften Rome. But the Cato of the dialogue is full of Greek poetry and Greek philosophy, and Cicero himself seems to notice the problem, because in his preface he says that if Cato speaks more learnedly than usual, we should put it down to his love of Greek literature in his old age. I do not know what to make of this. Is Cicero admitting that the character is not historical? Or is he making a joke at Cato's expense? I have a feeling that this is the key to the whole dialogue, but I cannot tell whether I am onto something or whether I am making too much of a prefatory aside.

I would be grateful for your comments. Please tell me where I have the details wrong, whether the redescription thesis is a fair reading, and what you would suggest I think about next. I am not asking for a bibliography; I am asking for your judgment on the essay.
