I'm running the third session of a reading group on formal epistemology and AI safety, and I need a teaching plan rather than an explanation. The audience is about a dozen graduate students, mostly in logic and computer science with two from philosophy, who know the incompleteness theorems at the level of a good popular exposition and who have all heard the phrase the Löbian obstacle dropped casually in talks, but none of whom has worked with provability logic or seen the proof of Löb's theorem. I have ninety minutes, a whiteboard, and no expectation that anyone reads anything afterwards. What I want from you is the order in which the pieces must be introduced, with the reason each step has to come after the one before it, since the dependency structure is the thing I'm least sure of. Specifically: does the arithmetic machinery -- the provability predicate, and the fact that the system can verify simple facts about its own proofs -- have to be built before the modal reasoning about provability, or can it be black-boxed early and revisited later without the students feeling cheated; and where exactly does the diagonalization step belong relative to the statement of Löb's theorem, given that the theorem looks like a curiosity until you see what it does to an agent that tries to trust itself?

I've been assuming we would carry the whole session on one running scenario: a proof-based agent that acts only after deriving that the action leads to a good outcome, and which wants to hand control to a successor built on the same proof system and the same style of reasoning, only to find that it cannot certify the successor without simultaneously licensing actions it has already ruled out. I want your judgement on whether that scenario can actually bear ninety minutes of weight or whether it goes vague at precisely the point where the theorem is doing the work, and if it cannot carry the session, what should replace it and what the replacement must have that mine lacks. A colleague has been lobbying for the surprise-exam paradox as the opening, and I'd like to know whether that is a genuine on-ramp or a trap that will cost me twenty minutes and leave the room with the wrong intuition.

Then the misconception map, which is the part I'll actually lean on: what this group will get wrong, in the order they will get it wrong, and the point in the sequence at which I should head off each one. My guesses, offered for correction rather than endorsement: that the obstacle is the second incompleteness theorem in different clothes; that it shows no self-trusting agent is possible at all, rather than something considerably narrower; that it is an artefact of diagonalization trickery or of classical logic and would dissolve under a cleverer design; and that one simply escapes it by adding the reflection principle as an axiom, which I take to be the move that makes matters worse rather than better. Finally, give me a single question to put to the room in the last ten minutes that would show me whether they have understood why the obstacle is a theorem rather than a quirk of one formal system, and tell me what to leave out at this level, with the wording I should use when I tell them explicitly that we are skipping it.
