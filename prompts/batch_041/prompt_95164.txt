I'm running a seventy-five-minute session on consequentialist cluelessness for a reading group of non-philosophers — mostly economists and biologists, no background and no reading. Don't explain the topic to me; plan the session. I want to build it around a move I'll name for the group: credence-stuffing, the habit of assigning oneself coarse, invented probabilities to distant and grand outcomes so that expected-value arithmetic can proceed as though nothing had been lost. I take that move to be where the real difficulty sits, but I don't want a debunking session — a placeholder credence may sometimes be the only honest option, and I'd rather the room leave holding that tension than a verdict.

So give me the order the pieces have to come in, and what depends on what; the one small, everyday case that should carry the whole session, with what it must have and must not have to do that work (I don't want a trolley, and I don't want a which-charity comparison); the misconceptions to expect and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off — I'm counting on at least 'this is just ordinary uncertainty,' 'this is the butterfly effect,' 'so anything goes,' and 'this is the demandingness objection in new clothes'; the single closing question that would show whether they've grasped the distinction rather than caught a mood; and what I should openly decline to settle at this level.
