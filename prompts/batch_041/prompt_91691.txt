You are the instructor for an advanced undergraduate seminar in normative ethics. The course has covered additive axiology, the structure of value comparisons, and the principal objections to aggregating small harms. You are now grading the midterm. Below is the exam question and one student's response. Grade the response and provide the specific comments that justify the grade. Do not merely summarize what the student wrote: identify what is correct, what is incorrect, and whether each error damages the argument as a whole or is peripheral. Where the student's reasoning is defective, explain the defect precisely enough that the student could repair it; where it is sound but incomplete, say what is missing.

Exam question:

Consider a device with two settings. On setting A, one person is subjected to severe, unremitting torture for fifty years. On setting B, each of some number M of distinct people receives a single dust speck in the eye: an irritation so slight that it is barely noticed and is gone within seconds, leaving no lasting effect. M may be set to any finite number.

(1) Reconstruct the strongest argument that, for every finite M, setting A is worse than setting B; and the strongest argument that, for some finite M, setting B is worse than setting A.
(2) Does the first argument require denying that "worse than" is transitive, or denying that the values of separate harms can be added? Explain.
(3) Now suppose that, instead of being spread across M distinct people, the dust specks fall on a single person, one per second, for fifty years. Does this change your answer to (2)? Explain.

Student's response:

The problem is whether many very small harms can, in sufficient number, outweigh one enormous harm. On the additive view the value of an outcome is the sum of the values of its parts. Let each dust speck have a small negative value, minus d, and let the fifty years of torture have value minus T. Setting B then has value minus Md, and since M may be increased without limit while d and T are fixed, Md eventually exceeds T. So there is a finite M beyond which B is worse. Notice that this argument needs no infinite quantities; it needs only that the sum of finitely many small disvalues grows without bound.

The rival argument denies that the specks can be summed at all. It treats torture as lexically prior to mere irritation: any amount of torture is worse than any amount of irritation, however many irritations there are. Its intuitive backing is that a dust speck is not a smaller version of torture but a different kind of thing, and that there is no one who experiences the aggregate of M specks; each person feels only her own, so the total is an artifact of the accounting.

It is sometimes claimed that this second view escapes the additive argument without cost. I do not think so. If torture is worse than each speck, and each speck is worse than nothing, the lexicographic view still has to order whole outcomes, and I take it that any view which refuses to let a finite number of specks outweigh torture is simply denying that worse than is transitive. A relation that ranks A above every B and yet refuses to rank A above the sum of the B's is not an ordering at all; it is a refusal to compare, dressed up as a value judgment.

Moreover, the additive argument here is just the repugnant conclusion in another guise. The repugnant conclusion tells us that a sufficiently large population of lives barely worth living can be better than a smaller, flourishing population; the dust-speck argument tells us that a sufficiently large number of barely-noticed irritations can be worse than one tortured life. Since the two have the same form, enough tiny items outweighing a few large ones, anyone who accepts the additive argument about specks has already accepted the repugnant conclusion, and anyone who rejects the repugnant conclusion must reject the additive argument here.

For part (3), moving the specks onto a single person does not change the additive total, since minus Md is the same whether the specks are spread across M people or piled on one. But it does change the dialectic. The strongest anti-aggregation reply in part (2) was that no single subject undergoes the aggregate; if all the specks fall on one person, that reply is unavailable, and it becomes much harder to deny that the accumulation is a genuine harm to a genuine subject. So the same-person version strengthens the additive case even though the arithmetic is unchanged.

In short, the additive view is the only one that takes the comparison seriously, at the price of accepting that enough trivial harms can outweigh a monstrous one.
