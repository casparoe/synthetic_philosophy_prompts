So my friend has gotten really into the idea that AI safety via debate is basically just the adversarial legal system, and that's his whole reason for thinking it'll scale: you put two AIs on opposite sides, a human (or a weaker model) sits as judge, and because each side is incentivised to tear apart the other's bullshit, the truth tends to come out on top. I'm skeptical but I can't quite articulate why, so I want you to do a proper audit of the analogy for me.

The thing that bugs me is that in a courtroom the lawyers aren't the only source of information: there are witnesses, physical evidence, a record, an appeals court that can revisit a bad verdict, and a coercive authority behind the oath. Here's the case I think kills it: imagine a debater who wins with a claim that is in fact true, but whose justification is something the judge could never verify in the time available -- or, worse, a claim that's false but whose refutation is a computation too long for anyone in the room to actually run. Does the trial analogy have any resources to handle that, or is that precisely the kind of thing the analogy is hiding by importing the courtroom's background machinery?
