I'm in an epistemology reading group where a friend and I have spent weeks arguing about whether a condition can be self-intimating without being luminous, and we have worn out the usual illustrations — the hand that slowly warms, the growing heap, the barn facades — all of which drag in vagueness or luck and then leave us bickering about whether that was really the point. His line is that if a condition is self-intimating, so that whenever it obtains the agent believes that it obtains, and the agent's belief-forming mechanism is reliable, then the condition is luminous: whenever it obtains, the agent is in a position to know that it obtains. I don't believe that, but every case I build either proves less than I want or quietly stipulates its own verdict, so I'd like you to construct one properly. What I need is a scenario in which the condition is self-intimating in the sense that the agent never fails to believe it when it obtains, and yet there are occasions on which the condition obtains and the agent is not in a position to know that it obtains, with the shortfall falling out of some structural feature of the case rather than out of the agent's carelessness, irrationality, or self-deception, and not out of anything as blunt as the belief's being formed by a coin toss. The constraints are these: one ordinary human being in a situation that could actually happen — a kitchen, a hospital ward, a walk home — with no brains in vats, no demons, and no stipulated perfect introspection; no borderline-vagueness cases, no fake barns, no lottery tickets; and the condition itself has to be substantial, so merely being existent, or merely being appeared to in some way or other, will not do. Since the case is meant to settle the argument rather than decorate it, it has to generate its verdicts from the mechanism you describe rather than have them written into the setup, and it should not turn out to be one of the familiar illustrations in new clothes.

Once you have it, walk me through it properly: state the condition, the belief, and the method by which the belief is formed; show why the case qualifies as self-intimating; and then, at the particular moment where the condition obtains without the agent's being in a position to know that it obtains, identify exactly which requirement on knowledge is doing the work and why it fails there, naming the requirement — safety, sensitivity, reliability, or whatever else you invoke — and defending that choice rather than gesturing at all of them at once. Then tell me what happens if that single feature is removed: does the case collapse into the ordinary fallibility of introspection, into a familiar failure of the KK principle, or into nothing at all? And say what, if anything, the case implies for the KK principle, in particular whether the same mechanism can be extended so that someone who genuinely knows fails to know that she knows, and if you think it cannot be extended, why not. Please close by arguing the other side as hard as it can be argued: what is the strongest thing my friend could say to show that the case is rigged, or that it proves less than it appears to, and does that objection succeed? I would far rather have you take a definite position — say whether the case works, on which theory of knowledge, and which of us is right — than lay out the options and leave the verdict to me; and if you think no case can do what I have asked, commit to that and argue for it. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, with no headings, no numbered steps, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables — the way you would set it out in a long email to someone who asked for exactly this.
