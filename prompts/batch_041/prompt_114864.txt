I teach a seminar on formalism and its limits — mostly philosophy grad students, plus four or five CS people who keep the rest of us honest — and every single term, around week four, somebody announces that Gödel showed no system can be complete, or that machines can't think, or (my personal favourite) that physics will never have a final theory, and then we lose forty minutes to it and never get to the reading. I've decided to stop fighting it and make it a feature. What I want from you is a one-page handout: a decision tree that takes a student from a commitment they already have to a named position about what the incompleteness theorems do and don't show *outside* mathematics, so that they sort themselves rather than me sorting them. Start the tree at the thing the whole room can agree on — I accept both theorems as proved results about consistent, effectively axiomatized theories that can represent enough arithmetic — and then make every branch node a question about the theorem or about its target that a student can answer without already knowing where the branch leads. Roughly the distinctions I care about are these: (i) does the result expose arithmetic truths that no possible reasoner could ever know, or only truths that no single formal system can prove? (ii) does the theorem even apply to the thing being theorized about — is the target a consistent, effectively axiomatizable system strong enough for arithmetic, and if it isn't, then for the mind in particular: is the mind a single machine at all, are its axioms recursively enumerable, is it consistent? (iii) does the position need the reasoner to have some insight into its own soundness or consistency, or does it get by with no such self-knowledge? (iv) does the reading presuppose a notion of arithmetic truth that outruns provability (if the student is a formalist or an anti-realist about arithmetic, the phrase true but unprovable is already tendentious), and (v) is incompleteness a permanent structural fact or a defect you keep patching by climbing to stronger systems — and if you climb, is that ladder a rational procedure or a run of arbitrary stipulations? I want one concrete case used at least once, because it's the one that actually splits people in my experience: a mathematician reads the proof, concludes the Gödel sentence for PA is true, adds that sentence (or the consistency of PA) as a new axiom and carries on — is that a demonstration of a faculty no machine has, or just a machine that changed its axioms?

The leaves have to include at least these: Lucas–Penrose style anti-mechanism; the later Penrose where the gap is supposed to be filled by non-computable physics; the deflationary reply that Penrose's critics in the logic community make, which says the argument only shows a machine can't certify its own soundness and that the disputed insight is exactly what's at issue; the view that the mind is no single machine but an open-ended succession of machines, each stronger than the last (I think Putnam floated something like this); Hofstadter-ish strange-loop naturalism; the position that the theorems constrain what a self-improving AI can prove about its own successor (the AI-safety crowd call this the Löbian obstacle I think, I may have the name slightly off); the no-final-theory-of-physics reading, Hawking gave a whole lecture on it; the popular-apologetics leaf where the theorem is meant to refute materialism or to prove the existence of a divine mind; and the flat denial that the theorem bears on the target at all, because the target fails the hypotheses. Also include, as its own leaf, the disjunction Gödel himself drew — mind outruns any finite machine, or else there are absolutely undecidable arithmetic propositions, not merely unprovable-in-this-system ones — and tell me whether anyone actually occupies the horn he didn't like. Each leaf should carry the position's name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment; flag any two leaves your tree can't really separate (I suspect the succession-of-machines view and strange-loop naturalism are hard to prise apart, and I'd like to know exactly where the two anti-mechanisms diverge); and for any combination of answers that turns out empty, say whether nobody's there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Twelve to sixteen leaves is about right. Don't bother with a reading list, I'll add that myself — I've been meaning to make this thing for two years now and keep puting it off, I just want the structure.
