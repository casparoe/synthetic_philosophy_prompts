I've been on a Szasz kick latley — my brother says he's a crank, but the stuff about involuntary commitment has been rattling around in my head — and now I can't stop noticing how weird the actual legal machinery is where I live.

Three things that seem to sit side by side without anybody blinking:

- A psychiatrist, not a judge, not a jury, can have you held in a hospital for a few days on the say-so that you're a danger to yourself or others. No crime required.
- If you do get charged with something and win on not guilty by reason of insanity, you go to a state hospital and can end up staying there way longer than the prison sentence for the same act would have been. So: too messed up to be punished, but fine to be locked up indefinitely. And the test itself varies — some places it's whether you knew it was wrong, others add whether you could have controlled yourself anyway, which are not the same theory of the person at all.
- A handful of states offer guilty but mentally ill instead, which is neither of the above.

And there's a bill moving through my state legislature that would let a court order you into outpatient treatment and, if you'd signed a psychiatric advance directive refusing medication, override it on the grounds that your insight is impaired. That's the one that really gets me. The directive is the one thing you did while you were well, and the state treats it as void precisely because you aren't well.

So what does this whole apparatus presuppose — about mental illness, about agency, about what makes a choice a choice? Does it need a strong claim that illness destroys voluntariness, or can something weaker do the work (unreliable preferences, distorted risk perception, the state's interest in preventing harm whether or not anyone is to blame)? Szasz thought the whole thing was a category error; I want to know whether he's right that no consistent theory is in there, or whether there is one and it's just ugly. Also tell me where the law is only underdetermined: which bits strictly require a commitment to a disease model, which are equally at home in a Szasz-style problems-in-living view, and which are just historical residue. And if you swapped the presupposed theory for a rival one, what concretely changes — who gets held, for how long, and on whose authority?
