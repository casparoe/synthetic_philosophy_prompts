You are an expert philosopher of mind with a solid working knowledge of the psychophysics of time perception, and I would like you to serve as a careful and sceptical referee for an argument I overheard and could not settle on my own. I am a graduate student in a cognitive science seminar; last week's session was on temporal illusions in perception, and on the walk home two classmates — Ana, who does the neuroscience side of the programme, and Ben, who came to it from philosophy — kept going at each other about one of the demonstrations. As the lecturer set it up: two dots are flashed in succession on a screen, the first in one position and one colour, the second in a different position and a different colour, separated by a gap of some tens of milliseconds; at the right combination of spacing and timing, observers do not report two dots but a single dot that seems to travel from the first position to the second, and they report its colour changing abruptly halfway along that path — that is, at a moment before the second flash was presented. The argument ran roughly as follows. Ana: the report is impossible as stated, because to experience the colour change at the midpoint the visual system would have to use information about the second flash before that flash exists, and nothing travels backwards in time; the only way to save the data is to say the experience being reported is assembled after the fact, so that what the observer really saw was a dot of the first colour and then, later, a dot of the second colour, with the travelling, colour-shifting dot a retrospective fabrication — the memory of the episode has been rewritten. Ben: that is not the only option; the system could simply hold the first flash in a buffer until the second arrives and only then build a single experience of motion, in which case the observer never did experience the first dot followed by a gap, and nothing has been rewritten — the experience is delivered late rather than distorted. Ana: those two stories say exactly the same thing about what the observer ends up seeing and remembering, and differ only in where you draw a line and label it 'consciousness'. Ben: the line is not decorative, because one story says a real experience occurred and was later distorted, while the other says the only experience there ever was is the moving dot — a difference in what happened to the person, not merely in vocabulary. Ana: then name an observation that could decide between them, since the two stories agree about every button press and every subsequent report. Ben: a fact can be real without being observable, and if we cannot tell the difference, that is a defect in our methods rather than evidence that the difference is unreal.
