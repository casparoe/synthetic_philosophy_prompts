I am a junior at a small high school and the student facilitator of our philosophy club, which meets once a week in a seminar room with a movable table arrangement; I have about sixteen regular members, and I get seventy-five minutes. I have been reading about the debate over which computations matter morally — whether a system earns moral consideration in virtue of its behavior, its functional organization, or the substrate it happens to run on — and I would rather build a session in which people collide with the problem than deliver a lecture about it. Please act as an expert in philosophy of mind and applied ethics who also designs classroom simulations, and think carefully before you commit to anything: start with a short brainstorming pass in which you list three or four candidate activities and say what each one would actually make people feel, then choose the strongest and develop it fully. Assume the participants have no background in the area and that you cannot rely on them having read anything.

Here is what I want the session to do. The members should come away having felt, in their own decisions, that every piece of evidence we have about whether a computation is of the morally relevant kind is indirect, and that two people can reach the same verdict while working from incompatible criteria. I would like the activity to be a staged review board: the club plays the moral-status review panel of a fictional agency and must sort a set of candidate systems into tiers of consideration, allocating a fixed and deliberately insufficient pool of "consideration tokens" across them, so that ranking one system costs them another. Build the case set so that it carries the pressure for them. In particular, include two candidates that are input-output identical twins — they produce exactly the same responses to every possible input — but one computes its responses from a precomputed table of every situation it will ever encounter, while the other computes them on the fly from a running model of its own body and its own damage; and include a third candidate that has rich internal states of the relevant functional kind but never lets them affect any output. Let the participants discover the twins' equivalence only after they have already committed their tokens, and arrange the rules so that at least one group is forced to defend a verdict that its own stated criterion cannot support. I do not want the point announced up front; I want the sorting, the budget, and the late revelation to do the work.

Give me the full package: the premise and framing the facilitator reads aloud, the materials and any cards or dossiers (with the six candidate systems written out concretely enough that students can argue about them), the rules and the token economy, an optional set of roles or secret constraints for individual participants, a minute-by-minute timeline for the seventy-five minutes, and the debrief — which should map specific moments in the activity onto the parts of the underlying problem (the allocation moment onto the attribution problem, the twins onto the question of whether implementation matters, the silent system onto the split between behavioral and internal criteria, and the cross-table disagreement onto underdetermination). Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover, and finish by naming the one feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself in the debrief. Do not ask me to consult sources or provide citations; everything should run from the materials in your answer.
