I am a third-year philosophy student taking a course on aesthetics and the philosophy of criticism, and for our seminar I am expected to bring a puzzle drawn from my own experience of art rather than from the reading. I have one that I can describe precisely but cannot name, and I would like you to act as a philosopher of art and of testimony in working out what, if anything, it is.

Please begin by brainstorming the concepts that might cover the case — the plausible candidates and, for each, why it does or does not fit — and only then commit to an answer. Think carefully; I would rather have a carefully delimited answer than a quick label.

The case. A friend whose ear I trust tells me to listen to the second movement of a quartet and to wait for the moment when the cello enters beneath the repeated piano figure. I listen, I hear the entry, and the passage now seems to me the finest thing in the piece. She has not told me that it is good; she has told me where to listen. But if I try to say what my judgment rests on, I can point only to what I heard, and what I heard is what she told me to listen for. Had she pointed at a different bar, I suspect I would have heard something else, or nothing.

The same mechanism runs the other way. A colleague once remarked that a certain novelist's tenderness is really a way of flattering the reader, and I have not been able to read a page of him since without feeling the flattery — even though I have come to think my colleague's criticism cheap and his taste unreliable. The judgment outlived the grounds for it, and I cannot tell whether I am now seeing something real that he merely pointed out or an impression he installed. In both directions the result does not feel borrowed. It is not like repeating an opinion; it is like seeing something. Yet the mechanism that produced the appreciation and the mechanism that produced the aversion appear to be the same one, and either would have felt equally like perception.

What I am after. I do not think this is simply the familiar thought that you must experience a work yourself before you may judge it: I did experience it. Nor is it merely the fact that other people can change what we notice. The sting is that from the inside I cannot distinguish a judgment that testimony triggered from one that testimony fixed, and that no amount of checking can settle it, because the checking would consist in attending again, and attending is precisely what the other person supplied. Does this structure have an established name? If it does, tell me which, and say exactly what the established concept covers that mine does not. I will not be satisfied with a bare term; I want the carving: what the phenomenon is, what it excludes, and where its edges are. Consider, for instance, the case in which the pointer is later repudiated and the judgment nevertheless survives; the case in which the pointer is a machine rather than a person; and the case in which I already loved the piece and someone then supplies a reason I accept — is that the same phenomenon or a different one? If there is no established name, coin one and define it with the same care.

Say what would change your mind: which argument or example would lead you to a different verdict about whether what I describe is genuinely distinct from the nearest concept you identify, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.

End with a single final line of the form \answer{...} containing only the established term, if you find one, or the term you coin.
