I am a TA for a second-year undergraduate seminar in practical ethics, and next week I have to run a seventy-five-minute section on the relation between meritocracy and desert — in particular on why "the best candidate got the position" and "the best candidate deserves what comes with the position" are not the same claim, even though my students will treat them as interchangeable. I do not want an essay on the subject; I want you to plan the explanation I am going to give. Set out the order in which the ideas must be introduced and why each depends on the one before it, starting from whatever has to be in place before the word "desert" can do any work at all. I have been sketching a scaffold and want you to tell me whether the sequence is right or whether I have the dependencies backwards: first, that a desert claim has three separable parts — a basis (what the person is being credited or blamed for: effort, talent, contribution), an object (the particular thing said to be deserved: a place, a payment, an esteem, a punishment), and a currency in which it is paid — and that these can come apart; second, that meritocracy answers a question about allocation (who gets the position), whereas desert answers a question about fittingness (what treatment fits the person), and that the two answers can diverge; and third, that institutions can make desert claims more or less apt rather than merely registering a desert that was already a fact before them. If any of the three belongs earlier or later, say so and say why.

For the single example that carries the whole section, I had thought of using a case where the merit is a genuine performance and the reward is genuinely large — a public orchestra audition, admission to a selective program, a professional draft — but I suspect the obvious cases arrive with too much baggage, since my students will simply cheer for meritocracy. Tell me what the carrying example must have and must lack, propose the one you would use, and explain what lets it do the work rather than the students' prior sympathies. Then map the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they will actually surface, and tell me exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off; I am already anticipating that they will treat effort as the only real basis of desert, that they will hear "you cannot deserve what you did not freely choose" as a knockdown rather than as a question about which basis is in play, that they will slide from "deserved" to "entitled" or "legally owed," and that they will take the natural lottery to settle the whole matter rather than to relocate the basis. Finally, give me one closing question that would show whether they have genuinely separated the allocative claim from the distributive one rather than merely been made uneasy about privilege, and tell me what I should openly decline to cover at this level and flag as out of scope — I am thinking the full Rawls-versus-Nozick exchange, the metaphysics of free will, and the empirical social-mobility literature, but tell me if that is the wrong cut.
