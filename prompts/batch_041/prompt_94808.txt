I'm taking a philosophy of physics course and I keep running into something that seems like it should be obvious but isn't, and I'd like to get it straight before the exam. Here's my reasoning, and I want you to tell me where it goes wrong if it does. The fundamental dynamical laws — Newton's equations, Maxwell's equations, Schrödinger's equation — are all time-reversal invariant: if a process is allowed going forward, the reversed process is allowed too. Yet the world manifestly has a direction: ice melts in warm water and never spontaneously forms, gases spread out and never spontaneously gather, and so on. The standard explanation is that this asymmetry comes from the second law of thermodynamics, the statistical tendency toward higher entropy. But the second law is supposed to be a statistical theorem that follows from the underlying time-symmetric dynamics plus a probabilistic assumption. So here is my puzzle: it looks to me as though the second law is being asked to do two jobs at once. On the one hand it is supposed to *explain* the arrow of time — to tell us why time has a direction at all. On the other hand it is supposed to be *derived* from the microdynamics plus a probability assumption, and any such derivation has to assume something about the state at one end of the process — that the gas started concentrated, that the ice started cold — which already presupposes which end is the "past." It feels circular: we explain the arrow of time by entropy increase, but the statistical derivation of entropy increase only works if we already know which way time runs. And if the second law is not doing the explaining, then it seems to me the arrow must either be a brute fact about the universe or else built into the laws themselves after all, which would contradict what I was told about time-reversal invariance.

What I want to understand is this: is the second law actually *the explanation* of the direction of time, or is it only a *consequence* of a time-asymmetric assumption that is smuggled into the setup? If the latter, what exactly is being smuggled in — is it a law, a boundary condition, a probability distribution, or something else — and is the resulting account of the arrow genuinely explanatory, or does it just relocate the mystery to an unexplained initial condition? And finally, if the arrow of time is not derived from the second law but presupposed by it, is there any sense in which the second law is still responsible for the arrow at all, or is that a category mistake on my part? I'd like the answer to be precise about which assumptions are doing the work at each step, since the hand-waving version ("entropy explains the arrow") is exactly what I'm trying to get past.
