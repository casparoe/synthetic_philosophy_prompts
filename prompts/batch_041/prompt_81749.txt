I was at a seminar dinner last week when an argument about a half-serious proposal of mine — abolishing inheritance above some threshold — was cut short by the familiar line, 'equality of opportunity, not equality of outcome.' The American at the table used it as a veto: the proposal was illegitimate because it went after outcomes, confiscating what people had rather than ensuring that everyone arrives at the starting line equally placed. The European at the table used the very same phrase to argue the opposite, that inheritance is precisely the mechanism by which opportunity is transmitted, so anyone who genuinely cares about opportunity should want it broken up. I suspect we were all mouthing the same words while meaning four or five different things, and I would like to know what the slogan can actually be claiming.

Please lay the readings out as a menu rather than defending one of them. Begin with the trivial entries — the readings on which the slogan is barely more than a definition or a tautology (opportunity understood as whatever you can achieve given your effort, so that any outcome counts as an opportunity fairly taken) — and work upward to the radical ones: that opportunity is the only thing a society owes its members; that opportunity and outcome cannot be distinguished at all, since today's outcome is tomorrow's starting line and children inherit not only money but expectations, connections, and capacities; that equalizing opportunity in any serious sense requires equalizing the resources that constitute it, so the distinction collapses in practice; that opportunity is not the sort of thing that can be distributed, because it is a relation between a person and a good that others also want. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false, and what accepting it would commit its holder to.

Then tell me which readings actually do the work the slogan was doing at that table — which entry on the menu supports using it as a veto on redistribution, which entry supports using it as an argument for redistribution, and whether my two companions could both have been right about what they meant while being simply mistaken that they disagreed. If it turns out that part of the dispute is empirical rather than conceptual — a matter of how much inheritance actually determines life chances, or of whether mobility tends to fall as inequality rises — say so, and say what kind of evidence would settle it, without assuming I already know any particular study. I do not want a reading list; I want the menu, the verdicts, and a sentence or two about which entry I should have been arguing about.
