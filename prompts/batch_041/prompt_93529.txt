I sit on the ethics comittee at a fertility clinic and I have to circulate a short written opinion before next month's meeting, so I'm asking for help drafting it.

Background, briefly: a genetics lab has pitched us embryo screening based on polygenic scores. Part of the panel is the disease stuff — type 2 diabetes, coronary artery disease, a couple of adult-onset cancers. The other part is a combined index they market as a "wellbeing score," which folds in height, educational attainment, and a few personality-adjacent measures. Our medical director wants to take the disease half and decline the rest, on the grounds that parents plainly have a reason to choose the embryo with the best shot at a good life, and disease risk is just the clearest case of that. Our senior counselor says that once you're scoring embryos and picking the best one, the disease/trait line is cosmetic, and she'd rather we decline all of it.

The director has floated a compromise and I can't tell whether its principled or just a way to feel better about saying yes: offer the panel only for conditions that make childhood worse, nothing that only helps or hurts you later in life. That would knock out the adult cancer and early heart disease scores, which are the ones nobody objects to, so I think it's broken — but maybe it can be repaired.

One more thing from our geneticist, who I suspect is right but I don't know what follows from it: if every family screens for height and test scores, no kid ends up better off in relative terms. She says that's fatal for the wellbeing score and irrelevant for the disease panel. I'm not sure that distinction holds up.

What I need: the memo itself, bottom line first — offer the disease panel, decline everything, or offer with specific conditions — plus when we should revisit the decision. Handle the strongest point on the losing side honestly: if you tell me to decline, say what I owe the parents who ask for it; if you tell me to offer, say what actually stops the slide to the wellbeing score. And don't let the word "eugenics" do the work by itself — spell out what the objection really is.

Under 600 words, American English, and something the committee can actually vote on, not a literature survey — no reading list.
