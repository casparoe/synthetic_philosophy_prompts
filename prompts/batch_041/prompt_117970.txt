I work on the experimentation platform at a mid-sized software company. My background is computer science and I have never taken a philosophy course, so I tend to stay quiet when the data scientists start arguing about what causation really is, but I would like to hold my own in these discussions. The dispute in question arose at a review of an analysis that used observational logs instead of a randomized rollout; the analyst had regressed an engagement metric on things like a user's region, account age, and whether the account had adopted a particular feature. A senior data scientist cut the discussion short with a line I have now heard him use three or four times — no causation without manipulation — and in that room it was clearly doing a specific job. The idea, as far as I could tell, was that because no one had assigned the treatment, and because some of the variables we cared about (region, age, sex, the industry a company is in) could not be assigned by anyone, we had no business speaking of causes at all. A different colleague pushed back with what sounded like the opposite instinct: that all you need for a causal claim is a counterfactual, namely that had the treatment been different the outcome would have differed, and that nobody has to be able to do anything — actually or even in principle — for that to be true. I suspect the first colleague's sentence is a compressed way of saying several quite different things, that some of them are innocuous and at least one is carrying all the weight while being false.

I would like a disambiguation I can take into the next review. Please lay out the distinct precise claims that the sentence could be making, ordered from the harmless to the ambitious: something like a rule of thumb about research design, something about which quantities a study can even estimate, something about which variables count as causes, and something about what the word cause means or about what causation is. For each reading I want to know whether it is a stipulation about how to use a word, a methodological heuristic, or a substantive claim about the world; whether it is plausible; and, most importantly, whether that particular reading is the one that would license rejecting an observational causal claim out of hand. Two cases I would like you to test the readings against, since I think they are where the slogan gets uncomfortable: the moon's gravitational pull and the tides (nobody can manipulate the moon) and a person's sex as a cause of an outcome (a variable that cannot be assigned). I want to know which readings imply that neither of these can be a cause, and whether the standard repair — replacing actual manipulation with a hypothetical or idealized experiment, or with the notion of an intervention defined independently of any human agent — genuinely saves the slogan or quietly substitutes a different claim that no longer does the work my colleague wants. No citations or reading lists, please; I am not going to look anything up, and a bibliography would not help me in the meeting.

Give me the answer as a table with one row per reading and exactly these columns: Reading (a short label, two to five words); Precise Claim (the reading stated as a single declarative sentence about causation, not a gloss); Kind of Claim (one of: stipulation about language, methodological heuristic, substantive metaphysical thesis); Verdict (one of: trivially true, defensible but contested, false, unclear as stated — with a clause of explanation if the verdict is not obvious); Supports Rejecting the Observational Analysis (Yes, No, or Only with the added premise that ___ , filling in the premise); What It Commits You To (the further consequences of accepting the reading, in one sentence); and One-Line Reply (a sentence I could actually say out loud if someone in the room meant that reading). After the table, add a short closing paragraph naming which reading is most likely what my colleague intended, which reading my other colleague's counterfactual remark amounts to on the same menu, and what would have to be true for one of them to be right rather than the other.
