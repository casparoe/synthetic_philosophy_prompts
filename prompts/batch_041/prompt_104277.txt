I teach a continuing-education seminar on Augustine, and the closing session pairs his treatments of lying with Adventures of Huckleberry Finn. Most of the class knows the novel; a few have read excerpts of the treatises; nobody has thought hard about the two together. I want a reading of the novel through Augustine's position, and I want it to run in both directions — the novel should be allowed to press back on the philosophy.

As I present him, Augustine's view has four parts. A lie is a double-hearted utterance: the mind holds one thing while the words or other signs produce another. The wrong lies in the will to deceive rather than in the falsity of what is said, so someone who says what is false while believing it true does not lie, and someone who says what is true while believing it false does. All lying is sin; there is no benevolent lie, not even to save a life or protect the faith. But lies differ in gravity: he ranks them by the harm they threaten and the good they aim at, and the lie told to help someone while injuring no one is the least grave of the lot, though still a sin. Concealment of the truth, by contrast, is not the same act as lying and is sometimes permitted, since not everyone has a claim on every truth.

What I want is a reading that works these distinctions through the novel's actual scenes rather than gesturing at Huck's moral growth. Huck spends the book lying — to the men hunting Jim, to the ferry captain, to Aunt Sally, and alongside the Duke and the King — and Augustine's taxonomy sorts those lies differently than a modern reader's instincts would. The lies told to people with no claim on the truth and aimed at protecting Jim are one thing; the Duke's and the King's lies are aimed at gain; Tom Sawyer's performance at the Phelps farm is a deception staged by someone who knows Jim is already free and which prolongs a man's captivity for entertainment. Where does the Augustinian ranking fall on each, and does the reader's sympathy track it?

The hard case is the decision in Chapter 31. Huck writes to Miss Watson to give Jim up, tears up the letter, and concludes that he will be damned. Notice that he frames the wrong he is about to commit as theft rather than as lying — his truthfulness would have consisted in sending the letter — and that what he then does is not a false utterance but a refusal to disclose, sustained by a false identity. Is that a lie in Augustine's sense, or a permitted concealment? Does Miss Watson have a claim on the truth about Jim, and if that claim is conferred by slave law, can Augustine's framework say so without either conceding the point to the slaveholder or smuggling in a consequentialism he rejects? And what does his rigorism make of a conscience educated by a slaveholding society to call the better act a sin? Huck believes he is doing wrong and does it anyway; is that the paradigm case for the doctrine that one must never act against conscience, or the case that exposes it?

I am not asking for citations; work from the novel as the class knows it, and no quotations are needed. I don't want the novel reduced to an illustration, and I don't want Augustine softened into a proto-consequentialist who would bless Huck's decision. Tell me where the Augustinian reading genuinely illuminates a scene, where the novel resists it and the honest critic has to say so, and whether the novel contributes anything to the philosophical question that the treatises cannot.
