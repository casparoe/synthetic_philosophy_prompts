Right, film club next month is Inception and I want to come at it sideways — not the usual 'did the top fall / was it all a dream' chat, which I'm a bit sick of. I want to read it against an old philosophy-of-mind row that I think the film walks straight into without noticing.

The row: Malcolm, in Dreaming (1959), takes a Wittgenstinian line that we shouldn't even say we have experiences while we're asleep — that 'I dreamt I was falling' isn't a report of anything that happened to you, since the whole idea of a dream is tied to the tale you tell on waking and there's no separate fact about what went on 'in' the dream. Dennett, in 'Are Dreams Experiences?' (1976), pushes back: that's an emperical question, not a conceptual one, and he floats the thought experiment of the dream as a kind of cassette played back at the moment you wake.

So read Inception through that. The film plainly assumes dream-time is lived time — hours inside the dream, shared dreams, the whole nested architecture — and its plot only works if what happens in a dream is something that happens to someone. Where does it quietly side with Malcolm against its own premise? Where does it come down on Dennett's side, or dramatise something neither of them saw? And where does it just cheat, because it's a film and has to show us the dream?

Don't flatten it into an illustration of the theory, and don't bend the theory to fit the film. Lead with your verdict in a single sentence, then the reading. 500 words max, no lists or tables.
