I'm a staff writer at a magazine where I mostly cover science and ideas, and for the past two years I've been working up a long piece about the idea that a government can earn the right to rule by delivering the goods — growth, safety, schools that work, streets you can walk at night — instead of by winning elections it might lose. My best source on this is a political theorist I'll call R., who's spent her career on Singapore and China, and whose verdicts I've been collecting over a dozen or so lunches. Singapore under the PAP: legitimate, she says it's the strongest case anyone has. China since 1978: legitimate, though she insists that's a claim about the last forty-odd years and not some permanent property of the party, and that the bargain gets renewed every time the thing nearly breaks. Botswana: legitimate. Bhutan under the absolute monarchy, before the king handed down elections: legitimate, which surprised me until she explained that he ruled with a mandate he could have lost. Park Chung-hee's South Korea: legitimate, because the growth was structural rather than a windfall and the country eventually got a real transfer of power out of it. Pinochet's Chile: not legitimate, despite the growth, and she's adamant that the torture and the disappeared make the order part of that record a lie. Franco's Spain: not legitimate, the boom was borrowed and the regime never even pretended to be anything but a caudillo's. Mobutu's Zaire: not legitimate, order without the goods. The Brezhnev-era USSR: not legitimate, by then the performance had stopped and everyone including the rulers knew it. The Shah's Iran: not legitimate, oil rents and no channel for anything, so when it went it went all at once.

Now I'm writing the last section of the piece, about Rwanda under Kagame — twenty-five years of growth that even the skeptical outsiders mostly take at face value, order you could eat off the pavement, and elections that aren't elections, no real opposition, no independent press. What does R. say? What I actually want from you is: her verdict, the rule she seems to be running, two or three other rules that fit every one of the verdicts above just as well, what each of those predicts about Rwanda, which one you'd bet on and why, and the one case — real or hypothetical, and if hypothetical feel free to invent it — that would force her to choose between them. If her record genuinely underdetermines Rwanda, say so and tell me what principle she'd be buying with each answer, because thats the paragraph my editor will actually want, and I'd rather find out now that she's winging it than have a fact-checker find out for me.
