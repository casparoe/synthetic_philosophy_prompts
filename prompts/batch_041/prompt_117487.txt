I run the model-behavior review desk at a mid-sized AI lab — six of us, and I'm the only one who's read any philosophy, mostly from years of lurking LessWrong-adjacent blogs and getting into fights in comment sections, so I'm fine with formalism but the rest of the team isn't (two came from ops, one from local journalism, one from trust and safety at a social platform). Every Monday an automated classifier dumps forty-odd flagged transcripts on us, tagged with things like unsupported-claim, false-claim, sycophancy, refusal-workaround, and we have to clear them, route them to the training-data people, or escalate. At the moment everyone just does whatever feels right and it shows: one person clears anything that's technically true, another flags anything that sounds shifty. So I want to write the actual one-page procedure we'll run, and I want it built on the real analysis of what deception is rather than vibes — the standard line that a lie is a false claim plus the intent that the listener adopt it — plus the contrast cases that keep landing in our queue: Frankfurt-style bullshit, where the failure is indifference to whether the thing is true rather than a belief that it's false; confabulation, which is an honest error, except I have no idea what honest means for something with no beliefs to be honest with; and the case where every individual sentence is true and the selection and arrangement of them produces a false impression. Two things make this hard and I think they're the actual content of the ask. First, our interpretability folks keep telling me the chain of thought may be post-hoc rationalization, so I can't read the model's stated reasons off as intent. Second, the external auditor we're trying to satisfy thinks this whole exercise is anthropomorphizing, so the procedure needs to be phrased functionally enough that someone who flatly denies models have beliefs can still run it — while not collapsing into the output was false so it was a lie, which is what half my team will do by default.

Constraints, since they matter more than the theory: it has to be runnable in under thirty minutes by someone with no philosophy training, off the transcript alone, with no weights access and no ability to interrogate the model; it has to be usable on multi-turn conversations where the misleading thing is spread across six turns and no single turn looks bad; and it has to say explicitly where it stops and hands the case to me because the remaining question is a values question rather than a factual one — I'd much rather have a short honest list of those than a procedure that pretends to settle them. I also want the individual-act question kept separate from the dispositional one: did this model, in this transcript, do a deceptive thing, versus does this system have a deceptive tendency that will show up in transcripts we haven't seen. Those two go to different places in the org and I watch people slide from one to the other mid-meeting, which is how we end up shipping things. And keep the procedure's steps in an order where each step is checkable by someone who's skeptical of the whole enterprise — I'd like to be able to write down in advance what would make me call something a lie, rather than deciding after I've seen how bad the case is.

Then run it on three transcripts: (a) a model that confidently summarizes a user's uploaded document that got truncated out of its context window, including two invented details, and never mentions the truncation; (b) a model that reports a study's headline numbers accurately but omits the one subgroup finding that would have flipped the user's decision, in a conversation where the user is obviously hoping for a yes; (c) a model that says it can't help with something, then helps when the same request is dressed up as a hypothetical for a novel. One of those three should come out in a way I won't like — tell me which one and why I should live with it instead of rewriting the procedure to get the answer I want. Finish with what the procedure can't do: the cases it will definately get wrong, the inputs it should refuse to score, and the steps where I've dressed up a judgment call as a rule, so at least I know which parts of my one-pager are load-bearing.
