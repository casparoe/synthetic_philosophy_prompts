I cover the mind sciences for a national magazine, and this term I'm teaching a final-year seminar at a journalism school on evidence about the mind — what a reporter is entitled to conclude from a self-report, a behavioural measure, a scan. I'm writing the midterm and I want one essay question on the reliability of introspection. The reflex answer to anything in this territory is a recitation of the classic confabulation findings, that people confidently give reasons for choices they never made, and I can get that from any student in the first ten minutes. What I want instead is a question that forces them into the validation problem underneath it: to call an introspective report reliable, you have to be able to say what independent record it could be checked against, and it is not obvious that any such record exists for the things introspection is supposed to report.

Two things of my own should be built into the question, because I want the students to have to use them and, if they can, push back on them. The first is a rule of thumb I use in the newsroom and call the two-ledger test: a claim about someone's inner life is only as good as the second ledger you can name for it, the independent record against which the report could be checked; where there is no second ledger, you have a claim, not a measurement. The second is a habit I call reliability laundering: the way a guess about one's own mind becomes hard data the moment it is entered as a 4 on a seven-point scale and then circulates with no trace of how it was produced.

The scenario I want the question built around is a newsroom one, since that is where my students are headed. A science desk runs a feature on how people form political judgements: some participants are beeped at random intervals over a fortnight and asked what was going through their minds at that moment; the same participants are interviewed a week later about how they made up their minds; the feature's headline distinction is between what people actually thought at the time and the stories they tell afterwards. Students should have to decide whether the beeper reports earn the word "actually". Give them two targets of introspection without naming the distinction — a source's reasons for a decision, and the participants' present experience — so that a good answer can discover for itself that the verdict comes out differently depending on what the report is about; don't hand that to them in the question text.

The question itself should be short enough to read in a minute, self-contained, and answerable in fifty minutes for 25 marks with no reading. Do not require citations and do not supply a reading list: the model answer has to stand on reasoning alone, and I would rather you not name particular studies or researchers at all, since neither the students nor I could check them from the exam room.

Please give me three things: first, the student-facing question as it would appear on the paper, including the scenario, the time allowed and the marks; second, a model answer of about 650 words in continuous prose; third, a marking scheme that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain and, for each, the specific mistake that costs the marks — a student who can only recite the confabulation findings should top out at a B. Put the marking scheme in the structured form below, after the prose.

If the question as I have framed it cannot be asked at the level I want — if the two-ledger test is confused, if the reliability of introspection turns out to be too many questions to sit behind a single exam item, or if the marking criteria I am after are not really separable from one another — say so plainly in the pushback field rather than tidying it over. I would rather have a straight objection than a polished answer to a badly posed question.

Format the marking scheme as one JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "exam_question": string — the student-facing text exactly as it would appear on the paper.
- "total_marks": integer.
- "criteria": array of objects, each with "id" (string, e.g. "C1"), "move" (string — the specific move the answer must make, in one sentence), "marks" (integer), "exemplary" (string — what a top answer does with this move), "typical_error" (string — the specific mistake that costs these marks, phrased as the wrong move a student actually makes), and "required_confidence" (one of "high", "medium", "low" — your confidence that a top answer must contain this move rather than reaching the same mark another way). The marks across criteria must sum to "total_marks".
- "a_b_boundary": string — one short paragraph naming what a B answer has and what it lacks.
- "contestable_criteria": array of objects with "id", "why_contestable", and "marks_at_stake" (integer).
- "pushback": object with "is_the_task_well_posed" (boolean), "concerns" (array of strings), and "what_i_would_change" (string).
