I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I've hit a wall on my current book, so bear with the long setup.

The book is about a woman with early-onset Alzheimers who signed an advance directive years ago -- no feeding tube, no antibiotics for pneumonia, no heroics -- and now she's in the late stages and honestly seems content; she likes the garden, she likes the dog, she doesn't recognize her daughter. The whole middle act is the fight between the daughter, who wants to honor the directive, and the care staff, who figure mom is happy now, so why not let her live.

To keep myself honest I had lunch with a med-ethics professor who's consulted on stuff I've written before, and he gave me his "thirty second version" of the debate, which went more or less like this:

"The whole thing is really about personal identity. An advance directive is your earlier self giving orders to your later self, and that only works if it's the same self. Alzheimer's wipes out the continuity -- memory, character, all of it -- so by the late stages the woman who signed the form is basically gone and there's a new person in her body. And that new person has her own interests, but they're just experiential, she's happy in the moment. The earlier person had what we call critical interests -- dignity, the shape of her life story. The standard line is that critical interests trump experiential ones, so you honor the directive. But if you're the kind of philosopher who thinks dementia destroys the person, then you're stuck saying the directive is a dead hand and the person who's actually sitting there should get to decide. That's the real fault line. Really the directive is just a Ulysses contract -- you tie yourself to the mast because you know the later you will be weak and won't want to do the hard thing."

He kept saying the phrase "precedent autonomy" too -- letting a past choice rule the present, is how he put it.

It's very tidy, and it hands me exactly the tragedy I need, so I'm inclined to believe it. But it's tidy in a way that makes me suspicious, and I have definately zero background here. Can you go through it like an auditor? Tell me which parts someone careful in the field would nod at, which are simplifications that lose something important, and which are just not what the actual views say -- and then tell me whether his punchline (the "new person, so the directive is void" bit) actually follows from the parts that are true, or whether it's a jump.

Please give it to me in this shape:

- "Bottom line" -- about 100 words, your overall read on how fair the summary is.
- "What he got right" -- a couple hundred words.
- "Where it's a simplification" -- the longest section, maybe 400 words, the places where the tidy version costs something.
- "What's just wrong" -- a couple hundred words, anything a philosopher would flatly deny.
- "My verdict" -- 150 words or so, on whether I can trust him and where I should double-check.
- "What I can actually use" -- a short list of the corrections that would change how my characters argue, since I'll be writing their dialogue.

No citations or reading lists please, I won't be able to chase them down and I just need to understand it.
