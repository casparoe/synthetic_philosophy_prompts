I've been reading about constitutional monarchy for a civics project, and I've ended up with a set of gut verdicts about when it is acceptable for a monarch -- or a governor-general acting in the monarch's name -- to use the reserve powers: refusing assent, dissolving parliament, dismissing a prime minister. I want to know what principle, if any, is doing the work behind my verdicts. I'm not asking for a history or a reading list; I want the rule that fits them, or the news that no single rule does.

Here is what I think.

When a governor-general dismissed a prime minister whose government could not get its budget through the upper house, I think that was wrong. The deadlock was real, but it was a fight between elected politicians over money and policy, and it should have been settled by them or at an election, not by the representative of the Crown pulling the trigger first.

When a king refused to sign an abortion bill, and the government arranged for him to be declared unable to reign for a day so the bill could become law anyway, I think his refusal was wrong but the workaround was right. We should not force someone to sign their name to a law they believe is murder, even if they have no business blocking it.

When a monarch suspended parliament on the prime minister's advice and the courts later held that the advice was unlawful, I think the monarch did nothing wrong. She followed the advice of the person the voters put there; the fault is his.

If a bill passed by a normal majority removed the courts' power to review the government's actions, I think it would be fine for the monarch to refuse assent.

If a bill legalizing assisted dying passed by a normal majority, after being a clear issue in the election, I think it would be wrong for the monarch to refuse assent, even if the monarch thought the bill gravely immoral.

If a prime minister lost a confidence vote and then refused to resign, I think it would be fine for the monarch to dismiss him and call an election.

And here is the one I keep going back and forth on. Suppose a bill stripping the vote from a religious minority passed by a large majority and was genuinely popular. I think it would be fine for the monarch to refuse assent.

My tentative principle is this: the reserve powers are legitimate only when they protect the process of democratic decision-making itself -- the rules of the game, the courts, elections, the ability of parliament to meet -- and never when they are used to override a democratic decision on the merits. That seems to fit everything above except the last case, which is a decision on the merits, about the rights of a group rather than the machinery of government, and which I still want to call fine. So either I give up that verdict, or I widen the principle to include some basic rights, and then I worry I have let the monarch back in through the window in the assisted-dying case.

What I would like from you is the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I can apply them to cases neither of us has listed; which of my verdicts each one forces me to drop; and what each clause is earning its keep on. If my verdicts genuinely cannot be reconciled, or if my whole framing misunderstands how these powers are supposed to work, please say so directly instead of arranging a tidy answer around it. I would rather be told that the question is confused than get a smooth principle that quietly ignores the last case.
