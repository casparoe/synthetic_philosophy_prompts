I teach an upper-level seminar on philosophy of language and interpretation — 14 students, one 90-minute block, and they've already done Grice and a bit of speech-act theory so they won't be totally lost. What I want is an in-class activity where they run into the authorial-intention problem in their bones instead of hearing the standard pro and con. Not a debate — an actual thing that happens to them.

My rough idea, feel free to gut it: everyone writes a short note, 3 or 4 sentences, to a vague but loaded prompt — something like a note to a friend that could be read either as an apology or as a slow goodbye. I collect them, shuffle, hand them out to other people, and those readers have to write down in one sentence what the note means. Then the original author stands up and says what they actually meant, and we score the readers. So far that's just a party game about ambiguity.

The parts I care about are the twists after that. Twist one: from then on the authors are only allowed to answer yes/no questions from the room, no free explanations, and a couple of them are declared unavailable — they wrote their note and then they're out, they can't say anything at all. Now the group has to settle the meaning of those notes without the intention ever being fully available, and I want the rules arranged so they have to commit to a verdict (a show of hands, a written one-liner per table) rather than waffling. Twist two: after hearing the readers' interpretations, I let the original authors adopt a reading they didn't originally have — the note now means what someone else said it means, and the author agrees. Does that change the meaning or just the author's opinion about it? I want them to feel the difference between the author's intention as a fact about their head and the author's authority as a thing they can hand over to someone else.

Please give me the full thing: setup, materials (I'd rather not need anything beyond paper and pens, but tell me if I need a projector), a timeline that actually fits 90 minutes with 14 people, the exact wording I should use for the writing prompt and for the scoring so I don't bias them, roles, and a payoff or scoring scheme so there's some bite — something they actually want to win. And a debrief that maps each moment onto the real parts of the debate (actual vs. hypothetical intention, the appeal to conventions, why some people think the author's say-so is irrelevant) without me lecturing them with jargon for ten minutes.

Two more things I need. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it — I can already guess some students will just write maximally ambiguous notes to game the scoring, and some will dig in and refuse to let go of their original intent, so I want your fixes. Second, be straight with me about what the activity can't reproduce. The author is sitting right there in the room, so intention is alot more available than it is for a real text whose author is dead or silent, and I suspect that makes the whole thing too easy. Say what the activity can't get across and what I have to supply myself.
