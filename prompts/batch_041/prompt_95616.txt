I serve on the ethics and risk committee of a small nonprofit that runs an annual online rationality conference, and I have been a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums—LessWrong, the old Overcoming Bias archives, the Slate Star Codex comment section—so I know how these arguments usually go. A researcher has proposed running a real-money Luring Lottery during the conference, and the committee has to decide whether to approve it. The basic design is this: any registered attendee may submit as many entries as they like. A prize of ten thousand dollars is divided equally among all entries. If no entries are submitted, the money goes to a charity chosen by the committee. The researcher argues that this tests superrationality in a way that a standard prisoner's dilemma does not, because the payoff to each participant depends on the total number of entries, not just on whether they personally cooperate. To try to induce cooperation, the researcher wants to add a pre-commitment round: before the lottery opens, each participant may pledge to submit zero entries if at least one hundred other participants pledge the same. If the threshold is met, everyone who pledged is bound to submit zero. If the threshold is not met, the pledges are void and anyone may submit any number of entries. The researcher wants to compare the pledge rates, the entry counts, and the final payouts across two conference sessions, one with the pre-commitment round and one without.

My committee is split. Some members think this is a clever and harmless experiment that the community will enjoy and that might actually teach us something about conditional cooperation. Others think it is a waste of ten thousand dollars that will almost certainly be diluted to a few cents per entry, that the pre-commitment round is a gimmick that will either fail or be gamed, and that the committee should not be in the business of running lotteries that could make participants feel foolish or manipulated. I am inclined to approve it with conditions, but I want a proper written opinion before Thursday's meeting.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—with a time frame for any conditions. It should engage the researcher's position seriously, including the strongest argument for rejecting the proposal, rather than just restating committee policy. It should say how confident you are in the recommendation and single out the point you are least sure about. Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write an email or a letter.
