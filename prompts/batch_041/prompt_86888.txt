I sit on the board of a private foundation that each year confers a single award for good government, and this year's shortlist has produced a dispute we cannot settle by procedure. The leading candidate is the government of a small Southeast Asian city-state that has delivered high and widely shared prosperity, safe streets, and competent public services while restricting political opposition, controlling the press, and suing its critics. One bloc on the board argues that the award is for what governments exist to do — secure the welfare of the governed — and that denying it to a competent non-democracy merely treats one political tradition as a neutral standard. The other argues that an award is an act of legitimation, and that conferring it would launder the suppression of dissent as good government. Please draft the memo I will circulate before Thursday's vote: a clear recommendation for what the board should do, the reasoning most likely to move the people who disagree with me, and a fair statement of the strongest case against my own position. Because we keep talking past one another about legitimacy, the memo must also say what the foundation is actually asserting when it confers this award, and confront the objection that we cannot in principle judge whether the governed accept this government, since the conditions that would let us find out — a free press, free association, the practical ability to leave — are precisely what such governments restrict.
