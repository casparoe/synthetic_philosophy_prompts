I hold a version of Arendt's thesis of the banality of evil, and I want to know how exposed it is. My version is deliberately narrow. It is not a claim about Eichmann's private psychology, and it is not the claim that perpetrators are ordinary men rather than monsters. It is the claim that large-scale evil is typically carried out by people who suspend judgment — who let rules, procedure, career, and cliché stand in for confronting what they are doing — and that this suspension, rather than any positive commitment to cruelty, is what does the explanatory work. Treat that as the position on trial.

What would count against it? Rank the defeaters by how decisive they would be, and keep two families apart: the historical record (about Eichmann, and about other perpetrators of mass atrocity) and the conceptual objections. Among the latter I especially want the charge that the thesis does not describe a fact about perpetrators at all but redescribes them, so that no evidence could count against it, and the charge that it quietly exculpates. Say which of these a defender would concede as genuinely counting against the thesis and which they would dismiss as changing the subject.

Then take a case of my own. An official has read Arendt, agrees with her, and signs the deportation order anyway, reasoning that refusal would only hand the task to someone more brutal, so that his scruples would then help no one. He is reflective rather than clichéd, and he is not deceived about what he is doing. Does this refute the thesis, illustrate it, or fall outside its scope — and does your answer show the thesis doing real work, or merely absorbing every case?

Finally: has the thesis been qualified into unfalsifiability, and is that a defect for a thesis of this kind?
