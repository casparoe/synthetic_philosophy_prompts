The teacher who runs our school's philosophy club did her graduate work in linguistics, and this term she has been leading us through the question of what exactly makes a word a slur — whether the insult lives in the word's meaning or in what the speaker does with it. She has never told us which theory she holds, and she has promised us a new case for next week. I would rather work out her verdict from the verdicts she has already given than guess at it, and I would like you to do that working out.

Her verdicts so far, on the cases we have discussed:

1. A politician refers to a group by a certain term in a sneering speech. She says the term is a slur here, and that the remark derogates the group even if the claim he is making about them happens to be true.

2. The same term written on the board during a lesson on its history, applied to nobody. Not derogatory.

3. A historian reads aloud, without endorsing it, a sentence from a nineteenth-century pamphlet in which the term is used. Not derogatory.

4. A member of the group greeted affectionately with the term by a friend from the same group. Not derogatory. But when one member of the group uses the term in a furious argument with another member of the same group. Derogatory.

The case for next week: a boy in our class, who is not a member of the group, learned the term from songs and videos in which members of the group use it of themselves, warmly and without insult. He now uses it in that spirit — sincerely, with no contempt whatever, and with no wish to be edgy — about the group in general and about a friend of his who belongs to it. The friend does not mind at all; some other members of the group who hear about it do.

What will she say about him, and why? More useful to me than the verdict: the rule she appears to be running; at least one other rule that fits every verdict above just as well; what each of those rules predicts about next week's case; which rule you would bet on, and on what grounds; whether either rule needs a special clause to get one of her earlier verdicts right; and the case she could be given that would separate the rules most cleanly. I am less interested in the answer than in the rules and the test.
