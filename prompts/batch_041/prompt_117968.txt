For the last two years or so I've been defending a position in a reading group and a long-running group chat, and I'd like someone to try to take it apart. The position has three parts. First: a rational agent's credal state need not be a single real-valued probability function. Where the evidence is sparse, ambiguous, or conflicting, the rational state is a set of probability functions, and the set is not a mere bookkeeping device for a sharp credence the agent happens to be ignorant of, nor a description of the agent's uncertainty about their own mind -- the set is the credal state itself. Second: a set need not be resolved before action. It is rationally permissible to decline to bet, to act only on options that no admissible member of the set disprefers (maximality), or to restrict yourself to options that some member of the set ranks best (E-admissibility). Third: in the two-urn setup usually called Ellsberg's -- where you may bet on a colour drawn from an urn of known 50/50 composition or on the same colour drawn from an urn of unknown composition -- the widespread preference for the known urn is a rational response to a genuinely different epistemic situation, not a bias to be trained out; it is the sharp theory, which forces a determinate fair betting quotient on everyone, that owes an explanation.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure: what would count against it, sorted by how decisive it would be, with an honest assessment of whether it has been insulated by qualifications. I have four probes in mind, though I'd rather you tell me if I've missed the important ones. (i) The dilation phenomenon. Suppose I am certain a coin is fair, so my credence in Heads is exactly 1/2, and I then learn only this: the coin's outcome matches the outcome of some other process I know nothing whatever about, the match being either perfect or perfect-and-inverted, and I am not told which. On the usual treatment, with a maximally uninformative set of priors over the unknown process, my conditional credence in Heads is now the whole interval [0,1] -- evidence that appears to say nothing about heads versus tails has made me maximally ignorant about the coin. If I have the case wrong, give me the correct version rather than a flattering one. Is that a reductio of the whole framework, or a correct lesson about what my evidence actually supports? (ii) Decision. Which of the standard ways of turning a set of credences into choices survives sequential versions of the classical Dutch book and money-pump arguments, and what does the position say about the value of evidence -- if a set-valued credence licenses refusing to bet, does it still predict that gathering evidence is worth doing, and is it true that more evidence always narrows the set? (iii) Collapse into a rival. Is there a sharp-credence model of the same behaviour -- second-order priors, ambiguity aversion reconstructed as risk aversion over the second order, and so on -- that does all the explanatory work? If so, what does my position predict that such a rival does not, and are any of those predictions the kind that could fail? (iv) Insulation. Which of the repairs a defender would reach for -- restricting the set to "reasonable" priors, adding a layer of higher-order imprecision, insisting that imprecision is epistemic rather than doxastic, adopting ever more permissive decision rules -- are substantive commitments and which are unfalsifiability with extra steps?

For each candidate defeater I want to know what it attacks in the three commitments above, whether a committed defender would concede that it counts against them or dismiss it as question-begging, and what observation or result would actually settle the matter. Don't give me citations or a reading list; I have no way to check them, so argue from the ideas themselves. (yes / qualified / no) | Defender's best reply | What would settle it.
