So my friend Maria's dog died on Thursday — Biscuit, thirteen, border collie mix, one ear that never worked — and she's doing a little thing at her place Saturday, memorial-ish, backyard, maybe fifteen people: her parents, her sister and her two kids (7 and 9), a couple of neighbours, and I've somehow ended up as the one who says something. Two minutes, fine, I can do that. The problem is Alex.

Alex is a friend who's finishing a PhD in philosophy, and last night we were up til 1am arguing about Descartes and animals, because at the vet on Thursday Alex said Biscuit's crying was just the body doing what bodies do, which did not land well. Alex's whole thing is that everyone gets Descartes wrong: he wasn't saying animals feel nothing — he leaves them the bodily side of it, feeling insofar as it goes with having organs at all, warmth, heart, that whole register — it's thought and reason he takes away. And his two tests are whether the creature ever uses words, or any sign at all, to tell you what it's thinking, and whether it acts from knowledge or just from the way its parts are arranged, like a machine that's brilliant at exactly one trick and has nothing left over. A dog on that reading is all appetite and sensation and zero understanding. I think that lets Descartes off easy, and I think the word test is a bad test (babies, my uncle after his stroke), but I definately lost the argument.

So Saturday. I want the speech to actually comfort Maria and not sound like a seminar, to not say anything Alex would call false, and to not be me surrendering either — I'm not going to stand in a backyard and imply Biscuit was a wind-up toy. Which leaves me with alot less to work with than the words that come naturally: he knew, he remembered, he understood, he was waiting for you, he was proud of himself. None of those survive Alex's reading. What's left is want, hurt, fear, comfort, sun on the patio, being near her.

Write it: roughly 350 words, spoken out loud to that room, with the kids there so nothing bleak. Invent the small concrete details, I don't care if they're made up as long as they're plausible. Put one line in somewhere that Alex hears as a concession and Maria hears as love, if you can manage it. You can say Descartes's name once if it earns its place, but no dates, no jargon, no lecture about the 17th century, and don't put any words in Descartes's mouth. Just the speech — don't explain it to me afterwards.
