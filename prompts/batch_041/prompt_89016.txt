I'm a second-year grad student working on Islamic philosophy, and over the last year I've talked myself into being an occasionalist about causation — in the neighborhood of the view Ghazali argues for against the philosophers' account of cause and effect. I've now got a pile of commitments I'm pretty attached to individually, and I'd like you to audit them as a package. Take the role of a philosopher of religion who knows the kalām tradition well and also works on contemporary metaphysics of causation, so you can put the friction in plain metaphysical terms instead of just telling me which medieval figure said what. And please don't assume I'm reporting Ghazali's actual position — these are mine. Start by paraphrasing the package back to me so I can check you've got it, then do the audit.

Here's where I've landed. (1) No created thing causes anything: fire doesn't burn the cotton, my arm doesn't move the cup, my deliberation doesn't produce my choice. God creates every event directly, including my intentions and even my beliefs, and I'll go all the way — nothing in the world holds together from one moment to the next by any power of its own. (2) There's no necessity in nature at all, only God's custom: the sun's rising is a habit God has, not something that follows from anything in the sun. The only thing that's impossible for God is the self-contradictory. (3) All the same, I'm no skeptic — the first principles of reason are certain, and sense-perception plus testimony give me real knowledge. The physician knows things about the body, the astronomer knows things about the heavens, and I don't want to be told that's merely opinion. (4) Miracles are no problem for me: a prophet's sign is just God acting outside his custom, and if anything my picture is what makes them intelligible. (5) I'm a hard voluntarist about value: good is whatever God commands, and there's no standard outside God — not even our idea of justice — that God has to answer to. (6) And yet I want to keep real moral responsibility and a just afterlife: the person who did the wrong thing is the one punished, the punishment is deserved, and God isn't being unfair in inflicting it. Methodologically I'm also committed to the kalām move — work out what's conceptually possible, and that tells you what God's power can reach. So yes, I fully expect the sun to rise tomorrow, and I also think it would be no contradiction at all if it didn't.

Now, where's the friction? I suspect a couple of these are only apparently in tension, so please seperate the real conflicts from the ones that dissolve once you spell out what I'm actually committed to, and say why the dissolving ones dissolve rather than just waving them away. Then rank the genuine ones by severity, and for each tell me precisely what has to give and what it costs me to give it. Finally, if I could only revise one thing to clean up the most, which one would it be and what would I be buying with it? Don't hedge, and don't tell me it all hangs together.
