I edit a small nonprofit magazine and maybe twice a month someone writes in furious that we, or another writer, "stole their idea" — a pitch we commissioned from someone else, an essay that runs the same argument as their blog post but in different words, a piece built on a structure they claim they invented. I have no lawyer, I have about a week, and the editor-in-chief signs off on whatever I decide. I keep muddling through on vibes and I want an actual procedure.

The thing I keep running into, and I think its the key: "owning an idea" splits into at least three claims that come apart — control (I get to stop you using it), credit (you have to name me), and priority (I was first, and that matters to my standing) — and every complaint mashes all three together. So write me a decision procedure my team can run on a complaint: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it has to hand the call to a person because it's values not facts. Ground it in what the law and the philosophy actually say about why expression is protected but ideas aren't, how plagiarism differs from infringement, when reuse is allowed, and whether it matters that the other person arrived at the idea independently rather than by talking to the complainant. No citations, just the machinery.

Then run it on three cases: (1) a freelancer says we ran a piece with the same thesis as their unpublished pitch; (2) an academic says a staff writer restated her argument in reworded form; (3) a photographer says we copied her composition. Include at least one where the procedure tells me we're the ones in the wrong, and say plainly what the procedure can't do.
