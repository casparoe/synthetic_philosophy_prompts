I have been trying to understand the Buddhist claim that there is no self, and I suspect I am missing something. As I understand it, the doctrine denies that there is a self — the thing that is supposed to make me the same person over time — and says that what we call a person is only a bundle, or a causally connected stream, of impermanent constituents. But then the doctrine of karma seems to have nothing to attach to. If the person who acts today is not identical with the person who will reap the result, either something persists and carries the debt forward — in which case the self has merely been renamed the stream and nothing has really been denied — or nothing persists, and the consequences fall on no one in particular, which looks like exactly the nihilistic reading the tradition says it rejects. Buddhists appear to want a third option, on which bare causal continuity suffices for the later person to be answerable for the earlier one's deeds. Why should that suffice? I would like an account of what exactly is being denied when the self is denied, whether my dilemma is a genuine problem for the doctrine or an artifact of assumptions I am importing, and — since I doubt I am the first to raise it — how much of this is settled among people who study Buddhist thought and how much is genuinely contested. Please keep those two apart rather than presenting a contested reading as the consensus.
