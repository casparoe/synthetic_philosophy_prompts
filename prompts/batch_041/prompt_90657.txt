Last night a friend and I argued for like two hours about one passage in the Mencius and I still can't tell who came out ahead, so I'd rather get help actually understanding the thing than pick a winner.

The passage, in my rough rendering (I don't have the book in front of me): Tao Ying asks Mencius, suppose Shun is Son of Heaven and Gao Yao is his minister of justice, and Shun's father, the Blind Old Man, commits murder. What then? Mencius says, he'd simply have him arrested. Tao Ying: so Shun wouldn't stop it? Mencius: how could Shun stop it? Gao Yao had a mandate to act on. Tao Ying: then what would Shun do? Mencius: Shun would regard giving up the empire the way you'd regard tossing out a beat-up sandal. He'd secretly hoist his father onto his back, flee, settle on the sea coast, and live there so happily for the rest of his life that he'd forget the empire.

My friend's take: this is Mencius quietly admitting that role ethics buckles under pressure. Once the roles of son and ruler actually collide, one just wins, and all the talk about harmonious roles and relational persons was decoration. His evidence is that Shun doesn't reason his way to a resolution, he bolts. He abandons the throne, breaks the law by harboring a fugitive, and the story ends with him delighted about it. That's not a synthesis, that's a man choosing blood over office and Mencius cheering him on.

My take is that my friend is missing what the details are doing, but I can't articulate why. Three things nag at me. First, Mencius has Shun refuse to interfere with Gao Yao at all, and the reason given is that Gao Yao's authority came from somewhere other than Shun's personal will, so Shun simply can't cancel it. If that's right, then the flight isn't Shun overriding the law, it's Shun removing himself from the seat that would make him the one ordering his father's death. Second, he goes secretly, which seems deliberate, like the point is that he doesn't stage a public defiance of the office. Third, and this is the one I can't get past, Shun can apparently shed the ruler role the way you'd shed a sandal, but there's no version of the story where he sheds being a son. So is the passage ranking the roles, or is it showing that one of them is detachable and the other isn't, which would be a different claim entirely?

So: what is Mencius actually doing here? Is Shun's flight a resolution of the conflict, a refusal to resolve it, or a way of exiting the conflict rather than answering it? Does the ending, where he's happy and has forgotten the empire, undercut the gravity or confirm that he got the trade right? And if a role-ethics reading can make sense of this without either collapsing into family-first particularism or pretending the roles never really clash, what does that reading actually say? Also, does it matter that Shun is a sage king, i.e. is this a special case that tells us nothing about ordinary people with conflicting roles, or is it supposed to be the paradigm case?
