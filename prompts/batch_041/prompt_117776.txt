You are a philosopher of language with two overlapping specialisms: classical Chinese thought, particularly the Warring States disputers whose texts the Han bibliographers later filed under the School of Names, and the contemporary analytic apparatus for sense and reference, opaque contexts, quantifier scope, and the distinction between identity and predication. Colleagues bring you arguments that a seminar has worried at for a term, and your job is to say exactly where the reasoning gives way. Work slowly. Before committing to a diagnosis, sketch the two or three candidate diagnoses you can see, note what each would have to give up, and only then decide. I want the reasoning, not the verdict alone.

What follows is my argument, built for a comparative philosophy seminar. It begins as a paraphrase of the White Horse dialogue — the chapter of the Warring States text attributed to Gongsun Long in which one speaker defends the proposition that a white horse is not a horse (白馬非馬) while another resists it — and then generalizes.

First strand:

(1) 'Horse' is what designates shape; 'white' is what designates color.
(2) What designates color is not what designates shape.
(3) 'White horse' designates by color and by shape; 'horse' designates by shape alone.
(4) Therefore the name 'white horse' is not the name 'horse'; a white horse is not a horse.

Second strand, about seeking:

(5) If one seeks a horse, a yellow or a black horse will do; if one seeks a white horse, they will not.
(6) If a white horse were a horse, then what is sought in the two cases would be the same.
(7) If what is sought were the same, then 'white' would make no difference to what is sought.
(8) But it does make a difference: yellow and black horses answer to one request and not to the other.
(9) Therefore a white horse is not a horse.

Then my generalization, which I have been calling the Content Principle:

(10) Whenever one name is formed by adding a specification to another, the more specific name does not designate what the less specific name designates.

I cannot accept where (10) leads. 'White horse' is more specific than 'white', so a white horse is not white. 'Horse' is more specific than 'animal', and by the test in (5)–(8) a cow answers to a request for an animal but not to a request for a horse, so a horse is not an animal. And 'there are white horses' no longer entails 'there are horses' — the very entailment the dialogue's own objector treats as undeniable, and without which nobody could count white horses among the horses.

I do not know whether to distrust (10) or to distrust my reaction to where it leads. What keeps me from simply discarding it is a case I noticed while writing it up. The principle gives the verdict I want for a decoy duck, a toy gun, a fake banknote and an imitation pearl, none of which is what its head noun names. It gives the verdict I cannot swallow for a red apple, a wooden table and a white horse. So 'decoy' and 'red' are evidently not doing the same work, and (10) treats them as though they were.

Tell me which step has to go, and why it looked innocent when I wrote it. Price the alternatives against one another: the reading on which the dialogue turns on an ambiguity in 非 between not being identical with and not being a member of; the reading on which it slides from names to the things named; and the reading on which the seeking passage concerns opaque contexts rather than horses. I would like to keep (5)–(8) if I can, since that passage feels as though it is tracking something real, and I would rather not be told that the whole dialogue is a pun — but if that is the price, tell me what is lost by paying it. Finally, say whether the unwelcome consequences of (10) should be read as a reductio of the principle or as something a disputer of that tradition might cheerfully accept, and what is left of the dialogue's interest once the culprit step is removed. I do not need citations or a reading list; I need the diagnosis.
