Frege kept a diary in his final years whose antisemitic and reactionary political entries became public only after his death. It is easy to restate the familiar alternatives — the work stands or falls on its arguments, or the man's politics contaminate the work — so I do not want the alternatives restated. I want the implications worked out. Please describe the most important implications of the diary for how Frege's philosophy should now be read and used, organized around the distinctions below, and rank those implications by weight.

First, separate three questions: (a) the truth or falsity of Frege's philosophical claims; (b) the epistemic standing of our reliance on his arguments — whether we are entitled to keep using them, and for what purposes, once we know about the diary; and (c) the genealogical question of whether his political commitments causally shaped his philosophical ones. I am most interested in (c), but I want to know whether (c) can be pursued without collapsing into ad hominem, and what a non-ad-hominem version of it would actually look like — what evidence would distinguish "his politics shaped his logic" from "one man held both views."

Second, consider a specific structural parallel. Frege's anti-psychologism, and his insistence that logical laws and thoughts are objective — not constituted by, and not answerable to, what any thinker or community happens to think — is among his most influential doctrines. His political entries include contempt for the idea that truth or right could be settled by the many, by public opinion, or by the masses. These may be two expressions of one habit of mind: truth as independent of the judging subject, and contempt for the judging subject. Suppose that diagnosis is correct. What follows? Does it threaten the anti-psychologism, leave it untouched, or merely relocate the question to the psychology of the philosopher? And does the doctrine, by its own lights, make the diary irrelevant to logic — and is that self-exemption a reason to distrust the doctrine or a reason to accept it?

Third, consider the instrumental inheritance. Contemporary philosophers of language and mathematics routinely use Frege's distinctions and apparatus — the sense/reference distinction, the function/object distinction, the idea of a formal language purged of ordinary grammar, the logicist strategy — while rejecting much of his broader philosophy of logic. What are the most important implications of the diary for whether that division of labor is stable? Is there a sense in which using the tools commits one to something of the outlook, or is the tools/outlook distinction clean?

Fourth, consider the discipline's self-image. Frege is often treated as a founding figure of analytic philosophy, and the founding is often narrated as a turn toward clarity, rigor, and argument. What are the most important implications of the diary for that narrative — for how the history is told, for what is taught, and for whether the discipline's self-understanding should change? Be specific about which implications are action-guiding for working philosophers and which are merely of historical or biographical interest.

Rank the implications by importance and explain the ranking. Do not spend space on the general question of whether biography ever matters; assume it sometimes does, and tell me what it matters to here.
