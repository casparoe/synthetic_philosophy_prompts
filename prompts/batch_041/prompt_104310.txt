My two housemates got into a weird fight last night and I got dragged in as referee, and honestly I couldn't call it. We'd been talking about Dennett's multiple drafts picture of consciousness — the idea that there's no single place in the brain where everything comes together for an audience, and that "when something happened" is fixed by which probe you run rather than by a fact sitting in the head. Housemate A said this is basically a dodge: the moment you say some content becomes the draft that gets reported and drives behavior — the one that wins — you've reinstalled the theater, you've just renamed the stage "whatever the probe happens to catch." Housemate B said no, that's the whole point, there is no single winner; different probes catch different drafts, and the feeling that there must be a central audience is a leftover from assuming experience needs a witness.

A came back with a scenario: suppose you build a system where a stimulus reliably produces a report, and every probe you can run agrees on the timing and content — isn't that just a Cartesian theater with the lights on, an audience that always happens to get the same play? B said that begs the question by building the single report in from the start, and anyway a system can be unified enough to give consistent reports without having any place where everything converges. A said fine, but then the model can't tell the difference between a genuinely unified subject and a lucky pile of processes, and that difference is the only thing we actually cared about.

Then B tried a different tack: under multiple drafts, being conscious is more like being famous than like being spotlit — content is conscious when it's won enough influence over the rest of the system, and influence is measured by downstream effects, not by anyone watching. A pounced: fame still needs a public, and the public here is just the rest of the brain, which is the audience again, so B has smuggled the theater back in under a new name. B said no, influence can be graded and distributed with no single audience, which is exactly what fame is not.

Please adjudicate this move by move — who's right at each step, where each of them is smuggling an assumption, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Don't just pick a winner overall. Keep it under 500 words.
