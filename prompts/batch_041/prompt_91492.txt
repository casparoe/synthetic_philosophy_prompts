You are a literary fiction writer with a strong grasp of game theory and moral psychology, and I need a short story for a seminar I am putting together on practical rationality and commitment. The story should do for the dollar auction what a good chess problem does for tactics: a reader should be able to reconstruct the mechanism from the narrative alone, without a lecture. The setup is an auction in which the highest bidder pays their bid and receives the prize, while the second-highest bidder also pays their bid and receives nothing. Please write a polished literary short story, not an essay or a fable, set at a small-town estate auction in a church basement after a family funeral. The contested object is a battered 1970s pickup truck that belonged to the narrator's late father; it is being sold, along with the rest of the estate, to settle debts, and its monetary value is modest but its sentimental value is not. The auctioneer is a smooth, slightly theatrical man who understands exactly what his rules do to people and enjoys it a little too much. The bidders should include the narrator and at least one other person with a reason to want the truck that is not simply resale value, perhaps a former employee of the father or a neighbor with an old grievance. I want the story to move through at least three distinct engines of escalation: sunk-cost reasoning, the pressure to remain consistent with one's earlier bids and self-image, and the strategic realization that the other side cannot afford to back down either. Include a moment when a third party proposes a rational exit, such as a side payment, a coin flip, a joint purchase, or an agreement to split the loss, and show why it fails or nearly works. Let the arithmetic of the trap be visible in the dialogue and the characters' calculations, but never have anyone explain the concept in a classroom voice. The ending should be ambiguous but earned: the reader should feel both the absurdity and the inevitability, and should be able to work out the auctioneer's profit. Before writing, give me a brief brainstorm of five to seven bullet points, under 150 words total, covering the setting, the object, the cast, and the escalation engines. Then write the story. The entire response, brainstorm and story together, must be no more than 1,100 words. Use American English. Think carefully about the game theory and the psychology before you begin, and make the prose polished enough to read aloud in a seminar.
