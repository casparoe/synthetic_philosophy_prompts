I am a novelist, and I am drafting a scene in which two characters — one a philosopher of biology, the other a metaphysician — argue across a dinner table about when, if ever, several things compose a further thing. I once heard an argument of this shape between two friends of mine, and I have set it down below as faithfully as I can, intending to give it to my characters more or less as it stands. I am not equipped to judge it myself, and I would rather not have my narrator deliver a verdict that a knowledgeable reader would find callow. What I want from you is not a general essay on the subject but a point-by-point adjudication: for each move in the exchange, who has the better of it and why; and, at the end, what each of them is missing. You need not cite anything; I shall see to the references myself.

The dispute turns on a case that one of them raises. Suppose there is a creature — call it a glom — that begins as a single cell, divides into some hundreds of cells that remain in contact and act as a unit, and then, under stress, separates into hundreds of free-living cells that each forage for themselves; later the cells may reunite and behave as one creature again. It has no central organ; whatever coordination there is arises locally, each cell responding to its neighbours. The question the two of them fight over is whether, while it is together, the glom is one thing or merely many things in a convenient clump, and, if it is one thing, whether it is the same thing before and after it comes apart.

I will label them B (the philosopher of biology) and M (the metaphysician).

B: When the glom is together it is one thing, because it is one life — its parts are caught up in a single self-maintaining process. When it comes apart it is many things, because there is no longer one life but many. So composition is restricted: some pluralities compose a whole and others do not, and the ones that do are marked by being a single life.

M: Your criterion is unstable, and your own case exposes it. You say the glom is one thing together and many apart, but nothing has come into or gone out of existence in any ordinary sense — the same cells persist throughout. You have made the population of the world rise and fall with how tightly things happen to be touching. And you have not told me whether the reassembled glom is the same thing as the first. By your lights the first creature died when it scattered and a second was born when it regathered, which is an odd thing to say of something that merely came apart and joined again.

B: It is no odder than saying that a shrub dies back to the root in winter and returns in spring. I am not counting cells; I am counting lives, and a life may be interrupted.

M: Then I will take the simpler view. Composition is unrestricted: there is a thing composed of those cells whether or not they touch, and there is a thing composed of your left shoe and the moon. The glom is simply the name we give to one of these sums, the one we happen to care about. I pay for this with shoe-moons; you pay for it with a world in which objects blink in and out of existence as their parts drift together and apart.

B: Unrestricted composition is not simpler, it is profligate. If every plurality composes a thing, then there is a thing made of the three nearest grains of salt and the second chair, and there are endlessly many such things, and no reason to care about any of them. You have not explained why the glom is a thing; you have explained why nothing in particular is.

M: Nothing needs to be special. Objecthood can be cheap; the interesting work is done by our concepts, not by a privileged class of sums. And your life criterion has a boundary it cannot draw without vagueness. Where does life begin — a virus, inert until it enters a cell? A prion, which replicates though it is not alive by most reckonings? A mule, alive but childless? A tumour, which is alive, has its own metabolism and its own evolution, and yet is part of a larger living body? If you cannot draw the line, you have not given me the fact of the matter you promised.

B: My uncertainty about where to draw the line is not uncertainty about whether there is a line. I may be unsure where a mountain ends without doubting that there are mountains. And you have a boundary problem of your own: if any plurality composes a thing, then the sum of the chair and my wine glass is exactly as real as the chair, and the word object has been emptied of content.

M: Made abundant, not emptied. And your view cannot accommodate the things we care about most. A marriage, a corporation, a conversation, a species — none is alive, yet we individuate them, count them, and argue about whether they survive change. Either your criterion excludes them, or you stretch life until it covers any organized activity, and then the storm outside and the fire in the grate are alive too.

B: Then let me stretch it. What unites the cells of the glom, the members of a corporation, and the notes of a conversation is that they hang together as one ongoing activity directed at maintaining or extending itself. Call that life, or call it something else; the name does not matter. What matters is that there is a real difference between a heap and a thing, and unrestricted composition denies it.

M: Then your stretched criterion now has the glom doing what nothing else does. If life is any self-maintaining activity, a whirlpool is alive while it lasts, and the glom is alive whenever it is together; and you still owe me an account of the reunion. Is the reassembled glom the same life as the first, or a second? You cannot say the same without allowing a life to be interrupted and resumed, and you cannot say a second without conceding that what makes something one thing is how we tell its story, not what there is.

B: I can say the same, in the way I say the shrub is the same — by the continuity of the process and its power to resume. The scattering is a phase, not a death.

M: Then we agree that a thing can survive being scattered into many things, which is only to say that being one thing was never a matter of the parts at all, but of a story we tell about them. And if that is so, my sums are no worse off than your lives.

Please make the adjudication as granular as the exchange itself. Where one of them scores a point the other simply ignores, say so; where a move is clever but proves less than it appears to, say that too. Tell me, for each of the four or five cruces, who is right and why — for instance, whether M's charge that the life criterion is vague actually lands, whether B's mountain reply answers it or merely postpones it, and whether M's closing claim that identity has collapsed into storytelling really follows from what B has conceded or is a sleight of hand. And tell me plainly whether one of the two is more nearly right overall, or whether the honest ending is that the case has driven a wedge between two views that are each defensible — because that will decide whether my scene ends in concession, in stalemate, or in a quiet change of subject.
