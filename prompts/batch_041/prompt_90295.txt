I cover artificial intelligence and the philosophy of mind for a general readership, and for an upcoming feature I want to run a constructed dialogue — two voices, roughly a thousand words — between two researchers who take opposite sides on whether the alignment proposal usually called HCH is a defensible idealization of deliberation. My defender treats HCH as the only usable stand-in for what a sufficiently careful deliberator would conclude: one human takes a question, carves it into pieces, hands the pieces to further humans, recurses until the pieces are trivial, and the output of that whole process is simply stipulated to be the right answer — which, she says, at least defines the target operationally instead of leaving it as a philosopher's fiction. My critic grants the construction's practical value but denies that it idealizes deliberation at all: on his view it idealizes delegation, a hierarchy of managers passing work downward, and its verdict is fixed by an arbitrary choice about how the question gets carved up, so two equally competent factored processes could return different answers to the same question — which is to say the ideal is not well defined in the first place. Have them argue the decomposition problem head-on: whether the carving is the hard part of thinking or a mere convenience, whether an insight that arrives only when many things are held in view at once — an analogy, a reformulation, one move that collapses several subgoals at a stroke — survives factoring or is precisely what factoring cannot represent, whether verification really is easier than generation in the cases that matter, and whether a recursion that must bottom out in finite, context-limited humans is a fixed point or a regress. They should also disagree about the general thesis behind it, that hard cognition factors into easier subtasks whose answers recombine, and about whether that is a discovery about minds or just a description of offices. Let each land a real point: the defender should force the critic to say what unfactored ideal deliberation could even mean and how anyone would recognize its output, and the critic should extract a concession about arbitrariness or convergence. Both should stay competent, neither a strawman, with no appeals to who said what and no titles, dates, citations, or invented figures. Ground the abstractions in at least one concrete question they can both chew on — something ordinary but genuinely hard, such as whether a proposed rule would do more harm than good, or whether a tangled argument commits a fallacy.
