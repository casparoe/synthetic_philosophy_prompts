I teach one ninety-minute session in a combined history-and-philosophy graduate seminar, twelve students, on overdetermination and multi-causal explanation in history. The room is split down the middle: the historians think "there were several causes" needs no theory and resent the metaphysics; the philosophers will test every historical claim with a counterfactual and have never handled a source. Plan how I should teach this — not the content itself but the sequence, the carrying example, and the failures to expect.

Begin with the dependency order. Say what has to be in place before the question "was this outcome overdetermined?" can even be posed to this room, what depends on what, and why the session should end where you put the end.

I want the session organized around a three-way classification I use, which I give you now so that you can build the sequence on it rather than invent one: (i) redundant sufficiency, where each strand on its own would have produced the outcome; (ii) joint sufficiency, where only the strands together produce it; (iii) contributory strands, where no strand is sufficient either alone or jointly, but each is an insufficient, non-redundant part of a condition that was sufficient in the circumstances. The payload is not the classification but what follows from reading one archive under all three headings, and then whether the choice among readings is settled by the past or by the question being asked — why this outcome rather than some other, against what background of normalcy — and finally whether that makes historical causal structure mind-dependent.

For the carrying example I have invented the following case, which I will hand out on a single page and which comes from no real archive, so that nobody can short-circuit the reasoning with prior knowledge: In 1907 a landlocked state's legislature votes 31–29 to enter a customs union with its larger neighbor. Three strands converge: a harvest failure in the eastern provinces makes the standing grain tariff politically intolerable; a foreign bank offers a loan whose terms require tariff alignment; and a press campaign after the harvest turns the vote into a test of whether the government survives. Two deputies are absent through illness, which is what makes the margin look decisive; but the party that would have taken office had the vote failed had itself committed to accession. I can defend each of the three readings above from this same set of facts. Tell me whether this case can carry the whole session or must be replaced; if it must be, specify what its replacement must have and must not have rather than substituting a famous episode about which half the room already has opinions. Tell me also whether to reveal the three strands at once or one at a time, and in what order.

Then the misconceptions. I expect at least these: that "several causes" means each cause contributed a share, on an additive or vector-sum model; that overdetermination implies inevitability; that a strand which was not necessary was not a cause at all; that counterfactual reasoning about the past is fiction; and, from the other side of the room, that once causal selection is granted to be pragmatic the selection is arbitrary. For each, tell me where in the sequence it should be headed off, the exact move that does it, and which of them will re-form in a new guise later in the session.

Close with: one question to put to the room at the end that shows whether they hold the distinction rather than remember the three labels; what to leave out at this level and to say so openly (I expect to cut the possible-worlds semantics of counterfactuals, probabilistic causation, and the covering-law debate); timing and board layout; and how to keep the historians from checking out during the metaphysics and the philosophers from treating the case as decoration. No readings or citations — I have the syllabus.
