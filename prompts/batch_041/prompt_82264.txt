Write a dialogue of the kind a decision-theory reading group would circulate as a handout. It takes place in the briefing room of a new experiment in the Platonia-dilemma family — call it the Toll Lottery — an hour before the participants are taken to their rooms. The rules, read aloud to all one hundred participants and common knowledge among them: each participant is given one token and may either drop it into the envelope provided or keep it; if exactly one token is sent in total, its sender receives $1,000,000 and everyone else receives nothing; if no tokens are sent, every participant receives $1,000; if two or more tokens are sent, everyone receives nothing. Nothing said in the briefing room is binding, and once the doors close there is no communication of any kind and the rooms are soundproofed.

The dialogue is between two of the participants, both equally sharp and both arguing in front of the other ninety-eight. The first holds that everyone should keep their token, and argues it from the fact that the hundred of them are alike in the relevant respect: a decision procedure run in a hundred identical places does not yield a hundred independent answers, so the comparison that matters is between the outcomes the group can jointly reach — all send, or none send — not between my sending and my not sending with everyone else held fixed. The second holds that this is not a principle of rationality at all but a collective standpoint smuggled in under an individual one: once you ask what any one participant has reason to do, abstaining is weakly dominated by sending, since if nobody else sends you take home a million rather than a thousand, and if anyone else does send you get nothing either way. On this view the demand to abstain is either a false causal claim (my keeping my token somehow produces yours) or an appeal to what we ought to do together, which is not the question I face.

Requirements. Neither character may be a strawman, and neither may simply announce a position: no appeals to causal decision theory, evidentialism, team reasoning, or any other named view, and no citations — every argument must be made in the characters' own words and in the terms of this lottery. Each must respond to what the other actually said rather than restating their own line, and each must land at least one point the other has to concede. The exchange should be pushed far enough that a reader can see both where the two positions genuinely diverge and where they may be talking past each other, and it should confront at some point whether a public argument that one of the two rejects can still guarantee that all hundred reason alike. Close with the two of them, as the doors are closing, saying what each will do and what each takes the other's choice to show.

Give the response in three named sections, in this order:

1. The setup — at most 100 words, stating the rules and the two theses in plain language, the way the characters themselves would put them.
2. The dialogue — 1,000 to 1,400 words, speakers consistently labelled, in fairly short alternating turns.
3. Commentary — 150 to 250 words, on which argument did the most work, where each side was weakest, and what the exchange leaves undecided.
