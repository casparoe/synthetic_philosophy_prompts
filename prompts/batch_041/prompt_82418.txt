Begin from the Sleeping Beauty problem, and from a feature of it that usually goes unremarked: the standard setup makes three things vary together. The number of candidate worlds, the number of awakenings, and the total time the subject spends experiencing anything all rise and fall in lockstep with the outcome of the coin. As a result, two genuinely different principles about what the subject should treat herself as a random sample of — a random awakening, or a random stretch of experienced time — are never forced apart, and the familiar disagreement between halfers and thirders can be conducted without anyone having to choose between them.

Construct a self-locating scenario, in the Sleeping Beauty family, that forces the choice.

Requirements:
1. The subject is an ordinary adult human, told the full protocol in advance. The only departure from existing technology permitted is a drug that blocks memory between awakenings; no duplication of persons, no creation of persons from nothing, no infinite or branching worlds, no exotic physics. The scenario should be no more outlandish than the original.
2. The chance device is fair and ordinary.
3. Three counting principles must yield three strictly different credences for the same non-indexical hypothesis, each stated as a ratio of branch weights to total weight: (i) counting each possible world once; (ii) counting each awakening once; (iii) counting each awakening in proportion to how long it lasts.
4. The awakening-count and the duration-count must land on opposite sides of the world-count, so that the two self-locating principles push the same credence in opposite directions — the reverse of the standard case, where every self-locating count pulls the same way.

Then, in order: give the three credences with their arithmetic, and say what each count presupposes about the subject's sampling; identify the one feature of your scenario that makes the counts diverge, and give the minimal change that makes the awakening-count and the duration-count agree again, with the resulting credence; say which count you would defend and whether your scenario shows that the standard problem is under-specified as usually stated; and say how a defender of the count you reject would try to block your case. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph.
