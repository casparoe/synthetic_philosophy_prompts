Okay so this is a slightly weird ask for a software engineer, but here goes. I'm on the autonomy team for a home-care robot — it lives in a resident's flat in an assisted-living building, reminds them about medication, keeps an eye on whether they've eaten, can ring the front desk or a family member, and in the next build it's getting what product calls "nudging": it can hold a reminder back, escalate to a human, or just stand aside and let the resident do the thing it thinks is a bad idea (going out in the cold, skipping lunch, waving off the pills). Right now every one of those calls goes to a person at the front desk and the queue is killing us, so they definately want a decision layer inside the robot instead. We did a lunchtime reading group on machine ethics and I came out of it more confused than I went in, and I've read alot of this stuff since and it doesnt converge — one camp says write the rules down, another says let it learn from cases, a third says the hard part isn't the algorithm, it's that nobody can actually tell you what the rules should be, and everyone agrees the trolley stuff is a distraction, because our robot will never see a trolley, it'll see a hundred boring Tuesday-afternoon questions about whether Mrs. Alvarez may skip her walk when it's 2 degrees out. A mate of mine who does human-robot interaction keeps saying the real danger is that when a semi-autonomous system screws up, the blame always lands on whichever human was nearest the controls, even when they had no way to stop it, and I can already see that here: it'll be the front-desk person who gets blamed for whatever the robot decided at 3am.

What I actually need from you is a procedure, not a survey. Something my team can implement as a decision layer and that our clinical lead and our legal person can read and sign off on without a philosophy degree: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops and says no, and where it deliberately hands the decision to a person instead of pretending the machine can make it. It has to run in under a second with nobody in the loop, it has to be auditable afterwards (we need to be able to say why the robot did what it did in a sentence a seventy-year-old resident could follow), and it has to cope with the fact that the resident may be cognitively impaired while the family is the one paying us and the building is the one carrying liability. Pick an approach and commit to it — if you think this has to be a top-down rule set with a hard escalation path, say so, defend it, and tell me exactly what I give up by not letting the thing learn; if you think the learning approach is right, argue that instead. Don't hand me five frameworks and a shrug.

Then run it on three cases so I can see where it bites: a resident who's refused her medication three days running and says she understands the risk; a resident who wants to walk to the corner shop alone in bad weather and gets angry every time the robot suggests waiting; and a family member who's asked us to have the robot quietly report where the resident goes during the day, without telling her. I'm expecting at least one of those to land somewhere I don't like — if the honest procedure ends up saying we shouldn't ship the nudge feature at all, or that every override has to be reviewed by a human within a day, then say that, and tell me what it would take to change your mind. And finish with an honest list of what the procedure can't do: the cases it will get wrong, the steps that are really just my team's engineering choice dressed up as ethics, and the point where I should stop pretending and go hire someone who actually knows this stuff.
