I am a novelist, four books in, and I have contracted to write a fifth: a novel set in the middle of the nineteenth century, largely inside the household of a provincial official serving an empire. I have spent three years reading in the period, and I can now hear its idiom in my sleep, which is exactly what worries me. The book obliges me to make, at a rate of perhaps once a page, a decision for which I have no method: how to render conduct and attitudes that my characters take entirely for granted and that my readers will find monstrous — the ordinary management of servants, the correction of children and of servants, casual religious contempt, the assumption that some lives are worth less than others. Occasionally the polarity reverses, and I must render something my characters take for granted that my readers will find admirable.

I have read enough history to distrust the easy answers. I know that the standards of a period are not one thing, that some of my characters' contemporaries were already arguing against what my characters do, and that when a person invokes the standards of the time they usually mean the standards of whoever in that time was powerful enough to have theirs recorded. I know that the question whether a person could reasonably have known better is not the question whether what they did was wrong, and that both differ from the question whether I, four generations later, may condemn them. I know that my narrator is a present-day instrument and that a novel has no footnotes with which to hold my judgments at arm's length. And I know that the two failure modes — the costume drama in which the reader is invited to enjoy cruelty because that is how it was, and the moral pageant in which my characters are merely late-arriving versions of my readers — are not opposites but the same failure approached from two directions.

What I want from you is a procedure, not an essay. Give me an ordered set of questions I can run on a single scene while drafting: what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops and tells me to render the scene in period terms with no signal at all; where it stops and tells me the scene cannot be written as drafted; and where it hands the decision back to me as a question of values rather than craft. Keep it short enough to run in the middle of a working day, and mark which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Tell me where it will misfire, and on what kind of scene.

Then run it, in full, on three scenes. First, my protagonist — whom I want the reader to love for eighty pages before this moment — orders a flogging that was lawful and unremarkable in his world, of a man we have not met and will not meet again. Second, a genuinely funny comic set piece at the expense of a religious minority, in which the most charming character in the book is also the funniest; the scene works on the page and I want to keep it. Third, a scene in which the practice in question was already contested by articulate contemporaries and my character is knowingly on the wrong side of a live argument — this is where the procedure must do the most work.

At least one of these should come out of your procedure with an answer I will not like; do not soften it on my account. Then tell me what the procedure cannot do, and in particular whether it can answer the question I actually lose sleep over, which is not whether a scene is anachronistic but whether the book should exist in this form at all.

No bibliography, no reading list, no citations — I am not writing a thesis and I have no way to check them. Argue from positions and reasons I can follow without having read the secondary literature.
