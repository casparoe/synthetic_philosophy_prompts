Last night a friend and I argued about composition until nearly two in the morning, and we got nowhere. She holds the unrestricted view: any things whatsoever compose a further thing, so there is an object whose parts are the Eiffel Tower and my left shoe. I could not accept that, but when I tried to say when things do compose something, I found I was going on feel rather than on principle. I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, my verdicts amount to. I am not asking for a survey of what has been written on the question; I want the principle, stated precisely enough that I can test it. I would rather be told that my verdicts are inconsistent than be handed a principle that saves them by brute force.

Here are my verdicts, as plainly as I can put them.

1. A cat asleep on the windowsill. One object. Whatever composition is, it had better deliver cats.
2. Two LEGO bricks pressed together until the studs snap. One object; something came into being at the snap, and I can pick the pair up as a single item.
3. The same two bricks lying side by side, touching but not snapped. Two objects. Mere contact adds nothing.
4. A dry-stone wall, no mortar, the stones stacked so that each holds the next in place. One object. If someone said they would move the wall, I would not take them to have said they would move several hundred objects and no wall.
5. The same stones in a heap where they fell. Not one object. This troubles me, because the difference between the wall and the heap is arrangement, and I am not sure arrangement is enough to make a thing.
6. A lichen on a rock, a fungus and an alga living as a single crust. One object. Two organisms, one thing.
7. A clownfish living in a sea anemone. Two objects, not one, though neither thrives without the other. I cannot say what separates this from the lichen except that the lichen looks like one thing and these look like two, which is no principle at all.
8. A sandcastle, dry or damp. One object. I will not give this up, and I know it sits badly beside the heap of stones.
9. A traffic jam. One thing: it has a place, a cause, a beginning and an end, and it creeps backwards up the motorway. Yet it is not composed of its cars, since the cars that caused it are long gone and it outlives them.
10. A murmuration of starlings at dusk. Not one object. A pattern, and a lovely one, but nothing new has come into being. This sits badly beside the traffic jam, and I suspect the only difference is that I find one of them interesting.
11. A bronze statue. One object. I do not believe the statue and the lump of bronze are two objects sharing a place; they are one object under two descriptions.
12. A ship whose planks are replaced one at a time until none of the original timber remains. The same ship. If the discarded planks are later reassembled into a ship-shaped thing, that is a replica, not the ship.
13. Two strangers standing in a lift. Two objects. If one is holding the other up because the other is unsteady, still two objects; help is not composition.

My best attempt at a principle was this: parts compose something when they act together as one. That is plainly too vague, since on one reading it admits the sandcastle and the lift and on another it excludes them, and I would like to know whether it can be made precise without collapsing.

Please answer in six sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths.

1. The shape of my verdicts (about 150 words): what pattern the verdicts suggest, and which of them sit badly with which.
2. Candidate principles (about 500 words): two or three principles, each stated precisely enough to apply, each with the verdicts it gets right and the ones it fails.
3. Repairs (about 350 words): take the most promising candidate and revise it to handle its failures, or say why the failure is fatal.
4. Cases I did not list (about 300 words): what the surviving principle or principles say about new cases, including at least two cases chosen to separate the survivors from one another.
5. What I have to give up (about 150 words): the verdicts of mine that no acceptable principle can keep.
6. Bottom line (about 120 words): the principle I should adopt, and the verdict I should abandon along with it.
