I'm on a small working group that's building a wellbeing index for our city's budget process -- the kind of thing where every department has to show that its proposal improves residents' lives, and the economists have (fairly) told us that until we can say what improves lives means, we're just measuring GDP with extra steps. So before I go to bat for any particular view, I want the spec: what does a theory of wellbeing actually have to deliver to be usable as the welfare standard in a public cost-benefit analysis? Not which theory is true -- the requirements list. The cases it has to get right, with the consideration behind each: the experience machine, sure, but also the ones we keep running into in practice -- the person who's adapted to a genuinely bad situation and reports being fine with it, the achievement nobody ever finds out about, the preference we satisfy without them ever knowing, the thing that's bad for someone now and good for them later, the person who wants their life to have gone a certain way even though it didn't. The distinctions it can't collapse -- how well a life is going for the person vs. how much they like it vs. what they'd choose; one person's welfare vs. the size of the pie; worse for someone vs. worse on balance. And the constraints that come from the fact that we're going to have to measure, compare, and add these things up across millions of people and defend the result in a council meeting, which I suspect is where half my list is going to die.

Mark which requirements are genuine desiderata and which are just the prejudices of the utility-maximizing crowd I'm stuck with, and don't pad it with a reading list -- I'm not going to read anything before Thursday, I just need to know what I'm demanding. The part I most want is the collisions: any two requirements you think can't both be met, and why the standard moves for rescuing one (say, admitting paternalism to fix adaptive preferences, or dropping comparability to keep the theory faithful to individual subjects) end up costing you somewhere else on the list. I have a hunch that don't tell people what's good for them and don't count preferences formed under duress can't coexist, but tell me if I'm wrong, and whether the same tension shows up in a place I haven't thought of. Then one line each on hedonism, desire-satisfaction, and objective-list views against the list, and your pick for the single requirement that does the most killing.
