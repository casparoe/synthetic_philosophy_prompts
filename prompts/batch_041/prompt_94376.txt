I am preparing a graduate seminar session on peer disagreement, and before we read any further I want the specification written down rather than assumed. Give me the desiderata that any adequate theory of how to respond to a disagreement with an epistemic peer has to satisfy: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, the results it must be consistent with. Attach to each requirement the consideration that motivates it, and mark which ones you take to be genuine requirements as opposed to prejudices of a particular camp.

Two cases I want the list to answer to, since I have not seen them treated. First, a disagreement that is symmetric from the inside and asymmetric from the outside: each party reasonably takes the other for a peer with the same evidence, while a third party who knows both track records can see that one of them is badly miscalibrated. Second, a disagreement in which the parties agree on the first-order facts and disagree only about whether those facts are a symmetry-breaker, so the dispute is about what would count as evidence of a mistake rather than about the original question. Say what the list implies about how peerhood is to be identified, and whether that identification can be carried out from the first-person standpoint at all.

Then tell me which single requirement does the most excluding work, and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — the demand that a theory apply to itself is my main suspicion, but say so if a sharper conflict is available. Close with a few sentences on how the equal-weight and steadfast families fare against the list; skip the full scorecard.
