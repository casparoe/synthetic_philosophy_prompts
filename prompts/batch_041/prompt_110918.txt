Write a dialogue between two philosophers: an expressivist, who holds that a judgement such as "eating meat is wrong" expresses a practical commitment rather than a belief about a moral fact, and a descriptivist critic, who holds that moral sentences must have ordinary truth-conditional content if they are to figure in valid inferences. Let the expressivist defend an unorthodox version of the view: the meaning of a moral predicate is given by its inferential role in practical reasoning — the transitions it licenses from non-moral premises to a decision — and embedded occurrences (under negation, in the antecedent of a conditional, inside an attribution to a third party, inside a supposition adopted only for the sake of argument) are handled by a separate account of how practical commitments are inherited across contexts, rather than by assigning those occurrences truth-conditions. The critic should press the standard embeddings seriously but without caricature, and should in particular press: what attitude "not wrong" expresses, and why it is inconsistent with the attitude expressed by "wrong" rather than merely different from it; how modus ponens can be valid when the conditional's antecedent is never asserted; whether the account can distinguish genuine moral disagreement from a mere divergence of attitude; and whether the appeal to inferential role has quietly smuggled a belief about a norm back in. The expressivist should have real replies, including at least one concession, and should turn at least one of the critic's objections back on the descriptivist's own account of how non-moral predicates earn their inferential roles. Neither party should be a strawman, and neither should win outright; the dialogue should end with the disagreement sharpened rather than settled.
