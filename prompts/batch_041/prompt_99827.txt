I am a novelist by profession, though for the past six years I have also taught an upper-level seminar — cross-listed between the philosophy department and the writing program — on the ethics of ordinary speech, and I am now composing the final examination for it. In the middle third of the course I introduce a framework of my own devising, which I call the ledger of interpretive labour, and I would like the examination to put that framework under real pressure rather than merely rehearse it. The idea is this: every utterance distributes work between two parties — the work of constructing a meaning, the work of inferring which meaning was intended, the work of managing one's own and the other's feelings, and the work of deciding whether to notice at all what has been said. On this view the interesting differences among Dutch directness, Japanese indirection, and the ordinary white lie are not differences in veracity but in who performs which labour and who retains which option. The blunt speaker keeps the labour of tact for herself and leaves the hearer no room not to have heard; the indirect speaker hands the labour of inference to the hearer but leaves her the option of declining the inference; the liar removes the labour of inference and closes the option altogether. I came to this framework the hard way: the Dutch editor who told me my ending was sentimental and unearned and the Tokyo editor who told me the same thing by observing that the ending might be difficult for their readers were, I am fairly sure, reporting the same judgement — and I have lost half a friendship by insisting for a year that a manuscript was coming along nicely when I knew it was not.

Please take on the role of a philosopher of language who is also a seasoned examiner, and before you write a single line of the examination, think the problem through on your own: where exactly is the crux — the point at which the ledger either earns its keep or collapses into restatement? Only then draft, for a ninety-minute closed-book final, a single essay question that cannot be answered by reciting the standard maxims of conversation or the received face-and-politeness machinery, and that obliges students to do three things at once: to say whether white lies are wrong and on what principle; to say whether Dutch directness and Japanese indirection are two techniques for one task or two genuinely different tasks; and to confront the hardest case for the ledger — the case in which indirection is so thoroughly conventional that insiders experience no labour whatsoever, while an outsider is simply misled — and to state what would have to be true for the ledger to be the wrong instrument altogether. After the question, supply a model answer of roughly seven hundred words and a marking scheme built on six must-hit points worth twenty-five marks in total, with a one-line note against each point naming the characteristic error that costs a candidate the mark.

Keep the question pitched at students who have only the course materials and no library to hand: nothing in the question, the model answer, or the scheme should require them to cite or even recall a named theorist, a title, or a date, and please do not pad the model answer with proper names to make it look authoritative — the substance must carry it. The scheme should genuinely discriminate, so that an A answer and a B answer differ in the moves they make rather than in the number of terms they deploy, and it should reward the candidate who notices that the ledger's treatment of the Japanese case may rest on an assumption about who counts as an insider, while docking the two standard failures: collapsing into whatever the local norm happens to be, and quietly crowning one culture's speech style as correct under the heading of universal respect. Phrase the question so that a student who has memorized the vocabulary but not followed the argument has nothing to hide behind, and make sure the whole thing is answerable, at the level of a strong answer, in about fifty minutes of writing.
