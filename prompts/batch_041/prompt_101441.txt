Last night I lost three hours to an argument with a close friend, a philosopher of biology who holds that the whole literature on finks and masks is a parlor game: that these cases are artifacts of specifying stimulus conditions unnaturally narrowly, and that no working scientist has ever been helped or hindered by them. I took the other side, and somewhere in the second hour I found myself defending a thesis sharper than the one I began with. Next week I have to present that thesis to a much less sympathetic room, and I want to walk in with a map of the objections rather than a hope that they will not arise.

The thesis I mean to defend is that finks and masks are not two kinds of interference but one phenomenon seen from two sides, and that which label a case receives depends on where the speaker has drawn the line between a disposition's stimulus condition and its manifestation condition. The sketch I will be working from: the standard Lewis-style conditional analysis treats a disposition as a conditional, such that if the stimulus condition obtains the manifestation follows. A fink is a process that removes the disposition's causal basis before the stimulus arrives; a mask is a process that blocks the manifestation after the stimulus has arrived, while the basis persists. My claim is that every mask can be redescribed as a fink relative to a narrower stimulus condition, and every fink as a mask relative to a broader one, so that the taxonomy is index-relative rather than world-relative. Two consequences follow, on my telling: the two customary repairs to the conditional analysis, a no-fink clause and a no-mask clause, are one repair wearing two hats; and the real explanatory work is done not by the taxonomy but by whatever principled account fixes the stimulus/manifestation boundary. For that last part I will propose that the boundary is fixed by the point at which the disposition's causal basis is fully assembled, so that anything preventing assembly is a fink and anything interfering afterward is a mask.

The room is a one-day workshop on dispositions in the special sciences. Present will be several philosophers of science who work on dispositional concepts in biology and psychology — fitness, disease susceptibility, trait talk — and who distrust intuition pumps; two graduate students who know the fink and mask literature but are impatient with metaphysics; and a pair of dispositional essentialists who will say that finks and masks were disposed of years ago, when the conditional analysis was shown to have been only ever a rough first pass. The chair is a Humean about laws who regards talk of powers as a category mistake.

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet. I have a twenty-minute talk and roughly ten minutes of questions. Give me the objections I will actually get in this room, not the ones I would get in a metaphysics seminar; order them by the damage they would do; phrase each in the vocabulary of the person likely to raise it; give me a two-minute reply for each; and note where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front rather than fight, which single reply has to land for the thesis to survive the session, and — since my friend's complaint will surely reappear in some form — what the strongest version of it is and how I should meet it. Finally, name the question I am hoping nobody asks: the one that exposes the weakest joint in what I have sketched above.
