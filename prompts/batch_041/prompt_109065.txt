I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been asked to join a small internal group drafting our company's position on whether the models we build can be said to understand, want, or intend anything. We have read enough to know that the interesting version of the question is not about consciousness but about whether anything in these systems has original rather than merely derived intentionality. The usual pressure points are the Chinese room and Dennett's two-bitser (a device so minimal that the intentional stance would still, on his account, license us to describe it in belief-and-desire terms), and the intuition most of my colleagues start from is that a shopping list is about groceries only because of the intentions of the person who wrote it. What I need is not an overview of that debate but a prioritized agenda: given roughly one engineer's worth of time over the next year, which open questions in this area should we actually pursue, in what order, and why?

Defend the ordering rather than asserting it. For each candidate question, tell me (a) how upstream it is — how many other questions in the area get settled, or at least reframed, if this one is answered; (b) whether it is tractable for people with engineering backgrounds and no philosophical training, or whether it genuinely requires a specialist; (c) which parts of the area are settled well enough that pursuing them would be rehashing; and (d) what specific argument or piece of evidence over the next year would move the question up or down the list.

Two constraints shape the ranking. First, we are not a research lab; the deliverable is a defensible policy, so weight questions by whether their answers would change what we actually write — for instance, when we may describe a model as trying to do something, whether a model's own account of its reasoning may be treated as testimony about its internal states, and whether an "intentions" field in our public API is a harmless convenience or a substantive claim about the system. Second, I want you to confront one framing directly, because if it is right then most of the agenda disappears: that original intentionality is just derived intentionality with more moving parts, so the distinction collapses into a difference of degree or of engineering detail. If that is correct, say so and tell me what, if anything, remains worth doing. If it is wrong, tell me exactly where it breaks down and what follows for the policy.

Finally, tell me which of the usual questions you would deprioritize because answering them would not change anything downstream. I would rather have a short list I can defend than a complete survey, and if there is an open question here that is not on the standard lists but that a team with our background and access is unusually well placed to make progress on, say so and explain why.
