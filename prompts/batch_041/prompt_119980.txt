Last night a friend and I argued for the better part of three hours about a claim I had floated as a provocation and then found myself defending: that it can be rational to make yourself genuinely care about something you do not currently care about, purely because caring about it would render a threat of yours credible.

The setting is one in which the ordinary machinery of commitment is unavailable. The other party predicts your behaviour too well for bluffing to work, or the interaction is acausal in the sense that no decision of yours could causally reach them — the only thing connecting you is that they can reason about what sort of agent you are. Under those conditions, if you want concessions, you cannot simply announce that you will retaliate in the event of refusal; the announcement will be discounted unless you really would retaliate. So you might try to become someone who really would: to acquire, as a genuine end of your own, a goal whose sole function is to keep your future behaviour aligned with the threat. My friend called such a thing a surrogate goal and said the idea is either incoherent or self-defeating. I said it is coherent and can be the rational thing to do. We did not settle it, and I would like a proper accounting of what my side is exposed to.

Tell me what would count against the claim — which arguments, formal results, intuitions, or conceivable observations — and rank them by how decisive they would actually be, distinguishing what would sink the position from what would merely make it awkward.

I am especially interested in the following. Does a genuine terminal goal destroy the very conditionality that made it useful? A threat is supposed to be something you carry out only if the other party refuses; an agent who really wants to retaliate regardless becomes dangerous rather than threatening, and perhaps unbargainable. If that tension is real, is it fatal? Second, how much does the whole position inherit from contested decision theory — if acausal influence has no bite under whichever theory of rational choice is correct, does the argument collapse into irrelevance? Third, the cost side: you pay the price of the surrogate goal in every branch where the threat is never tested or fails to move anyone, and I am not convinced I have been honest about how large that bill is. Fourth, is the act of installing an end even intelligible, or does the instrumental reason for having the goal evaporate the moment you genuinely have it?

I also want to know which of these objections a defender of the view would concede rather than deflect, and whether the position has been qualified to the point of unfalsifiability — every counterexample answered with a claim that the other agent was modelled incorrectly. If that is what has happened, say so, and say whether it is a defect in a view of this kind.

Finally, tell me what the position predicts that rival accounts of commitment do not, and whether any of those predictions could in principle be checked. The consideration that would move me most is a demonstration that credibility and negotiability cannot both be had, or an argument that no reasoning process on the other side can distinguish the self that adopts the goal from the self that follows through on it. Argue from first principles; I am not interested in being pointed at a literature.
