I'm a third-year PhD student and in ten days I present the penultimate chapter of my thesis to a work-in-progress seminar in a large analytic philosophy department. The chapter defends what I call the ratchet account of hinge certainty, and what I need from you is preparation for the question period, not for the talk. The view, in brief: I take up Wittgenstein's thought in On Certainty that our most basic certainties are not conclusions we have reached but unexamined conditions of any inquiry at all, and I make three claims. First, that hinges really are held with a certainty that no evidence produced, and that trying to justify them from the ground up is incoherent rather than merely difficult. Second, that they are not therefore arational, since they are held responsively to the shape of a practice rather than blindly. Third, and this is my own move, that no hinge is individually immune to revision but the body of them is immune to simultaneous revision — you can lose one only by leaning on the rest, so there is never a standpoint from which all of them could be assessed at once. On this account global doubt is not a hard psychological feat but a conceptual impossibility, and the familiar charge of relativism dissolves, because any two frameworks between which a real dispute can arise already share enough hinges for the dispute to have a shape. I also claim that hinges are lost by attrition rather than by refutation: they go when they stop being presupposed, not when they are disproved. My treatment is deliberately not exegetical; I'm using the idea rather than defending a reading of the text, and the third claim is the part I'm least confident of.

The audience is where the trouble lies, because this department is large enough to give me three quite different kinds of pressure in one hour. Two Wittgenstein scholars will be there, and they will care about fidelity to the text and will suspect that I have quietly converted a therapeutic remark into a piece of theory, and one of them has already told me, in the corridor, that my ratchet is not in the book. There is a formal epistemologist of some standing who thinks in credences, priors, defeaters and updating, and two of her students, all three of whom will want to know what a hinge is as an object of credence and whether my account is anything more than the trivial observation that some priors are very hard to shift. A visiting epistemologist who works on the epistemology of disagreement will smell relativism within a minute and will want to know what my view implies about rational conversion from one framework to another, and whether my "no simultaneous revision" thesis leaves any room for criticising a whole practice from outside. Finally there is a historian of philosophy who always asks what work the word "certainty" is doing in an argument and whether I have conflated a psychological fact about conviction with a normative claim about warrant.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight to ten objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they would do if I fumble them, each phrased in the vocabulary of the person who will raise it — credences, priors and defeat for the formal epistemologists; textual fidelity for the Wittgensteinians; rationality, conversion and incommensurability for the visitor; the fact/value or psychological/normative distinction for the historian — with a two-minute reply to each, a note on precisely where that reply is soft, and an explicit list of what I should concede at the outset rather than attempt to defend. Flag any place where the concession I need to make to one part of the room costs me standing with another, and tell me which single reply has to land for the chapter to survive the hour. I have about ninety seconds per answer in practice and the chair enforces time strictly. End with the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and say plainly why it is the dangerous one. Don't give me a reading list; I need the arguments that will be in the room, not the literature.
