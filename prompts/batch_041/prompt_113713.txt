For the past several months I have been locked in a dispute with a former colleague — it began over how we split the work on a shared project and has since metastasized into something neither of us can quite name — and the sentence I keep hearing myself say, and then wincing at, is some version of 'you don't get to say that to me.' The person saying it has done precisely what he is blaming me for, more often and less carefully, and has never once acknowledged it. I am convinced that this matters, but I cannot tell whether my objection is a serious moral point or a dressed-up tu quoque. I have started sitting in on a reading group on moral responsibility to try to get clear on it, and what I have concluded is that the question I actually want answered is a design question rather than a doctrinal one: what would a theory of standing to blame have to deliver in order to be adequate? Please write me that specification — not the theories, and not a survey of them, but the desiderata: the cases the theory has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the results it has to be consistent with, each accompanied by the consideration that motivates it. I would also like a verdict on which of those requirements are constitutive of the phenomenon and which are merely the commitments of one camp, plus a flag on any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, since I have a suspicion of my own along those lines, and a note on which single requirement does the most to rule out the views people actually hold.

The cases I most want handled, because they are the ones I can feel pulling the requirements in different directions: (a) my colleague, the blamer who has committed the same wrong repeatedly and never acknowledged it; (b) the reformed wrongdoer, who did the same wrong years ago, has owned it and changed, and now blames someone else for it — I want to know whether acknowledgment restores standing, and whether it restores it fully or only partly; (c) the stranger who blames, with no relationship to me, to the wrong, or to anyone wronged — the 'mind your own business' objection, which I take to be distinct from hypocrisy and which I am told by some people is not a moral objection at all; (d) the complicit blamer, whose own participation in a shared practice is part of what makes the wrong possible, blaming a fellow participant for their share of it; (e) the institutional blamer — an organization or a state blaming an individual for conduct it engages in itself on a far larger scale; (f) the blamer who is right about the norm and wrong about the facts, or right about both and insufferable in the delivery; and (g) the case in which the object of the blame is myself. Two further things I want the list either to accommodate or to reject outright. First, I am drawn to the thought that standing comes in kinds rather than in degrees — that a person might have the standing to raise a wrong with the wrongdoer privately, or to resent it, without the standing to demand a public accounting or to withdraw cooperation over it — and I want to know whether one unified account can hold that together or whether the list has to be pluralized to do it. Second, I want a ruling on whether a lack of standing, where it is real, makes the blame wrong or merely out of place — whether the blamer is doing something he ought not to do, or only something that will not land — because I suspect that is the fault line on which the whole dispute turns, and that a great deal of what gets reported as disagreement about hypocrisy is really disagreement about this.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — I would rather have your verdict on whether the requirements can be jointly satisfied at all than a warm-up — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
