You are a scholar of Buddhist thought and a philosopher of ethics, and I want you to proceed the way a careful rationalist would: steelman before you criticize, distinguish the objections that should actually move a rational agent from the ones that merely sound damning, and be explicit about which background assumptions each objection depends on. Before writing anything final, brainstorm privately — the strongest defeaters, the ones that only bite given particular background commitments, and the ones the position's own defenders would themselves grant — and then present only what survives that filtering.

I have come to hold the view that Buddhist ethics is, at bottom, a form of consequentialism. On my reading, its central good is the reduction of suffering — the welfare of sentient beings more generally — and the precepts and the cultivated virtues are best understood as reliable rules of thumb for producing that good rather than as exceptionless constraints. Karma, on this reading, is a causal feedback mechanism that encodes the long-run consequences of action, not a deontic ledger kept by anyone; and the notion of skillful means is precisely the license a consequentialist would want for overriding a rule when the outcome demands it. I find the reading attractive because it makes the tradition continuous with the broadly consequentialist intuitions I already hold, and because it dissolves what otherwise look like tensions between the compassionate ends the tradition praises and the rules it lays down. What I want from you is not reassurance but exposure.

Tell me what would count against this reading — and I mean against it as a claim about the tradition, not as a claim about what I would like the tradition to be. Which doctrines, which well-attested practices, which features of the tradition's own self-understanding, and which cases it is committed to describing would tell against the consequentialist reading specifically, as opposed to telling against some generic ethical theory? For each, say whether a sympathetic scholar would concede it as a genuine cost or dismiss it as a misreading, whether the reading can absorb it by retreating to a thinner claim, and — where relevant — what the consequentialist reading predicts that a virtue-ethical or deontological reading of the same material does not. I am especially interested in whether the reading has been insulated from disconfirmation by qualification, and whether, if it has, that is a defect in the reading or simply the price of any interpretive claim about a large and internally varied tradition.

Give me exactly five defeaters, as a numbered list, each one or two sentences, ranked from the most decisive to the least, with no introduction or conclusion.
