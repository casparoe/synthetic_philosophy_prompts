I teach a graduate seminar on historical method. We begin with Herodotus and Thucydides -- the invention of inquiry, the weighing of testimony, the question of what to do with the speeches a historian puts in his speakers' mouths -- and then work forward through the modern literature on archives, silence, and the limits of reconstruction. In almost every term the same sentence surfaces, and I have stopped being able to tell whether the students who deploy it mean the same thing. In a session on the Persian Wars one student used it to wave away a passage from a source that plainly flattered the winning side; a week later, discussing how anyone could write the history of a population that was defeated and dispersed, another used the same words to mean something far stronger, not that our evidence is skewed but that the event itself is somehow constituted by who prevailed. I have also heard it reduced to the modest observation that archives keep what the powerful chose to keep, and -- memorably -- stretched into the claim that writing the history of a massacre from the perpetrators' own records is not merely difficult but illegitimate. These cannot all be one thesis, and I suspect at least one of them is being made to carry weight the sentence will not bear. (I should add that the line circulates without any reliable attribution; the students credit it to whoever is fashionable that year.)

What I want from you is a disambiguation I can hand to the seminar. Set out the distinct precise theses the sentence could be expressing, ordered from the near-truism to the implausible, and for each one say: what it is a claim about (the survival of records, the content of records, the events themselves, the practice of history as a discipline, or the canon of what gets taught); what would have to be true for it to hold; whether it is trivial, substantive, or simply false; and -- this is the part that matters to me -- whether it actually licenses the move my student was making when she used it to set a source aside. I want the readings that sound alike pulled apart: the difference between evidence that is missing and evidence that is distorted, between a record shaped by its maker and an event shaped by its outcome, between a claim about what happened and a claim about what we can know happened. Note any reading under which the slogan is both interesting and defensible, and if there is none, say so and say why the defensible readings collapse into platitudes. Finish by telling me which reading the people who repeat it most likely intend, and what the honest reply to my student would have been.

Please give this to me as structured data rather than an essay: a single JSON object with the fields readings, likeliest_intended_reading, cheapest_reading_that_does_the_work, any_reading_both_substantive_and_defensible (a boolean), and suggested_reply_to_the_student (a short paragraph I could say aloud). The readings field should be an array, one object per reading, each with the fields label (a short name for the thesis), thesis (the reading stated precisely, in one or two sentences), claim_about (which of the five targets above it concerns), what_it_requires (the conditions under which it would be true), status (one of trivial, substantive, or false), licenses_dismissal (yes, no, or partial, plus a clause saying why), and what_would_refute_it (the kind of evidence that would settle the matter). Keep the entries in order from weakest to strongest, and do not pad the list -- if two readings are really one, merge them.
