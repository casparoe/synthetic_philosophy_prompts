I'm a philosophy professor and next month I have to give a toast at the wedding of a former student of mine, and I want to actually build it on anekāntavāda, so I could use a hand. Here's the mess: Dev's parents and Meera's parents were buisness partners for years and it ended badly, and neither couple has ever accepted the other's account of who ruined it. Ten years of frost, and the wedding is the first time there all in one room. Dev asked me to speak because I'm on nobody's side, and because he sat in on my Jain philosophy course and knows I'm fond of the doctrine — that every claim the two families make about what happened is asserted from a standpoint, a naya, and is only true under the syāt qualifier, only 'in some respect', and that the sevenfold predication (it is; it isn't; it both is and isn't; on into the inexpressible cases) shows how far the qualification reaches. What I can't do is get up and tell two families that both of them are right. I've always taught that anekāntavāda isn't relativism, since the aspects are aspects of the thing itself, and anyway I privately think one family's version is a good deal closer to the truth, which is another thing I obviously can't say out loud at a wedding. Half the room will be Jains who know the doctrine properly and will catch it if I mangle it; the other half are colleagues and Meera's work friends who've never heard of it and will hear every 'in some respect' as me weaseling. Five minutes, please — warm and honest, not a lecture and not a dodge.
