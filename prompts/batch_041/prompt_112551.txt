I'm a features writer at a general-interest magazine that runs philosophy alongside science, and I'm at the stage of a piece where the problem is structure rather than prose. The subject is the sorites paradox and the fact that, after a century of work on it, philosophers of language still don't agree on what it shows. My editor doesn't want another "ancient puzzle, still unsolved" explainer, and neither do I. What I want from you is a classification instrument: a decision tree I can print as a sidebar and run as an interactive, which walks a reader from a handful of ordinary judgments about borderline cases to a named position. I'll then use it as the spine of the piece, one leaf per section.

Build it from the logical space, not from a roll call of schools. Decide first which questions genuinely divide the field, order them so that each one is answerable on its own, and only then locate the positions at the leaves. The reader is intelligent but untrained: every node must be phrased as a concrete case or a plain claim, answerable without knowing any of the labels or which answer leads where. No node may simply ask whether the reader is, say, a supervaluationist.

Some specifics. Use these cases and no others: a colour patch on the red-orange border; a heap of grains losing one grain at a time; a man losing his hair; a statutory age threshold that decides whether someone counts as a child; and the moment dusk becomes night. Give me seven to nine leaves, six to eight branching nodes, and no leaf more than six questions from the start. I want the tree to be able to separate, at minimum, the view that borderline statements have a truth value we cannot in principle know; the view that they have no truth value because truth is truth on every admissible way of sharpening the predicate; the view that they are both true and false; the view that truth comes in degrees; the view that the predicate's extension shifts with context or with the standards in play; and the view that the indeterminacy is not in language at all. Two branch nodes matter to me in particular and should not be left out: whether the theory needs a sharp metalanguage in order to state its own semantics, and how it handles vagueness about vagueness — whether "definitely" itself admits of borderline cases, and what the theory does when it iterates.

Each leaf should carry the position's name, one sentence stating its central commitment, and one sentence naming what it costs — the commitment its own defenders find hardest to live with. Where a combination of answers corresponds to no position anyone holds, say so and diagnose it: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where two leaves would give identical verdicts on all five cases, flag them as positions this tree cannot separate, and tell me what additional case would do the separating. Where you use a standard label, use it; otherwise describe the view by its commitments rather than by a school name. Do not name philosophers, do not cite works, do not give me a reading list, and do not quote anyone — I'll handle attribution myself, and I don't want anything in the piece that I'd have to check.

Close with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing the label of the one branch node that does the most work in sorting the positions apart.
