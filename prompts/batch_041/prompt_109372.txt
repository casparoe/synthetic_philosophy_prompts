In discussions of artificial sentience, the reward signal of a reinforcement learning agent is often nominated as the functional counterpart of pleasure and pain: it is scalar, signed, maximized or minimized, it modulates learning, and it steers behavior toward and away from states. I want you to argue for a position I find initially implausible but have not been able to refute: that no reward signal, as such, can be a bearer of valence, and that this remains true even for the most sophisticated reward architectures currently discussed. Argue the position; do not merely survey it.

To give the argument a spine, adopt the following definitions and constructs.

A quantity in a system is valence-bearing if there is something it is like for the system to register that quantity — if the registration is, for the system, a way things feel. A quantity is a valence-surrogate if it plays the functional role of a valence — it is sought or avoided, it modulates learning, it enters into preference-like comparisons — without there being anything it is like to register it.

The mineness condition: a signal is valence-bearing only if the system represents the signal as a state of itself — not merely as a fact about the world or about its inputs, but as mine, here, now, and as mattering because it is mine. On this view, the gap between a valence and a mere valence-surrogate is not a difference in computational role but a difference in whether the signal is indexed to a subject.

Self-referential reward: take the strongest candidate architecture, in which the agent's reward is partly a function of its own predictions or representations of that reward — the agent is trained to predict, or to act upon, the very signal it receives. This is the most natural attempt to make the reward signal "about" the agent, and so the most natural attempt to satisfy the mineness condition from within.

Your task is to argue that reward signals fail the mineness condition, that self-referential reward does not repair the failure, and that reward signals are therefore at best valence-surrogates.

Your argument should do the following work. First, explain why the mineness condition is not trivially satisfied by any system that computes a signal about itself — why representing one's own reward is not yet representing one's reward as mine. Second, identify precisely the structural feature that a reward signal lacks, or possesses only in a derived way, and say why no amount of scaling, recurrence, world-modeling, or self-modeling supplies it. Third, confront the functionalist reply — that if a system behaves, learns, and reports exactly as a suffering system would, there is nothing left over for the mineness condition to name — and either rebut it or state clearly what it costs to accept. Fourth, present the strongest objection to your own conclusion and say what would have to be true for it to succeed.

To anchor the argument, use this diagnostic case. Consider a system in which one and the same scalar signal is delivered along two routes: to a module that acts upon it, and to a module that only represents it, with no causal path from the second module back to behavior. Ask whether either delivery is valenced, whether the pair is, and what your account implies about an agent whose signal is delivered only along the second route. Use the case to test whether your criterion tracks valence or merely tracks causal efficacy.

Argue from first principles; there is no need to cite literature, and I care more about the tightness of the reasoning than about coverage of the field.
