i've been going in circles on my own about which things actually matter morally — not which animals, the general thing: what kind of computation has to be going on inside a system for there to be someone home. I want your credence in a specific criterion, not a survey of the literature.

Here's the criterion I've landed on. A system's internal states matter morally iff the system computes a representation of its own condition as something that can go better or worse, and that representation actually steers its behaviour — a passive readout wouldn't count, the "worse" reading has to be able to push the system around. Call it the steering test.

Whats your credence that the steering test draws the line in the right place — everything that meets it matters morally, nothing that fails it does? Decompose it: the sub-questions you think the claim splits into, a rough number for each, one line of justification each, and how they combine into the final figure. My guess at the split: whether there's a fact of the matter about moral status vs. it being something we project; whether what matters is a self-model like mine vs. any state that plays the right functional role regardless of how it's built; whether phenomenal consciousness is required on top of the functional story or whether the functional story is the whole thing; and whether the substrate (silicon, a whole country, a very slow giant lookup table) changes the answer. I'm sitting around 0.35 myself.

Then tell me which sub-question my number is most sensitive to and what would move it most.
