Right — I need to think through the other side of something I've been arguing for, and I'd rather have it properly attacked than agreed with.

The position: the Löbian obstacle is usually presented as a curiosity about formal arithmetic. You know the shape of it — Löb's theorem says that if a theory like PA proves, for some sentence P, that P is true if it's provable, then P is already provable, and the upshot is that such a theory can't prove the general claim that everything it proves is true, on pain of proving its own consistency. The standard gloss is that a reasoner who can prove things and can also reason about its own proofs can't certify its own soundness.

I want to defend a much stronger version. I think the obstacle isn't really about provability; it's about what I've been calling closed trust. Any agent that (a) can represent its own reliability and (b) is willing to use any conclusion it endorses as a premise for further reasoning — including conclusions about itself — is either unsound or can't have the trust it wants. On that reading it isn't a theorem about arithmetic at all but a general predicament for anything reflective: a self-improving AI, a person who trusts her own judgement, an institution that certifies its own procedures (a court deciding the limits of its own jurisdiction, say). I've been calling it a structural feature of reflective agency rather than a bug in one formalisation.

So: what counts against that? Sort the defeaters by what they'd actually cost me rather than giving me a survey. In particular, which of the assumptions is doing the real work, and what would have to be true of an agent for the obstacle simply not to apply? I know the standard escape hatches already — weaken the logic, restrict the trust schema, tower up through a hierarchy of theories, go probabilistic instead of deductive. Which of those geniunely get out, and which merely relocate the problem somewhere less visible? Is there an agent that is provably fully self-trusting and still not exploitable — and if so, does it have to give up something I'd have to count as a real loss (deductive closure, classical reasoning, finiteness)?

The bit I'm most worried about, and I'd like you to be honest about it: my generalisation may be unfalsifiable. If the formal analogue of 'proving your own soundness' can't be pinned down for people and institutions, then saying they face the obstacle is a metaphor with nothing at stake. Tell me whether that's the situation, and if it is, whether it's fatal or just means the only interesting version is the narrow one. Also tell me what a defender of the strong version should themselves concede counts against them — not the cheap stuff critics say.

Personally, what would move me most is a concrete finite agent, real or clearly constructible, that keeps full self-trust without inconsistency. A proof that every such agent must be either infinite or exploitable would do the opposite. And awkward-but-survivable would be cases where the obstacle is real but the cost of escaping it turns out to be negligible — that would make the whole thing a good deal less interesting than I've been claiming.
