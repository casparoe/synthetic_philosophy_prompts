I am a doctoral candidate writing a thesis chapter on epistemic logic, and I have constructed an argument that I can no longer tell whether to believe. I would like you to diagnose it: tell me which step fails, or, if no step fails, which step is cheapest to give up, and what giving it up costs.

The chapter is meant to show that epistemic logic can model an agent who reasons — one who arrives at knowledge by drawing inferences, rather than an agent stipulated in advance to know everything that follows from what she knows. What follows is what happened when I tried to say precisely what the knowledge operator must do to serve that aim, and it concludes that the aim is incoherent.

(1) An epistemic logic adequate to the modelling of reasoning must be a normal modal logic: it contains every truth-functional tautology, it is closed under modus ponens, and its knowledge operator distributes over the conditional, so that K(φ → ψ) → (Kφ → Kψ). The last requirement is not optional. Without it, knowing a conditional and knowing its antecedent does not put the agent in a position to know the consequent, and then no derivation can be reconstructed as a sequence of items the agent knows.

(2) Every normal modal logic obeys necessitation: if φ is a theorem, then Kφ is a theorem.

(3) The logic in question is a logic of knowledge, so its theorems are truths about knowers: if the logic proves Kφ, then φ is something the agent knows.

(4) Every tautology is a theorem; so by (2) and (3), every agent knows every tautology.

(5) By (1) and (4), knowledge is closed under logical consequence: if the agent knows A and A entails φ, then A → φ is a validity, hence a theorem, hence known, and distribution does the rest. So an agent who knows a finite set of axioms knows every theorem of the theory they axiomatize — if the theory is strong enough, theorems that no one has derived and perhaps no one ever will.

(6) No actual agent — not a child doing sums, not a mathematician at the blackboard — knows all of that.

(7) Therefore (1) must be given up. But a knowledge operator without distribution cannot formalize reasoning. So there is no formal model of a reasoning agent: logical omniscience is not a defect of epistemic logic to be repaired, but a theorem about it.

I would like (7) to be false, and if I must choose, I would rather keep (1) and give up (6), since the chapter is about reasoning and without distribution I have nothing to say. But I do not know what I am buying.

I should say what I have already tried, so that you do not simply hand it back to me. I replaced the possible-worlds semantics with a neighbourhood semantics, hoping a non-normal operator would let me keep distribution while blocking necessitation; it blocked necessitation, but it blocked distribution too, so I was back where I started, minus the completeness proof. I then relativized knowledge to an awareness set, so that the agent knows only what she is aware of; but the moment I tried to derive the distribution principle I wanted, I found I had to assume that awareness is closed under logical consequence, which reinstates the omniscience one level up. I do not know whether that failure was mine or a fact about the strategy.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a survey. Which numbered step should I give up, and why did that step look so obviously true when I wrote it? If the step is sound and the argument is valid, tell me which premise is least costly to abandon, and itemize the bill for each of the escapes I have heard of: modelling the operator as tracking implicit rather than explicit knowledge; adding a primitive awareness component; replacing possible worlds with impossible ones; and splitting the agent into fragments or frames, each of which knows less than the whole. For each, say precisely what it buys and what it quietly gives up, and tell me which one a working logician would actually take. I am not asking for a reading list; I want the diagnosis and the price list.
