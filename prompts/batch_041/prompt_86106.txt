I'm a philospher at a small liberal arts college and I've been asked to give this year's commencement address. The graduating class picked me, which is flattering and also terrifying. They want me to talk about what to live for — not careers, not service, just the big question.

I want to give them Keynes's answer. Not Keynes the economist, Keynes on the good life. The view he held as a young man in Cambridge and Bloomsbury, which he described much later in a memoir piece: he and his friends took from G.E. Moore the idea that the good is a certain kind of state of mind — the enjoyment of beautiful things, the pleasures of human company — and they threw away Moore's moral code. They thought no one is under any obligation to do anything; duty was, for them, a fiction. Keynes described them as immoralists in the strict sense. You live for the good states of mind, and the whole apparatus of conventional morality is mostly an obstacle.

But here's my problem. Keynes himself came to think the early view was too thin. He wrote later that they had no proper account of feeling, of the unconscious, of the dark forces in human nature, and that civilisation is a thin crust, precarious, easily cracked. And in his 1930 essay about the economic prospects of his grandchildren, he imagined a future in which the economic problem is solved and people are finally free to enjoy leisure and the art of living — but he also said that until then we have to keep pretending that greed and usury and caution are virtues, because they're useful, even though they aren't the real goods.

So the speech has to do two things at once. It has to present the early Keynesian/Moorean vision — the good life as states of mind, not achievements or possessions — without turning it into a list of duties, because that would betray the whole point. And it has to be honest about Keynes's own later misgivings, without collapsing into "well, it's all fragile and we're probably doomed." The audience is mixed: some faculty who know Keynes, lots of parents and grandparents who don't, and the students themselves, who are twenty-two and not in the mood for a lecture.

You're a philosopher and historian of ideas who knows Keynes's essays well. Please write the address. It should be about eight to ten minutes spoken. Don't name-drop too much — you can mention Keynes by name, but don't assume anyone in the audience knows who Moore was or what his book was called. The philosophy has to be doing real work: the reason the speech can't just be a list of advice is itself part of what the speech is about. Start by brainstorming a few possible structures or opening moves before you write the final version, and think carefully about how to handle the tension between the early and late Keynes. Also, keep the tone warm — it's a commencement, not a seminar.
