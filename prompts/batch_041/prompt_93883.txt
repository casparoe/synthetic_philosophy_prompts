For about fifteen years I have read the rationalist and effective-altruism blogs, and I have a set of settled verdicts about acting on very small probabilities that I cannot get to fall out of any single rule. I would like you to tell me what principle, or small set of principles, actually generates them, and which of the verdicts has to be abandoned if the principle is to be respectable rather than a patchwork.

My verdicts, stated as what I do or would do:

1. A stranger tells me that unless I hand him $5 he will have me tortured, along with a great many other people: 3^^^3 of them, which in Knuth's up-arrow notation is a power tower of 3s some 7.6 trillion entries high, vastly more people than there are particles in the observable universe. I put perhaps one chance in 10^40 on his being able to do anything of the kind. I refuse, and it does not feel close; I would refuse if he asked for a penny.

2. I give roughly a tenth of my income to organizations that I estimate have something like a one-in-a-thousand chance of measurably reducing the probability that humanity fails to survive this century, where success would be worth on the order of 10^15 lives. I take this to be among the best uses of my money.

3. I do not subscribe to a service that charges $9 a month and claims to reduce the probability of human extinction by one part in 10^12. Its method is proprietary, but a ratings outfit I half-trust rates it highly, and someone has checked the arithmetic inside the model that the company was willing to show. I am mildly scornful of people who subscribe.

4. I would not buy a $2 ticket in a lottery with a one-in-10^8 chance of a $1 billion jackpot, though the expected value is about $8. I would not buy one at a one-in-10^7 chance of $100 million either, where the expected value is also about $8. I know the arithmetic; I would still not buy.

5. I buy travel and health insurance, which have plainly negative expected value, and I do not think I am making a mistake.

6. If a well-understood physical experiment carried, on the best available calculation and after review by competent people, a one-in-10^40 chance of destroying the observable universe, I would let it run. That is the same probability as the wizard in case 1, and the harm the wizard threatens is if anything larger, yet I reach the opposite verdict without hesitation.

7. I have been told that a publicly known expected-value maximizer is a money pump: anyone who can attach a large enough number to a claim can extract from him whatever he will bear, so the correct policy is to be the kind of agent who refuses offers of this shape on principle rather than to price them one at a time. That thought does real work in my refusal in case 1, and I suspect it does less than I pretend. If I knew the wizard were the last of his kind, and that no threat of this shape would ever be made again, I believe I would still refuse.

8. For one cent I would buy protection against a one-in-10^9 chance that everyone I love dies in a fire, and I would not think about the probability at all.

The rule I have been using on myself is: maximize expected value, but ignore any probability below about one in a million. It returns verdicts 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6 correctly as far as I can tell, which is suspicious. It says nothing useful about 5, since insurance is not a small-probability problem but a deliberately negative-expected-value purchase. It returns 7 correctly only if I let the money-pump argument in, and I have just told you I doubt that argument. And it fails 8 outright: by that rule the one-in-10^9 fire risk is beneath notice, which seems obviously wrong, and so does the implication that I should ignore a one-in-a-billion chance of losing everything I own even when the insurance costs a penny.

Please do the following. State the principle, or the two or three competing principles, precisely enough that I could compute with them, and say for each clause which of my verdicts forces it. Tell me what each principle implies about cases I have not mentioned, in particular a charity that offers, for $1,000, to reduce the probability of a nuclear war by one part in 10^9, and a wizard who asks for a sum small enough that I would not notice paying it. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and whether those are the ones I should revise. I would rather end up with a simpler principle and one changed verdict than a rule with a clause invented for each case, but I would like to know which of my cases I am paying to keep.
