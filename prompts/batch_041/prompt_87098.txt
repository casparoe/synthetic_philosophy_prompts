I sit on the standards panel of a small nonprofit forecasting platform that supplies probability estimates to institutional clients. One of our senior forecasters, Dr. R, has begun publishing interval forecasts—for each event he gives a range of acceptable odds rather than a single probability—and he updates those ranges by a rule of his own rather than by conditioning on new evidence. A quantitative trader on our board has shown that if our settlement rules treat every point in Dr. R's range as a standing offer, a counterparty can place a sequence of bets, some before and some after the next data release, that guarantees the counterparty a profit no matter how the events resolve—a Dutch book. Dr. R replies that this only demonstrates a defect in our settlement rules, not in his credences: he is not obliged to accept every bet at the edge of his range, and his ranges are a more honest representation of his uncertainty than a false precision would be. The trader answers that the sure-loss result can be rebuilt even if Dr. R may decline bets, so long as he must announce in advance which odds he would accept, and that a forecaster who cannot be beaten by a sure-loss bet is the least our clients should demand.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. Recommend a concrete policy—point-valued coherent probabilities, permitted intervals with stated constraints, or something else—with an implementation timeline, a clear rule for when a forecaster's outputs may be used in client products, and what to do about forecasts already published. Engage Dr. R's strongest point seriously rather than merely restating the sure-loss construction, and say plainly what the panel is and is not entitled to conclude from it. Write in American English.
