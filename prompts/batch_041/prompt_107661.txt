I'm a second-year PhD student in intellectual history, and next month I have to present at a small workshop on the philosophy of historiography. My paper is nominally about the war-guilt controversy that Fritz Fischer touched off, but the thing I actually want to discuss is a prior question that the historians in the room tend to wave away: when we say that a state or a nation "bears responsibility" for a war, what is being asserted, and is it the sort of claim that can be true or false in a determinate way? My supervisor keeps telling me to just pick a side and get on with it, which is precisely what I want to avoid doing. So I'd like your credence, given as a number with its derivation rather than as an essay.

What is your credence in the following proposition? "The German Empire bore primary responsibility for the outbreak of the First World War in 1914," where I stipulate that primary responsibility means (i) that the decisions taken by the German political and military leadership during the July Crisis were a necessary condition of the war's beginning in 1914, and (ii) that those decisions were taken either with the intention of producing a general European war or with conscious indifference to whether one resulted. I want the number decomposed into the sub-questions it actually rests on — at minimum: the probability that the German leadership intended or consciously accepted a general war, as against intending a local Balkan war and merely risking the larger one; the probability that, given Austrian resolve and Russian mobilization, a general war would have broken out in 1914 even without those German decisions, so that clause (i) fails; the probability that collective ascriptions of responsibility to a state or a nation are more than a shorthand for a set of individual responsibilities, so that the proposition has a proper subject at all; and the probability that the necessary-condition-plus-intention test is the right test of "primary responsibility," rather than a comparative-contribution test or a norm-violation test that would give a different verdict on the same facts. Give each term a rough probability and one line of justification, show how they combine — and where the combination is not simply multiplication, as with the question whether the proposition has a proper subject, say what it is instead — then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to and what single piece of evidence or argument would move it most.

Two things before you answer. First, don't give me citations, a reading list, or a survey of what other people have said; I know the literature well enough and I'm not asking you to reconstruct it — I want the argument structure. If any historical premise you lean on is something you would really need to check rather than something you know, flag it and use a deliberately conservative value instead of inventing a detail to fill the gap. Second, I would put my own credence at about 0.3, and I'm aware that this may reflect a post-1945 reflex that catastrophes of this size must be somebody's fault rather than a considered judgment about 1914. If you think the request itself is confused — if the but-for-plus-intention stipulation begs the question, or if demanding a determinate truth value here is a category mistake — then say so plainly and argue it, rather than producing a number to keep me company.
