The city has no courts and no constables; it has the Registry. Before two strangers may exchange anything of value — a workshop for a brother's papers, a name for a name — each deposits a sealed rule with the Registry. A rule is a written procedure that reads the text of the other party's rule and returns one of two words: Keep or Take. At the appointed hour the Registry feeds each rule the other's text and executes whatever comes back. If both return Keep, each leaves with what he brought. If one returns Take, he leaves with both. If both return Take, both forfeit everything to the Registry. A rule that has not returned by the closing of the hour is read as Take. No one enforces any of this; it is simply what the Registry does, and anyone who wishes to trade at all trades there. Neither party sees the other's deposit until both are sealed.

Write a short story about a woman who must enter a Reading against a man known in three districts for having taken from everyone who ever faced him. What she brings is her workshop; what he brings is her brother's papers, and there is no other way for her to get them back. She is one of the few people in the city who understands that a rule may be written so that what it returns depends on what the other rule returns when it is fed her rule — and that this dependence either settles into an answer or spirals, and that the hour is short. Every rule he has ever deposited is a matter of public record.

The story should be faithful to the machinery: whatever rule she writes, a careful reader should be able to work out what it returns against his, and the ending should follow from that reckoning rather than from what would be satisfying. Let her way through be reasoned out from the terms of the Registry rather than recalled from somewhere else. Show at least one other Reading in full, so the machinery is visible in operation, and let the story carry the cost of the arrangement as well as its ingenuity — what it asks of a person to be legible rather than trusted, and what kind of city would rather have the Registry than a court.
