I retired last year from a career in experimental geophysics, and I have spent the time since trying to teach myself moral philosophy — mostly by working through a reading group with two former colleagues and a long-suffering graduate student who keeps us honest. We have arrived at the dispute over whether sound moral judgment requires any stock of principles at all: the claim that one might judge well, and know that one is judging well, without there being any true moral generalisations to appeal to, and the several forms of generalism that resist that claim. The trouble is that the labels we bandy about do more to obscure than to sort us; half our disagreements turn out to be about which question we were answering. What I would like from you, then, is a decision tree — of the kind one uses to key out a specimen, with each branch node a question someone could answer without already knowing where it leads — that takes a person from a small number of commitments to a named position. Please build it from the logical space first and only then populate that space: identify the axes that genuinely divide the disputants, locate in the resulting grid the positions that have names, and mark the combinations nobody occupies, saying in each case whether the vacancy is due to the view's being incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The axes I am fairly confident belong on the tree are these. First, whether a consideration that is a reason in one situation can be no reason at all, or a reason pointing the other way, in another — whether reasons are holistic or atomistic. Second, whether there are true universal moral generalisations; and third, if there are, whether they must do any work in reaching a particular verdict, or may be true yet idle in the deliberations of someone who judges well. I want these kept separate, because my colleagues run them together, and I suspect one can be a holist about reasons while still believing in universal principles, and conversely. Fourth, the status of exceptions: whether a generalisation is defeated by a finite, specifiable list of defeaters, or whether each apparent exception raises a further question about the exception itself, so that no list ever closes — and, relatedly, whether an exception is best understood as part of the rule or as a rival rule that displaces it. Fifth, whether the position is offered as an account of what makes an action right or as an account of how we come to know that it is right, since I take those to be separable, and I would like the tree to say where each leaf stands on both.

As for the leaves, please ensure the tree can distinguish at least the following: a codifiability view on which a complete and usable set of principles is possible at least in principle; a view on which there are genuine principles but they are pro tanto, to be weighed, with judgment left over; a view on which the generalisations are real but default-like, holding unless defeated, where the defeaters are themselves capable of being defeated; a view on which norms are progressively specified rather than either fixed once and for all or abandoned; a view that reasons from paradigm cases and analogy without pretending to a closed system; a thoroughgoing particularism on which principles are neither necessary nor sufficient for sound judgment; and whatever you would call the position on which principles serve as reminders or mnemonic aids but never as justificatory premises. My own suspicion, which I would like the tree either to support or to embarrass, is that the first axis does most of the diagnostic work and that the second and third are largely downstream of it. Two further requests. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate by any question a person could answer without already knowing which label it leads to — I expect trouble, for instance, between progressive specification and default-like generalisation, and between thoroughgoing particularism and a view that privileges moral perception. And keep the questions concrete: ask about the lie told to the person at the door, about whether the pleasure an action affords its agent counts in its favour when the action is cruel, and about what becomes of an excuse for breaking a promise when that excuse is itself excused, rather than asking anyone to declare whether they believe in principles.
