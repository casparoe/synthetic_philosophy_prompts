You are a poet and a scholar of the vernacular verse and political vocabulary of sixteenth-century Italy — a specialist who can move between the technical apparatus of terza rima and the conceptual grammar of fortuna and virtù — and I would like you to collaborate with me on a short piece of verse for a novel I am finishing.

The occasion. The novel is set in Florence and its hinterland in the years just after the Medici return, and its central figure is a former republican secretary, now in forced retirement, who spends his evenings composing a book of advice to a prince. I need a poem of his — or rather a poem in his idiom, one he might have written or merely carried in memory — to be read aloud in a single scene and, later, to stand as an epigraph. He holds that human affairs are governed jointly by fortuna and by what he calls virtù, and the poem must bear the weight of that conviction without ever deploying the two words as slogans.

The form, and why it is not decoration. Write the poem in terza rima: tercets in roughly iambic pentameter, chained by interlocking rhyme (aba, bcb, cdc, and so on), closed in the traditional way by a final line that rhymes with the middle line of the last tercet so that the poem ends as a quatrain — seven tercets plus the closing line, unless you can justify another length. The form is the argument. A terza rima has no natural place to stop: each tercet inherits a rhyme from the one before it and passes a new one forward, and the poem can end only because the poet decides to cut the chain. That is precisely the relation I want between virtù and fortuna. Virtù is never answering from a standing start; it is always responding to the throw that fortuna has already made. And it never wins a final victory, only a postponement. Build the poem so that a reader who knows the argument can feel the chain doing that work.

Conceptual constraints — be exact, so that a specialist reading the scene does not wince.

First, virtù here is not moral virtue. It is the capacity to act well given the circumstances — skill, nerve, timing — and it is particular and finite: a mode of proceeding that suits one season and fails in another. The sharpest thought available to the speaker is that fortune masters a man not because his virtù is small but because his nature is fixed while the times are not, and that a man who could change his mode as the times change would not be mastered at all. Let that thought be present in the poem even if the speaker cannot live up to it.

Second, fortuna is not mere luck. It is the disorderly and the unforeseen, the portion of events no calculation reaches: it is the flooding river as much as the wheel. The speaker may use the image of the violent river that can be banked with dikes and dams, and he may use the familiar figure of fortune as a woman to be seized and coerced — he is a man of his century, and I do not want him anachronistically cleansed — but the poem should register, at least in its turn, the cost of that figure rather than merely repeat it. Do not let the poem collapse the two images of fortuna, the river that can be banked and the woman who must be beaten, into a comfortable slogan.

Third, the poem must not end in triumph or in resignation. Terza rima wants closure; the thought refuses it. Let the closing line complete the quatrain while leaving the argument open — a cut, not a conclusion.

Voice and register. Contemporary English that reads like a plausible translation of a period poem: nothing mock-archaic, no thee and thou, no capitalized abstractions posing as allegory. Each line should be speakable aloud in one breath.

Before you write anything, work the problem through in a short preamble. State in your own words the claim about fortuna and virtù that the poem will embody, and name the one point on which the speaker's position is most vulnerable. Then sketch what each tercet must accomplish and what rhyme-chain the argument will ride. Then decide where the turn falls and what the final line must do. Only after that preamble should you draft the poem. When the draft is done, check it line by line against the conceptual constraints and against the rhyme scheme, and revise anything that only names the ideas instead of enacting them. Do not supply a bibliography, citations, or references to editions; I have my own sources and want only the poem and your working notes.
