Ok, so I'm about a year into a three-year postdoc with basically no teaching, which is probably the most research time I will ever have, and I keep flip-flopping on what to spend it on. The area I know best is the chance-and-credence stuff — the idea that if the objective chance of something is x, your credence in it should be x, where "chance" means the physics-y kind of probability, the thing that makes "60% chance of rain tomorrow" true or false regardless of what anyone believes. I'm fine with formal machinery but I'm not a measure-theory person and I don't want to spend a year becoming one. I still need to publish, so "it'll take five years and a coauthor who does stochastic processes" is a genuine cost to me, not a footnote.

What I can't tell is which of the open questions in this area are actually worth three years. The ones I can see from where I sit are: whether the principle needs repairing for cases where the proposition whose chance I'm deferring to is about *me* or my location rather than a coin in a lab; what to do when I don't know the chances and there's no sensible prior to put over them; whether "defer to chance" is really just a special case of "defer to someone else's credence," in which case maybe the whole thing dissolves into the general expert-deference swamp and there's nothing distinctively about chance here; and whether the fight over what chances *are* (facts about frequencies/patterns in the world vs. something more primitive) has any bearing on any of the above, or is just a sinkhole for someone with my skill set.

Three things about me that should shape the answer. First, I moonlight one day a week advising a small forecasting shop, so if a question's answer would actually change how a working forecaster pools model outputs with historical base rates, that's worth a bump. Second, I'm more drawn to "here is a subtle case where our principle gives an absurd answer" than to "here is a new formalism," which I suspect biases me toward the fun problems over the load-bearing ones. Third, I'd rather spend the three years on one question that two other questions silently depend on than on three questions nobody downstream needs.
