so I wrote this short thing for a reading group on Skolem's paradox and I want you to tear into it — not looking for encouragement, looking for where it's wrong, where it's just hand-waving, and whether there's anything worth keeping. Here's the draft:

**Uncountable is an indexical**

Downward Löwenheim–Skolem says every consistent first-order theory with an infinite model has a countable model. ZF, if consistent, has an infinite model, so it has a countable model, call it M. Now "the set of all subsets of ω" is a definable object inside M, and M satisfies the sentence saying that object is uncountable. But M is countable, so that object has only countably many members. So a set that is really countable comes out uncountable according to M. That's the paradox.

The standard line is that this isn't a contradiction, because "countable" and "uncountable" are only ever relative to a model — M just doesn't contain the bijection that would witness countability, and "there exists a bijection" gets quantified over M. Fine. I want to claim something stronger: it's not merely that cardinality ascriptions are model-relative, it's that they're indexical in the way "here" and "now" are. When I say the reals are uncountable, I mean uncountable-from-where-I-stand, and there's no view from nowhere to check it against. The Skolemite who says we can always climb to a richer meta-theory and look down at the countability from there has only changed the index — the meta-theory is itself a first-order theory with its own countable model, and if it somehow isn't, that's a fact some further model can deny.

Consequence I want to draw: CH isn't just independent of ZFC, it's the kind of thing that might be indeterminate in a stronger sense, because "the cardinality of the continuum" doesn't pick out a determinate fact once you take the indexicality seriously. I think this also makes the set-theoretic multiverse look less like a radical option and more like the default.

Two things I can't sort out. (1) I keep wanting to say M's "uncountability" is an illusion, but M is a perfectly good model and its internal theorems are true-in-M, so calling it an illusion seems to privilege my outside vantage in exactly the way I said we can't. Is there a non-question-begging way to say what's actually going on here? (2) Does the indexical analogy earn its keep, or is it a fancy restatement of "relative to a model"? If it's just a restatement, what would a real indexical thesis predict that the boring relativist reading doesn't?

Also there's a bit at the end where I try to make the paradox bite even the relativist, by imagining a theory with a predicate that's supposed to mean "is countable" and asking wether a countable model can get that predicate right. I think it shows the relativist can't help themselves to a notion of getting it right, but a friend says it's circular. I can't tell.
