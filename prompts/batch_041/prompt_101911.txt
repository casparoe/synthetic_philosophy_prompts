I've been going down a rabbit hole on the strategy-stealing assumption in decision theory — the idea that in a symmetric decision problem, an agent can reason as if its own choice and the other agent's choice are the same variable, so it can "steal" the best joint strategy rather than just best-responding. My friend thinks it's basically just superrationality with extra steps, but I suspect there's more to it, especially when you try to build a formal theory around it. I want the desiderata first: what would a theory of decision-making that incorporates the strategy-stealing assumption actually have to get right? Not a list of existing theories, but the requirements any such theory must meet. For instance, it has to handle the classic cases — prisoner's dilemma with identical agents, Newcomb's problem, maybe the transparent Newcomb case — but I'm more interested in the edge cases: two agents that are identical except for a single random tie-breaking bit, or agents that share the same source code but one has a different prior, or a case where the symmetry is broken by the agents having different information sets. It also has to say something about mixed strategies: if the assumption forces identical outputs, can it accommodate randomization, and if so how? And it can't imply that my choice magically causes the other agent's choice in a way that violates physical causation — or if it does, it has to own that and explain what's going on.

I also want to know which of these requirements are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp, and whether any pair of them can't be jointly satisfied. If some subset is unsatisfiable, that's the most interesting part — tell me which requirement does the most to rule out the popular contenders. I'm not asking for a scorecard of existing theories, though if you want to briefly say how the main contenders do against the list, that's fine. The main thing I want is the criteria themselves, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and a clear statement of which requirements are doing the real work. I'm trying to figure out whether the strategy-stealing assumption is a genuine constraint on rational choice or just a useful heuristic that falls apart under slight asymmetries.
