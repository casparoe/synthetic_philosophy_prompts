Last night I had a long argument with a close friend about Aristotle's treatment of money and exchange, and we broke off without either of us conceding. I would like you to write an essay that adjudicates the dispute and actually lands somewhere, rather than surveying the options and trailing off.

My friend's case, stated as fairly as I can manage, is that Aristotle's analysis of exchange is a failure dressed up as a theory. In the discussion of justice in the Nicomachean Ethics, Aristotle insists that a just exchange requires the things exchanged to be commensurable -- that there must be some one thing in terms of which their equality can be stated -- and then offers only two candidates for that common measure: need (or demand), and money, which he treats as a purely conventional device that merely represents need. My friend says neither can do the work required of it. Need, he argues, is either too indeterminate to yield a measure, since two people can need the same two goods in ways that cannot be compared, or else it covertly smuggles in a labour theory of value that Aristotle never states. Money, being conventional, cannot supply a natural measure, and so cannot explain why an exchange is just rather than merely agreed. On this reading, Aristotle sees a genuine problem -- the one we now call the problem of commensurability -- and then dissolves it with a word.

He adds a second charge: that the denunciation of lending at interest in the Politics is inconsistent with the account of money in the Ethics. If money is a conventional convenience for facilitating exchange, then nothing in its nature forbids a return on its use; the charge that interest is contrary to nature, on the ground that money was meant only for exchange and cannot breed, must therefore be prejudice wearing the costume of metaphysics.

I am inclined to think he is wrong, or at least too quick, but I cannot put my finger on where the account can be rescued. So I want the essay to do the following. First, reconstruct the strongest coherent reading of the commensurability passage: is there a defensible account of what the common measure is, and does it survive? In particular, consider the reading on which need is not individual desire but a social or political standard -- what the community requires -- which would make Aristotle a kind of conventionalist about value; if that reading works, does it save him or merely relocate the difficulty? Second, decide whether the critique of interest follows from or contradicts the Ethics account. Does Aristotle hold a distinction between the use of a thing and its exchange, or between household management and acquisition, that renders the two discussions consistent -- and is that distinction defensible on its own terms, or does it collapse? Third, reach a verdict: on the best reconstruction, is Aristotle's account of money and exchange coherent and worth defending, and if so, which version of it; if not, say precisely where it breaks, and whether the break is Aristotle's or my friend's misreading.

I am not asking for a reading list or references; I want the argument itself, in the weeds of the texts. And tell me how confident you are in the verdict you reach, and single out the one point in your reasoning you are least sure of.
