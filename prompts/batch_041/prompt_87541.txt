In my logic seminar the professor regularly invokes Skolem's paradox to argue that uncountability is 'relative' — that there is no absolute fact of the matter about how many real numbers there are, only facts about what a given model can see. Last week he went further: since set theory has a countable model, he said, the real numbers might really be countable, and our conviction that they are uncountably many is like insisting that someone is tall in a room where everyone happens to be short. A classmate objected that if 'uncountable' behaved like 'tall', then 'there are more real numbers than natural numbers' would be no more objective than 'she is tall', which he took to be a reductio; the professor answered that this only seems absurd if one assumes a God's-eye inventory of all the sets, which is exactly what the paradox is meant to undermine. Neither of us could say who was getting the paradox right, so I want to bring the whole thing to office hours as a disambiguation rather than a verdict.

Please lay out, as a menu I can work from, the distinct precise claims that 'Skolem's paradox shows uncountability is relative' could be making — from the near-trivial reading to the most radical — and for each say whether it is true, contentious, or false, and what it would take for it to be true. In particular, sort out three things. First, what actually happens in the countable model: does it contain the real numbers, or only something it calls by that name, and does that difference bear on the professor's conclusion? Second, whether 'uncountable' is a context-relative predicate in the way that 'tall' or 'nearby' is, or whether pressing that analogy is itself the reductio. Third, whether the relativity, if it is genuine, actually supports the claim that the reals might 'really' be countable or leaves that claim untouched. Finish by saying which reading the professor most likely has in mind, and whether on that reading his claim about the real numbers survives.
