I keep getting into the same argument about the definition of genocide and I want to know why it never goes anywhere. The setup, as I understand it: the 1948 Convention definition covers national, ethnic, racial and religious groups, leaves out political and social groups, and requires intent to destroy the group in whole or in part. Lemkin's own 1944 conception was much broader — he was after the destruction of a group's political and social institutions, its culture, language, religion, economic life, not just killing. And the drafting history is pretty obviously a compromise: states worried about their own records, the political-groups exclusion pushed by the Soviets among others. So one side says the definition is a historical artifact, too narrow, it lets perpetrators off on a technicality, and we should broaden it to cover political groups and cultural destruction. The other side says the narrowness is a feature, the whole point is a category that can actually be prosecuted, and if you broaden it you dilute it and it becomes a rhetorical weapon with no legal teeth. Neither side has moved in decades. I've read alot of this and its the same moves over and over.

My hypothesis — and I want you to tell me if its right — is that this isn't really a disagreement about the definition at all. It's a disagreement about what the label does. Once something is called genocide it triggers a whole set of norms and obligations (never again, R2P, the duty to prevent and punish), so the definitional fight is really a fight about who gets obligated to do what, and about who gets to be the victim of the worst crime there is. If thats the diagnosis, then the two sides aren't even answering the same question: the lawyers are asking what we can prove and punish, the moralists and historians are asking what we should call this and what it means. Two questions under one word.

But I'm not sure thats the whole story. There might be a real substantive disagreement underneath about whether there's something specially wrong with destroying a group as such — the groupist view — versus the view that whats wrong is the harm to individuals and genocide is just a particularly awful way of doing that. If that's the real disagreement, then the definitional fight is downstream of a moral one and no amount of legal drafting will settle it. There's also an empirical question nobody seems to have answered: does broadening the legal definition actually make enforcement worse, or does it make prevention better? Both sides assert their answer but I dont think either has the evidence.

So what I want is a diagnosis, not a survey. Is this a verbal dispute, a substantive moral one, a disguised political one, two questions under one name, or some combination? Use what each side actually says — the dolus specialis requirement and how it plays out in cases like Darfur or Srebrenica, the in-whole-or-in-part clause and the substantial-part gloss, the cultural genocide debate, the political groups exclusion, the naming fights in Armenia, Rwanda, Ukraine, the Holodomor. And then tell me what would count as progress, given your diagnosis. Would it be a stipulative definition with a clearly stated purpose? Separating the legal category from the moral one and being honest about which is doing what? A formal result about intent? Empirical work on enforcement? Or is this one of those categories where the contest is the point and no progress should be expected — and if so, what follows for how we should actually talk and act?
