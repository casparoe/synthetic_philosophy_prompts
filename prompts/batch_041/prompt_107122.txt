I'm a philosophy professor and I keep getting stuck on a case my students bring up in my epistemology seminar. A woman in a workplace meeting has been harassed by a senior colleague, but she doesn't report it because she's sure the committee will find her unreliable or hysterical, and she's seen other women get dismissed that way. I've been calling this testimonial smothering, following the literature on epistemic injustice, and I tell the class it's a clear injustice. But a colleague in political philosophy pushed back hard at happy hour: he said if she chose not to speak, then nobody actually did anything to her. At most she suffered a private cost, like being too shy to ask a question. For it to be an injustice, he says, some agent has to wrong her, and here the only agent is her own decision. I tried to say the social environment is the agent, but that sounds like a metaphor, and he said I'm just re-describing her fear as a wrong. Now I'm worried he's right. If the silence is self-imposed, how can it be an injustice rather than just a bad outcome? And if the remedy is for her to speak up anyway, isn't the theory just blaming her for not being brave enough?

But then I think: maybe I'm confusing an unjust act with an unjust state of affairs, or maybe he's confusing harm with injustice. I can't tell which of us has the false presupposition. Can you lay out what testimonial smothering actually is, what makes it an epistemic injustice rather than mere self-censorship, and whether the no-agent-no-injustice objection has any force? I'd also like to know whether the concept needs a perpetrator at all, or whether structural accounts can make sense of it without turning the environment into a spooky agent. And if my colleague is wrong, where exactly does his argument go wrong?
