I'm a retired seismologist -- thirty years of watching squiggles from earthquakes and arguing about whether the mantle convects in one layer or two -- and after I hung it up I finally got around to all the philosophy I'd been meaning to read my whole life. Now I've written a paper and I'm giving it at a little interdisciplinary seminar next month. My topic is Hofstadter's complaint that machine translation is shallow, and I want to argue he's pointing at the right thing but naming it wrong. My line is that the real test of whether a translator understands isn't sentence-level fidelity, it's what I've been calling the commitment test: a translator that understands can tell you what a translated sentence commits the original speaker to -- whether two sentences in the text can't both be true, whether one is an answer to another, whether a paragraph contradicts something forty pages back. Statistical translation passes fidelity and fails commitment, and I think that's the whole story. Problem is the audience. It's the department's language-and-computation seminar, so it's mostly the machine translation people -- engineers and linguists who've actually built these systems and have a low tolerance for philosophy -- plus two philosophers of language who think the entire does-it-understand question is a category mistake, plus, because the organizer has a sense of humor, a professional literary translator. I need a prep sheet for the question period. What will each of them actually throw at me, in their own vocabulary, ordered by how much damage it does -- the engineers' objection that the commitment test is just another benchmark with a metaphysics smuggled inside it, the philosophers' charge that I'm treating understanding as a single thing a system either has or lacks, the translator's we-fail-that-test-constantly-so-what -- and for each one tell me what to concede, what to hold, and a reply I can deliver in two minutes, plus a note on where the reply is thin. And tell me the one question I'm hoping nobody in that room asks. Write it out as plain prose, please -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just paragraphs I can read straight through like a letter, because that's how I actually prepare for these things and there's alot riding on this one.
