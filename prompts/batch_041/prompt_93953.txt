I teach a third-year seminar that's half social choice and half political philosophy - we do Arrow, then Sen, then a few weeks on rights as side-constraints - and I'm writing the final this weekend. I want one essay question on Sen's liberal paradox that students can't get through by reciting the prude-and-the-lewd example, because at least half the class has that memorised and its all they can produce. So I've built a fresh example and three seperate proposed repairs, and I want the question to hang off those. It's a 90-minute exam, they write about 800 words, 20 marks.

Here's the material - please use it as-is in the question.

Three alternatives:
- N - neither Devi nor Marco has a nose piercing.
- D - Devi has one and Marco doesn't.
- M - Marco has one and Devi doesn't.

Two rights, understood as decisiveness: Devi is decisive over the pair {N, D}; Marco is decisive over the pair {N, M}. 'Decisive' means: if the named individual strictly prefers one member of the pair to the other, the social ranking has to go the same way, whatever anyone else prefers.

Preferences (no intensities, no comparisons between people):
- Devi: N > D > M. She doesn't want one herself; but if one of them is going to have one she'd rather it were her, since she reckons a nose ring would cost Marco at interviews.
- Marco: D > M > N. He'd rather Devi got one; he's squeamish about his own; and he'd least like it if neither of them did.

Three repairs for them to assess:
(A) the sphere restriction: require every individual's preference to depend only on their own sphere - in this case, only on whether they themselves have a piercing - so nobody's ranking turns on what the other person does with their own body.
(B) the procedural reading: stop demanding a transitive social ranking at all; let each right-holder simply choose within their own pair and call whatever results the outcome.
(C) lexicographic priority: keep both the rights and Pareto, but make the rights lexicographically prior, so Pareto only gets applied where no right is at stake.

The question itself should ask them to (i) run the paradox through this example and say which assumption is doing the work at each step, (ii) say exactly which assumptions the paradox needs and which are idle, (iii) take the three repairs one at a time - does each one actually break the cycle here, does it generalise, and what does it cost someone who cares about liberty, (iv) close with a judgement about whether any of the repairs is free.

Then give me a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme of five must-hit points at 4 marks each, total 20, where each point says what a top answer has to contain and a one-line note on how candidates usually get that point wrong. The two things I care about most: the question shouldn't be answerable by writing out the textbook example or by name-dropping, and the marking scheme should punish the students who spot the cycle and then go hand-wavy - 'rights are trumps, so it's just a technicality'.
