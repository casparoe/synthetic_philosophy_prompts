Question (25 marks). In the handout we modelled anthropic reasoning with an urn. A hypothesis specifies N, the total number of humans who will ever have lived. You are a ball drawn uniformly at random from the urn, and the number on your ball is your birth rank r, i.e. how many humans were born before you plus one. We defined two assumptions for the course. Self-sampling assumption (SSA): reason as if you were a random observer drawn from your reference class. Self-indication assumption (SIA): hypotheses that entail more observers get proportionally more prior probability. Take as given that r is about 100 billion, roughly how many humans have been born so far. Consider two hypotheses only: DOOM SOON, on which N = 200 billion, and DOOM LATE, on which N = 10 trillion. Both get prior probability 1/2. (a) Set out the doomsday argument in this model and compute the posterior probability of DOOM SOON. Show the likelihoods. (10) (b) State the strongest objection to the argument, explain what exactly it is objecting to, and say whether you think it succeeds. (8) (c) What does your answer to (a) and (b) imply, if anything, for how we should weight near-term versus far-future work? Be explicit about what does and doesn't follow. (7)

Answer. (a) We are a random ball in the urn, so under any hypothesis our rank is uniform on 1 to N. If DOOM SOON is true then N = 200 billion and the chance of drawing the ball numbered 100 billion is 1/200 billion. If DOOM LATE is true then N = 10 trillion, i.e. 10,000 billion, so the chance is 1/10,000 billion. The likelihood ratio is 10,000/200 = 50, so the observation favours DOOM SOON by 50 to 1. Both hypotheses started at 1/2, so the posterior odds are 50:1 and the posterior probability is 50/51, about 98%. So our position is strong evidence that the total number of humans is small. This is not the gambler's fallacy — we aren't saying that because there's been a long run of humans we're overdue for the end, the update comes from the fact that a large N makes our particular position a much rarer draw. And it's 98% rather than certain: DOOM LATE is still on the table, just unlikely.

(b) The strongest objection is the reference class problem. The urn only means anything once you've said what the balls are, and "all humans" is one choice among many — all observers, all mammals, all beings capable of asking the question. Change the class and r changes with it, so the numbers in (a) are an artifact of a choice the argument itself can't justify. There's also the self-indication assumption. Treating ourselves as a uniformly random draw from all the balls just is SIA — we're letting our own existence be a random sample from the whole population — and SIA is precisely the thing people dispute. If you drop it and reason from a flat prior over N without the random-draw step, the doomsday update doesn't go through at all. So I don't think the argument succeeds as stated: the entire force of (a) comes from an assumption about how our position relates to the population, and that assumption is doing all the work.

(c) Since the posterior puts almost all its weight on DOOM SOON, the expected number of humans still to be born is roughly the same as the number who have already lived, about 100 billion, and the expected total is close to 200 billion. So the future is probably not the astronomically large reservoir of value that longtermists assume, and the case for spending on existential risk reduction is weaker than it looks, since you'd be buying insurance on a future that probably doesn't happen. I don't want to overstate it — DOOM LATE still has 2%, and 2% of 10 trillion isn't nothing. But the direction of the correction is clear and it runs against the far future.
