I have defended eternalism about time for most of my career, and the argument that first convinced me — the one that moves from the relativity of simultaneity to the conclusion that all events are equally real, usually filed under Rietdijk and Putnam — still seems to me the strongest consideration in its favour. I would like you to tell me what counts against it, and to rank what you find rather than merely list it. Keep two questions apart, because I care about the difference.

The first is about the position. Eternalism says that past, present, and future events alike exist, and that nothing metaphysical is special about the present. What would have to be true for that to be false? Which of those possibilities are ones we could plausibly come to have reason to accept — from physics, from the philosophy of causation, from the logic of tense — and which are merely not excluded? Does the view predict anything its rivals do not, and does it carry any commitment that a serious rival or a future physics could falsify? If it does not, say whether that is a defect of the view or simply what one should expect from a metaphysical thesis of this kind.

The second is about the argument. Grant the relativity of simultaneity and treat the inference as an inference. Which step is most exposed? The move from simultaneity to reality looks to me like the weak point, but I want the other candidates as well, each ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Consider at least these: whether the argument equivocates between what is real and what is real for a frame; whether someone who denies that the present is privileged can accept every premise and still reject the conclusion; whether the thesis that simultaneity is conventional rather than discovered, if true, helps or harms the argument; whether a frame-independent but local notion of becoming, defined by causal connectibility rather than by a simultaneity slice, would be a genuine alternative to the block or only a redescription of it; and whether a physics that privileges a foliation of spacetime would refute eternalism or merely make one version of it more natural.

Then a variant I have been toying with and cannot decide about. Replace simultaneity in the argument with the frame-independent relation of spacelike separation, so that the premise concerns events outside an event's light cone rather than events on a slice. Does the conclusion survive the substitution, or does the reformulation quietly reintroduce the very notion of a present that it was meant to eliminate?

Finally, separate the objections that eternalists themselves would concede from those they would dismiss as question-begging, and give me a verdict on whether the position as it is usually held has been qualified into something no argument could touch. If it has, say whether that is a problem for eternalism or a sign that the question was badly posed.
