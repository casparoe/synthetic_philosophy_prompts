I am the secretariat of a standing expert advisory panel at a national food-safety authority. Ten members, roughly ten meetings a year, about forty binary questions annually: approve or refuse a proposed maximum residue limit, permit or reject a novel ingredient, keep or revisit an existing approval. The authority follows our recommendation in the overwhelming majority of cases, and the stakes are asymmetric, since a wrongful approval is slow and costly to reverse while a wrongful refusal blocks a product and invites a legal challenge from the applicant. Questions below a size threshold go to a three-member sub-group. The full panel votes in closed session after discussion, votes are recorded with names attached, every member has read the same staff-prepared dossier, most of the ten were trained in the same two laboratories, and two have co-published with applicants in the past. Our legal office wants a rule it can defend in writing; I have about an hour per question and no statistician on call. Over the past five years, roughly one positive recommendation in eight was reversed when new evidence arrived, while almost none of our refusals was.

Write me a decision procedure I can run before each vote, grounded in what Condorcet's jury theorem and the work it has generated actually support, rather than in a slogan about the wisdom of crowds. The theorem's conditions — a binary question with a defensible right answer, each member better than even at getting it right, errors independent across members — are never fully met in our room, so the procedure has to be a sequence of tests that identify which condition fails and what follows when it does. In order, it should tell me: whether this is a question on which a majority could be more likely to be right than its typical member, or a question of acceptable risk on which competence is undefined and the vote is only a show of hands; whether to use the full panel or a sub-group, and how that turns on whether our members are better or worse than chance; what to do about independence when everyone has read the same dossier and most of the panel shares a training lineage; how to elicit opinions so the vote approximates independent judgment rather than deference to the two most senior members; how the required threshold should move when the two errors cost different amounts, in terms the legal office will accept; what to do when the panel splits near the middle; and what to do when a majority rejects a conclusion that follows from premises each of which a majority accepted — as happens when our separate votes on whether the pivotal study was reliable and whether the effect it reports is adverse both pass, yet the conclusion that the substance is unsafe fails, because different members defect at different steps. Keep the arithmetic to what I can do on a spreadsheet. Mark the points where the procedure stops and a person must decide, and say plainly what it cannot do.

Then run it on three cases: the split-premises case above; a 6-4 vote to approve in which the four dissenters are the two most senior members and the two who have published most on the substance in question; and one case where the procedure gives an answer I will not like, since I would prefer it to tell me to vote and take the majority, and I suspect it will not.
