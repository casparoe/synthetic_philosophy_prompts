For a talk I'm giving to my school's philosophy club, I want to read Christopher Nolan's Memento through the literature on money pumps and diachronic rationality, rather than as a clever puzzle about memory. Leonard's tattoos, Polaroids and notes look like a self-binding device of the Ulysses-contract kind, and Teddy and Natalie both profit from getting him to take one locally reasonable step at a time, so the money-pump frame is tempting; I would rather you test it than assume it. Is the film really showing an agent pumped through a cycle of intransitive or time-inconsistent preferences, or is Leonard a stable agent whose problem is execution rather than preference change? Do Parfit-style successive selves, or resolute choice — keeping faith with an earlier plan by refusing to renegotiate it later — fit what he does to himself, especially in the chronologically last scene, where he chooses to plant a false clue for his own future self? Tell me where the analogy holds and where it breaks, and finish by saying what the film shows about agency across time that the money-pump arguments, which usually turn on a few trades, do not.
