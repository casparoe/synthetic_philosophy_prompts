For about a decade I've been a regular reader of rationalist blogs and forums — the decision-theory and existential-risk corners of them especially — and I now host a small interview series for a local meetup, recorded and circulated to an audience that already knows the standard arguments and does not need them restated. Next month's guest is a decision theorist who has spent several years arguing that the unilateralist's curse is misdiagnosed.

Her position, as I understand it, is this. The familiar version of the curse is stated as an aggregation problem: if each of many agents will unilaterally undertake some action whenever their own estimate of its expected value is positive, and each estimate carries some independent chance of error, then the probability that at least one agent acts climbs with the number of agents, so a world with more capable actors can fare worse than a world with fewer. The usual remedy is a principle of conformity — do not act unless you have reason to think that others in your position would act too. Her claim is that this gets the mechanism wrong. The real engine, she argues, is adverse selection: the agents who act are not a random draw from the population but precisely those least sensitive to the downside, so adding agents does not merely raise the chance that someone errs; it degrades the quality of whoever errs first. Conformity, on her account, is the wrong remedy twice over — it does nothing about selection, and it fails outright when errors are correlated rather than independent, since correlated agents do not cancel one another but synchronize. What she proposes instead is a costly pre-commitment: anyone contemplating a unilateral action with global and irreversible consequences must publicly register the intention and their confidence in advance, and act only if they would still act after seeing the registry. She also insists that the mirror-image case — everyone declining to act because each assumes another will — is not the same problem with the sign flipped, and should not be treated symmetrically.

Draft nine to eleven interview questions for this conversation. They should press on the actual content of her position, and each should come with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give; where the standard reply is predictable, name it explicitly rather than hedging, so that the follow-up lands. Please make sure there is at least one question each on (i) whether the adverse-selection diagnosis is even empirically coherent — in particular, whether overconfidence should be expected to correlate with incompetence in the direction her argument requires, and what the aggregation version of the curse predicts that hers does not; (ii) the correlated-errors objection to her registry proposal, including whether a registry whose entries are visible to one another might convert independent error into a stampede rather than preventing one; and (iii) her asymmetry claim, i.e. whether the inaction case really is disanalogous or whether it is the same curse with the sign reversed and an omission bias smuggled in. Close with a question about what evidence would count against her view, and a follow-up on what she would do if that evidence turned out to be unavailable.

Two things to avoid. First, no questions that ask her for reading recommendations, citations, or names of others working on the problem — the audience can look those up, and I want the hour spent on arguments. Second, no biographical warm-ups or questions about how she came to the topic; assume the audience knows the standard version of the curse and open in the middle of the disagreement.
