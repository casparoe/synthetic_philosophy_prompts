I'm in second year and doing a module on public memory and commemoration, and I've realised I've been nodding along in seminars without actually knowing what I think. So here are my gut verdicts on a pile of cases. They're all made up or local, but you'll get the shape.

1. The Pike statue. Alderman Pike's money came out of a firm that ran slave ships; the bronze has been in the market square since 1901 and his old business partners paid for it. Gut: it should come down, or at least go into the museum.
2. Pike Street. Same man, named in 1904. Gut: leave it. It's an address — when I tell someone to turn left off Pike Street I'm not paying anyone a compliment.
3. The Cordwainers' memorial. A stone obelisk in the churchyard with 41 local lads who died in a colonial war I think was pointless and unjust. Gut: don't touch it, ever. It's basically a grave.
4. General Halloway's bronze, outside the town hall. He commanded that campaign. Gut: that one should go.
5. Dr Ashworth's statue in the park. She did genuinely first-rate work — surveyed the whole valley — and by all accounts she was a horror, bullied her students and nicked a junior colleague's discovery. Gut: keep it. It's about the survey.
6. The Rennie statue. The plaque says he invented the safety lamp; he didn't, he lifted it off his assistant. Gut: change the plaque, keep the statue.
7. Gibbons Walk. There was never a Gibbons — a clerk made a mistake in 1929 and the name's stuck for a century. Gut: keep, obviously. It's a fossil, not a compliment.
8. Marshal Vek's bronze in the park, put up by the regime in 1974, nobody looks at it, kids climb on it. Gut: still take it down. I don't want the council maintaining a statue that says he was great.

1 and 6 look like the same case to me and I've definately got them opposite. So do 3 and 4 — same war, opposite verdicts. Something's going on that I can't see.

My working theory is that it's about whether the thing asks you to *admire* someone or only to *remember* them — remembering the Cordwainers is fine, admiring Pike isn't. Sam in my seminar reckons the real test is whether the thing is doing any work: if nobody would notice it gone, it isn't commemorating anything, so it can't be doing any harm. And I keep coming back to the idea that a street name is a thing you *use* in a sentence, while a statue is a thing you just look at, and that that's the actual difference between 1 and 2. Am I making that up?

There's also a bench on the seafront with a little brass plaque to a mate of my grandad's who, it turns out, did something seperate and genuinely horrible in the seventies. His mates put it up and only they ever sit there. My theory says leave it. My gut says take the plaque off. I don't know which one I believe.

So: is there a principle that gets all of this, or close? If it saves every verdict, tell me what the complexity costs me; if it doesn't, tell me which verdict to drop and why. If it needs more than one clause, say which case each clause is there for, and be honest about the verdicts it can't accommodate. And say what it says about two cases I haven't mentioned — a statue of someone who's still alive, and a memorial put up by the family of the man who did the wrong, to the people he wronged. British English please, my tutor marks me down for 'color' and 'organize'.
