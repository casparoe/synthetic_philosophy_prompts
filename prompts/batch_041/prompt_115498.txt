There is an old dispute about whether dreams are experiences, and it is easy to make it look either trivially verbal or trivially empirical. On one side stands the Wittgensteinian line associated with Malcolm: the concept of dreaming is anchored in the practice of telling, a dream report cannot be checked against the episode it purports to report, and so calling a dream an experience misplaces it — dreaming belongs with sleeping rather than with seeing and hearing. On the other side, associated with Dennett, the thought is that the received view of dreams as experiences occurring during sleep is one hypothesis among others, to be assessed by finding out how and when dream content is generated rather than by consulting the grammar of our words.

I want an explanation of the conceptual issue that lies between these two lines — one that does real work rather than rehearsing the familiar framing. Please address the following.

First, the received view is usually stated as a single claim, but it conjoins two: that a dream is an experience, and that it occurs during sleep. Can these come apart, and does the dispute look different once they do? Is there a coherent position on which dreams are experiences but are not had during sleep, and what would a defender of the received view say about it?

Second, consider two constructed cases. The night writer: a person whose brain composes a full dream narrative in the moments around waking, so that the narrative is generated as an integrated whole and then remembered as having unfolded over hours. The silent dreamer: a person who undergoes long, vivid dream episodes during sleep but whose memory of them is erased at the instant of waking, so that no report is ever possible. Which of these, if either, is a case of dreaming? What would each side in the dispute have to say about the other's case?

Third, what must each side assume about the concept of experience — whether it is a natural kind, a success term, or something whose criteria are essentially first-personal and retrospective? What role does the fact that dreams misrepresent duration play here, given that the timing of an experience and the temporal content of an experience can come apart?

Finally, is this a substantive disagreement at all, or does it dissolve once the two sides are seen to be answering different questions? If it is substantive, say what could settle it, and whether the two sides could agree on the facts while still disagreeing about what those facts show.
