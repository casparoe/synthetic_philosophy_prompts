So a few of us have been doing a weekly metaphysics reading group and we keep getting stuck on Black's two spheres — the universe whose only contents are two iron spheres of the same size, material, color and temperature, some fixed distance apart, no other objects, no observer. Every week we get about fifteen minutes in and then somebody says "well, each sphere has the relational property of being two miles from the other one" and we're off to the races, and I'm realizing I don't actually know how many different positions there are here or where the real forks are. Can you build me a decision tree for this — the kind where every branching node is a yes/no or two-or-three-way question that a person could answer without already knowing which position it lands them in, and every leaf is a named position, or explicitly marked as "unnamed / nobody defends this" if that's the honest answer? Start from something bland that basically everybody will grant, like "there could be a world whose only contents are those two spheres," and then let the tree do the work on the things I think really seperate people: whether relational properties count as properties at all (and whether counting them makes the principle come out trivially true, since each sphere already differs from the other in being identical to itself), whether the two spheres are even two objects rather than one scattered thing with two lobes, whether identity is primitive or has to be grounded in qualities, and whether possible worlds are individuated only up to permutations of indiscernible things. I want the leaves to include at least: the Leibnizian who says the principle is true but only because relational and identity properties are doing the work; someone who accepts it only for intrinsic, non-relational properties; the bundle theorist; the haecceitist who says the spheres are two in virtue of a primitive thisness; the anti-haecceitist who says worlds aren't distinguished by swapping indiscernibles; and whoever thinks the whole question is a verbal dispute or malformed. And please force the tree through some cases beyond Black's original, because I think that's where the positions actually come apart: a world with three spheres at the corners of an equilateral triangle, a world that's just a rotated or mirror-image version of the first, a world where a little mechanism swaps the two spheres every second so that at each moment there's a fact about which one moved, a world with one sphere of the same size sitting at the midpoint of where the two would be (I keep wondering what would even make that a different world from the two-sphere one), and a case where an omnipotent being has to annihilate exactly one of the two and has no reason to pick either, which I think drags in the principle of sufficient reason. If some combination of answers is empty — nobody's there — say so and tell me whether that's because the view is incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored, and if two leaves are really the same view wearing different hats then flag it instead of pretending the tree split them. No citations or reading lists, obviously, I just want the instrument itself; keep it to like eight to twelve nodes and six to eight leaves, and it's for a reading group of grad students, though I'll probably put it on my blog afterward, so alot of hand-holding is fine.
