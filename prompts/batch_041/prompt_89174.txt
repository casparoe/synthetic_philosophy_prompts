I have followed the long-running argument over perfect forgeries for years and have come to think it never converges, because the disputants are answering three different questions while believing they are answering one. I would like you to commit to a number on one of them, and then to show me its skeleton.

The proposition is this: there is a reason to value an artwork's being the original rather than an atom-for-atom duplicate of it that is not fully accounted for by (a) the experiences available to a viewer of either object and (b) the social and market facts about what others believe or will pay. Call it the residual-authenticity claim. It is stated so that it can be false: it fails if every reason anyone has offered for preferring the original can be cashed out in terms of (a) and (b) alone.

Give me your credence in it as a number between 0 and 1, followed by the decomposition — the sub-questions the number rests on, each with its own rough probability and a single line of justification, then how the terms combine into the final figure. I expect the sub-questions to include at least: whether the question has a determinate answer at all, or whether "a reason to value" is indexical to a person's preferences and so collapses into the trivial claim that some people happen to care; whether, granting realism about aesthetic value, the value attaches to the type (the image, which duplicates share) or to the token (the particular object); whether the disvalue of a forgery is located in the deception rather than the duplication, so that a duplicate honestly presented as a duplicate is not defective at all; and whether the empirical fact that provenance changes how people actually experience a work is evidence about the value or merely a fact about us. Add any sub-question you think I have missed, and say why it belongs.

Two cases should be folded into your reasoning rather than handled in a footnote. First, conservation: a painting is conserved over a century, canvas fibers and pigment molecules replaced as they degrade, until none of the original material remains while the visible surface is indistinguishable from what it was. Is the displayed object the original, a copy, or something the available vocabulary does not cover — and does your verdict there constrain your verdict on the duplicate? Second, detection: a duplicate that no one will ever detect, versus one whose status is discovered a century later. If the value of the original turns on counterfactual detection, say so plainly.

Then tell me which single term your number is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument would move it most; be specific about what you would have to learn.

For calibration: my own credence is about 0.3 and a friend's is about 0.75, and we have failed to locate our disagreement. Say which sub-question it most likely lives on, and what each of us should do to find out.
