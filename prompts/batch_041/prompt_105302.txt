I'm in a reading group that has spent the term on personal identity, and for our final session I've been asked to bring a poem rather than a paper. What kept derailing our discussion was the gradual-replacement version of mind uploading: not the dramatic cutover, but the slow one, where over months or years each part of a person's brain is swapped out for a functionally equivalent piece of hardware, with the subject awake and talking the whole time. The thing that unsettled everyone was that no single step looks like a step you could object to, and yet the endpoint is a machine — and worse, the only witness we'd normally consult is the person undergoing it, whose testimony is itself part of what's being replaced. So I want a poem written from the inside: the narrator is the person being converted, and at every stage reports that nothing has happened, that the lights are still on, that it is still them. I don't want the poem to adjudicate this; I want it to leave the reader standing exactly where the narrator is, having been assured and having assured you that everything is fine, and unable to check.

Formally, I'd like a ghazal, somewhere between nine and eleven couplets, with a single-word radif — the word "still" — closing every couplet, unchanged from first to last. The couplets should advance the conversion by one stage each, beginning with something unmistakably biological and ending with something unmistakably engineered, and the trick is that the radif must not be decoration: the same word has to carry the continuity of the person while everything around it is replaced, and the reader should be able to locate each couplet on the timeline without any couplet ever announcing its stage. Rhyme before the radif is welcome wherever it doesn't force the sense, but sense wins. Two details I'd like threaded through: the narrator has a small habit — some way of counting stairs, a phrase they have always mispronounced — and the reader should be left unsure whether the habit survived because it was carried across or because it was rebuilt from a blueprint; and at some point the narrator should mention a memory they can no longer tell whether they lived or merely have a record of, without the poem diagnosing which. The closing couplet should name the narrator in the traditional way, and it should be genuinely unclear whether what's being named is a person or a file. Please avoid the standard labels and any names of philosophers; someone who has never heard of the debate should still find it a good poem, and someone who has should be able to point at each couplet and say what has just been swapped out.
