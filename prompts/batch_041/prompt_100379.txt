I'm a junior at a small high school where our philosophy club has spent the spring on Montaigne's essay about the Brazilian cannibals, and for the club's end-of-year zine I have been asked to contribute a poem instead of the essay I would normally write. Two things in the piece have stuck with me, and I cannot work out whether they are one gesture or two opposite ones. The first is the way the essay turns the Europeans' disgust back on the Europeans, so that a people reviled for eating their dead come out looking no crueller than a people we admire for burning the living. The second is the way, having knocked us off our perch, the essay seems to set them on a perch of its own, calling them close to nature and uncorrupted, as though they were still living out some original condition we have lost. I suspect the second move is the first one in a different costume, that the mirror image is still a mirror, but I want a poem that leaves that suspicion standing rather than settling it, and I want it to keep in view the essay's own uneasiness about where its picture comes from: the whole account arrives secondhand, through people who crossed between the two worlds, and it has been translated at least twice before it reaches us.

Would you write me a poem of roughly eighteen lines in three movements: an opening movement in the voice of a European describing the cannibals and passing judgment; a second movement that answers the first almost line for line, carrying the same images and sentence shapes but turned back on the Europeans, so that a reader feels the reversal in the form before putting a name to it; and a short coda that breaks the pattern to catch the speaker in the act of the second, subtler move, the idealizing. If strict line-for-line mirroring becomes contorted, let the second movement mirror the first's images and syntax without forcing the count. A reader who knows the essay should be able to tell, without being told, which movement is the reversal and where the speaker catches himself; a reader who does not should still find it a good poem rather than a lecture. Please do not let it end on a comfortable relativist moral, do not resolve whether the idealizing is a genuine correction or simply the old parochialism wearing the other culture's clothes, and do not use the words "relativism" or "ethnocentrism" anywhere in it. Begin your reply with the poem itself, with no preface, no explanation, and no commentary afterward; if you want a title, let it be part of the poem.
