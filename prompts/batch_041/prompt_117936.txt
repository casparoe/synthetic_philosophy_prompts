I'm a 3L and I edit the satire issue of our law review — it comes out the same week as the alumni giving letter, which is the one joke nobody has to write — and I've been putting off the centerpiece for two weeks because I'm also stuck on a moot court brief about maritime salvage. What I want for the centerpiece is a mock judicial opinion, and I'd like you to draft it. Setting: Vardania, a made-up country that spent 1968 to 2014 under a military Directorate and is now three years into being a constitutional democracy, with a shiny new Constitutional Court and a first article of the constitution saying the state is founded on human dignity. The case is tiny: Iosif B. is suing Mrs. M. for defamation because for three years she has stood at the vegetable market calling him a murderer. The undisputed facts are these. In 1981 Iosif filed a written denunciation with the Directorate's district office saying that Mrs. M.'s husband — his brother-in-law — had been listening to foreign radio. The husband was tried under Decree 7 of 1971, which was properly enacted, properly published, and applied by real judges in real courtrooms, and he died in a labor camp in 1987. So the truth of the word murderer turns entirely on whether Decree 7 was law. I want the lead opinion to reason its way, in completly deadpan judicial register, to the positivist answer: the decree was valid law when it was applied, everyone who acted under it acted lawfully, so what Mrs. M. said is false and Iosif recovers. The whole thing being satirized is the idea that once you've shown a rule was validly made there's nothing left to ask, and the horror should come only from the logic being right — no winks, no authorial nudging, no judge who's a buffoon, and no stacking the deck (Iosif isn't a cartoon villain, Mrs. M. isn't a saint; the point is that the law doesn't care). So use the real arguments: the separation of law and morals, the awkward fact that a court which voids the old statutes ab initio is doing something with retroactivity it would never do to anyone else, the way valid and just come apart, and the little cascade in both directions — if the decree was law, then the guards and clerks and prosecutors and judges all acted lawfully and the only unlawful act on the record is a slander at a vegetable stall; if it wasn't law, then every one of those people is a criminal and this court is legislating backwards.

After the lead opinion, give me a short concurrence and a short dissent. The concurrence should be queasy, reach the same judgment by a different road (it thinks the decree was so unjust it never was law at all, yet still thinks she loses, and it shouldn't pretend to be comfortable with that), and the dissent should do the Fuller thing — a system of rules that is partly secret, retroactive, impossible to comply with, and not even followed by its own officials doesn't meet the conditions for counting as law, so the decree never was law, the killing was unlawful, and she was telling the truth — and it should notice that its own conclusion makes the new state's title to rule look shaky. Everybody argues in good faith from the best version of their own position. If a reader who's been through the Hart–Fuller exchange can't tell which judge I think is right, and can't quite stop laughing, it's working. Around 900 words, caption plus numbered paragraphs, then the concurrence and dissent under their own headings, and legible to somebody who only knows the outline of the debate. Start with the piece itself — no preface, no explanation of what you're up to, no notes to me.
