I'm 71, retired after thirty-five years as a field ecologist -- insects, mostly, and mostly on farmland -- and since I packed it in I've been reading moral philosophy in a pretty undiscplined way. Those two halves of my life have just collided: the county land trust has asked me to give the after-dinner talk at their annual dinner, and the theme this year is what we eat and what it costs the land, and I said yes before I'd thought it through. The room is going to be awkward. There are three or four cattle families who've worked the same ground for generations and who I'm fond of, there's the Tuesday volunteer crew, most of whom are vegetarian and quite serious about it, and a table of people from the university. And the dinner is the same beef dinner it's always been, so I'll be eating a plate of it while I talk, which I don't want to be coy about or make a performance of either. Seven or eight minutes, a bit of humour won't hurt, and it has to be true -- I'm not willing to stand up and say things I think are false just to keep the peace, and I'm not willing to spend the evening lecturing people I eat dinner with.

What I want the talk built around is an inversion that's been bothering me since my mowing and ploughing survey days. On the usual animal-welfare accounting, chicken and eggs look worse than beef per kilo or per calorie, because you're killing a lot more individual animals to feed the same number of people. On the wild-animal side I think it runs the other way: beef, and especially grass-fed beef and dairy, takes far more land per calorie than pork or chicken, and cropland or pasture that gets mown, ploughed, sprayed or grazed is land where a great many small animals die, or never get born in the first place. If both of those are roughly right, then the two ways of counting point in opposite directions, and which diet comes out ahead ends up depending on whether invertebrates count for anything and on whether you're counting deaths or counting lost lives. I'd like the spine of the speech to be exactly this, explained from scratch in ordinary language for people who've never heard any of it -- no citations, no jargon, no names of philosophers, and if you use a number make it plain it's a rough guess and say what it hangs on.

Then I'd like it to land on whatever actually survives the inversion -- the thing, if there is one, that both ways of counting agree about -- rather than on a rule like eat less meat, because I honestly don't know which way it comes out and I'd rather leave them with the discomfort than with a slogan. If you think nothing survives, put that in the speech and explain why, and if you think the whole thing turns on some empirical question I could actually answer with a season's worth of fieldwork, I'd like the talk to say what that question is.
