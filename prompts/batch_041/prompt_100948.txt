I am a doctoral student in cognitive science, and I have been asked to lead a seventy-five-minute session of our department's interdisciplinary reading group on the frame problem. The audience is a mix of philosophy students and machine-learning students, and none of them have read the technical AI literature in which the problem was originally posed. I do not want an exposition of the problem itself; I want a plan for how to teach it. Tell me the order in which the ideas have to be introduced and why each depends on the one before it, the single example that should carry the whole session and what it must contain — and must conspicuously lack — for the problem to land as a philosophical difficulty rather than an engineering nuisance, the misconceptions you expect this particular mix of people to arrive with or to develop (I anticipate that the ML students will treat it as a question about computational cost, and I suspect the philosophers will hear it as a restatement of the problem of induction), and the precise point in the sequence at which each of those should be headed off. I also want the one question I can put to the room at the end that would distinguish someone who has actually grasped the problem from someone who has merely learned its name, and an explicit list of what I should decline to cover at this level and say out loud that I am declining.

Two constraints. First, the session has to bring the audience to the point where they see that the difficulty survives the standard formal repairs, without my walking them through the formal machinery itself. Second, the carrying example must not be one they have already met — if you reach for the robot-and-the-bomb story, half the room will nod along at a story they believe they already know. My own instinct is that the session should move from the technical statement of the problem to the epistemological one, and that the sequence should make that move feel forced rather than announced, but I am not attached to that arc if you think the dependencies actually run the other way.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate my question.
