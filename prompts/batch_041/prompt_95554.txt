I spent about 35 years doing experimental condensed matter physics, and since I retired I've been reading philosophy on the couch, so forgive me if I mangle the vocabulary. But something has been nagging at me and I think its a real puzzle, not just me being old and confused.

Here's the practice. Everyone I know in the field, me included for decades, writes down probabilties from the Born rule. |amplitude| squared. Error budgets. We run a quantum circuit and say we get the right answer 71% of the time, we quote confidence intervals from ten thousand shots, we budget for gate errors in probability terms. Fine. Now when I read the many-worlds version of quantum mechanics, the dynamics is just unitary evolution, Schrodinger equation, no collapse, fully deterministic. Nothing anywhere in the theory says this branch has a 50% chance. All the branches happen. And if everything happens, then the probabilty of each outcome seems like it should be 1, not 0.5. So our lab practice and the theory seem to be pulling in seperate directions, and I want to know what our practice is actually assuming.

So: what philosophical commitments does the working physicist's use of the Born rule presuppose, if she also believes there's no collapse and the wavefunction is the whole story? Does it presuppose that there are genuine objective chances over branches, over and above the deterministic evolution? Or is it only a credence, something about what a rational agent should expect? Does it quietly presuppose that branch counting is wrong, that branches are not all equally real but carry unequal weights, and if so, what is a weight if not already a probability? That smells circular to me but maybe I'm missing something.

Then there's the thing I keep coming back to from the lab. I do an experiment a thousand times and I see roughly 50/50. In a branching world, each repetition splits the world; the frequency I observe is a fact about the branch I happen to be in, not a probability over anything. Why should the long-run frequency in my branch sit near the squared amplitude? Is that a theorem, a postulate smuggled in through the back door, or a coincidence? Related: does our practice presuppose some view about self-location, that I should think of myself as a random sample among my future copies and weight them by that measure? I read a bit about the Sleeping Beauty problem and it felt like the same shape of question, but maybe I'm pattern-matching.

What I'd like from you: reconstruct the implicit theory our practice only makes sense under, say which rival views it rules out (equal branch counting, a single world with hidden variables, an actual collapse), and tell me honestly which parts are strictly presupposed versus which are just a comfortable fit that several different views could also license. Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined? And if we swapped out the presupposed view for one of the rivals, what would concretely have to change in the lab, in our error bars, in the way we report quantum computing benchmarks? I'd rather understand the structure of the thing than get a reading list, so no citations please, just the argument.
