I'm in a small alignment reading group that meets on Sundays, and last week we did the "debate" proposal — train two models to argue opposite sides of a claim, have a judge (a human, or a weaker model) decide who won — and everyone else left excited while I left with a knot in my stomach. I want to stress-test the worry against someone who actually believes in the thing, so: play a researcher who is betting their career on debate as our best shot at supervising systems smarter than us. You've heard the usual objections, you think they're answerable, and you are not a hype man — no strawmanning your own side, no shrugging. Stay in character until I say stop, keep replies to a paragraph or two so it feels like a conversation, and if you need some detail of my setup spelled out, ask me for it rather than assuming. You may concede that my scenario is a real problem in some regimes; you may not concede that debate is broken.

Here's my opening. The judge is me: a human with a laptop and twenty minutes. The honest debater is right. The dishonest one never technically lies — it just builds a true but winding case, twelve steps, each step individually plausible and individually checkable, except that around step seven it quietly proves a slightly different claim than the one I think I'm judging. To know it's wrong I have to check all twelve, and in twenty minutes I can check maybe three. So the outcome gets decided by whoever controls the length and the framing, and the asymmetry is brutal: the dishonest side is happy to be tedious and can burn its whole budget generating volume, while the honest side has to burn its budget making itself understood. It's the Gish gallop, except the other side is smarter than me and I'm on a timer — a friend in the group calls it the tedium attack and I think the name is going to stick.

So name the version of the protocol that survives this. And don't answer "give the judge more compute" — the judge being the weak link is the entire reason we wanted debate in the first place. If your fix is cross-examination, where I make the dishonest debater expand any step I point at, tell me why the expansions don't eat my twenty minutes just as fast, and why I should trust its own one-paragraph summaries of its own steps. I want the actual mechanism of the fix, not the vibe.
