Convene an adversarial collaboration on the socialist calculation debate and draft the joint statement its two parties could sign. The parties are the calculation camp in the tradition of Mises and Hayek, and the market-socialist camp in the tradition of Lange and Lerner. Both have agreed in advance to three stipulations, and the statement must show which of their remaining differences survive them. First, that the planning problem is to find a vector of shadow prices that clears markets under some specified welfare criterion, and that the abstract existence of such a vector is not in dispute. Second, that the parties must separate three distinct challenges and say, for each, whether it is a genuine crux between them: the static allocation problem (can the relevant information be gathered and the computation performed?), the dynamic discovery problem (can the system generate and revise knowledge of production possibilities, preferences, and innovation?), and the incentive problem (will agents act on the plan once it exists?). Third, that they may take modern computational and forecasting capacity as given but must state precisely what, if anything, that capacity changes.

As a pressure test, both parties have also agreed to consider one hypothetical and to say whether their disagreement survives it and, if so, in what form: an economy with granular, near-real-time data on production and consumption, together with a mechanism that makes truthful revelation of preferences incentive-compatible. A further sub-question to be addressed, and located within the three challenges rather than treated as a separate quarrel: whether prices function primarily as information carriers or as incentive devices, and whether the answer bears on the feasibility question at all.

Deliver the following. (a) A numbered section of claims both parties can sign, covering the nature and function of prices, the informational role of markets, the distinction between in-principle and in-practice feasibility, and what the debate has and has not established. (b) For each of the three challenges, the residual disagreement stated in wording each side would accept for its own view. (c) The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side — with, for each, a case, argument, or hypothetical that both parties agree bears on it, and each side's prediction about it recorded in advance. (d) A judgment about which parts of the dispute, if any, are verbal rather than substantive, for instance whether the parties mean the same things by efficiency, rationality, or feasible, and what would remain in dispute once the verbal parts are set aside. (e) For each side, the strongest consideration it would count as evidence against its own position. Also state, where a crux turns on a claim about tacit or inarticulable knowledge, whether that claim is being advanced as an empirical prediction or as a conceptual impossibility, since the two would be settled by different things.
