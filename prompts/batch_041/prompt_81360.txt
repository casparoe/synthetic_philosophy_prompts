I am a novelist, and for four years I have sat on the editorial board of the translation series at a small literary press — the body that awards our annual prize and also sets the house rules for the series. On Thursday the board votes on this cycle's prize, and I have to circulate a written opinion beforehand. Two translations of the same short poem are finalists, and they have resolved the poem's constraints in opposite ways. The source is a fifteen-line poem built on a refrain and on four recurring end-words that serve as its four key nouns; two of those words rhyme in the original only as homophones distinguished by spelling, and the poem's argument turns on the last line repeating the first with exactly one of the four words swapped. The first finalist keeps the line count, the refrain, and all four end-words as end-words, and finds a true rhyme in English for three of the four — but to do it she changes the speaker's gender, turns a refusal into an invitation, and pads two lines with filler; her cover letter says she would rather be wrong about the argument than about the music. The second keeps the speaker, the argument, and the one-word substitution, abandons rhyme, and lets the key nouns migrate to the middle of lines, where English word order will not let them land at the end; his letter says that a rhyme he cannot make honest he will not make at all. Meanwhile our series editor proposes that we stop choosing and publish both side by side in a facing-page edition, which would cost us one other title this year. One board member wants the first finalist disqualified as an imitation rather than a translation; another calls the second a prose summary with line breaks. Please draft the memo I will circulate: one recommendation — award it to the first, award it to the second, split the award, or approve the two-track edition and hold the prize this cycle — with reasoning the losing side can actually answer, a time frame tied to Thursday's vote and the catalog deadline, and, since the chair wants this cycle to settle the matter for future cycles, a short statement of the rule we should adopt about what counts as preserving a formal constraint in translation, including whether a constraint counts as preserved when it survives in the text but cannot be perceived by a reader who does not know the source language. I write my own books under hard formal constraints, so I have a stake in this and do not want the memo pretending otherwise; give me a defensible pick and a usable rule rather than a survey of translation theory.
