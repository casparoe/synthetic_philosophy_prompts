I am in a graduate seminar on Buddhist philosophy, and a classmate and I have spent two weeks circling an argument we cannot decide whether to keep or abandon. I would like you to identify the step to reject and tell me what rejecting it costs, because every step looks plausible to me on its own and the conclusion cannot be right. We reconstructed it as cleanly as we could:

(1) Nāgārjuna's tetralemma, applied to any phenomenon, rejects all four alternatives: that the phenomenon exists by its own nature (svabhāva), that it does not, that it both does and does not, and that it neither does nor does not.
(2) The statement that all phenomena are empty is itself a phenomenon — a conceptual construction, a view that someone holds and utters.
(3) So the tetralemma applies to it: we may not assert that this statement is ultimately true, that it is not, that it both is and is not, or that it neither is nor is not.
(4) In particular, we may not assert that the statement is ultimately true.
(5) But Madhyamaka does assert that emptiness is the ultimate truth — that is, that it is ultimately true that all phenomena are empty.
(6) So Madhyamaka is committed both to refusing and to making the assertion that its central claim is ultimately true; it is inconsistent.
(7) Suppose instead that Madhyamaka refuses to assert the ultimate truth of its own claim. Then the claim that all phenomena are empty is just one conventional view among others, with no special standing; and the Madhyamaka critique of rival views — that they reify — loses its point, since by the same token the Madhyamaka view reifies nothing less.
(8) Either way, Madhyamaka is either inconsistent or toothless.

My classmate believes the trouble is in (7), which he calls a false dilemma: he says a view can fail to be ultimately true yet still be better than its rivals at curing the reifying habit, and that this is all Madhyamaka ever claimed. I am not persuaded, because I do not see how being better at curing is anything but one more ultimate claim in disguise. I would most like to keep (1), (2), and (4), since I take both the tetralemma and the emptiness of views seriously; but if something has to go, I want to know exactly what the defender of Madhyamaka pays by giving it up, and whether (6) or (7) is the premise he is actually forced to deny. Please do not simply assure me that Madhyamaka survives this objection — I want a step-by-step diagnosis, with each step that looked fine explained, and the alternatives priced against one another.
