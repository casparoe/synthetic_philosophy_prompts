I've been caught up in a long argument with a friend about the French Revolution, and I'd like your help settling it properly rather than trading slogans. The specific episode I want to work through is the sequence of internal purges that runs from the fall of the Girondins in June 1793, through the guillotining of the Hébertists in the spring of 1794 and then of Danton and the Dantonists a few weeks later, to the fall of Robespierre himself on 9 Thermidor in July 1794 and his execution the following day. Treat that whole arc as a single episode and analyze it through the competing theories we keep invoking. First the class-struggle reading: identify the classes and class fractions the theory needs, say what each faction is supposed to be really fighting over at each stage, and then judge how much of the sequence the reading actually accounts for and where it has to lean on the political detail it claims to explain. Then do the same for the ideological or political-culture reading, on which the motor is a revolutionary discourse of virtue and conspiracy that manufactures its own enemies, and for the contingency reading, on which the purges turn on factional feuds, timing, and the personalities of individual deputies rather than on any deep logic. For each, be explicit about what it explains that the others cannot, and where it falls silent or smuggles in what it is supposed to derive.

The second thing I want is a reckoning with the claim that revolutions devour their children — a line people repeat as if it were a law of nature, when it seems to me to bundle together several quite different assertions. I'd like you to pull those apart and test the episode against each: whether it is meant as a causal law of revolutionary dynamics, as a selection effect on the sort of people who rise to lead a revolution, as a retrospective rhetorical trope that commentators impose on violence after the fact, or as a normative claim about the moral price of revolutionary politics. Using the purge sequence as the test case, tell me which of these the evidence supports, which it leaves untouched, and whether the thesis in any of its senses is the kind of thing that could be shown false — and, finally, whether the episode on balance confirms the thesis or undercuts it.
