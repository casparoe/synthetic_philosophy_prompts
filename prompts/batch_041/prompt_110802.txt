Title: Two Kinds of Rationality in the Absent-Minded Driver Problem

The standard absent-minded driver problem is usually presented as follows. A driver travels along a road with two exits. At the first exit, leaving yields payoff 0. At the second exit, leaving yields payoff 4. If the driver passes both exits, the payoff is 1. The driver cannot tell the exits apart when reaching them, and after passing the first exit cannot remember having done so. At each exit, the driver must choose to exit or continue without knowing whether it is the first or the second. The standard mixed-strategy analysis says the driver should exit with probability 1/3 at each opportunity, yielding an expected payoff of 4/3. Yet if the driver could precommit to a plan, the driver would choose to continue at the first exit and exit at the second, yielding 4. The problem is often taken to show that act-by-act expected utility maximization and plan-based expected utility maximization can diverge under imperfect recall.

I want to argue that the divergence is real but that it is often described too quickly. There are two different questions. First, what should the driver do at an exit, given the information available at that exit? Second, what plan should the driver adopt before starting, given that the plan will be executed without recall? The first question is act-relative. The second is plan-relative. The standard 1/3 answer is an answer to a third question: what stationary randomization over exit and continue maximizes expected payoff if the driver must use the same probability at every exit and cannot carry information between exits? That third question is not the same as either of the first two.

My proposal is that the absent-minded driver problem is best understood as revealing a gap between act-relative and plan-relative evaluation, but that the gap does not by itself show that act-relative evaluation is irrational. It shows that act-relative evaluation is incomplete when the agent's future selves cannot coordinate. The driver at an exit cannot make a binding choice for the other exit, and cannot even know whether the other exit has already been passed. So the driver's choice at an exit is not a choice about the whole trip. It is a choice about one node in a decision tree whose other nodes are controlled by relevantly similar but numerically distinct temporary agents.

I then want to introduce a variant to test this. Suppose the driver has a coarse memory signal. With probability q, the driver can correctly distinguish the first exit from the second. With probability 1-q, the driver cannot distinguish them and has the standard absent-minded experience. The signal is not under the driver's control and is not remembered after the exit. I claim that as q increases, the optimal policy changes in a non-obvious way. When q is high, the driver should exploit the signal and exit at the second exit when recognized. When q is low, the driver should fall back toward the standard stationary randomization. But there may be a middle range where the driver should randomize even when the signal says first exit, because exiting at the first exit is so bad that it is worth gambling on a later opportunity. I want to know whether this middle range actually exists under plausible parameters, and whether it undermines the idea that the driver's problem is fundamentally about coordination rather than about information.

The essay concludes that the absent-minded driver problem is not a paradox about rationality as such. It is a problem about the unit of rational evaluation. If we evaluate acts, the driver looks irrational for continuing at the second exit when the signal says second. If we evaluate plans, the driver looks irrational for exiting at the first. The resolution is to say that the unit of evaluation must be specified, and that different units can yield different recommendations without contradiction.

I would be grateful for comments on the following specific points. First, is my reconstruction of the standard problem accurate, especially the claim that the 1/3 policy is the answer to a stationary-randomization question rather than to the act-relative question? Second, is the distinction between act-relative and plan-relative evaluation stated clearly enough, or does it collapse into a distinction between ex ante and ex post evaluation? Third, does the coarse-memory variant actually produce the non-obvious middle range I describe, or does the optimal policy have a simpler form? Fourth, is the conclusion too deflationary? Does it leave the original problem without any philosophical bite? Finally, please suggest one or two concrete ways to tighten the argument, and identify any step where I am likely to be confusing a mathematical fact about expected value with a normative claim about rationality.
