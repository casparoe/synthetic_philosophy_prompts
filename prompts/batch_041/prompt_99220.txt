So here's my situation. I've got a grad student who's gotten wierdly attached to tranquilism — the view that the only thing that ultimately matters is the absence of craving and suffering: not pleasure, not preference-satisfaction, not even the presence of positive states. I don't blame her, it's a bracing view with a tidy structure. But every time we meet she asks what she should work on, and I keep handing her the standard menu (the experience machine, the repugnant conclusion, whether tranquility is even coherently describable), and I've started to think most of that is a waste of her three years.

So I want your help. Suppose you had one strong PhD student, three years, full time, no empirical fieldwork, and you wanted her to make the most progress on tranquilism as a philosophical position. What are the actually open questions, and which two or three should she take first? Defend the ordering — I care much more about the reasoning than the list. For each, tell me whether it's upstream of the others (i.e., whether other questions can't be settled until this one is), how tractable it is for someone with no math beyond intro logic, and what a real advance on it would even look like, because for some of these I honestly can't picture the deliverable.

Two specific things I'd like you to fold in, because I think they're where the action is and they don't get discussed enough:

First, there's a distinction I keep tripping over between craving as a *motivational* state (the wanting, the pull) and suffering as an *affective* state (the hurting). Tranquilism as usually stated bundles them — you're supposed to extinguish both — but I suspect a lot of the view's work is done by an unargued assumption that the two come as a package. If they can come apart (someone who craves intensely but never hurts, or hurts without craving), does tranquilism have anything to say about which one is the real bearer of value? And is that a question you'd rank high, or a distraction?

Second, uncertainty. Every version of the view I know is stated as if we already knew which states are which and how to compare them. Does tranquilism survive being held under uncertainty about the nature of craving itself — e.g., if you're not sure whether some state is a craving or just a strong preference, does the view still give action guidance, or does it collapse into something like a precautionary principle that no longer looks distinctively tranquilist?

Also tell me what would move a question up or down your list — what argument or evidence over the next couple of years should change my mind about the ranking. And if the honest answer is that tranquilism is a research dead end and she should work on something else, definately say so and say why; I'd rather hear that than a padded agenda.

Write it in American English, and don't bother with references or a reading list — she'll find the literature herself. I just want the agenda and the reasoning.
