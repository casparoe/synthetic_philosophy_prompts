Okay so I'm a novelist — through a series of poor life choices I've ended up teaching this upper-level seminar, it's cross-listed, half the room is from the philosophy department and half from my fiction workshop, and the theme is thinking machines and whether they can think. The final is next week and I have to write the essay question, and I'm a bit out of my depth on the technical side, so I want you to draft the whole package for me: the question, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

Here's what I want it to be about. Near the end of Turing's 1950 paper, the one with the imitation game, he has this strange little detour where he worries that telepathy could wreck the whole test — if the interrogator can read minds, or the machine can, then the results don't tell you what you think they tell you. His fix is basically to run the thing in a room shielded against telepathy, or to pick participants who aren't telepathic. I find this definately more interesting than the students do, and I don't want them just reciting that Turing mentioned ESP and it's sort of a joke. The question has to make them take the move seriously and see where it bites.

So roughly: make them reconstruct what Turing is actually worried about, in their own words, no page-and-verse; then have them decide whether his remedy is a principled methodological fix or a bit of ad hoc hand-waving; and then — this is the part I care about — get them to see that the ESP worry is a special case of a much more general problem: any test that judges a mind by its outputs can be gamed by a channel the test didn't mean to be measuring. I want them to build their own small present-day case of that, where a machine passes by exploiting a channel the test doesn't know it's measuring. Don't let them just say it looks the answer up — make them say what makes that structurally the same as, or different from, telepathy. Then I want them to say what it would take to close the hole in general, and whether that's even possible, or whether the question of whether a machine can think is one you can only answer relative to some background theory about which channels exist. That last bit is the deep end and I want a good student to get there.

Level: upper-level, mixed backgrounds, 45-minute in-class essay, closed book. It has to be answerable without the paper in front of them.

Deliverables. First the essay question itself, phrased so a smart student who never memorized the ESP section can still do well by thinking. Then a model answer, hard cap 700 words, that actually makes the moves instead of gesturing at them. Then a marking scheme, total 20, listing the specific moves a top answer has to make, the one move that seperates an A from a B (I'd guess it's the generalization step, where they see ESP as one instance of the gamed-channel problem — but tell me if I'm wrong), and a one-line note on how students typically botch each point. Also give me two or three things that should NOT earn credit even though they sound smart, the kind of confident filler that tempts a B student. Keep the whole thing tight, no waffle, and don't quote anything from the paper — paraphrase it, I can't stand fake-precise quotes.
