I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I've just written an essay I've got no business writing. Background: my last novel was set on a rewilding estate in the Borders, and while I was researching it I fell down a hole about whether wild animals actually have good lives. The upshot is a 3,000-word piece, currently sitting with a magazine that shall stay nameless until they say yes, arguing something that's already losing me friends in conservation: that destroying habitat isn't obviously bad for animals, and may in many cases be good for them. I'd like your comments before I send it back.

Here's the argument, in the order it appears.

1. The default assumption — that a thriving ecosystem is a good thing for the animals inside it — smuggles in a claim nobody ever defends: that the average wild animal's life is worth living. If it isn't, then more habitat means more animals, which means more suffering, and 'biodiversity' starts to look like a measurement of misery rather than a good.

2. But not all habitat is equal, and this is the part I'm most pleased with. I've been calling it the turnover ledger. Two seperate patches can hold the same biomass and wildly different welfare totals, depending on how fast the animals in them turn over. A hedgerow and rough grass will crank out thousands of short lives a year — voles, shrews, nestlings, insects — nearly all of which end in predation, exposure or starvation before the animal has done much of anything. Old-growth forest holds fewer animals, living longer. If you're adding up suffering, the hedgerow is doing vastly more of it per acre of 'nature'. The same bulldozer, pointed at one or the other, is either a catastrophe or a mercy.

3. Which means the usual conservation metrics — species richness, habitat area, biomass — are close to useless as proxies for welfare, and possibly anti-correlated with it. A habitat can be degraded in conservation terms and much better in welfare terms.

4. The uncomfortable corollary: the strongest case for keeping a given patch is that it holds long-lived animals whose lives are net positive — elephants, parrots, whales — and the strongest case for clearing it is that it's a machine for manufacturing doomed offspring.

5. Then I try to head off the obvious objection, that this licenses paving the planet. The turnover argument is conditional: it tells you nothing about what to do until you know the sign of a wild life, and the sign is genuinely unknown. So the honest conclusion is that the burden of proof in conservation should fall on showing that the animals in question have lives worth living, rather than on showing that destroying their habitat harms them.

What I want from you:

- Is the central move valid — from 'habitat destruction reduces animal numbers' to 'habitat destruction may reduce suffering'? Or am I quietly assuming something away, e.g. that preventing an animal from existing is morally free?
- Where does the turnover ledger break? I suspect it inverts somewhere. Long lives with terrible endings? Species whose lives are so bad that dying comes as a relief? Give me the counterexamples.
- Point 3 strikes me as the interesting one and point 4 as the glib one. Do you agree? What would a serious critic call the weakest link?
- Is there a version of this that isn't monstrous? I want the reader to feel the argument's pull without thinking I'm advocating for anything.
- And if I'm reinventing a wheel that's already rolling around in the academic literature, say so — I don't need a reading list, I just don't want to be the person who rediscovers something that was settled decades ago.

Be blunt, I can take it, and don't spare the biology — if I've got something flatly wrong, I'd rather be corrected now then in print. Write it in British English, please: my copy-editor is a stickler and I want to be able to paste your notes straight in.
