So my town just lost the mill — the big one, the one that was basically the reason the town existed — and my kid is in this year's graduating class at the high school, and they've asked me, since I teach history there, to give the commencement address. And I've spent the last three months reading about the Bronze Age collapse and the Maya and Easter Island, because I wanted to know whether there's an honest thing to say to these kids that isn't either "everything ends, get used to it" or "don't worry, communities are resilient." I think I've found the angle I want, but I need help actually writing it.

Here's the thing I want the speech to genuinely argue, not just gesture at. I want to pull apart two senses of collapse. One is when the people are gone — depopulation, famine, whatever. The other is when the people are still here, still farming, still showing up to stuff, but the thing that made them a society in the old sense has evaporated: the shared expectations about who owes what to whom, who's actually in charge, what's supposed to happen next year. My gut says the second one is the real one, and that most of the "collapses" we talk about — including whatever's happening here — are the second kind, and that this is somehow both less catastrophic and harder to mourn, because there's no ruins to point at. I don't actually know if that distinction holds up under pressure, and I'd rather the speech be honest about it straining than pretend it's a clean theory.

Two constraints. First, there are going to be maybe three or four people in that audience who actually know this material — my old advisor is coming, and a couple of the parents teach anthropology at the state school — and I do not want to be the guy who swings "collapse" around as a technical term in a way that makes them wince. So the speech shouldn't imply that archaeologists all agree on what collapse even means, or state as settled fact anything that's actually contested (the Easter Island story in particular is, as far as I can tell, a mess, so don't lean on it as a clean moral). Second, the other four hundred people in the room are families of kids who are probably leaving town for good, some of whom just lost their jobs last month, and none of them came here for a lecture about the Maya. It has to land as something said to them, at a graduation, on a Saturday morning in June.

No citations and no book titles — I'm not standing up there citing sources. Maybe five or six minutes. And please, whatever you do, don't end it on "the future is unwritten" or anything in that family.
