I'm an American who moved to Copenhagen two years ago for my partner's job, and I work in a mid-sized municipal office -- not government exactly, but adjacent enough that I deal with the same agencies the papers are always yelling about. Something keeps catching my eye that I don't have a word for, and it's bothering me enough that I'm asking strangers about it.

Here's the pattern. Where I'm from, the default assumption is that the person behind the counter will do the least they can get away with, so everything is built to catch them at it -- forms, countersignatures, an audit trail. Here the default is the opposite and the paperwork is wierdly thin. Fine, culture difference. But what I actually noticed is what happens to the *evidence*. When an official here gets caught doing something shady, everyone treats it as an exception, a bad apple, "that's not how it works here" -- the case never touches the general picture. When an official at home does something decent, refuses a favor, turns down a bribe, everyone back there treats *that* as the exception: you should see the rest of them. Same sort of event, opposite reading, and in both places the reading protects the frame it came from. So both frames look unfalsifiable, which is annoying but not yet interesting, because I don't think this is just ordinary bias -- the frames seem to *manufacture their own evidence*. The reason nobody steals my bike here is presumably that nobody steals bikes here. The reason the auditors back home keep finding things is that they're looking; suspicion means checking, checking finds something, and there's always something, so the something feeds the suspicion. Meanwhile my colleague's plain belief that the office is honest seems to be part of why people in it act honestly -- and she was genuinely a little hurt when I started locking my laptop in a drawer, which is what really got me, because it means even *voicing* the suspicion is costly here, so there's no cheap way to go collect the evidence that would tell me whether my attitude is calibrated.

The part I can't get past is that you can't run the experiment. You can't have a society hold the suspicious attitude for five years just to see whether the trust was warranted, because holding it changes what's there to be measured, and the same in reverse. So the comparison I actually want -- which of these two attitudes is the more reasonable one to have -- looks structurally unavailable to me, and yet my American friends and my Danish colleagues both think the answer is obvious, which I find suspicious in its own right. I know there's old sociology about self-fulfilling prophecies and about places getting stuck in a high-trust or low-trust groove, and that's close but I don't think it's the thing, because a self-fulfilling prophecy is about a belief causing the outcome it predicts, whereas here the belief seems to fabricate the *evidence* for itself, so it can't be scored against the world at all, and the more carefully I reason about it the more I'm just restating the attitude I walked in with. Is there a name for this specific configuration -- a standing disposition that produces the observations that would justify it, so its accuracy can't be assessed from the inside, and where calling it a bias seems to miss the point because the bias is load-bearing? If there's an established term, which one, and how exactly does it differ from self-fulfilling prophecy and from the just-high-trust story, since neither of those seems to capture the evidence-making part. If there isn't, coin one -- define it sharply, tell me what would and wouldn't be an instance (is this the same shape as a placebo, or as the way a confident person's confidence gets read as competence?), and tell me where the edges are. I'd much rather have the carving than the label.
