I'm putting together a longish interview for a student reading group — I'm the one asking the questions, and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends Kant's answer in the murderer-at-the-door case. Not the watered-down "well, Kant meant something subtler" version: she genuinely thinks that if the would-be killer asks you where his intended victim is, you are not permitted to lie to him, full stop, and that the shudders everyone does at this are a failure of nerve. I've read enough to know the usual moves, so I dont want questions that just let her restate the position or that invite her to dunk on a strawman. I want to make it hard.

A few things I want baked in. First, I have my own angle I want to test on her and I'd like the questions to build toward it: I think the duty of truthfulness is better understood as something owed to the practice of asking and telling — a shared instrument, a public good — rather than to the particular person you're talking to. If that's right, then whether the murderer "has a right to the truth" or has forfeited it drops out as irrelevant; what's wrong with a lie is that it degrades an instrument everyone has to rely on, including people hiding from murderers. So I want questions that make her either adopt that framing or explain why the identity of the hearer matters if the real damage is to the practice. Second, press the "silence escape hatch": if she's allowed to just refuse to answer, or to say something true-but-misleading ("I haven't seen him today" when he's in the cellar), then I want to know whether her rigorism has any teeth left or is really just a rule about words. Third, a friend of mine staffs a domestic-violence hotline and is trained to give technically-true but misleading answers rather than lie — I'd like her to grade that practice. Fourth, get her on the asymmetry where Kant (I think) says the friend who tells the truth isn't answerable for the murder, but the friend who lies and thereby sends the killer somewhere else is — I want to know if that's a real theory of responsibility or a patch. And fifth, the possibility that the whole essay is about right rather than ethics, i.e. about what the law may coerce and not about what you ought to do — if so I want her to say whether that shrinks her claim to something much smaller than the scandal suggests.

Give me 8 to 10 questions, in sections, in this order: "Setting the stakes" (one or two questions that force her to state exactly what she's committed to and what she'd have to give up to abandon it); "Silence and the misleading truth" (two or three); "My practice-of-truth challenge" (two or three); "Who's on the hook for the murder" (one or two); "What would move you" (one closing question about what evidence or argument could actually get her to drop the view). For each question, add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give — one or two lines each, and flag the answer you're anticipating. Keep it conversational in tone but don't waste questions on warm-up or "how did you come to philosophy" stuff.
