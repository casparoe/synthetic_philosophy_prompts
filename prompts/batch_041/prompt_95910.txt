I am a high school student writing a paper for my school's philosophy club, and I would like you to act as an expert philosopher of mind — someone who has thought carefully about the metaphysics of consciousness and, just as importantly, about when a thought experiment does honest work and when it smuggles in its conclusion. Please begin by brainstorming several candidate principles before you commit to any one of them, and please reason step by step rather than reaching for the first tidy answer.

My question is whether the notion of qualia — the raw felt qualities of experience, the redness of red, the sting of a toothache — is a coherent idea or a confusion. I want to answer it by looking at my own verdicts on a series of cases rather than by appealing to authority. I have no library and no internet access, so please do not send me to the literature or give me citations; argue from the cases themselves.

Here are my verdicts. I have tried to report them honestly rather than consistently.

1. Two retired coffee buyers have tasted the same beans for thirty years, and both now say coffee does not taste the way it used to. One is sure the coffee has changed; the other is sure the coffee is unchanged and his own palate has dulled. No test could settle it: their reports about their own experience are the only evidence either way, and they agree about everything else. I think there is a real difference between these two men, and that it is not merely a difference in what they are willing to say. It makes sense to ask which of them is right.

2. Suppose instead that a man's palate has drifted so gradually over a decade that he never once noticed a change. I think there is still a fact of the matter about whether coffee tastes different to him now than it did ten years ago — a fact that nothing in his life could ever reveal to anyone, including him.

3. Two people are wired from birth so that the colour one sees when looking at a ripe tomato is what the other sees when looking at grass, and vice versa, with no behavioural difference of any kind. I do not think there is a fact of the matter here. The claim that his red is my green is not false; it is empty. I cannot imagine what would count as making it true.

4. A drug makes everything taste sweeter for an hour. Here I think we can say, without any mystery, that his experience changed and the food did not, and this seems to me a perfectly ordinary thing to say.

5. A device records the exact pattern of activity in your brain while you eat a mango and, a year later, reproduces that pattern in you while you eat nothing. I think you really do taste the mango. The replay is not a description of a taste; it is the taste.

6. A colour scientist who has spent her whole life in a grey room and knows every physical fact about colour vision steps outside and sees a ripe tomato for the first time. I think she learns something she could not have known before, and that this is not merely a new skill or a new word.

7. A machine behaves exactly as a person in pain behaves — flinches, withdraws, says that it hurts — and does so for exactly the reasons a person does, with nothing in its workings left unexplained. I cannot make sense of there being a further fact about how it feels for the machine. If nothing in its causes, behaviour, or internal workings is left out, then I do not think anything is left out.

My working principle has been this: a claim about the felt character of an experience is meaningful only if I can say what it would make a difference to — to what I can discriminate, to how I behave, to what causes it — and a difference in raw feel that could never make a difference to anything is no difference at all. But this principle seems to force me to abandon verdict 2, and perhaps verdict 1 with it, and I am not sure I am willing to.

So: is there a principle that saves all seven verdicts, or a simpler one that overturns some of them and is the better for it? Please state your principle or small set of candidates precisely, name the case that motivates each clause, and say what it predicts for at least two cases I have not listed — for instance a dog's experience of smell, a person blind from birth who gains sight at forty, someone under general anaesthetic, or two wine tasters who use the same word for a wine while behaving differently. Tell me plainly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and say whether the word qualia is doing any work at all in the view you end up with, or whether I should drop it and say something else instead. If you think I have described two of my cases as alike when there is a relevant difference between them, say what the difference is rather than splitting the difference.
