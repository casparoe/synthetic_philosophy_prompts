I write a sort of ideas/science column for a business-y readership — LinkedIn brain meets New Yorker ambitions, minus the fact-checking budget — and for the next one I want to run a parody, submitted as if it were a genuine guest column, of a productivity/life-coaching guru who has adopted Zorn's lemma as his entire method. Deadpan all the way through: no winking, no "of course this is silly," just let him sell it. Maybe 600 words, headline included, first-person, in the voice of the coach himself (invent him — give him a name, a brand, a beach).

The jokes have to come from the actual mathematics, so that a set theorist snorts instead of wincing. That means: he treats your life as a partially ordered set (better/worse, not just different), your streak of steady improvements as a chain, and he insists — this is his big selling point — that every chain of yours has an upper bound *inside* the set, i.e. some life you could actually be living that's at least as good as everything on the chain. From which, he says, it follows that a maximal life exists, and you can stop looking for the best one. He should be honest, even proud, that his method never hands you the maximal life, only proves one is there — he markets this as "we don't do the work for you, we prove the work is doable." Let him confuse nothing, but let his clients confuse maximal with maximum, and have him correct them smugly.

Good in-the-weeds touches to work in, all of which he gets right: the maximal life needn't be unique and needn't be better than every other life; he only ever needs to show you can always extend a chain a little, never that you can extend it forever; he mentions that his whole system rests on a choice principle that some of his more constructivist critics refuse to sign, and he waves this off as a personality difference. Maybe give him a rival coach who sells the well-ordering method — a guaranteed best life for everyone, but you have to have your entire preference order fixed in advance — and a beef with her. Also give him a numbered "five steps" list where each step is secretly just a restatement of the lemma's hypotheses.

A reader who's never heard of the lemma should still find it funny as self-help parody; a mathematician should find it funny because it's accurate. And somewhere in there he should half-accidentally say the one true thing about his own method, without noticing that he's said it.
