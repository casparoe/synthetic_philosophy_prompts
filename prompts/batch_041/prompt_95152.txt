I lead the engineering team behind a digital advance-care-planning tool, and in three weeks I am presenting to the clinical ethics committee of the hospital trust we work with. I am a software engineer by training: no medical degree, no law degree, no philosophy degree. I want to walk in ready for the questions this particular room will actually ask, not the ones a room full of engineers would ask.

Act as a philosopher who works in clinical ethics — someone who regularly sits on committees like this one, knows the literature on autonomy, personal identity and decision-making for people who have lost capacity, and is practised at putting it into plain language for non-specialists. Before you draft anything, think carefully and start by brainstorming: write a short list of what each member of this committee is likely to believe, what they are afraid of, and what each of them would count as a decisive objection. Build the prep sheet from that list. Explain any piece of philosophical or legal vocabulary the first time you use it, in a clause, the way you would explain it to me.

My argument, in two parts.

First, the substantive claim. An advance directive made by someone with capacity should normally govern their care after they lose it, even when the person they become would now say otherwise. I do not rest this on the idea that the earlier person and the later person are literally the same agent; I rest it on the idea that the earlier person was entitled to set the terms of a risk they were certain to run, and that the later person's contrary preference only has authority if it is a revision of the earlier person's reasons rather than a loss of the capacity to grasp them. If it is the latter, I want to say that the later preference is not an exercise of autonomy at all and should not displace the directive.

Second, the mechanism my team has built into the product, which is the part of the talk the committee will really be judging. The tool (a) records the directive as a versioned document, so that any change of mind shows up as a change with a date attached; (b) requires the user to nominate an interpreter, a person authorised to say what the directive means in situations the user did not foresee; (c) sets checkpoints, so that a directive lapses unless the user reaffirms it while they still have capacity, at intervals the user chooses; and (d) applies a test I call the revision/erosion test before allowing a later preference to override a directive.

The audience, as precisely as I can describe it:

- the chair, a palliative care consultant who has watched families fight over exactly this and who thinks documents are a way of avoiding the conversation that actually matters;
- a geriatric psychiatrist who has spent thirty years with people in moderate and severe dementia and who is, I expect, sceptical that someone who can no longer grasp their own earlier reasons is being done any favour by being held to them;
- a clinical ethicist with a philosophy background who will press the difference between the interests a person has in the shape of their life as a whole and the interests they have in comfort and company here and now, and who will ask me which of the two my tool is really protecting;
- a solicitor from the trust's legal team, who will care about who is liable when the interpreter and the clinical team disagree, and about whether a lapsed checkpoint leaves us exposed;
- a lay member, a former carer, who I suspect will speak for the person in the bed and ask why the person in front of us does not get a vote;
- and a junior doctor who will ask what happens at three in the morning on a ward when nobody can reach the interpreter.

Assume they are all competent, sceptical and short of time; assume two of them have already decided against me.
