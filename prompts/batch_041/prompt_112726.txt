I teach a 90-minute upper-level seminar on Rousseau's social and aesthetic thought, with 18 students, and I want a classroom activity that makes them feel the force of his case against bringing a theater to Geneva rather than recite it. The trap I want to avoid is the summary they can all produce before the session begins — theater corrupts, festivals are wholesome. What I want them to hit is the structural claim underneath, which is harder to state and much harder to feel: that a theater does not merely entertain badly but arranges people so that each of them is alone with a feeling that costs nothing and is owed to no one; that the actor's trade consists in manufacturing and discarding feeling on demand, so that the audience's access to what the actor actually feels is severed as a condition of the performance; and that Rousseau's alternative is not a better entertainment but a different arrangement of persons, one in which nobody is permanently a spectator and everyone is exposed to everyone else's view.

Design the activity so the group runs two short "public evenings" back to back — a theatrical one and a festival one — staged in the form of the thing itself rather than debated, so that the difference lands in their bodies before anyone names it. Give me rules, materials, timings, physical setup, roles, and a debrief that maps specific moments of the two rounds onto the three claims above. Be frank in the debrief about what the activity cannot reproduce and that I must therefore supply in words: Rousseau's argument about scale — why the theater belongs to a large state and the festival to a small one — is nearly impossible to stage with eighteen people in one room, so tell me how to hand it over verbally and what it will consequently fail to feel like.

Two further constraints, which are why I'm asking rather than improvising this myself.

First, the theatrical round must include a paid performer. One student receives a small sum of tokens to produce a specified emotion on cue for the whole round; partway through, I privately halve their pay without telling the audience, and they must keep the performance indistinguishable. Afterwards the audience is asked whether they can tell what the performer actually felt, and I want them to notice that the question has no answer available to them.

Second, in the theatrical round the spectators must be unable to see one another's faces, and at the climax each is handed a sealed note and told to write down what they would give up to undo the staged wrong — before being told that nothing is owed and nothing can be done. In the festival round, by contrast, nobody may watch for more than thirty seconds without having just been watched, and nobody may opt out. Tell me how to manage the transition between rounds so it doesn't read as an obvious "and now the good one."

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — where students will break frame, which round will collapse, and how to handle the moment when someone insists the theatrical round was simply more fun.

Keep the entire deliverable under 1,100 words, and the debrief itself under 200 of them.
