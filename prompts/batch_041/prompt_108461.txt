I'm a philosophy prof and I've been dragooned onto a provincial advisory panel on population policy — the usual setup, a shrinking province, a minister who wants a recommendation on whether to spend a fixed pot of money on pro-natalist subsides (child bonuses, cheap childcare, the whole apparatus) or on making life better for the people already here (housing, health, schooling). Two of my co-pannelists are sharp people I like, and they've been going at each other in the margins of every meeting, and I've stopped trying to referee because I keep losing track of what exactly they disagree about. So write me a dialogue between them. Ruth is a political philosopher and she's the pronatalist. Don't make her a simple "more people equals more total welfare" utilitarian — that's the easy target and it isn't her view. Her real line is that the goodness of a life is a reason to bring that life about, not merely a reason to be glad it happened once it's here, and that the popular intuition on the other side (that it's bad to create a miserable person but not good to create a happy one) is an asymmetry that has to be earned rather than assumed; she's also got a non-welfarist string to her bow, roughly that a larger population is a larger space of minds exploring and inventing, and that this is valuable in a way that doesn't reduce to the sum of individual happiness. She should also press the non-identity thing at some point — that the people who'd exist under the subsidy are differnt people, not the same people plus extras, so Marek can't just rest on "no one is made worse off." Marek is the skeptic. He shouldn't be a Malthusian caricature and his objection shouldn't be "we'll run out of food" — I want his best objection to survive even if we stipulate that every extra person would live a clearly worthwhile life on a full stomach. My own guess is that his strongest line is about the shape of the value function rather than its inputs, but you may have a better idea. Give him the "more now versus more ever" point too: a person born this year uses up the wherewithal for whoever might have existed later, so "a larger population" is ambiguous between a bigger total across all of time and a bigger total at any given moment, and the argument for the first doesn't obviously support the second.

A few things I care about. Neither of them should be a strawman: whenever one of them has the weaker move, the other should have the comeback a competent person would actually make, and they should answer each other's actual points rather than sliding past. Let them concede where a real person would, and let them push each other somewhere neither expected — I'd much rather have an honest "huh, I hadn't separated those two questions before" than a tidy victory for either side. I want them to eventually locate the crux: is this a dispute about total versus average value, about whether the person-affecting restriction is right, or about whether people are being valued as ends or as containers for value? And I'd like them to notice, at least once, that the policy question they're supposedly advising the minister on is a different question from the philosophical one they're actually having, and that settling the second may not settle the first.

No citations, no reading lists, no "as so-and-so argues" — the panel doesn't need a bibliography, it needs the argument. A page or so, eight to twelve exchanges, and let the last word be genuinely up for grabs. (The meetings are catered and the sandwiches are terrible; use that or don't.)
