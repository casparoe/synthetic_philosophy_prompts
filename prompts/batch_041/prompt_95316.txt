I spent my working life as an actuary and now read a great deal of academic history, mostly of revolutions and financial collapses, and I keep running into a pattern in how these books explain their events that I have never found a name for.

Here is the shape of it. A historian will list five causes of an outcome and will say of each one, often in as many words, that the outcome would have occurred even had that cause been absent: had the harvest been adequate, the fiscal crisis alone would have done it; had the fiscal crisis been handled competently, the harvest alone would have done it. Every item on the list is individually removable. Yet the same book will insist that the outcome was contingent and could have gone otherwise, and it will rank the causes anyway, naming one the deepest cause and another the immediate trigger, as though the five were not on a par after all. In my old trade, when two independent conditions each lead to the same outcome, we do not claim to know which one did the work.

What puzzles me most is what happens when one of the five is refuted. I have read a book that demonstrated at length that one of the standard causes was simply not operative. I expected the explanation to shrink by a fifth. It did not shrink at all. The remaining four were quietly re-weighted, and the explanatory force of the account was exactly what it had been before. It is as though a conservation law governs the business: causal weight is neither created nor destroyed, only redistributed among whatever causes remain standing.

There is a third feature, which may be the same one or may be something else. In several of these lists, items presented as independent causes turn out to be a single development wearing different clothes; a shift in military technology enters the list three times, as better artillery, as new tactics, and as obsolete fortifications. If that is what is happening, then the reason no cause can be shown to be necessary is not that the world supplied backups. It is that the list was padded with correlated items, and since one never occurs without the others, the claim that the outcome would have happened anyway is untestable rather than true.

So: does this have a name? I have come across the word overdetermination, but I cannot tell whether it names what I am describing or something narrower, and I have the impression that the philosophy of causation has built machinery for this that historians mostly do not use. What I want is not the label but the carving. What precisely is the structure I am looking at, and which parts of it are claims about the world as against artifacts of how explanations get written? How does it differ from the nearest concepts that do have names, and which of those fits best? Is the ranking of causes in such cases defensible, or is it a rhetorical residue left over once the redundancy is acknowledged? If nothing established covers it, coin a term, but define it sharply enough that I can tell, for any given historical account, whether it exhibits the thing or not.
