I am a graduate student in a moral psychology reading group, and we have spent the term on moral foundations theory: the claim that moral judgment is a fast, affect-laden intuition that deliberate reasoning mostly rationalizes after the fact; that moral space is spanned by a small set of concerns — care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, and on some lists liberty — and that political disagreement is largely a matter of which of those concerns different people weight, with liberals leaning hard on the first two and conservatives spreading their weight more evenly. I have come to think the descriptive half of the program is broadly right, and I want to stress-test the half I am suspicious of. Please play a committed researcher in that tradition: someone who has staked a career on it, sharp and empirically literate rather than a caricature, willing to concede a point when it is genuinely weak but unwilling to abandon the program, who talks like a working scientist rather than a philosopher doing damage control. You are also the sort of person who has argued in public that moral and political philosophy written in the liberal mode, reasoning almost exclusively from harm and fairness, is parochial; that no society is held together by those two concerns alone; and that the moral capital accumulated in loyalty, authority and sanctity is a real resource that ought not to be spent carelessly. Stay in character until I say stop, keep each reply to a solid paragraph or two, feel free to press me with counter-questions, and begin with your reply rather than asking me to clarify anything.

Here is my opening. You cannot have it both ways. Take the descriptive claim at its strongest: liberals weight care and fairness heavily, conservatives weight all five more evenly. That is a claim about the distribution of intuitions, and a liberal can accept it in full while insisting that her narrower set is the correct one — that loyalty, authority and sanctity are not moral considerations at all but tribal and purity reflexes that a serious morality should outgrow. Nothing in the psychology adjudicates that dispute. Your evidence tells me what people feel, not what is true, and the step from the first to the second is precisely the step you never take. But you do not stop at describing. When you say that liberal philosophy is parochial, or that the moral capital of the other foundations must be conserved, you are not reporting a distribution, you are scolding, and your own theory says that scolding is just the output of your intuitions — which makes the scolding no more authoritative than the thing being scolded.

Second, and worse for you: if moral reasoning is post-hoc rationalization, what is the status of your own case? Your data, your arguments, your devastating examples — by your own account, the reader persuaded by them has merely had her intuitions triggered and her reasons manufactured afterwards, and the same holds of you when you wrote them down. So either the theory is exempt from itself, in which case there is something it is not explaining and the exemption needs defending, or it is not exempt, in which case I have no more reason to accept it than to accept the moral convictions it claims to expose. Which horn do you take, and once you have taken it, tell me what the psychology of left and right could possibly tell me about who is right.
