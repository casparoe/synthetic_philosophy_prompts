I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy, and I have run into a problem that my training has not prepared me to handle. My aunt, a woman of considerable practical wisdom, has been diagnosed with an aggressive cancer. She has met the news not with despair but with a kind of calm bookkeeping: she speaks of the illness as a balance she is finally settling, and she has begun to keep a notebook in which she lists the people she believes she wronged decades ago, estimating what she calls the "interest" each old debt has accrued. She shares this practice with a small group of fellow patients, who compare balances and trade accounts of what they think they are paying for. She says it is the only thing that makes the disease bearable, and I believe her.

Her daughter—my cousin—finds the whole business intolerable. She says it amounts to accepting that some just power is punishing her mother for old and forgivable mistakes, and worse, that it implies her own future misfortunes would be deserved as well. She has asked me, as the family's resident philosopher, to talk my aunt out of it, and I find that I cannot. Part of me wants to say the belief is simply false, and that a false belief doing emotional work is still false; another part suspects that dismantling it would be an act of cruelty dressed up as rigor, and that the question of whether it consoles is not the same as the question of whether it is true.

What I need is not consolation but a way to think. Is my aunt's interpretation of her illness one that I should challenge, indulge, or leave alone? How should I weigh the fact that her framework assigns suffering to its sufferer against the fact that it evidently helps her carry it? And is there any principled way to distinguish a piece of moral accounting that is doing honest work from one that ought never to have been applied to illness in the first place?
