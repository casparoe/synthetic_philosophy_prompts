Ok so I'm a novelist, mid-way through a book, and I need a scene or a whole short story out of you — but let me explain the thing properly first, because the easy version of this is boring and I don't want the easy version.

Everyone knows the complaint that machine translation is "shallow" — that it's surface statistics with no understanding behind it, that a system can hand you a perfectly fluent rendering while missing the point entirely. I broadly buy it. But as a premise for fiction it's too soft, isn't it, because "the machine doesn't really understand" is just the machine being a machine, and no reader is surprised. So here's the twist I want you to take seriously and not just decorate with: I think translation always leaves a residue. There's something in the source that the target language can't hold, and a good human translator is someone who feels that residue and writes *around* it — you can hear the strain, the slight awkwardness, the little apology in the prose. The machine doesn't feel it. It rounds the residue off so smoothly you never learn it was there. The machine isn't wrong. Its translations are frictionless. That's the horror of it.

So write me a short story — call it 2,000 words, whatever it needs — built on that. Some things I want in it, and one or two things I definitely don't.

Want: a machine translation system and a human translator rendering the *same* passage, where the machine's version is better by every standard anyone can name (more fluent, more accurate, more idiomatic, preferred by every reader you put in front of it), and the human's is worse and yet truer. I want a scene where two people argue about whether a particular translation was faithful, and neither can prove their case — the argument has to be genuinely undecided, not a strawman the human wins. I want one moment where somebody tries to say what the missing thing actually *is*, out loud, and fails, and we feel the failure as a fact about language and not just about them.

Don't want: the machine turning sinister, gaining consciousness, or secretly scheming. No Terminator stuff, no twist where the AI was alive all along. The machine should stay exactly as dull and competent as a very good autocomplete, and that should somehow be worse. And don't let the human "win" — no last word, no vindication.

Write it in British English please. Start with the story itself, straight in, no preface and no commentary around it — I don't want a note explaining what you were going for, just the piece. And go easy on the epigrams; my last reader said I have a problam with characters who talk like they've read too much philosophy, so let the ideas live in what happens.
