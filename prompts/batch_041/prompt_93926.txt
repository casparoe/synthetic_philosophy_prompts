I want to state a position as sharply as I can so someone can tell me what would kill it. I've been chewing on this for a couple years and I've basically stoped believing the Hofstadter take on machine translation — that it produces fluent output without any grasp of what the sentences are actually about — or at least I've stopped believing it says what people think it says.

Here's my position. What we mean by understanding a text just is being able to do the thing a good translator does: judge which features of the source are load-bearing for *this* purpose, and render accordingly. So a system understands the source if (i) its renderings are apt across a wide range of texts and purposes, (ii) that aptness is robust to perturbation — change the purpose and it tracks the change, change the source in a way that matters and the output changes accordingly, and (iii) its failures are the kind of failures a thoughtful human might make rather than the kind a lookup table makes. Not "simulating understanding," not understanding in some thin or deflationary sense. Understanding. On this view the shallowness critique is either an empirical claim about coverage and robustness (fine, that's testable) or a claim about the route the system took to get there (which is a claim about mechanism, and mechanism claims about the right kind of representation have a bad track record).

Two things I want to pre-empt. Someone will say a translator doesn't need to know whether the text is true, so subject-matter understanding obviously isn't required — fine, I'm making a claim about grasp of the text's point, not about knowledge of the world. And I know "apt for the right reasons" is doing a lot of work and might be circular: if the only way to tell whether the reasons were right is to look at more behavior, maybe I've just built a bigger behaviorist box.

Some cases I think my view has to get right, and I'd like you to stress-test it with them. (a) A text where the point is that something *can't* be translated — a novel scene where a character's failure to render a word is the whole event — where a system maximizing adequacy will smoothly translate the word and destroy the point. (b) A deliberately ambiguous document, like an internal memo whose author wants deniability, where the right rendering preserves the ambiguity even though resolving it would read better. (c) A pun or idiom where the correct move is to substitute a *different* joke rather than approximate the original one, which seems to require knowing what the joke is for.

So: what would count against my position? Rank the defeaters from decisive to merely awkward, and say which ones a defender of the shallowness thesis would concede and which they'd just deny. Has my position been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that actually a problem for a claim of this kind? The thing that would personally move me most is a case where the output is apt by my lights but there's an independent, non-question-begging reason to say it didn't understand — if you can construct one, that's the crux.

Last thing: please seperate what's actually settled here from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this. I don't want a contested reading of what "understanding" amounts to presented as if everyone agrees on it.
