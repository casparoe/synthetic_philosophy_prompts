I have no philosophical training — I work as a hospice aide, and I've spent a lot of the past few years sitting with people in pain and thinking on my own about what we are actually measuring when we measure it. I'd like you to act as a philosopher who has thought hard about the nature of pleasure and pain and about medicine's attempts to quantify them, and to walk me carefully through something that has been bothering me. Take some time first to lay out for yourself the different things people have meant when they called an experience pleasant or painful, and only then start to answer.

Here is the practice that puzzles me. Where I work, every patient is asked to put their pain on a number from zero to ten, and that number goes into the chart next to pulse, temperature, and blood pressure; the nurses treat it as a vital sign. It decides who gets seen first on a busy ward, whether a dose goes up, whether a patient is "controlled" enough to be sent home. One single number is meant to cover a migraine, a bedsore, bone cancer, and the dull ache of an old back injury. Nobody ever tells you whether the number means how intense the sensation is or how much it distresses you, and I have never met two nurses who agreed. And the same people who use the scale will tell you, in the next breath, that pain is subjective and that you cannot compare one person's pain with another's — while the whole apparatus depends on the numbers being comparable enough to move a patient up or down a queue, to argue that a drug "works," to announce that someone's pain is now a three and was an eight.

What I want to know is what this practice has to assume about pain, and about pleasure and pain more generally, for it to make any sense at all. Does it require that all these pains share some single common quality, one thing that varies only in how much of it there is? Or could it survive a looser view — that "pain" names a family of quite different experiences that happen to move us in similar ways? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out, for example the view that the burning of a burn and the grief of a bereavement have nothing in common but the name? I'd like you to reconstruct the theory the practice is committed to, separate what it strictly needs from what it merely happens to fit well with, and then tell me what would have to change on the ward if we took seriously the idea that these experiences are not one thing. Would the number have to go, or only be broken into several? And where is the practice simply underdetermined, so that more than one theory of pleasure and pain would license it just as well?

Please write to me as one person writing to another, in continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. And if it turns out that the practice is not so much underdescribed as outright incoherent, say so plainly.
