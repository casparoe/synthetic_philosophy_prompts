I'm in a grad seminar on population ethics, and last week we did Benatar's asymmetry — the four claims about pain, pleasure, and the absence of each. The professor says the asymmetry is the load-bearing beam in the whole "coming into existence is always a harm" argument, and he set us the challenge of seeing whether it proves too much. I've built a chain that I think shows it does, and now I honestly can't tell if I've caught something real or just talked myself in circles. I'd like you to take the role of a philosopher who works specifically on population axiology and is known for being careful — start by brainstorming the available moves, then commit. Here's the chain; I numbered the steps so you can just point at one.

(1) Take any possible person whose life would contain at least some pain, even if it also contained alot of pleasure. By the asymmetry, it would have been better for that person never to have existed: the pain they'd have had is absent, and that absence is good even though there's no one around to enjoy it; the pleasure they'd have had is also absent, but that absence isn't bad, because there's no one who's being deprived. (2) So for every possible person with any pain in their life, their nonexistence is better than their existence. (3) Now imagine a world — call it Empty — in which no sentient being is ever conceived, ever, human or animal or otherwise. Every pain that anyone would ever have felt is absent, and by the asymmetry that's good; every pleasure is absent too, and by the asymmetry that's not bad. (4) Compare Empty with Bliss, a world holding a trillion beings living long, near-perfectly happy lives with barely a twinge of suffering each. (5) Running (2) being by being, the nonexistence of each of the trillion is better than their existence, and Empty's absences of pain are all goods that nothing on Bliss's side outweighs, since Bliss's pleasures don't get to count as compensating anything. (6) So Empty is better than Bliss, and better than our actual world by the same reasoning. (7) And if a state of affairs is better, we have at least some reason to want it, and, where we can, to move toward it — so we have reason to prefer, and to work for, a universe with no sentient life in it at all.

That last step is where I get off the bus. I'm fine following Benatar as far as "don't have kids," but I don't believe I'm committed to wanting the stars to go dark, and I doubt he'd accept it either. So either I've slipped somewhere or the asymmetry costs more than it looks like it does. Tell me which step to give up, and be specific about the mechanism — don't just tell me the asymmetry is ad hoc and leave it there. Then price it: what do I have to give up to reject that step, and does the antinatalist conclusion survive the repair? I'd most like to keep (1) and the asymmetry itself if there's any way to. One more thing — keep the settled parts of this separate from the parts that people who actually work on it are still fighting about; I don't want a contested patch handed to me as if it were the consensus fix.
