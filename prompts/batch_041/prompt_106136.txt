I run a monthly discussion night at our community center -- we're not academics, just a bunch of people who like argueing about big ideas over coffee -- and next month I'm doing a session on the idea that a whole crowd of people guessing can somehow be smarter than any single one of them. I read about this Condorcet jury theorem in some pop book about crowds and I've been chewing on it ever since. I want to set the group one written question to take home and bring back, with a model answer I can hand out and a marking scheme so we can all score each other's papers. I'm the one who picked the topic, so I guess I'm the examiner.

Here's what I want the question to do. I don't want people to just write back something like "Condorcet proved that big groups get it right" -- that's the bumper sticker version and everybody already remembers it. I want the question to force them to actually say what the theorem assumes and then watch the assumptions break one at a time. Please build it around these three pressures, which is where I got stuck when I tried to think it through myself:

1. Independence. As I understand it, the theorem needs each person's chance of being wrong to be their own private thing. So what happens if a big chunk of the crowd is wrong in the same direction because they're all getting their information from the same one newspaper, or they all take their cue from the loudest person in the room? My gut says the crowd then isn't 999 voters, it's one voter wearing 999 hats, but I can't say why that follows from the setup, or whether the theorem can be patched to survive it.

2. The worse-than-a-coin-flip case. What if each person is just a little worse than a coin flip -- right 49 times out of 100? The way I read it, the theorem doesn't just stop working, it runs backwards, and a bigger crowd gets worse. That sounds insane to me (more opinions, worse result?!) but I think it's what the math says. I want them to have to explain why that isn't a contradiction and what it means for how they'd use the theorem in real life.

3. When there's nothing to be right about. If the question is whether the water in a well is poisoned, there's a fact of the matter. If the question is whether we should spend the money on a new park or on road repair, I don't think the theorem has anything to say at all, because there's no answer sitting out there for the crowd to converge on. I want them to say clearly what the theorem is and isn't a theorem about, and whether that makes it useless for the stuff we actually vote on. And I want them to notice the awkward thing that the same Condorcet also came up with that famous case where a majority prefers A to B, and B to C, and C to A all at the same time -- which feels like the exact opposite mood about majority rule. I want them to try to hold both of his results in their heads at once and say what each one actually covers.

Aim the question at somebody who's read a chapter about this and is smart but not a specialist -- hard enough that a vague answer gets exposed, but nothing that needs a textbook. Give me one essay question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme built around must-hit points, the marks each is worth out of 20, and a one-line note for each point on how people usually get it wrong.

And please don't hedge on me. I definately want the model answer to come down on one side of the real question, which is whether this theorem is actually good for anything outside a math class once you take the shared-newspaper problem seriously, or whether it's a beautiful result about a world nobody lives in. Pick a side and defend it -- don't hand me a both-sides list and leave me to sort it out.
