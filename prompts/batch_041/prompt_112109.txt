I'm in a phil of cog sci seminar that's been all about bounded rationality and I have to turn in a short position paper next week, so before I commit to a line I want to check whether my commitments actually hang together. Here's roughly where I've landed: (1) the classical expected-utility model is a useful idealization but it isn't the right normative standard for agents with real computational limits -- for those agents "maximize expected utility" isn't even well-defined as a procedure. (2) Satisficing is a genuinely rational stopping rule, not a second-best approximation to maximizing; stopping at an option that clears your aspiration level can be fully rational. (3) Heuristics are ecologically rational: whether a heuristic counts as rational depends on the structure of the environment, so there's no context-free ranking of heuristics. (4) There's a real normative theory of bounded rationality -- we can say an agent reasoned well or badly, not just describe what it did. (5) The right way to build that theory is to ask which algorithm is optimal given the agent's computational constraints, i.e. its time, memory, and so on. (6) Rationality is a general capacity: a rational agent shouldn't be brittle, meaning it shouldn't become irrational just because you move it to a nearby environment. And (7) there's no non-arbitrary way to fix the aspiration level for satisficing -- any stopping rule either generates a regress (how long do you search for the stopping rule?) or covertly optimizes something.
