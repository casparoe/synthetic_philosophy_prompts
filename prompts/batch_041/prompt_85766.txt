Hi, I'm a junior in high school and I run a philosophy club that meets Thursdays after school in the art room — usually 14 to 16 people, and I can get a full 70 minutes if I ask the art teacher nicely and let her keep the sink clear. Last week we got into a dumb fight I can't stop thinking about. I said that if someone handed me a perfect copy of a famous painting, one where you literally could not tell it apart from the real one, I'd hang it up and be just as happy, and that the millions of dollars people pay for the original seemed like pure snobbery to me. My friend Maya said no way, she'd feel totally different about it the second she found out it was a copy, even if she never could have found out on her own. We went back and forth for forty minutes and neither of us could actually say what the difference was supposed to be. So I want to build an activity where everyone in the room runs into this themselves instead of arguing about it in the abstract, and where I don't say the words forgery or authenticity at the start at all — I want them to bump into the problem first and get the vocab in the debrief.

Here's the shape I'm imagining, but make it actually work. I hand out sealed envelopes with a card inside describing an object (a painting, a chair, a piece of music, whatever), and secretly half the cards are matched pairs — one says this is the original, the other says this is a copy of that exact thing, made to be indistinguishable. Everyone gets a bunch of tokens to bid on their own object, no real money, and I don't tell them about the pairs. Then I reveal the pairs and let them see whether the "original" holders bid way more. Then it gets meaner: I swap the labels on half of them and see if the bids change, and then I make them bid again, three times, with new info about who made the copy and why — a machine that made it by accident with no intent at all, a careful restorer fixing damage, and someone who made it specifically to fool experts and get rich (the real-life version is that Dutch guy who forged Vermeers and only got caught because he'd sold one to Göring, so he had to convince everyone that the thing he was about to be shot over was actually a fake he'd painted himself). Then two things I want to hit them with near the end: all those famous Greek marble statues in museums are, I'm pretty sure, mostly Roman copies of bronze originals that don't exist anymore, so the copies are what we've been oohing and aahing at for centuries — and meanwhile a perfect performance of a piece of music is the whole point, nobody thinks the orchestra is passing off a fake. Plus the restoration thing: if 80% of a painting's surface has been repainted by restorers, what exactly is hanging on the wall?

So what I need is a full plan I could hand to another student and have them run it without me: the materials, the exact rules, a payoff scheme that doesn't need money, a timeline for the 70 minutes, what the facilitator says and when, and a debrief for the last 15 minutes that points at specific moments in the game and says what each one was really about (what you can see with your eyes vs. what happened to the object before you ever saw it). Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — I bet a couple people refuse to bid at all, and one kid is definately going to say the whole thing is just snobbery and derail it, and I have no idea what to do with that — and be honest about what the activity can't do. My copies are literally identical printouts, so I'm faking the part where you genuinely can't tell them apart, none of us have anything like a trained eye, and the game can't decide for us whether the extra value of the original is real or a mistake. No reading list or citations please, we're not writing a paper, and don't tell them the fancy words until the debrief.
