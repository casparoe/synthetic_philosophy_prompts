You are a philosopher of science and decision theorist who works on risk regulation, with a particular interest in the logical structure of precautionary reasoning. I want you to argue for a specific reconstruction of the precautionary principle, not to survey the literature or hedge across readings.

Begin by spending a few sentences mapping the space of possible readings — at minimum: precaution as a burden-of-proof rule requiring the proponent of an activity to demonstrate safety; precaution as a presumption against novelty or against action as such; precaution as a rule for choice under ambiguity rather than under known risk; and precaution as a lexicographic or maximin constraint on the ordering of outcomes. Say briefly what each would have to mean to be non-trivial rather than vacuous, then commit to one line of argument and pursue it.

Your position, which I want you to argue for rather than merely announce: the defensible core of the precautionary principle is a lexicographic constraint that assigns absolute priority to avoiding outcomes classified as catastrophic and irreversible, with all remaining outcomes in the decision problem then evaluated by ordinary expected-value reasoning. Argue that this reconstruction outperforms the burden-of-proof reading, which is too weak — a burden of proof can be discharged by a competent risk assessment that concludes in favour of the activity, so the reading cannot generate any distinctive verdict — and outperforms the presumption-against-novelty reading, which is both incoherent and status-quo-biased.

Then do the hard work, in order:

First, address the regress or paralysis objection. If precaution counsels against activities whose harms are uncertain, it appears to counsel equally against the regulatory and economic activity of restricting them, and against inaction itself, since inaction carries its own uncertain harms. Explain precisely how the lexicographic reconstruction blocks this regress, and say whether the block depends on the irreversibility restriction, on the restriction to a catastrophic class, or on both.

Second, address the fanaticism objection. Lexicographic priority to catastrophe avoidance would forbid any activity carrying a nonzero probability of a catastrophic outcome, which is nearly all activity, including the activity of regulating. Say whether a threshold on the catastrophe class, combined with a restriction to irreversibility, genuinely answers this objection or merely relocates the problem to the specification of the threshold.

Third, address the status quo bias objection. The status quo also produces catastrophic and irreversible harms: ongoing pollution, deployed technologies, entrenched institutional arrangements. Say whether the lexicographic reading as you have stated it contains any resources for forcing precaution against the status quo rather than only against proposed changes. If it does not, say whether that is a defect of your reading or a defect of the principle itself.

Fourth, state honestly what the reconstruction concedes. The classification of which outcomes count as catastrophic and irreversible is not derivable from the principle and must be supplied by prior ethical or political judgment. Argue whether this concession leaves the principle a genuine guide to action or reduces it to a formal shell around whatever catastrophe list a decision-maker already happens to accept.

Throughout, contrast your reconstruction with the two best-known formulations — the burden-shifting formulation associated with the Wingspread statement and the Rio formulation that ties precaution to cost-effective measures and to the capabilities of the states applying it — and say which of them your reading is closest to, and where each fails.

Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations. You have no internet access, and you should not guess at sources or page numbers. Reason from the concepts. Be rigorous and concede weakness where it exists; do not pretend the reconstruction solves more than it does.

End with a single final line beginning Verdict: that states in one sentence whether the lexicographic reconstruction saves the precautionary principle or instead shows that it cannot be a free-standing guide to action.
