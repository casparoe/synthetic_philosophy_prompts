I am a retired experimental physicist who has come to philosophy late, and I keep getting stuck on institutions rather than arguments. In Switzerland a change to the federal constitution is adopted only if it wins a majority of the votes cast nationwide and, separately, a majority of the cantons, where a canton's contribution is simply the outcome of the vote inside it and the six former half-cantons count for half a cantonal vote each; an ordinary federal statute, by contrast, may be challenged by referendum and is then decided by the national popular majority alone, with no cantonal weighting at all. What conception of democratic legitimacy does that arrangement presuppose? Having spent a career measuring things, I notice that a rule counting cantons equally regardless of population and a rule counting citizens equally are two different notions of what it is for votes to weigh the same, so my suspicion is that the scheme quietly holds both at once and never says which one is doing the work. So: does the arrangement commit Switzerland to the view that the canton, rather than the citizen, is the bearer of political authority, or can the cantonal majority be explained by something weaker, such as shielding smaller units from being outvoted, which would leave the underlying view of legitimacy underdetermined? Tell me what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely accommodates, and which rival accounts of why democratic outcomes bind it rules out. Then consider a proposal to let a canton cast its cantonal vote not through its voters but through a panel of a few hundred residents chosen by lot, whose decision would bind the canton: is that reform coherent with the presupposition you identified, or does it expose the arrangement as resting on two incompatible ideas?
