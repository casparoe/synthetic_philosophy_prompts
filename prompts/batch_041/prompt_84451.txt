I teach philosophy of science, and I've somehow ended up as the informal vetter for our state education department's research office — they get maybe five of these a week and the board reads the briefings cold, so the stakes are real but nobody has time to read a paper. The problem is that lately everthing crossing my desk has a heritability number in it: twin studies, polygenic scores, "40% genetic," that kind of thing. The analysts are sharp but not trained in this, and I'm tired of answering the same questions one at a time, so I want to hand them a procedure instead of a reading list.

You're an expert in behavioral genetics and the philosophy of biology. Think this through carefully before you start writing, and open by brainstorming the ways a heritability estimate gets misread — within-group vs between-group, what "the environment" is doing in a twin model, why a high estimate doesn't fix a trait, why a low one doesn't tell you which lever to pull, and so on. Then turn that into the thing itself.

What I need is a decision procedure they can actually run on a claim in fifteen minutes, no math past arithmetic: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, the point where it stops and says "keep this out of the briefing," and the point where it stops and says "this is a values question, kick it to a human." It has to work for someone who doesn't know what a gene-environment interaction is — define terms inline where you must, but don't lecture. Every question should be answerable from the claim itself or a five-minute call with whoever sent it, and the director signs off, so the procedure has to end in something she can defend to the board.

Then run it on three claims: (1) "Educational attainment is about 40% heritable, so the achievement gap is largely genetic." (2) "Reading ability is highly heritable, so intensive tutoring will have limited effects." (3) A briefing our own equity office wrote: the heritability of some outcome is well below 1, therefore the rest is environment, therefore their program is the thing to fund. At least one of these should come out in a way that annoys the people who sent it — including, if it falls out that way, our own side — and don't soften it to be polite.

Finish by being honest about what the procedure can't do: which steps are judgment calls dressed up as arithmetic, and where a smart analyst will still get it wrong.
