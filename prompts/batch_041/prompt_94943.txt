Reading group did the old Thomson's lamp puzzle last week — the lamp switched at 1/2, 1/4, 1/8... and you're asked whether it's on or off at 1 — and my friend Maya turned up with this argument that no supertask can have a determinate outcome. I can't tell if it's airtight or if it's proving way too much. Here it is:

(1) A supertask is an infinite sequence of discrete steps, each taking half as long as the one before, all completed by some time T.
(2) For a supertask to have a determinate outcome, something has to fix the state of the system at T.
(3) The only thing that could fix it is the last step.
(4) There is no last step.
(5) So nothing in the setup fixes the state at T.
(6) So no supertask has a determinate outcome.
(7) A thing that never fixes its own outcome isn't a process, it's a description with a hole in it.
(8) So supertasks are impossible.

Here's why I think it proves too much: picture a needle starting at 0° that swings half the way to 180° on step one, half of what's left on step two, and so on. It definately closes in on 180°, and I'd say it ends up at 180 — determinate, even though there's no last swing. Zeno's ball bouncing itself to rest looks like the same deal. But when I say that, Maya says I'm smuggling in a continuity assumption that isn't part of the supertask, and that a supertask is just the list of steps, so I'm adding something that was never there.

So which step is doing the damage, and what does each repair cost? I'd rather keep (1)-(4) than (8) if I can, but tell me what I'm buying. Last line: Verdict: <the step you reject> — <one sentence why>.
