I have been defending an account of hard choices and I want a map of what it is exposed to. The view has three layers. The first is the familiar one: some pairs of options are genuinely incommensurable — neither better than the other, not equally good, and not merely awaiting more information, since no further factual or evaluative inquiry would settle the ranking. The second is that this is a stable feature of the value landscape rather than a defect that better theory or finer measurement would remove. The third is the one I am least sure of and most want tested: that incommensurability is not just a gap in the ranking but a source of reasons. On this view, the very fact that there is no fact of the matter about which option is better gives the agent a reason of a distinct kind — a reason to commit, to make the choice her own — and the resulting choice is to be assessed not by whether it tracks a pre-existing ordering but by whether the agent can endorse it from within the commitment she has made. A case I keep returning to: a researcher can spend her one free year either finishing a body of work she has labored on for a decade and believes will matter, or moving away to care full-time for a parent in decline. I do not think this is a case of uncertainty, and I do not think the two options are equal.

What I want is the map of what this risks. Which observations, arguments, intuitions, and formal results would count against it, and how decisive would each be? Three lines of pressure interest me most.

First, the formal ones. I have been told that any partial ordering can be extended to a total ordering, so that any pattern of incommensurabilities could in principle be completed into a full ranking, and that an agent facing incommensurable options can be exploited in a sequence of trades — a money-pump. How much of the view do these actually threaten? Do they bear on incommensurability itself or only on particular uses I make of it, and does the order-extension point show that incommensurability is eliminable, or merely that it can always be arbitrarily resolved?

Second, the self-constitution move in the third layer. Does it save the view, or does it quietly change the subject from value to something else — from what is worth choosing to what a coherent agent can own? If the latter, has the view been made unfalsifiable, and is that a problem for a view of this kind or an acceptable feature of it?

Third, what the view's own defenders should concede. I do not only want what critics say; I want to know which consequences the position is committed to that could turn out unacceptable, which cases it cannot describe, and whether it collapses into a rival — for instance into the view that hard choices are just cases of uncertainty, or of ignorance about one's own values.

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say what this view predicts that its rivals — a total-ranking maximizer, and the uncertainty view — do not, and tell me honestly whether the position has been insulated from test rather than exposed to it, and if it has, whether that is a defect or simply what a view at this level of abstraction looks like.
