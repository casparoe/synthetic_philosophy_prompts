I teach a seventy-five-minute session in an undergraduate seminar on self-reference, and the topic is Hofstadter's typogenetics and the claimed isomorphism between DNA self-replication and Gödelian self-reference. My students have had exactly one lecture on Gödel: they can state the incompleteness theorems and have met the liar paradox, but they could not reconstruct the diagonal argument, and they know no logic beyond truth tables. They know the double helix and that DNA codes for proteins at the level of a good high-school biology course; they know no biochemistry. I have a whiteboard and a one-page handout carrying the typogenetics rules: strands built from a four-letter alphabet; a fixed but arbitrary translation rule that reads a strand and yields a sequence of amino acids; each amino acid specifying a typographical operation (deleting a letter, copying a letter, switching two adjacent letters, shifting a letter left or right, and the like) together with a preference for the letter it acts on; an enzyme is such a sequence, and it works its way along a strand applying its operations in order. Do not explain the isomorphism to me. Plan the session.

What I need from you is the plan, in this order. First, the order in which the ideas must be introduced, and why each depends on the one before it — in particular, tell me whether the students must trace a strand by hand before the enzyme-as-program point can land, and whether they must see that the translation rule is arbitrary before the isomorphism is anything more than a pun. Second, the single carrying example: what strand to put on the board, and what it must have and lack — short enough to trace within ten minutes, long enough to code for more than one enzyme, and either genuinely self-replicating or instructive in its failure. Do not invent a specific strand unless you are confident it behaves as you say it does; if you are not, give me the properties it must satisfy and I will construct it myself. Third, the misconceptions, in the order they will surface, and the exact point in the sequence at which I should head each one off. I expect at least these: that the strand must contain a description or blueprint of itself; that the enzyme somehow knows what to do; that the isomorphism is a loose analogy rather than a structural mapping; that if the code is arbitrary then coding-for is arbitrary too, so the whole comparison is wordplay; and the over-reading that Gödel's theorem therefore applies to biology, or that a cell just is a formal system. Supply the ones I have not thought of. Fourth, one question to put to them at the end that would show whether they have grasped the structure rather than memorized the vocabulary — my own candidate is to ask what would break if I altered the translation table, but I want yours. Fifth, what to leave out at this level and say so out loud: I already intend to cut the proof of the incompleteness theorems and the biochemistry of replication, and I want to know what else would only muddy the water, including whether the strange-loop framing and the quine analogy should figure at all.
