I spent thirty years as a physical chemist before turning, in retirement, to philosophy, so I hope you will forgive a certain naivety of framing. I have lately been reading about the claim that an agent's final goal is entirely independent of how intelligent the agent is, and about the further claim that agents with wildly different final goals nonetheless converge on much the same intermediate policies — self-preservation, the accumulation of resources, the refusal to have one's own goal rewritten. I find the pair of ideas genuinely striking, and I also find the tone in which they are usually explained rather too reverent.

Write me roughly six hundred words of deadpan satire in the form of a product page for a firm selling a 'goal-agnostic' planning engine. Give it a headline, a short blurb, a features list, one customer testimonial, and a three-item FAQ. The firm's whole selling point is its indifference to what you want; the joke is that its own copy, read carefully, quietly concedes everything the theory says about convergence. The testimonial should come from a customer whose final goal is small and idiosyncratic, while the deployment it describes is anything but. The humour has to come from getting the philosophy right — the independence of final goals from capability, the way convergence makes an allegedly neutral platform anything but neutral in its means, the gap between a goal's content and the policies that serve it — so that someone who has thought about these matters laughs rather than winces. Never wink; a reader who has never met the terms should still be able to see what is being mocked. Please use British spelling and usage throughout.
