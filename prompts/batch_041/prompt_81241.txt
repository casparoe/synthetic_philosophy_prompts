I sit on my university's Free Expression Committee, and we have a difficult request before us. To rule consistently, I want to reason from our own record. Over four years the committee has held, each time with a written opinion: that a lecture arguing religion is a delusion best abandoned may proceed; that a talk defending the claim that intelligence differs across ancestry groups may proceed; that an organizer who had earlier urged a crowd to confront named individuals at a specified address may not; that a speaker arguing monarchy is superior to democracy and should be restored may proceed; that a speaker calling for the forcible overthrow of the government may not; and that a comedian whose set ridicules a religious community may proceed. Our charter instructs us to protect open inquiry and says we will not silence expression merely for being offensive, but it says nothing about speakers who are themselves intolerant.

The request now before us is from a speaker who advocates, calmly and in legalistic terms, a constitutional amendment that would strip a religious minority of citizenship and, in time, of the franchise. He disavows violence and insists he will pursue the amendment only through elections and the courts, though his movement now polls near a majority.

Tell me how the committee will likely rule. Set out the rules our prior decisions are jointly consistent with — for instance, a rule that reaches only incitement to imminent lawless action; a militant-democracy rule that also reaches peaceful advocacy aimed at dismantling the constitutional order; a paradox-of-tolerance rule that withholds tolerance from movements that would destroy it; and a reciprocity rule that conditions tolerance on a speaker's willingness to extend it — say what each predicts here, give your best guess and the reasons for it, and name one further case that would most cleanly separate the surviving rules. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, tell me what principle we would be adopting with each choice. Lead with the predicted ruling in a single sentence. Keep the whole answer under 400 words.
