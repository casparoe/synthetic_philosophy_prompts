I am one of five adjudicators on a publicly funded no-fault compensation scheme — a statutory body that pays lump sums and ongoing allowances to people left with lasting impairment by injury or illness, out of general taxation, with an independent appeals tribunal above us. We decide roughly two hundred claims a month. Each adjudicator is expected to dispose of a file in under forty minutes, working only on the papers: a medical assessment commissioned by the scheme, a statement from the claimant, and whatever treatment and employment history the claimant chooses to supply. We cannot compel medical records, we cannot interview the claimant, and we have no investigators. Awards above a fixed threshold need the chair's second signature, and every refusal has to be written up in a form that will survive a tribunal reading it aloud.

Our governing statute was drafted by people who had absorbed a good deal of luck egalitarianism, and its purpose clause says, in substance, that the scheme exists to relieve disadvantages that claimants could not reasonably have avoided and is not intended to underwrite risks they knowingly ran. That is a defensible sentence and an almost unusable rule. My colleagues and I have drifted into doing it by feel, and the drift is showing: two adjudicators will split on the same file, the reasons we write are post hoc rationalisations of a snap judgement, and the appeals tribunal has started to say so. I have no philosophy training and neither does anyone on the panel; our staff turn over every couple of years.

What I want from you is a decision procedure we can actually run. It has to be faithful to what luck egalitarianism actually holds rather than to the slogan version of it — in particular it should respect the theory's own narrow account of what counts as a knowingly run risk, which is not the same thing as any decision that happened to turn out badly, and it should have something to say about people whose choices were made under pressure, in ignorance, or with no real alternative in front of them. But it also has to survive contact with a forty-minute file review, which means you must decide which of the theory's distinctions are load-bearing and must be preserved in the simplification and which can be dropped without turning the procedure into something else. Where the theory is indeterminate, or where the honest answer is that this is a values question rather than a factual one, the procedure should stop and say so rather than manufacture a verdict.

Please write it as follows.

1. What the principle commits us to — roughly 250 words. State the luck egalitarian core in terms a claims officer can hold in their head, and name explicitly the two or three distinctions you have decided must survive simplification, with a sentence on what is lost by flattening each of them.

2. The procedure — roughly 800 words. Numbered steps in the order they are to be run. For each step: what the adjudicator is looking for, what counts as a sufficient answer, what evidence in a normal file could supply it, and what to do when the file is silent. Mark each step as mechanical, evidence-dependent, or a values call. Say clearly where the procedure terminates in a refusal, where it terminates in an award, and where it must be handed to the chair or to the tribunal because it turns on a question the procedure cannot settle.

3. Worked applications — roughly 700 words. Run the procedure in full on three files: (a) a motorcyclist, helmetless by choice, now paraplegic; (b) a woman with a degenerative neurological condition with no behavioural component whatever; (c) a man with severe lung disease who began smoking at twelve in a household where everyone smoked, and who has not stopped. At least one of the three should come out in a way I will find hard to accept, and I would rather you say so and tell me what the scheme should do about it than soften the result.

4. Where the procedure misfires — roughly 350 words. The points at which it will produce answers that are defensible on the theory but that a decent person running it will find harsh, humiliating to the claimant, or administratively absurd; and the objections the theory itself attracts from its critics, in the form in which they would actually bite on an adjudicator's desk rather than in a seminar.

5. What the procedure cannot decide — roughly 250 words. The questions it hands upward, and the ones it leaves to the individual adjudicator's judgement no matter how carefully it is written.

6. The one-page version — roughly 200 words. The same procedure compressed into something I can tape inside a file cover, in the imperative.

No citations, no reading lists, and no names of books or articles — I cannot get hold of any of it and will not chase references, so anything you rely on has to be stated in the procedure itself. Assume the reader has never opened a book of political philosophy and will not read one.
