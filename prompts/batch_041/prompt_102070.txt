Last night a friend and I argued for hours about global workspace theory, and we came away with a construction that neither of us could break. I would like you to adjudicate it.

The background, as we settled it. Global workspace theory treats a mental content as conscious when it is ignited: a coalition of processors wins a competition for a limited-capacity workspace, and the winning content is broadcast to a wide array of otherwise specialized subsystems, thereby becoming available for report, for flexible reasoning, and for the control of action. The distinction we kept returning to is the familiar one between access consciousness — a state's being poised for such global use — and phenomenal consciousness — there being something it is like to undergo the state. On its face the theory is an account of the first. What we could not settle is whether the second comes along for free.

The argument we reconstructed, numbered:

(1) A representation is conscious just in case it is globally broadcast — selected into a limited-capacity workspace and made available to the system's many specialized consumer subsystems.
(2) So described, global broadcasting is a functional and architectural property; nothing in the description requires neurons, or carbon, or life.
(3) That description is satisfied by systems that are not brains: a large firm's incident-reporting channel, which selects among competing signals and distributes the winner to every department; a market's price system, which compresses dispersed information into a single public signal that coordinates countless independent actors; an operating system's scheduler, which serializes competing processes onto a shared bus.
(4) By (1) through (3), each of those systems has conscious representations.
(5) A firm, a market, and an operating system do not have conscious experiences.
(6) Therefore (1) is false.

My friend reads this as a vise. Either the criterion in (1) is sufficient for consciousness, in which case we are committed to conscious firms and markets; or it is only a criterion for access, in which case it never touched phenomenal consciousness and the theory should stop calling itself a theory of consciousness. I would rather keep (5) than (1) — the conclusion in (4) strikes me as intolerable — but I cannot see what I am entitled to give up.

Tell me which premise or step to reject, why it looked so plausible in the first place, and what rejecting it costs. I want to know whether the honest escape is to deny that the theory ever promised phenomenality, to add a further condition that the non-brain systems fail, or something else entirely — and whether that further condition is principled or merely a patch. If the argument is in fact valid, say which premise is cheapest to abandon, and be candid about the price.
