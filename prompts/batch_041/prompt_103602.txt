I'm a junior at a science magnet high school, and three weeks from now I have to defend my position paper at our school's Science and Society symposium, in front of an audience I can already picture in detail. The panel is drawn from the faculty: two biology teachers, one of whom wrote our cell-biology unit and treats the cell as the only genuine unit of life, and the other a physiologist who thinks metabolism is the whole story; a chemistry teacher who is a strict physicalist and believes the word life is a folk category that serious science should abandon, and who will want to talk about entropy and self-organizing systems far from equilibrium; a physics teacher who judges every definition by whether it earns its keep predictively; our computer science teacher, who runs the robotics club and will ask whether my definition is substrate-neutral, and whether answering yes turns the whole thing into a reductio; and the faculty advisor of the ethics club, who will want to know what all of this does to moral status. There will also be about fifteen students in the room, several of them on the debate team, who will go for the cheapest gotcha available. The question period is scheduled for twenty minutes, and I have been warned that this panel asks hard questions but has no patience for hedging.

My thesis is that viruses are alive, and that the list of characteristics of living things in our textbook is not a definition at all but a description of the organisms biologists happened to study first. I have my own criterion, which I call the lineage test: something is alive if it belongs to a lineage that (i) copies a heritable template with enough fidelity to retain information and enough slop to vary, (ii) maintains a bounded, self-produced organization that keeps itself away from equilibrium, and (iii) is exposed to selection in a way that lets the lineage accumulate open-ended novelty. I am aware of the odd consequence: on this test no individual entity is alive in its own right, only as a node in a lineage, so a single cell is only derivatively alive too, and I genuinely cannot tell whether that is a feature or a bug. My borderline cases are viruses, including the giant ones that carry some of their own translation-related genes; a virus that makes its living by parasitizing another virus; a prion; a strand of self-replicating RNA in a test tube; a mitochondrion; a viral sequence that has sat quietly in a genome for millions of years; a mule; and a red blood cell.

Please prepare me a Q&A sheet for this panel: the objections I will actually get, from these specific people, not the ones I would get from a room of philosophers of biology. Put them in order of how likely they are and how much damage they would do to my thesis, phrase each one the way that particular panelist would phrase it, and give me a two-minute reply for each, followed by an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front so that I am not defending more than I need to, which single reply has to land for the thesis to survive, and, since I would rather know now, the question I am hoping nobody asks.
