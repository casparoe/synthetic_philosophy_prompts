I'm in yr 12 and I'm doing my EPQ on digital autonomy — basically whether we're still choosing anything for ourselves now that our music, our shopping and half our homework get suggested to us by something that has been watching us the whole time. We did Sartre in philosophy club (freedom, bad faith, the waiter doing his job like he was born to be a waiter) and the bit my teacher wouldn't shut up about is that for Sartre freedom isn't a thing you can ever hand back or opt out of — you're definately stuck with it, and every excuse you make is itself a choice. She also mentioned something from his later, much more political phase that I've been thinking about constantly, the practico-inert: the idea that our free actions harden into stuff — buildings, systems, machines, other people's habits — and then that stuff comes back at us looking like a force of nature, like gravity, even though humans built it. Honestly a recommender system seems like the purest example of that I've ever heard of, which is sort of why I'm asking.

So the task: Sartre somehow lives through everything since he died in 1980 and gets asked about three things that didn't exist when he was around. First, feeds that learn you — the way people say the algorithm knows them better than they know themselves, sometimes proudly, sometimes as the reason they can't stop scrolling at 2am. Second, this precommitment app my friend's mum uses where you set the rules now and your future self just isn't allowed to break them (no phone after ten, no takeaway, no texting the ex), and the paid version tells you what you actually wanted based on your data when you try to override it. Third, AI companions — a load of people I know talk to one, and some of them say it's the only relationship where they can be completely themselves. For each one I want his verdict, and more importantly which of his own tools is actually doing the work: bad faith, or the attitude where you treat values as facts that are just sitting there in the world waiting to be discovered, or his thing about how demanding sincerity from someone is itself a way of turning them into a fixed object, or the practico-inert? And the one I really can't work out myself: does the AI-companion case even fit the bad-faith machinery at all, since as far as I can tell the whole thing runs on being looked at by another free person, and a chatbot isn't one — so does the diagnosis break there, or turn into something else entirely? Don't just give me vibes: mark which parts you think are genuinely his and which parts you're extrapolating, and tell me what would actually change your mind — if some notebook of his turned up saying the opposite, or if I've got the practico-inert wrong, or if the chatbot thing is closer to a case he already wrote about than I think it is.
