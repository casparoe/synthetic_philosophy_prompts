I'm a science journalist — I mostly cover climate and biotech, but I've fallen down a rabbit hole on the history side because I'm writing a feature about the way people fight over whether scripture can function as a natural-history source. Which has landed me in Galileo's long letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, from around 1615, and I'm stuck on one specific move in it. My Latin and my Italian are both bad, so I've been working from an English rendering, and there's a stretch where he argues roughly this: the Holy Spirit's intention in scripture isn't to teach us astronomy, it's to teach us what's needed for salvation, and so where the sacred writers touch on natural things they accommodate themselves to ordinary speech and to the capacities of their audience — hence talk of the sun rising and setting and running its course, which is how things appear to us, not how they are. So far so good, and I can see why this is the bit everyone quotes. But then a few pages later he turns around and argues about the Joshua passage — where Joshua commands the sun to stand still — and he insists that this is not an accommodation at all, that it really did happen in the heavens, and that the Copernican arrangement is actually the one that makes the miracle intelligible, because if the sun's motion is really the Earth's rotation then stopping the whole system is a grander miracle than tugging one body to a halt. And he accuses his opponents of being the ones who read the passage non-literally, since on their reading nothing happens up there at all.

What I can't get my head around is how those two moves are supposed to fit together. If the Holy Spirit doesn't care about astronomy and speaks in the language of appearances, why does the Joshua passage suddenly have to be astronomy that literally happened? And if some passages do have to be read as literal natural history, doesn't that wreck the accommodation principle he leaned on to keep the astronomers out of trouble in the first place? I have a nagging feeling he's using literal in two different senses and I can't tell whether that's a deliberate distinction or a slip, and I also can't tell whether the accommodation principle is meant to be a claim about what the text means or a claim about why God would bother saying anything about nature at all — those seem like very different theses and he seems to slide between them.
