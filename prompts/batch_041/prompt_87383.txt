In my distributive-justice reading group, a friend put up an argument that he says shows the priority view cannot escape the leveling-down objection — the objection long thought to sink egalitarianism but which prioritarians insist their own view is immune to. I have gone back and forth on whether it is a real problem or a trick, and I would like it diagnosed rather than summarized.

The case he used: two people, Ana and Ben. Ana is very well off; Ben is badly off. Compare two outcomes.

- Outcome A: Ana at a high level, Ben at a low level.
- Outcome B: Ana is brought down to Ben's level, and Ben is left exactly where he was.

Outcome B is a pure leveling down: no one's level rises, and Ana's falls.

His argument:

1. The priority view says that a benefit matters more, morally, the more badly off the person who receives it, and less the better off that person is.
2. So the same benefit to Ben counts for more than it would count for if it went to Ana, and how much it counts for is fixed by how the two of them stand.
3. The view is therefore concerned, at bottom, with how people stand relative to one another; that relational fact is what makes Ben's interests weigh more heavily than Ana's.
4. If relative standing matters in that way, then a state of affairs in which people stand closer together is, in that respect, better than one in which they stand further apart.
5. So the priority view assigns positive value to the reduction of disparity, independently of whether anyone's level improves.
6. Now take Outcome B. It reduces the disparity between Ana and Ben to nothing, and it costs Ben nothing. By (5), the priority view must count this reduction as a good — as something to be said for Outcome B that is not to be said for Outcome A.
7. But a view that assigns positive value to a change that improves no one's level is precisely what the leveling-down objection accuses egalitarianism of. So the priority view is exposed to the objection too.
8. Therefore the priority view does not escape the leveling-down objection after all.

Where does this go wrong? I am a prioritarian; I want to keep (1), and I want to keep the thought behind (7) — that a pure leveling down is not an improvement in any respect — since that is not the part I am willing to trade away. If something has to go, I would rather give up whichever earlier step is doing the illegitimate work than either of those. So: name the step I should reject, explain why it looked fine when he ran through it, and tell me what rejecting it costs me. In particular, say what I am forced to give up elsewhere, and whether the same diagnosis, run with equality in place of priority, is what makes the objection succeed against egalitarianism.

Be explicit about which parts of your answer are settled and which are live disputes among people who work on this; do not present a contested reading of the priority view as though it were the consensus.
