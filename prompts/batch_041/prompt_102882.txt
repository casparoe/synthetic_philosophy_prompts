I am a high school junior and the student representative on my district's Fine Arts Advisory Committee, and I need help drafting the written opinion I will circulate before next week's vote. Please take the role of someone who knows both the philosophy of art and the intellectual history of African American thought, and think carefully before you write; if it helps, brainstorm first and then organize. The dispute is over a proposed rule for our spring student exhibition, whose working theme is Art and Justice. One faction on the committee wants the exhibition guidelines to require that every submitted piece carry an explicit social or political message, and they have been invoking W. E. B. Du Bois's 1926 address "Criteria of Negro Art," delivered at an NAACP conference and published later that year in The Crisis, as their authority. Their reading is that Du Bois held that art ought to serve the struggle, and that any artist who claims to be above politics is either naive or complicit. The other faction, which includes two art teachers, wants the guidelines to remain open to any subject, and they invoke Du Bois too, pointing out that the same address spends a great deal of time on beauty, on the freedom of the black artist to depict black life without being told in advance what it must say, and on the artist's need to be free of both white and black censors. Both sides are quoting him at each other and neither seems to have read the whole piece, and I suspect the honest answer is that his position is more tangled than either side wants.

What I need from you is the written opinion I will submit and read aloud: it should recommend one concrete course of action for the committee to adopt, with a definite rule stated in language the committee could actually put into the exhibition guidelines, and it should give the reasoning behind that recommendation in a way that my classmates and the teachers can respond to rather than just feel overruled. It must engage the strongest version of the position I end up rejecting, not a strawman, and it should be honest about which parts of Du Bois's view genuinely support each side. Where his text is ambiguous or where scholars disagree about what he meant, say so plainly rather than smoothing it into a single doctrine, and do not present a contested reading of him as if it were the consensus. Separate for me what is settled about his views on art and propaganda from what is genuinely disputed among people who study him. I would also like you to flag any place where my framing of the debate is itself a distortion of what he was arguing, since I would rather find that out before the meeting than during it. The memo should be formal enough to read to a committee that includes the superintendent's designee, but it is still being written by a student, so it should not pretend to a scholarly authority I do not have.
