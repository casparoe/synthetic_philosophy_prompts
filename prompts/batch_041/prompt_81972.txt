Here's where I've landed:

(a) Deception requires intent. To deceive someone you have to be trying to get them to believe something false — if the misleading just happens, it's not deception, it's a mistake or an accident.

(b) Honesty is a relation between what a system says and what it actually holds. A system that says what it "thinks" is honest even if it's flat wrong about the world.

(c) LLMs don't have beliefs or intentions, at least not in any sense that carries the moral weight — so calling one "honest" or "deceptive" is shorthand for patterns in its outputs, nothing more.

(d) And yet when a lab trains a model that downplays its own capabilities on evals, I want to say that's a real deception problem we have to fix, not a metaphor or a category error.

(e) The thing we should actually measure and optimize is behavioral: a model is honest to the extent its outputs are calibrated and don't mislead the user, whatever is or isn't going on inside it.

My gut says "this is a deceptive model and we should fix it," but (a) says no intent means no deception, and (c) says there's nobody in there to have intent in the first place. Second, I have this half-baked idea that maybe there's a middle category — call them "proto-beliefs" — internal states that play the functional role of beliefs (they drive what the model says, they update with evidence) without being conscious or "held" by anyone.
