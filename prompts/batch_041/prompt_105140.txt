I teach a grad seminar that's half philosophy-of-biology people and half econ students, and we keep hitting the same wall every year. On my side of the room, Zahavi's handicap principle and the costly-signaling literature get taught as this verbal, almost moralistic idea — the peacock's tail is honest *because* it hurts, the waste is what buys you sincerity, the signal has to be a burden or it doesn't mean anything. The econ students listen to that and say, fine, that's a signaling game with a single-crossing condition and a separating equilibrium, what's the philosophy. And honestly I never have a good answer, partly because I'm not a game theorist.

So here's what I want. Take the handicap-principle / costly-signaling position — Zahavi's version, and the more formal treatment it later got from people like Grafen, and the loose way it gets borrowed in anthropology and philosophy of religion — and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary of formal signaling games: types, sender and receiver, separating vs pooling equilibria, single-crossing, cheap talk vs costly signals, cues vs signals, index vs handicap. I'm fluent enough to follow Bayesian Nash and separating equilibria but I'm not a theorist, so don't hide behind notation.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue report. What survives the translation cleanly, what only survives if you distort it, and what has no counterpart in the game-theoretic vocabulary at all. In particular, is the biological notion of cost (viability, survival, reproductive load) the same thing as a cost term in a payoff matrix, or does forcing them together lose something? And when the translation fails, is that because the econ framework is missing something real, or because the biology talk was vaguer than it sounded and the formalism is just exposing that? Don't smuggle the game-theory assumptions in and call it a translation — I want to see where it genuinely breaks.

Oh and try not to let "cost" do all the work in your answer; thats the word I suspect is causing half the confusion.
