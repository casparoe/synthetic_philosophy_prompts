I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, working on idealization and feasibility in epistemology and decision theory, and for the past term I've been meeting roughly weekly with a postdoc in my department who works on bounded rationality. A standing exercise in these meetings is that I put a classical norm to her and she tells me whether she regards it as a genuine requirement on agents like us or as an idealization we should stop treating as binding; after a dozen or so of these I have noticed that her verdicts are remarkably stable even though she has never once stated the criterion she is applying. The pattern, as best I can reconstruct it, runs like this: an agent is genuinely required to keep her credences in line with the probability axioms and to avoid believing a contradiction; an agent is not required to believe everything that follows from what she already believes; an agent is not required to conditionalize her credences on her total evidence, because the required update is intractable in the worst case (she usually puts it as: exact inference in a general probabilistic model is #P-hard, so the norm can demand a computation that no procedure will ever finish in time); an agent is required to maximize expected utility when the options are few, explicitly enumerated, and their probabilities given; and an agent is not required to find the exact optimum of a combinatorial problem once exact optimization is NP-hard, but is required to use a procedure guaranteed to land within a fixed factor of the optimum whenever such a procedure exists.

I want to work out what rule she is actually running before I put the next case to her, since I suspect the case is going to embarrass one of my current guesses and I would rather see it coming. The case is this: a norm requiring the agent to select the option that is exactly optimal for a family of combinatorial problems. Given her track record, what is she going to say about this one?
