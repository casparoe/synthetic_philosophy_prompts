I am putting together a position paper for a workshop on phenomenal consciousness, and before I circulate it I should like my own commitments audited. I hold the following, and I hold them with roughly equal confidence, which is exactly why I want them tested rather than summarised back to me.

1. I am a physicalist, and I do not think there is an explanatory gap in principle. A complete account of the relevant information processing would be a complete account of experience.
2. I accept the case against qualia. The four marks traditionally ascribed to them — ineffability, intrinsicality, privacy, and direct apprehensibility — cannot be jointly satisfied by anything that also does explanatory work, and the notion is best treated as an incoherent posit.
3. I am nevertheless not an eliminativist about phenomenal character. There really is something that it is like to taste this coffee, and I take that to be a species of representational content: what it is like is a matter of how things seem, and seeming is relational. So phenomenal character is real, and in principle describable — though not necessarily describable from the outside in the terms the subject would use.
4. On the Chase-and-Sanborn coffee-tasters, I take the lesson to be that there is no fact of the matter about whether their inner feel changed. Once their reports and preferences are fully specified, the two rival stories — the coffee altered and the taster unchanged, the taster altered and the coffee unchanged — describe the same situation under different descriptions. Nothing further is at stake.
5. I am, however, a robust realist about the consciousness of other animals. I think there is a determinate fact about whether a fish feels pain, and that this fact is not fixed by the fish's behaviour: it is fixed by what is going on in the fish. Behavioural evidence is evidence for it, not constitutive of it.
6. I hold the phenomenal-concepts line on the knowledge argument. Phenomenal concepts are recognitional and reflexive, they refer without describing, and the property they pick out is not identical with any functional or representational property.
7. I am untroubled by zombies. I think the zombie idea is incoherent rather than merely empirically false, and I take that to be a point in my favour.
8. Introspection is not a faculty that delivers a given. It is theory-laden, culturally conditioned and revisable, and our vocabulary for our own experience is a historical artefact rather than a transparent medium.
9. All the same, a sincere first-person report of how things currently seem is the best evidence there could be about the subject's phenomenal state, and a theory that tells her she is wrong about how things seem has overstepped its remit.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a literature survey. Which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, and which only look as though they do? Please order the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say precisely what would have to be given up and what the cost of giving it up would be. I should particularly like to know whether any of the pressures I have listed are really the same pressure wearing different clothes, and whether any of my formulations are quietly doing double duty — being read one way in one commitment and another way in the next. Finally, tell me which single revision would resolve the most at the least cost, and be candid if the honest answer is that one of these commitments is doing no work at all and should simply be dropped.
