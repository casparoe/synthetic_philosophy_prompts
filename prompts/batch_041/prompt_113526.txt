I am in the final year of a law degree and taking a jurisprudence option, and I keep running into the maxim that an unjust law is no law at all. I have now met it in three rather different settings, and I cannot get the three uses to line up. At a demonstration against a statute I happen to find indecent, a friend of mine — otherwise a careful thinker — used the maxim to conclude that the statute imposes no duty on anyone, and further that the officials who enforce it are under no legal obligation to do so; he took this to follow more or less immediately from the maxim, and was rather annoyed when I asked him which bit of it was doing the work. In a seminar, by contrast, our tutor disposed of it in a single sentence: either it is true by definition, in which case it tells us nothing, or it is plainly false, since unjust enactments are applied by courts every day; and he then moved on to the rule of recognition as though the matter were closed. Third, in a piece I read about how a post-authoritarian legal order dealt with people who had denounced their neighbours to an oppressive regime, the maxim appeared in a stronger key, being used to suggest that such a regime had never really had law at all, rather than merely bad law, and that the courts which applied its decrees had not been applying law in the full sense.

What I want from you is a disambiguation rather than a defence of any one side. Lay out, as a menu I can take to my tutor, the distinct precise claims that this one sentence could be making, ordered from the trivial to the radical. Some of the contrasts I am already half-aware of, and you should place them explicitly: readings on which the maxim is a terminological stipulation about how to use the word law; readings on which it states a conceptual truth about the conditions of legal validity; readings on which it is a moral claim about when law loses its authority or its claim on our obedience; readings on which it is an interpretive instruction to judges about how to decide hard cases; and readings on which it is hyperbole or a rhetorical flourish with no propositional content at all. You should also say where the ambiguity lies in the phrase no law — as between not law in this system, not law anywhere, law but defective or less than fully law, and law that is valid yet not binding — and where the maxim trades on sliding between legal validity, moral legitimacy, and the existence of a legal system.

Then give me a verdict on each reading: whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, contentious, or simply false, and what kind of claim it is (conceptual, moral, interpretive, or merely rhetorical). Say which of the readings a legal positivist can accept without abandoning positivism, and which of them the natural lawyer actually needs if the maxim is to do any real work; say which reading, if any, would get my friend his conclusion about the duty to obey and about the officials' obligation to apply the statute, and whether he needs one reading for the first conclusion and a different one for the second. Tell me which reading my tutor is attacking when he says the maxim is either true by definition or plainly false, and whether his dilemma really exhausts the options — in particular, whether the strong reading amounts to nothing more than a stipulative redefinition of law, and what would show that charge to be fair rather than a cheap tu quoque. Finally, say which reading people most probably intend when they repeat the maxim in each of the three settings I have described, and which single reading you think is the most defensible. I am not after reading lists or references — just the analysis, with each reading kept clearly distinct from the others.
