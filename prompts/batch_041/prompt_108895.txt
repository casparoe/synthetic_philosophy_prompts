I spent thirty-five years in palaeontology and retired into philosophy about a year ago; I now sit in a reading group of other retired scientists — chemists, biologists, one astronomer — who are impatient with the subject and suspicious of its tendency to dissolve into vocabulary. I want to hand them a single-page decision tree that carries a reader from the one observation none of them disputes — that our words for gradual things have no sharp cutoff — to a named position, so that our next argument is about which answer each of us actually gave at each node rather than about who has read what.

Build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a list of doctrines: settle the questions first, then locate the positions in the combinations of answers they leave open. Every node must be a question my colleagues can answer by consulting their own reactions to a concrete case, phrased so that answering it does not reveal which branch it leads to, and no node may simply ask which doctrine the reader accepts. Every leaf must carry the name of a position and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Four cases must do real work, at least one of them at every node that turns on a case: the grain-by-grain heap; a series of colour patches, each indistinguishable from its neighbour, with the two ends plainly different; a forced march in which the reader must answer yes or no at every step and is not permitted to refuse; and one from my own trade — a continuous stratigraphic sequence in which one named species grades into another with no level at which anything observable changes, so that the question becomes whether the boundary dispute is about the world or about words.

The leaves must include epistemicism, supervaluationism, degree theory, contextualism in both its shifting-boundary and its open-texture forms, the view that the argument is merely a fallacy of ambiguity, the view that vague words have no extensions at all, and the quietist view that there is nothing here to explain. Add any further leaf the space seems to require, and say why. Include a node on higher-order vagueness — what becomes of each account once we ask where the clearly-F cases give way to the merely borderline ones — and a node on where the indeterminacy is located: in the world, in our language, in the limits of what we can know or discriminate, or in the purposes for which we use the word.

Finish by telling me which leaves the tree cannot separate, and for each combination of answers that no position occupies, whether the gap is there because the view would be incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Eight nodes or fewer. What I most want to see is which answers make a live boundary dispute in a science come out merely verbal.
