I'm in my final year and I'm doing a placement with our county council's behavioural insights team for my applied ethics module (I get course credit, they get free labour, everyone wins). The team gets proposals from other departments basically every couple of weeks -- can you nudge people to recycle more, can you nudge people to pay their council tax on time, can you get more residents onto the garden waste subscription -- and at the moment we just sort of eyeball them and say yes or no, which is probaly going to blow up in our faces eventually. My supervisor has asked me to write an actual screening procedure we can run on any proposal that lands on our desk, and I'd rather not just invent a vibe-based checklist, I want it grounded in the real literature on paternalism and nudging.

So please write me that procedure. It should be something two of us can run on a one-page proposal in about fifteen minutes, and it should end in a verdict: green light, green light with changes, or escalate to the ethics board. Constraints: we have no stats people, we can't run RCTs, and half the time we don't know the current behaviour rate, so the procedure has to say what to do when we can't get a number, rather than stalling.

Things it has to force us to decide, and I'd like you to explain why each one matters rather than just listing it:

- Whether we're helping people reach ends they already have, or substituting our ends for theirs. Our lecturer spent a whole seminar on this and it seemed like the crux, but I couldn't tell you how to operationalise it.
- Whether what's proposed is a nudge or a boost. Apparently steering people by leaning on a bias is a different animal from building their competence so they decide well on their own, and I suspect we've been blurring them.
- The transparency question: would the thing still work if we told residents plainly that we're doing it and why? And what it means for the proposal if the answer is no.
- Who actually benefits -- the resident, the council's budget, or somebody else entirely. I've realised some of these aren't paternalism at all, they're just us trying to change behaviour that costs us money, and they shouldn't be run through the same tests.
- Whether the proposal adds friction anywhere else while removing it in one place, and what it costs someone to opt out.

Then run it on three real-ish cases: (1) moving the veggie option to eye level in the council-run cafes; (2) a letter to late council-tax payers saying most of their neighbours have already paid -- I like this one and think it's fine, so push back on me if it isn't; (3) switching everyone to paperless billing by default, which saves the council a fortune and which my team is under pressure to approve. I'd genuinely like the procedure to come out against something the team wants, so don't make it a rubber stamp.

Finally, be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and tell me what the procedure can't settle and has to hand to a human.
