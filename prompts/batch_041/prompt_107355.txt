Write an in-universe FAQ page — about 550 words, in the flat, reassuring register of a utility company's customer-help section — published by the agency that administers the following contest. Each round, every adult in the country may submit a claim for the prize. If exactly one person claims, that person receives the entire prize. If two or more claim, every claimant forfeits and pays a fixed penalty, and the prize rolls over. If nobody claims, the prize also rolls over. The agency has run the contest for thirty rounds and has paid out exactly once.

The page is written by someone who sincerely believes the contest is well designed and that its record vindicates the theory behind it. Let the comedy come from getting the decision theory right: the dominance argument for claiming, the superrational argument for abstaining (reasoning as though every claimant shares your decision procedure), the assumption of identical agents that the latter argument quietly requires, and what becomes of that assumption once the public is actually heterogeneous. Let the agency's own analysis presuppose that every claimant is, for purposes of the calculation, a copy of every other, and let some footnote-sized aside reveal how much work that presupposition is doing. The piece should land the standard objections to the whole framework — that superrationality is either false or question-begging, that the scheme rewards irrationality, that coordinating on an equilibrium is not the same as each agent's being rational, and that abstaining is only safe if you can be sure of everyone else's reasoning — without ever stating any of them outright and without the author winking. Include one dissenting voice from inside the agency, quoted at length (a board member, a support agent, or an aggrieved sole claimant). Do not resolve the paradox; let the deadpan refusal to notice it be the joke. The satire targets the practice of superrational reasoning and the assumption of identical agents, not any named person.
