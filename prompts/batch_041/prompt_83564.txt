I cover science and ideas, and I'm writing a piece on the extended mind — the idea that my phone, my notes, my calendar aren't just tools I use but are genuinely part of where my thinking happens. Fine, I buy it. But something's bugging me and I can't get a clean answer out of anyone.

The law seems to draw the mind/world line exactly where the extended-mind people say you can't draw it. Under the Fifth Amendment you can't be forced to say what you know — the contents of your head are yours. But prosecutors can subpeona your diary, your notes app, your texts, and the contents are fair game, even though for a lot of us that stuff is functionally our memory. The only hedge is that the act of handing something over can itself sometimes count as testimony, which feels like a loophole rather than a principle. Meanwhile the law will happily protect what you told your lawyer, which is also outside your head by then. So which is it?

What does the privilege against self-incrimination actually presuppose about where a person's mind stops and the world begins? Does it need internalism — memory is you, your notebook isn't — or is it really just a doctrine about coerced statements that happens to fall out this way? I want to know what the practice strictly committs to versus what it could give up without changing, and which rival views it quietly rules out.

And the case I keep coming back to: a guy with a brain injury who can't form new memories and lives off a running note on his phone. Is making him unlock it forcing him to testify against himself? If the extended mind thesis is right, what has to change in the doctrine? And if the courts are right to draw the line where they do, explain why the externalist story doesn't touch it. No case names needed, I can't cite them anyway — I just want the argument.
