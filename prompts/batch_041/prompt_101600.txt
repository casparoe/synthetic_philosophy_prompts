I teach a final-year seminar on the problem of divine hiddenness, and I want to close the term with Endō Shūsaku's Silence — the 1966 novel, with Scorsese's 2016 film standing in for those who won't do the reading. The familiar debate is built around one figure in particular: the seeker who wants to believe and finds nothing, the unbeliever who never had a fair chance, the "non-resistant" doubter of Schellenberg's argument. Endō's novel is not about that figure at all. It is about people who already believe — a missionary and a peasant congregation being killed for it — and who receive nothing back but silence. Rodrigues's crisis is not whether God exists; it is why God will not speak to those who are already his.

What I want is a reading of the novel through the hiddenness literature that is faithful in both directions, taking the novel and the philosophy equally seriously. Begin with the mapping: where does Silence dramatise the evidential argument as the analytic literature frames it, and where does it quietly substitute a different problem — hiddenness as suffered by the believer, or by the sufferer, rather than as encountered by the seeker? Does the debate have a place for Rodrigues, or is he a case it never anticipated? If the tradition of the dark night and the apophatic theology of divine darkness serves here better than the contemporary literature, say so and use it.

Then take the climax. As Rodrigues looks at the fumi-e, a voice that seems to be Christ breaks the silence and tells him to trample, and that it was precisely to be trampled on that he came into the world. Is that an answer to the problem — a hiddenness that is itself a mode of address, a God who can only be met as the trampled image — or does the novel leave it genuinely undecided whether Rodrigues hears a revelation or his own exhausted conscience speaking in the idiom it has? Tell me what the reading does to the argument if we take the voice as real, and what it does if we do not.

I also want the awkward parts handled rather than smoothed over: Ferreira's apostasy and his argument that mercy towards others outweighs fidelity, and the officials' verdict that it was the mudswamp of Japan, not the torturers, that defeated Rodrigues — does the novel make hiddenness culturally or psychologically relative, as though God were silent because the soil will not hold him, and is that a real third position or a confusion? And at the end, the honest critic's reckoning: where does the reading fail, where does the novel resist being turned into a case in the debate, and where would a philosopher say it begs the question or trades on an ambiguity between absence and hidden presence?

Conclude by telling me what the novel adds that the papers cannot, and, just as important, what the analytic debate makes clear that the novel leaves murky. Assume I know the plot and the scholarship; I want the criticism, not the background. No plot summary, no quotations, no reading list. And please point me to the scenes I should actually put in front of the class, with a question for each.
