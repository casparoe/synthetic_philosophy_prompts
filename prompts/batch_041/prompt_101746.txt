I've been reading around drug policy for a seminar and I keep tripping over a pattern I can't square. The jurisdictions I'm looking at have spent the last two decades decisively turning away from treating drug use itself as a crime: Portugal stopped jailing people for possessing small quantities and routed them instead to panels that mostly offer treatment; a long list of countries and states have legalized cannabis outright; and even where possession is still illegal, the same governments fund needle exchanges, drug-checking at festivals, and supervised consumption sites, and have passed so-called good Samaritan laws so that a person who calls in an overdose won't be prosecuted. The implicit picture underneath all of this is that the user is a patient, or at worst someone making a poor choice about their own life, and that the state's proper role is to reduce the harm rather than to punish the choice.

Yet the same legal systems have moved the other way on everyone standing next to the user. Supplying the drug remains a serious offense even when the quantity is tiny and the supplier is a friend sharing a bag; a growing number of jurisdictions charge people with homicide or manslaughter when the drugs they passed on turn out to be lethal, even though the person who died took them willingly; and cannabis legalization has been paired, in the very same statutes, with continued criminalization of those who grow or move the product outside the licensed channel. What philosophical commitments does this whole package presuppose? In particular, does it presuppose a view on which the user's consumption is not a genuine exercise of agency worth blaming while the supplier's act is, or a view on which the harm of an overdose attaches to whoever last handled the drug rather than to the person who ingested it, or simply a view about who counts as a legitimate target of state coercion? Reconstruct the implicit theory as best you can. Then tell me which parts of the practice actually require it and which would be equally well served by rival theories — a bare harm-to-others principle, a paternalist one, a luck-egalitarian one that treats addiction as brute bad luck and the dealer as the author of the risk — and be honest about where the practice is underdetermined, i.e. where several incompatible theories would license exactly the same statutes. Finally, tell me what would have to change if we replaced the presupposed view with the consistent position you think the practice is groping toward, and whether that replacement would make the current arrangement more or less defensible.
