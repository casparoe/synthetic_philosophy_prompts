At a workshop dinner last month I read aloud a chapter from the novel I'm finishing, and two of the other writers, Marguerite and Tomás, spent the rest of the evening arguing about a single page of it, and I've been turning their argument over ever since without settling it. The page runs like this: my character Vera works nights as a dispatcher for a rural ambulance service; a call comes in for a man who, years earlier, gave the testimony that sent her brother to prison; she does not refuse the call, does not misreport its location, does not lose the slip — she simply finishes logging a routine transfer for another patient first and enters the man's address four minutes later than she could have, and he dies before the ambulance reaches him. Marguerite's line was that I had written a killing and dressed it up as an omission: Vera had the man's life in her hands and spent the first minutes of it on someone else, and a death that hangs on that choice is one she caused, whatever we call it; if I rewrote the same events as bare narration — she put the other call first — nobody would call it anything but murder. Tomás's line was that this is a trick of narration: whether the ambulance was delayed four minutes or never came at all, Vera never touched the man and never started the thing that killed him, and a death that would have arrived on its own unless she intervened is not a death she brought about. If we let description decide it, he said, then any novelist can convert a killing into a letting-die by choosing a passive verb — and could equally convert a letting-die into a killing by choosing an active one. When I offered that at least in Vera's case her inaction is what makes the difference, since the man lives if she types faster, Tomás granted the point but said it shows only counterfactual dependence: a parent who withholds food from a child is counterfactually decisive too, and we still describe that as letting the child starve rather than poisoning him, because what matters is the kind of thing done, not whether the outcome hung on it. Marguerite answered that this shows only that our descriptions are sentimental about parents, and that where the parent is monstrous either way the doing/allowing line is doing no moral work at all — it is an excuse for people who would rather not be called killers. Tomás replied that the line is doing precisely the work it should: it separates the person who thrusts a knife from the person who fails to catch a falling baby, even when both are unforgivable. Neither of them argued badly, and I couldn't referee, which is partly why I'm writing to you instead of to them.

Could you take their exchange move by move — Vera's page, the description point, the withholding-parent point, and the excuse point — and say who is right about which specific claim, and where each of them is missing something the other has hold of? I'd like a verdict on each move rather than a winner overall, and I'd especially like to know what both of them are assuming without argument. Please also tell me plainly where your own reasoning here is likeliest to be weakest, and which consideration or variation on the case, if it went the other way, would change your verdict on any of the four moves.
