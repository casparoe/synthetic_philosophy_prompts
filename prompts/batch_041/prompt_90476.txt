I am an undergraduate enrolled in a seminar on historical method and environmental history, and in three weeks I am to present my term paper at the department's work-in-progress workshop, where the paper will be circulated in advance and then discussed for fifty minutes. I would like you to prepare me for the question period, not to improve the paper.

My thesis is that historians should admit pathogens, climatic shifts, and volcanic events into their narratives as historical actors, but in a deliberately thin sense: as difference-makers that alter the payoffs of human choices, never as purposive agents and never as sufficient causes. The paper distinguishes three roles such factors can occupy — background condition, multiplier, and trigger — and argues that the charge of environmental determinism has force only when these are collapsed into one another. The running case is the volcanic cooling of the 530s and 540s and the plague pandemic that began within a few years of it. There I claim that the cooling changed which human strategies — grain storage, migration, fiscal reorganization, quarantine — were viable, while the plague's demographic consequences remain genuinely contested.

The audience is small and I know exactly who will be there. There is an environmental historian of the macro-scale school, sympathetic to large causal claims and impatient with what he regards as the discipline's fetishization of contingency. There is a social historian who treats any invocation of non-human causes as a way of evading the analysis of power, class, and empire. There is a historical climatologist who works from proxy reconstructions and is exacting about what may and may not be inferred from them. There is a historian of medicine and empire who believes that "germs as actors" quietly exonerates the human agents who moved pathogens around. And there is a philosopher of history who will want to know whether "actor" is a metaphor, a category mistake, or a substantive causal claim.

Prepare me a briefing sheet. I do not need the argument restated or the field explained to me; I need the room. Give me the objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each phrased in the vocabulary of the person who will raise it. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in roughly ninety seconds and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede at the outset rather than defend, which objection is really a misunderstanding that I should name as such instead of answering, which single objection would sink the paper if I lost it, and the question I am hoping nobody asks.
