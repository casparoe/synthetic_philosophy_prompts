A mate in my political theory group keeps pushing this argument and I genuinely can't tell if it's clever or just sophistry, so I'm outsourcing the verdict. He lays it out like this. (1) Corruption is the misuse of entrusted power for private gain. (2) A ward boss in a clientelist machine steers public jobs and contracts to his own network of supporters, who turn out and vote for him in return. (3) That network is a private party rather than the public at large, and the power he's wielding was entrusted to him by the public. (4) So by (1) he's corrupt. (5) But now take an ordinary MP who campaigns for a new hospital in her constituency and gets re-elected because the voters reward her for it. (6) Her constituency is also only a part of the public, not the whole of it, and she's also used entrusted power to send public money its way in return for an electoral payoff. (7) So if (4), she's corrupt too. (8) But nobody seriously thinks ordinary representation is corruption. (9) So the definition in (1) proves too much, and the whole anti-clientelism line is a Western norm smuggled in as a universal one.

I want to keep (8), and I'd rather not give up the thought that the machine politician is definately doing something wrong. So which step goes, and what does dropping it cost me? If the answer is that (1) is just too thin, tell me what you'd add to it and why the addition isn't itself parochial.
