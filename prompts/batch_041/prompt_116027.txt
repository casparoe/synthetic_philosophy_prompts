I am assembling a small anthology of poems that function as arguments, for a seminar on early modern metaphysics, and I would like you to write one contribution.

Write a sestina — six stanzas of six lines, closing with a three-line tornada — spoken by an ordinary perceived object (a table, a lamp, a page; your choice) that has been reading Berkeley and has taken him seriously. Its six end-words should be drawn from the vocabulary the argument turns on: exist, perceive, conceive, mind, idea, and a sixth of your choosing that names the thing itself. The form should carry the philosophy. A sestina permutes the same six words through every position and finally gathers them all into the tornada, and that is what Berkeley's central argument does to anyone who tries to say what a thing is when no one is looking: we cannot conceive of anything existing unconceived, since conceiving is itself a mental act, and so the attempt is spoken, hence perceived, hence already inside a mind. Let the rotation feel less like a game than like a trap the poem keeps walking into.

Three further requirements.

First, in at least three stanzas, let one end-word also appear earlier in the stanza in its other grammatical guise, so that to conceive and a conception, or to perceive and a perception, stay audibly entangled. The poem should keep the act and the thing conceived in one frame; that overlap is exactly where the argument is most exposed, and where a critic would press.

Second, in the fourth stanza the object should attempt the experiment of describing itself with the lights off, and should fail in a way that is visible on the page — the sentence should not be finishable without a perceiver turning up as its subject.

Third, the tornada must turn on the reader. The object should end by noticing that it is still here only because someone is reading, and should address that someone directly, quietly installing the reader in the place Berkeley reserves for the mind that keeps things in being when no finite mind perceives them. Do not decide whether this is a discovery about the world or a trick of the poem's own grammar. End where the argument ends: with both still open.

Keep the technical labels out — no idealism, no solipsism, no epistemology, no commentator's name. Someone who knows the argument should be able to reconstruct it from the poem alone; someone who does not should still find it a good poem.
