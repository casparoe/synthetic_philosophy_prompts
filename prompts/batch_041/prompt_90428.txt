I'm putting together an essay on the frame problem and would like your comments on the argument. My notes are below.

Thesis: the frame problem is usually stated as the difficulty of specifying, in a formal representation, everything that stays the same when an action is performed — the problem of writing the "frame axioms." I want to argue that this is a symptom rather than the core. The core is that any finite agent must first determine which features of a situation its current task makes relevant, and that this determination cannot be made by the agent's own reasoning without an obvious regress, since deciding what is relevant is itself a task that presupposes relevance judgments.

I then try to separate the frame problem from two neighbours: the qualification problem (specifying when an action succeeds) and the ramification problem (tracking indirect consequences). My claim is that all three collapse into one once you notice they each presuppose a fixed set of relevant features, and that the frame version is the hardest of the three because it has no analogue of an explicit precondition list.

Proposed solution: relevance is imposed rather than discovered — it is fixed jointly by the agent's current goal and a prior causal model of the domain. On this view the frame problem reduces to the qualification problem conditional on a causal model. I then argue that this reduction fails, because a causal model is itself a representation of what matters, so the solution smuggles in the very relevance judgments it was supposed to explain.

I'd like comments on three points: (1) whether the regress argument is sound, or whether it equivocates on "relevance"; (2) whether my collapse of the three problems is defensible or overstated; and (3) whether my objection to the proposed solution is fair, or whether there is a non-circular way to specify the causal model.
