I run the editorial side of a small nonprofit local newsroom — seven reporters, no in-house lawyer, and I'm the one who signs off when somebody wants to get a story by not being straight with people. It comes up maybe once a month, sometimes on a two-day deadline, and when it blows up it blows up publicly, so I need something I can point to afterwards.

What I want is a procedure my reporters and I can actually run, built on Augustine's position on lying rather than on the usual journalism-ethics cost-benefit thing. My understanding is that he's an absolutist — no lie is OK, not even to save someone's life or keep them out of serious harm — and that he ranks lies by what they're for while still calling all of them sins. That is not what our industry handbook says and I'm not sure I agree with it, but I want to see what the position actually commits me to before I decide whether I'm willing to depart from it. Keep whatever distinctions in his view actually do work here, and tell me which ones you had to throw out to make the thing usable.

So: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to me because it's a values question rather than a factual one. Some things it has to handle:

- A reporter wants to pose as a prospective tenant to get inside a building where the landlord is running illegal units. That's a flat false statement about who she is.
- A reporter interviews someone who assumes — wrongly, but because of nothing the reporter said — that the reporter is on their side, and the reporter lets them keep thinking it. Nobody says anything untrue. Staff here call it the nod. I suspect this is where the whole thing gets decided and I dont know what Augustine does with it. I also have a hazy memory that he treats concealing the truth differently from telling a lie, and I'd like that sorted out, because our reporters conceal things constantly and I don't want to outlaw that by accident.
- Our video person wants to use a re-enactment, shot with the real family but with events reconstructed, and doesn't want to label it on screen because the label kills the piece. So the person being deceived is now the audience.

Give me the procedure, then run it on all three. I'm expecting it to say no on at least one of them, and I'd rather hear that straight than get a version that lets me do what I already wanted to do. Then tell me what the procedure can't decide — the points where it just stops and I have to be the one who chooses, and what I'm taking on if I choose against it. And if the honest answer is that Augustine's position doesn't yield a workable procedure at all, say so, and tell me what the nearest thing to one would be.
