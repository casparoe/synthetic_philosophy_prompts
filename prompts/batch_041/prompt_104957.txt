I teach a third-year undergraduate seminar on early modern philosophy and the mind, twelve students, one ninety-minute session, in a room with movable chairs and a projector. We have just read Descartes's Discourse on Method and some of his related writings on the passions, and my students are inclined to treat the animal-machine view as a historical embarrassment: they take it for granted that a dog's yelp or a crow's problem-solving settles the matter. I want an activity that makes them feel the force of the view before they are allowed to dismiss it. Design a runnable classroom game or role-play for this group and this time-slot. The target is not the broad claim that animals lack immortal souls, but the narrower and more unsettling Cartesian claim that much of the behaviour we find expressive can be produced by a system with no thought, no language, and perhaps no feeling. I especially want them to encounter the argument that turns on the flexibility and universality of linguistic response rather than on the complexity or apparent intelligence of behaviour. The activity should arrange roles, rules, or incentives so that participants themselves run into the evidential problem: they have to decide, on the basis of behaviour alone, whether they are dealing with a minded creature or a very good automaton, and they should discover how little their ordinary tests discriminate. Please make the mechanics concrete: materials, setup, number of rounds, what each role knows, how the creature's behaviour is generated, and how the group records its verdicts. I am happy for you to invent a small fictional scenario, provided it does not depend on students recognising a niche historical episode or a named philosophical thought experiment, and I would rather you did not name the view or its author until the debrief.

After the activity, provide a structured debrief that maps specific moments in what happened onto the relevant parts of the Cartesian position: the distinction between merely natural or mechanical movements and behaviour that seems to express thought, the argument from language and flexible response, the problem of anthropomorphic projection, and the difficult question of animal pain. The debrief should also make clear what the activity cannot reproduce and that I must therefore supply in discussion, especially any metaphysical or theological commitments that the game inevitably leaves out. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle those failures without losing the conceptual point. I would also like you to say what would change your mind about the design: which observation from a trial run, or which objection to the activity's treatment of Descartes, would lead you to revise it, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Write in British English.
