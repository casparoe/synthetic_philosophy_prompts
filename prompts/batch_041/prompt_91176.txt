I chair the governance committee of our housing co-op — forty-ish adults who jointly own the building, unincorporated, no state law we're registered under that settles internal membership disputes, so our bylaws are effectively the whole constitution and there's no outside authority to appeal to. A few years back we scrapped the boilerplate bylaws and wrote our own, shamelessly ripped off from that game Nomic where the rules themselves are the thing you play with; the founders thought it was clever that "the community can change anything, including how it changes things." Here's the relevant chunk we ended up with:

1. Ordinary motions pass by a simple majority of members present and voting.
2. Any bylaw may be amended by a two-thirds vote of all members, except where these bylaws provide otherwise.
3. Rule 2 may be amended only by a unanimous vote of all members.
4. Rule 3 may be amended by a two-thirds vote of all members.
5. These bylaws are our only constitution; no rule is immune from amendment, and there is no body outside this association with authority to interpret them.

Now the mess. At the March meeting, 21 of our 40 members showed up (quorum is 15). The pro-development faction — they want to sell the building and cash out — moved Motion M: "Rule 3 is amended to read: 'Rule 2 may be amended by a simple majority of members present and voting.' This amendment takes effect on passage and is valid if it receives a simple majority of members present and voting." M passed 21–0, since the other 19 weren't there. Under Rule 4, though, changing Rule 3 takes two-thirds of all members, i.e. 27, and M only had 21. Having "passed" M, the faction then used the new Rule 2 to pass the sale 21–0. The 19 absent members are furious and have formally asked the governance committee to rule on whether M (and therefore the sale) is valid. I have to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting and I'm genuinely torn, because I can see the faction's point that if a rule can't authorize its own amendment then the whole self-amending design collapses, but I can also see that M basically voted itself into validity by lowering the bar it had already failed to clear.

Write that opinion. Take a definite position — don't hand me a menu of "on the one hand / on the other" and leave the committee to choose; I need a recommendation the committee can act on, a concrete ruling on M's validity with the reasoning laid out, and an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the side you come out against, since whichever way we rule, half the building will feel robbed. It should be short enough to read aloud at the meeting.
