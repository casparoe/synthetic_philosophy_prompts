How old are nations? The question looks like an empirical one, but after a year of arguing about it in a reading group I am no longer sure that it is, and I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict on the substance.

The positions, as concretely as I can put them. The modernists hold that nations and nationalism are products of modernity: Gellner ties them to the needs of industrial society, Hobsbawm treats national traditions as recently invented, often fabricated from above, Anderson treats the nation as an imagined community made possible by print capitalism. The ethnosymbolists, above all Anthony Smith, grant that nationalism is modern but insist that the nations it produced rest on premodern ethnic cores — his ethnies, with their proper names, ancestry myths, shared memories and links to a homeland — and that the modernists' own cases show this. Hastings goes further and argues that a nation in the full sense already existed in medieval England.

The odd thing is how much we agree about. Everyone accepts that many ostensibly ancient traditions are recent: the modern small kilt is credited to an English Quaker, Thomas Rawlinson, in the 1720s, whereas the belted plaid it was cut down from is attested from the sixteenth century. Everyone accepts that Smith's myth-symbol complexes are genuinely premodern. Everyone accepts that nationalism is a modern ideology. The argument is entirely about what follows from that, and it has not moved.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that we are running together several questions that can be pulled apart: (1) when a mass political identification with a bounded people claiming self-rule first appeared (an empirical question about dating); (2) how much premodern ethnic material was available for nationalists to draw on (also empirical); (3) whether something counts as a nation only if it is modern in the modernists' sense, which is a decision about what the word 'nation' is to mean; (4) what would show that a given tradition is old — documentary continuity, continuity of practice, or the participants' own belief in its antiquity; and (5) what the debunking is for, politically and morally.

Please diagnose this. Which of (1)–(5) is doing the work, and in what proportions? Once the definitions are fixed, is the residual dispute a genuine empirical one, or does it dissolve? Work through the cases where the definitional question bites hardest — whether the medieval university 'nations' or the voting nations of the fifteenth-century church councils count as nations, and whether Hastings's medieval England is a counterexample or a redescription — and say what turns on the answer. If the impasse is mostly definitional, is that a defect in the debate or a legitimate division of labour between parties asking different questions under one heading? And what would count as progress: a stipulated definition, a properly dated case study, a criterion for 'ethnie' that does independent work, a change of question — or is there reason to expect none?

Argue the diagnosis from what each side actually claims; I do not need a reading list. Close with a final line of exactly this form: \answer{X; Y}, where X is your primary label from the list below, with a second label after a plus sign if one is needed, and Y is a clause of no more than fifteen words stating what would count as progress.
