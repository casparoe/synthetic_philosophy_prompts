I'm a year into a cog sci MA and I've got a reading group going with a guy named Ravi who's smarter than me and much more anoying about it. We've been fighting through global workspace theory for a paper on whether the access/phenomenal distinction survives it, and he has an argument I keep losing to in conversation even though I'm fairly sure the conclusion is nuts. Here it is as he states it. (1) A state is phenomenally conscious when there's something it's like for the subject to be in it. (2) The best-supported empirical story we have says a state is conscious when its content is broadcast in a global workspace -- that broadcast, i.e. availability to the rest of the system for report and control, is what the difference between a conscious and an unconscious state amounts to, not some extra glow laid on top. (3) If that's all the difference amounts to, then anything with the right kind of global availability has conscious states; there's nothing left over for such a system to be missing. (4) Global availability is an architectural-functional matter, and functional organization doesn't care what it's made of -- neurons, silicon, or, as he likes to put it, people with a very good filing system. (5) Nothing in the theory says the workspace has to be fast, or has to fit inside one skull; it says contents have to be broadcast widely enough to be available for report, control and integration. (6) So take a large insurance company whose internal communication has been ruthlessly optimized for a century: every department files into a central clearing house, everything routes through it, decisions draw on information from all over the firm. Give it a slow clock, one "moment" of processing per week, and by (2) through (5) it has a global workspace. (7) So there's something it's like to be the company. It's just very slow, and it can't tell you about it in under a year. (8) I say that's a reductio. He says it isn't: functionalists were always going to have to say that a big enough organization with the right internal organization is a subject, "it sounds weird" isn't an argument, and if you won't pay that bill you were never really a functionalist about consciousness, you just liked the neuroscience.

I'd rather not give up (2) -- GWT is the only story about consciousness I can actually see how to test, and I'm not going back to thinking consciousness is soul-stuff. And I'd rather not give up (8). So which step is doing the illegitimate work? If it's (3), tell me exactly what a system could have all the availability in the world and still be missing, and say whether that thing is supposed to be detectable from the inside, from the outside, or from neither -- because if it's neither, I want to know why I should believe in it at all. If it's (4) or (5), tell me what "global" is supposed to be relative to, and whether the boundary around a system is a fact about the world or a fact about where we find it convenient to stop drawing. And price it for me: what do I lose by rejecting each one, and is there anything short of going full illusionist or full panpsychist that actually gets me out of this?
