Please argue against the thesis that a composite object is nothing over and above its parts, by attacking the analogy on which the thesis typically rests. The analogy I have in mind is the one that treats composition on the model of set-formation: just as a set is nothing over and above its members — there is no additional item, the "set," created when the members are gathered — so a whole is nothing over and above its parts, and so the demand for a special answer to the special composition question (an answer that says when composition occurs) is misguided, because composition is automatic, universal, and ontologically innocent. My colleagues in the department lean on this picture constantly: they say that asking when some things compose a further thing is like asking when some things form a set, and that since no one thinks there is a mysterious moment at which a set springs into being, no one should think there is a mysterious moment at which a whole does either. I want you to audit this analogy rather than simply reject it. Begin by laying out the correspondence explicitly — what is supposed to map onto what (members to parts, the set to the whole, membership to parthood, the axioms of extensionality and foundation to whatever plays their role on the mereological side, and so on) — and then tell me, correspondence by correspondence, where the mapping fails. I am especially interested in the disanalogies that bear on the conclusion my colleagues want, namely that composition needs no special answer: the fact that membership, unlike parthood, is not transitive; that sets are individuated entirely by their members while wholes notoriously survive the replacement of their parts; that sets are abstract and lack location, mass, and causal powers while wholes are supposed to have them; that there is a unique empty set but no mereological analogue; and that a set is a single thing that cannot be partially identical with another set, whereas overlapping wholes seem able to share parts without being identical. Separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the "composition is innocent" conclusion from those that are merely decorative, and then tell me whether the conclusion can be reached by some better route, or whether it collapses once the analogy is withdrawn. Finally, consider one repair that a defender might attempt — replacing the set analogy with the analogy of a heap or pile, where there is supposedly no fact of the matter about when the heap begins — and say whether that repair fares any better, or whether it merely relocates the original problem.
