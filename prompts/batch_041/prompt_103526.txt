I chair the research ethics committee at a mid-sized university hospital. Our recurring failure is not that we approve bad protocols or reject good ones; it is that we cannot agree on when to stop asking for further justification. One faction treats any unresolved question as grounds for deferral, so reviews drag on for months and investigators learn to bury us in documentation. Another treats the principal investigator's professional standing, or the mere fact that a practice is common locally, as sufficient warrant, so our approvals sometimes rest on nothing more than seniority. I want to replace this with a written procedure that any member of the committee can run.

Write me that procedure. It must be usable by reviewers who are not philosophers, in a thirty-minute first pass, and it must be defensible when the investigator appeals to the medical director, who signs off on the final record.

The procedure should take the form of an ordered set of tests: what question the reviewer asks at each step, what counts as a sufficient answer, when a chain of reasons may be closed, when it must be recorded as unresolved, and when it must be handed to the full committee as a values decision rather than a factual one. Ground the stopping rules in the three standard positions on the regress of justification — foundationalism, the view that a chain must terminate in something that needs no further support; coherentism, the view that a claim is justified by its fit with what we already accept; and infinitism, the view that justification may legitimately continue without end — and say explicitly which moves in your procedure come from which position, and where you are deliberately compromising among them rather than applying any one of them cleanly.

Our committee has already adopted two house rules, which the procedure has to accommodate and whose status you should assess. The No Private Anchors rule: no chain of justification may be closed by appeal to a claim whose only support is the proposer's own authority, seniority, or the fact that the practice is common here. The Two-Door requirement: a chain may be closed either at a door anyone on the committee could open for themselves — a published standard, a measurement, a legal requirement — or at a door the committee explicitly votes to hold shut, with that vote recorded as a values judgment. Tell me whether these two rules are coherent, whether they smuggle in one of the three positions under another name, and whether together they leave any protocol unjustifiable in principle.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First, a placebo-controlled trial whose justification for withholding an available treatment rests on the claim that the design is standard in the field. Second, a first-in-human dosing study whose risk estimate rests on an unpublished pilot the investigator ran in their own laboratory. Third, a protocol the committee is ethically sympathetic to but whose central assumption cannot be closed at an open door without a vote the committee would rather not take. For each, show the steps, the verdict, and the point at which the procedure stops and a person must decide. At least one verdict should be one the committee will dislike.

Finish by stating what the procedure cannot do: which disagreements it will merely relocate, which of its stopping points are conventions rather than discoveries, and how a reviewer should behave when the procedure returns unresolved.
