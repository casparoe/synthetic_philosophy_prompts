I'm taking a historiography seminar this term and I've got a term paper due on Rashomon in three weeks, and I've spent two of them circling it without writting a single paragraph. The course has basically been three positions slugging it out. Ranke, at least on the reading we got of him: the historian's whole job is to show the past as it essentially was, and the way you get there is back to the archive, the primary source, the eyewitness report, letting the evidence speak instead of your own opinions — self-effacement as method. E.H. Carr: no, the past isn't history until a historian selects something out of it and puts it to work, so facts of the past and historical facts are different animals, and the selection is where the historian actually lives. Hayden White: you're both being naive about your own prose, because before you can narrate anything at all you've already emplotted it as romance or tragedy or comedy or satire, and that's a choice with consequences, not a discovery. Then in week 6 the professor screened the 1950 Kurosawa film as a palate cleanser and the seminar split instantly. Half of them said it's naive relativism — four people, four stories, truth is whatever you say it is. The other half said no, the film obviously believes something happened in that grove and the horror is precisely that we can't get to it, which is a completely different claim. The professor refused to settle it, which I'm now fairly sure was deliberate, and everyone has an opinion and I have to write about it.

What I want from you is not a plot summary — I've seen it four times, the woodcutter and the priest and the commoner under the gate, Tajomaru, the wife, the medium, all of it. I want a reading of the film against those three positions that's honest in both directions: it shouldn't turn the movie into an illustration of White, and it shouldn't pretend the film is a philosophy paper. So: which of the three, if any, does the film actually dramatize? Where does each genuinely illuminate something — is the woodcutter's fourth account a Carr-style act of selection, or is the mere fact that he's telling it already a White-style emplottment, and does his admission about the dagger change what kind of testimony it is? Does the film have a position, or does the ending at the gate with the baby commit it to one? And say plainly where the film resists all three, because my suspicion is that it cares about something older and nastier — self-deception, everyone narrating themselves as the honorable one — and that historiography is my imposition rather than Kurosawa's.

Two things the professor tossed off that I didn't write down and want brought in properly. First, apparently White wrote a short piece later about history on film and coined a term for it — historiophoty, or something close — arguing that film represents the past in visual images rather than verbal discourse and so has its own rules and its own ways of being accurate or misleading. Does that help here? Is Rashomon doing something a written version of the same four testimonies couldn't — the grove looking different each time, the light, the staging of the medium scene so we can't tell whether the samurai is really speaking — or is film worse off because it can't quote, cite, or argue, and so can't be objective in Ranke's sense at all? Second, there's a different Carr, a phenomenologist (David, no relation to E.H., which is maddening), who wrote a book called Time, Narrative and History and thinks White and Louis Mink have it backwards: narrative isn't a literary imposition on a shapeless past, it's already a structure of lived experience, which is why we can't stop telling stories in the first place. If he's right, does Rashomon stop being a film about the impossibility of truth and become a film about the impossibility of not narrating? That's the question I most want answered, because I think it decides the whole paper. Finish by telling me what the film contributes to the debate that the arguments on their own don't, and where a careful viewer has to concede that it just isn't about any of this.
