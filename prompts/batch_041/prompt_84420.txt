I'm a third-year PhD in legal theory, and I'm trying to write the framing chapter for a thesis on legal moralism, and I keep bouncing off the Hart-Devlin thing because it reads like a shouting match where each side is answering a question the other one isn't asking. I want my chapter to actually move the thing, not just restate it.

So here's what I want from you: draft the joint statement that a committed legal moralist and a committed anti-moralist could both put their names to after a genuinely good-faith adversarial collaboration.

To fix the two positions: the moralist is Devlin-ish -- a society is entitled to criminalize conduct because it's morally wrong, even when nobody is harmed, because a shared morality is part of what holds the society together. The anti-moralist is Hart/Mill-ish -- the only legitimate ground for criminalization is harm to others, plus maybe some narrow business about offense. Both are sophisticated, neither is a cartoon, and neither gets to win by being stated charitably while the other gets stated as a strawman.

What I want in the document:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. Not platitudes -- real content. What they both agree the criminal law is for, what each concedes about the other's position, what the correct neutral description is of the tired old cases (Wolfenden, the "victimless" offences, consensual-adult stuff), where they agree on burdens of proof, and where they agree on what would even count as a harm.

2. A disagreements section -- table or numbered, your call -- with each side's position written in wording that side would accept for itself. If the moralist's position, stated honestly, turns out to be a form of paternalism, then say so and let them object in their own voice rather than papering it over.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions where settling one would actually move at least one side. My guess is one is empirical (does tolerating X actually erode the shared morality, and can "social disintegration" even be measured?) and one is conceptual (is "harm to the moral environment" a genuine harm or just offense wearing a trenchcoat?). But dont just take my guesses -- find the real ones, and tell me if one of mine is a dud.

4. For each crux, the case or argument both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. This is the part I care about most.

Two extra things to fold in.

First, I want the statement to test a proposal I've been kicking around, from a made-up theorist I've been calling practice-dependent moralism: the state may criminalize a morally wrong act only if (a) tolerating the act would undermine some norm the society actually depends on to coordinate an ongoing practice, and (b) that norm can't be sustained by less coercive means. I want to know whether both camps can sign that, and whether it's a genuine middle ground or whether it quietly collapses into one side. I suspect it collapses into Hart, but I want the moralist's best case that it doesn't.

Second, stress-test the whole thing against one concrete live case. A jurisdiction is deciding whether to keep criminalizing consensual sex between adult siblings -- both over 21, no coercion, no dependency, no reproduction. Nobody is claiming a third party gets harmed. The moralist says the incest taboo is load-bearing for family structure; the liberal says that's the exact Devlin move and it fails for the usual resons. Walk both camps through the case using the agreed claims, and be honest about whether they end up disagreeing or just describing the same facts in different words.

And at the end: if part of the dispute turns out to be verbal -- the two sides using "harm" or "society" or "morality" in incompatible senses -- say which part and why, and whether fixing the vocabulary would resolve anything or just relocate the fight.

Write it like something a journal would plausibly publish as a co-authored statement, so no hedging both ways in every sentence. I want real commitments from both sides.
