I retired last year after thirty-odd years as a chemical engineer, and I've been making up for lost time by sitting in on philosophy courses at the university near me. This term it's an upper-level value theory seminar, and the instructor — who has been very patient with my habit of asking whether everything isn't just a matter of error bars — has asked me to draft one of the essay questions for the final. I'd like your help doing it properly: please write the question itself, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme.

Here's the topic I want. We spent a week on a puzzle that has been eating at me, and I think it's exactly the kind of thing that seperates students who actually understand the problem from students who have memorized the vocabulary. Take a long series of things that can be compared in value — cups of coffee, draft versions of a paper, years of a life. Order them so each item is only a hair worse than its neighbour: shown any two neighbours, nobody would say the difference matters. Yet the two ends of the series are definately, plainly different in value. So where did the 'obviously worse' go? The moves on the menu, as I understand them, are: say that 'better than' simply isn't transitive; or say there's a sharp cutoff somewhere; or say many of the pairs aren't really comparable at all (some people in the seminar kept calling this being 'on a par', or 'parity' — if the model answer uses the word it should define it, since I gather it's contested). One of the grad students also insisted the real action is in the gap between 'better than' and 'at least as good as' — that you might keep betterness transitive and still get trouble if the tie-like relation misbehaves. I want the question to make the student commit: pick a position, say what it costs, and show they can't keep all three of transitivity, the absence of a cutoff, and the plainness of the endpoint difference — one has to go. And I want them to face the objection that if 'better than' is intransitive, a clever bookmaker can walk you round a cycle and charge you for the privilege; they should say whether that really shows the view is irrational or whether it's a trick. Coming from a world where every measurement has error bars, I keep wanting to say the neighbour-differences are real but sit below the resolution of our judgement — I gather that's one of the positions on the menu and not a free way out, so the model answer should say why.

Please make the question a single essay prompt a student could answer in ninety minutes — not a list of sub-questions — and then give me the model answer and a marking scheme naming the specific moves a top answer must make, the marks each is worth (total 20), and a one-line note on the mistake that typically costs students each of those marks. The students won't have web access, so nothing should depend on citing particular papers, and please don't let it become a name-dropping exercise about who said what — I'd rather it stand on its own.
