I was present at a seminar discussion between two fellow students, Ines and Marcus, and they have asked me to referee an argument I could not settle. The subject is Aquinas on tyrannicide. I would like you to act as an expert in medieval political thought and in Aquinas's moral theology, and to adjudicate their exchange move by move. Please think carefully about the main interpretive options before you commit to a verdict, but write your final answer as continuous prose. Do not use headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Do not give me a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Answer in British English.

Ines began: Aquinas is a consistent opponent of tyrannicide. In the Summa Theologiae he treats sedition as always a mortal sin, and killing a tyrant is a seditious act; in On Kingship he warns that private individuals must not kill a tyrant, and that a tolerable tyranny should be borne rather than overthrown, since the remedy may be worse than the disease. So the apparent permission for resistance is misleading.

Marcus replied: That is too quick. In the Summa Aquinas says a tyrannical government is not just and that disturbing it is not sedition, because the tyrant is the one who fosters discord. He forbids private individuals from taking over the role of public authority, but he does not forbid resistance as such. In the early Sentences commentary he even says that killing a usurping tyrant can be praiseworthy. So Aquinas allows tyrannicide under some conditions.

Ines answered: The early text is an outlier. The mature view, especially in On Kingship, repudiates assassination. It says the remedy for tyranny is public deposition or recourse to God, not the dagger. It also warns that killing one tyrant often produces a worse one. The Summa's restriction to public authority is in effect a ban on private tyrannicide, which is what people normally mean by tyrannicide. Marcus is conflating public resistance with tyrannicide.

Marcus said: But public deposition can involve killing, and Aquinas's principle is not never kill a tyrant but never kill a tyrant without public authority. If a legitimate public authority deposes a tyrant and he resists, lethal force may be justified. Also, in the Sentences he distinguishes a tyrant by usurpation from a tyrant who abuses a legitimate title. For a usurper, there is no legitimate authority to appeal to, so private killing may be permitted. That is not a retraction but a development: the later works specify the authority condition more clearly.

Ines replied: Development or not, the later works are more restrictive. The Sentences may have said that a usurper can be killed by anyone, but the Summa says it is not the business of private individuals to overthrow a tyrant, full stop. And On Kingship says even public deposition is dangerous and should be a last resort. So the mature view is that tyrannicide is almost never justified. The early text is not a reliable guide.

Marcus came back: Almost never is not never. And the mature view still permits resistance by public authority, and it still says that disturbing a tyrannical government is not sedition. So Ines is overstating the restriction. The distinction between a tyrant by defect of title and a tyrant by abuse of power explains why the early text is not simply abandoned: the later works focus on the case where the tyrant has some title, where private action is forbidden. The early text focuses on the usurper, where the title is absent. The two can be reconciled.

Ines answered: Then the reconciliation makes the view depend on a controversial theory of legitimacy. Who counts as public authority? If a usurper is in power, who has the authority to kill him? Marcus says anyone; Aquinas later says only public authority. That is a substantive change, not a mere clarification. And the Summa's exception for excessive tyranny still says recourse to God, not to the knife. So the later view is a principled rejection of private tyrannicide.

Marcus said: But the Summa also says that if the tyranny is excessive, the tyrant is guilty of sedition, and resisting him is not sedition. That gives a moral permission to resist. The question is only who may do it. So Ines is right that private assassination is forbidden, but wrong that Aquinas is a quietist about tyranny. He is a constitutionalist about resistance, not an absolute pacifist.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move, not merely overall. For each of the main claims they make, say whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, and briefly justify your rating. Then say who is right about which specific point. In particular, address whether the early and later texts can be reconciled by the distinction between a tyrant by defect of title and a tyrant by abuse of power, or whether the later texts represent a genuine development or retraction. Also say what principle best captures Aquinas's considered view, and what both Ines and Marcus are missing. Do not simply say that both are partly right; give a genuine verdict on the disputed moves.
