I sit on the board of a small land trust, and we are about to decide what to do with a 180-acre parcel that was left to us five years ago. As it stands the land is exhausted cattle pasture: close-cropped grass, a few thickets of invasive shrub, a straight drainage ditch, low species counts. Our conservation plan calls for restoring it to the tallgrass prairie and oak savanna that stood there before, which over a decade or so would bring back hundreds of plant species and, with them, very large numbers of insects, songbirds, voles, snakes, and the predators that feed on them. This is what every member of the board assumed we would do; it is essentially why the donor left us the land.

A retired biology teacher on the board has thrown a wrench into this. She argues that the restored prairie, judged by the interests of the animals themselves rather than by the richness of the species list, may be the crueler option. Her reasoning, as I understand it, runs roughly as follows. The pasture as it stands supports a few hundred large, long-lived animals — the cattle, some deer, the usual birds — whose lives are for the most part tolerable. The restored prairie would support millions of small, short-lived animals whose lives are mostly nasty and brief: nearly all of them die before they reproduce, and they die by predation, parasitism, exposure, or starvation. Restoration would therefore multiply the number of beings who live and die, and would shift the population away from creatures whose lives tend to go well toward creatures whose lives tend to go badly. If our real concern is the welfare of animals, she says, we may be about to do the wrong thing — and the same reasoning would apply to every habitat-protection project the trust runs. She added, only half-jokingly, that by her logic the kindest use of the land might be to pave it, since then no animals would be born to suffer. I do not believe that, but I cannot immediately say why not, and that bothers me.

A third board member thinks the whole line of thought is absurd: a world with more life is better than a world with less, and in any case we cannot seriously compare an insect's suffering with a cow's, let alone with a person's. I find myself agreeing with each of them at different moments, and I no longer trust my own reactions. I would like advice on what to do, but more than that I want to be told how to think about the choice, rather than being reassured with the usual things one says about conservation.

Please structure your answer as follows, in this order, with roughly the lengths indicated:

1. What the decision actually turns on (150–250 words): identify the real questions in dispute, separating the empirical ones from the conceptual ones.
2. The case for restoring the prairie (350–450 words): the strongest version of the position I assumed going in, stated so that it survives the teacher's objection rather than ignoring it.
3. The case against restoring it (350–450 words): the strongest version of the teacher's position, including whether her "pave it" remark can be avoided without giving up the rest of her argument.
4. The points that would settle it (300–400 words): the factual and conceptual uncertainties on which the choice hinges, and for each, what you would need to know and how confident you are that it is knowable at all.
5. My recommendation (200–300 words): a concrete course of action for the board, with the reasoning compressed.
6. What would change your mind (150–250 words): the argument or consideration that would move you to the opposite recommendation, and an honest statement of where your reasoning is most likely to fail.

In section 6 I want you to name the specific consideration that would flip your verdict, not merely a general caveat, and to say which of your steps in sections 2 through 5 you regard as the weakest link.
