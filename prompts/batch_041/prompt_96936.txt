I have been defending a view about the special composition question in a reading group, and I would like to know how exposed the view really is. In rough outline: composition is neither unrestricted nor impossible; it occurs exactly when the members of a plurality stand in a certain real, non-conventional relation to one another, which I have been calling cohesion. My working gloss is that a plurality is cohesive when there is a robust, counterfactual-supporting interdependence among its members' states — perturb one member and the others' states change in ways that depend on the perturbed member's contribution — and when that interdependence is not merely the aggregate of pairwise relations imposed on the plurality from outside. On this view a living organism, a molecule, and a running engine compose; a heap of sand, a scattered plurality of stars, and the set of all red things do not; and whether some things compose is a question about the world rather than about our concepts or our language. I am aware this puts me between the two answers that get most of the attention, and I want to know what my position actually risks.

So please tell me what would count against it. I want the observations, arguments, intuitions, and possible cases that should move me, sorted by how decisive they would be. I have some in mind and would like them assessed rather than merely listed: the argument from vagueness, which says any restriction on composition must draw a sharp line and so must either admit borderline cases of composition or be rejected; cases where cohesion appears to come in degrees, as when a heap is gradually cemented into a block, so that the view seems to inherit exactly the cut-off problem it was meant to avoid; cases of apparent causal unification without composition, such as a predator and its prey whose populations are mutually tracking, two clocks kept in synchrony by their coupling, or a pair of particles whose measured states are correlated in a way no local interaction explains; and cases of composition without cohesion, such as a corpse, a pile that everyone treats as a single object, or a whole whose parts are held together only by external pressure. I am also uneasy that the clause about relations imposed from outside may be doing all the work while remaining circular — if cohesion is defined as the relation that obtains just when composition occurs, the view may be trivially true — and I would like to know whether that is a real defect or only a presentational one.

Finally, I would like an honest verdict on the shape of the position rather than a list of objections. Which of these defeaters would a committed cohesion theorist concede as counting against them, and which would they reasonably dismiss? Which of them are fatal, which merely awkward, and which turn out on inspection to be demands the view never had to meet? If you conclude that the view has been insulated by its own qualifications, or that it collapses into one of the answers it was meant to displace, say so plainly and tell me whether that is a problem for a view of this kind or simply what a view about composition looks like once it is stated carefully. I would find it most persuasive if you tell me which single case or argument, if it held up, would leave me with nothing distinctive to say.
