Me and a few friends run a small philosophy reading group and we've spent three weeks going around in circles about promising, mostly because we keep sliding between different questions without noticing. I want a handout that actually sorts us. So please build me a decision tree for "which theory of promissory obligation do you actually hold?"

Rules for the tree: every internal node should be a question someone can answer without already knowing which view it leads to, so no "do you think promissory obligation is sui generis?" — that's just asking for the label. Make each split a concrete case or a plain yes/no claim. Some splits I definitely want somewhere on the tree (add your own as well): does keeping the promise matter because of what it does to the promisee — their expectations, their reliance, or their standing to demand — or because of what it does to you, like your integrity or your ability to bind your future self, or because of the social practice the promise is an instance of? Does the promise create a brand-new reason, or just change what the pre-existing reasons already imply? Does the promisee need to accept, or even know about, the promise? Can the promisee release you? Is breaking it the sort of thing the promisee can hold you to, or just a reason you've outweighed? And is a promise to yourself a promise at all?

Push the tree through at least these cases: a promise to a dying person who dies before you can keep it; a promise made to a mugger under threat; a promise you genuinely don't remember making; an implicit promise (you agree to meet someone at 8 and just don't show); a promise to a six-year-old; a promise whose point has evaporated by the time you could keep it; and a promise whose fulfillment you later learn will do real harm.

Each leaf should carry the name of the position — or "unnamed" if nobody actually defends it — plus one sentence on that view's most awkward commitment. I want at least: the practice/convention view, the expectation-or-reliance view, the "the promisee gets authority over you" view, the self-binding/integrity view, the second-personal/accountability view, a consequentialist reduction (say whether the act and rule versions land in different places), the virtue-of-fidelity view, and the flat deflationary view that there is no special promissory obligation at all.

Two extras. Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really seperate instead of papering over it with a branch. And note which combinations of answers nobody occupies, and say whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

American English, casual tone. Don't bother with citations or a reading list — I can't check them and I want the ideas.
