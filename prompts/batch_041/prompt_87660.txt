Ok so a friend of mine who's a few years into a lit-crit PhD gave me her thirty-second version of the whole "does the author's intention decide what a text means" debate, and I want a second opinion before I go around repeating any of it. Roughly what she said:

"Intentionalism is the view that a text means whatever its author intended it to mean — the author's intention is the thing that fixes the meaning. The standard refutation is the intentional fallacy, which shows the author's intention is irrelevent to what the text means: once the words are down on the page, the author has no special authority, and the only meaning that counts is the meaning the words have in the language. So the author's intention is just a fact about their psychology, not about the text. And since you usually can't recover the intention anyway — authors lie, forget, and die — the text just doesn't have a determinate meaning, and you're free to read it any way the conventions allow. The one exception is what the author meant the words themselves to mean; that you have to respect. But what they meant to *do* by writing — move you, shock you, make a point — none of that touches the meaning."

I think she's right that you shouldn't just take an author's word for what their book means — if a novelist says the novel is about grief but every page reads as jealousy, I'll side with jealousy. But "intention is irrelevant" and "so there's no determinate meaning" both feel too strong, and I don't know if that's actually what the intentional fallacy says or if she's just repeating something she half-remembers from seminar. Can you go through her summary claim by claim — what's accurate, what's the kind of thing that sounds right in a seminar but is actually a distortion, what a defender of authorial intention would push back on — and then tell me whether her jump to "no determinate meaning" actually follows from any of the true parts? Also, that carve-out she makes at the end, where only "what the words were meant to mean" counts and not "what the author wanted the writing to do" — is that the real dividing line or did she just make it up to save the view?
