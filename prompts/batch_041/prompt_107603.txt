I'm putting together a session for our lab reading group — half economists, half cog sci people — and we keep talking past each other about satisficing, so I want a decision tree I can hand out that sorts people by what they actually think rather than by which camp they'd say they're in.

Start from something nobody in the room will deny: real agents (and real models of agents) can't consider everything, and deliberation stops somewhere. Then build out. The first split I care about is whether, at the moment it stops, there's anything the agent is maximizing at all — and if yes, whether the stopping threshold itself was picked by a calculation or just given to the agent from outside. From there I want the tree to separate, at minimum: the view that the stopping rule is optimized subject to the cost of the computation itself; plain satisficing where the aspiration level is a fixed target that drifts with experience rather than being chosen; the view that stopping is a failure, i.e. a bias; the view that a simple stop rule is a *virtue* in the right environment; the view that what's bounded is the theorist's model rather than the agent, so 'bounded rationality' is really about tractable modeling; and a deflationary leaf where there's no fact about whether the agent optimized, only about which description earns its keep.

Other things I want the tree to force a decision on: whether the limits are on computation (time, memory, attention) or on information (noise, hidden variables), and whether the claim is meant as psychology or only as prediction.

Use concrete cases for the questions, not abstractions — the jam aisle with thirty jars, a triage rule that says stop screening after N, a hiring committee that takes the first acceptable candidate, a chess player who stops calculating once a move is good enough, an animal leaving a food patch, someone deciding they've read enough reviews to buy a toaster. Nobody should be able to answer by recognizing a label.

Each leaf gets the name of the position (or 'unnamed' if nobody really holds it) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and flag any leaves the tree can't pull apart — I'd bet the as-if optimizer and the deflationist can't be separated by any question about the agent's head, but tell me if I'm wrong. Also mark the combinations nobody occupies and say briefly whether that's because the combo is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Maybe 6-8 questions, 8-10 leaves. No citations needed, it's a handout.
