I am a retired physical chemist who has spent the past several years reading philosophy on my own, and I have reached the stage where I can follow an argument but not yet reliably tell a sound popularisation from a misleading one. I recently encountered the following summary of two-dimensional semantics in a general-audience book, and I should be grateful if you would audit it for me, sentence by sentence:

'Two-dimensional semantics is the framework, chiefly associated with David Chalmers, on which a single expression carries two intensions rather than one. The primary intension is the one we arrive at by asking what the term would pick out in a given world considered as actual; the secondary intension is the one we get by fixing the actual world and then asking what the term picks out in other, counterfactual worlds. So "water" and "H2O" have the same primary intension — both answer to the same descriptive conditions — but different secondary intensions, which is precisely why "water is H2O" is a discovery rather than a definition. The framework's philosophical dividend is that it explains how a truth can be necessary yet knowable only a posteriori, by showing that such statements carry two meanings. Chalmers presses the apparatus further, against materialism: since a world physically identical to ours but devoid of consciousness is coherently conceivable, and conceivability entails possibility, consciousness cannot be physical. In this way two-dimensional semantics restores the a priori to a central place in philosophy.'

I suspect that much of this is right, and the water example appeals to me because it sits close to my own training; but I cannot shake the feeling that the account has been bent in places so as to make the anti-materialist conclusion look like a consequence of the framework rather than a further step within it. Please separate what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what is simply wrong; indicate where a sympathetic defender of two-dimensional semantics would object and where an unsympathetic critic would be entitled to object; and tell me whether the concluding claim about consciousness follows from anything that is true in the summary. I should add that I have no access to the literature and am not asking to be pointed at sources — I want to grasp the substance itself, not to be sent away to read. Kindly answer in British English.
