Here's what I keep chewing on. There's an old puzzle — I think it's from a philosophy paper, I forget whose — about a man who runs from Death in one city to another, and it turns out the second city is exactly where Death had an appointment with him all along. The nasty part is the loop: whichever city he settles on, the other one starts looking better, because his choice is the very thing that tells him where Death will be. Fine, it's a fairy tale. But real institutions pull this move constantly, and I want to know what they're quietly assuming. A doping authority doesn't announce test dates. A health inspector shows up unnaounced. A tax agency audits a random slice and cheerfully says so. And the part that fascinates me is that secrecy isn't just about not getting caught — even a perfectly fair fixed schedule would be useless, because the people on the other end of it can see it coming and arrange their lives around it. So what view of rational choice does that practice presuppose? It looks to me like it presupposes that a rule can be bad as a rule even though, once you've adopted it, theres nothing better to do — that the value of a mixed policy isn't just the average of the pure policies it's mixed from. Does that commit the agency to a causal rather than an evidential way of evaluating its own options? And does the practice also presuppose something about how its targets think — that they're the sort of agent who'd reason, if I skip today that means they predicted it, so they must be here today, and talk themselves in circles?

Tell me which parts the practice strictly requires, which parts it merely fits well with, and where several incompatible theories would all license the same policy. Then the bit I actually need for the book: if you swap the presupposed view out for a rival one, what would the practice have to look like instead? Concrete, please — what would change on the ground.
