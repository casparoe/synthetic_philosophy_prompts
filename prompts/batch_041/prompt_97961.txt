I am a first-year doctoral student in a lab that sits awkwardly between cognitive science and evolutionary biology, and I have found myself in the middle of a disagreement that has split our group. A postdoc in our lab has been circulating a preprint arguing that the human appetite for immersive fiction is an adaptation for social learning: on her account, narrative absorption recruits our theory-of-mind and cheater-detection machinery so that we can simulate the payoffs of social strategies we would never risk attempting ourselves, and the disposition persists because it improved the calibration of our social strategies over evolutionary time. She proposes to test the idea with a combination of cross-cultural survey work, a developmental prediction (that narrative absorption should come online alongside pretend play and explicit false-belief reasoning), and a comparative prediction about whether other cooperative breeders show anything like it. One of our senior members, an evolutionary psychologist, regards this as a perfectly respectable adaptationist research program; another, who came to us from complex-systems biology and now works on cultural evolution, regards it as a textbook case of the unfalsifiable storytelling that has always embarrassed the field. They have been going back and forth over coffee for weeks, and I have been asked to write up their exchange as a teaching dialogue for our methods seminar.

Please write that dialogue. Both of them should be first-rate — I do not want a strawman on either side, and I would much rather they engage with each other's specific points than trade slogans. I would like the exchange to get genuinely into the weeds: the status of the so-called environment of evolutionary adaptedness; what a design signature is supposed to be and whether the postdoc's predictions actually supply one; whether cross-cultural invariance counts as evidence for adaptation or for something else entirely; whether a byproduct or cultural-evolutionary account is any more testable than the adaptationist one it is meant to displace; and the awkward symmetry that arises when the critic's alternative explanation is itself underdetermined by the data. I want them to concede real ground where it is owed, and to end by locating precisely what they still disagree about — ideally something sharper than a call for more data.

The dialogue should run to roughly 1,500 words and should be usable by students who have had a single graduate-level course in evolutionary theory but no background in the specific literature, so please make the technical commitments explicit enough that a reader can see exactly where the two positions come apart.
