I've agreed in a weak moment to teach Aumann's agreement theorem to a mixed room next month -- two ninety-minute sessions, whiteboard, no slides -- and what I need from you is a plan for the explanation, not the explanation. The room is roughly half third-year economists who've done some decision theory and half philosophy students who've done some epistemology; almost none of them have met the partition model of information, and the two halves have completely different reflexes about what counts as an explanation. I've known this theorem for twenty years, so don't tell me what it says. Tell me what has to land before the theorem can even be stated in a way they'll hear: what the opening has to do, what has to be in place before I'm allowed to say 'common knowledge' at all (I keep discovering to late that they've heard it as 'everyone knows', and then the infinite-regress point sails straight past them), and what order the pieces have to come in so that the conclusion feels like a consequence of the setup rather than a party trick. Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually surface -- 'so rational people always end up agreeing', 'so disagreement is irrational', 'it's an idealization, so it says nothing about my uncle and his politics' -- and whatever else you think they'll bring. For each one I want the specific point in the sequence where I should head it off, not just a list.

On the carrying example I'd rather have your judgment than a free choice. My candidate is two doctors who trained at the same place -- same prior, allegedly -- looking at different test results and disagreeing about whether to operate. Can that carry the whole thing, or does it cheat somewhere: does it quietly present the common prior as an obvious truth, when that's exactly the assumption I want them to leave suspicious of? If it can't carry it, give me one that can, and it has to be drawable on a whiteboad as two overlapping sets of possibilities, because I want to reuse that same picture at the end to show what breaks once the prior isn't shared -- and tell me which misconception that reuse actually kills, if any. Last two things: one question I can put to them at the end that shows me they've got the theorem rather than a slogan, and one thing I should openly refuse to prove at this level and say so out loud. I'm at a British university now and the handout goes straight to the students, so please write the plan itself in British English.
