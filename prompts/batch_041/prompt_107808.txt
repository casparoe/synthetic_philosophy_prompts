I am a retired experimental scientist — thirty years in the physical sciences, a good part of it spent building computational models of things that were only ever observed indirectly — and for the last four years I have been reading philosophy of mind in a more or less systematic way. I have reached the point where I would like to write something of my own rather than read, and I am applying to a small foundation that funds philosophy carried on in contact with the sciences. They ask for a two-page proposal for a one-year, part-time project. I would be grateful if you would act as a professional philosopher of mind with a working knowledge of the cognitive sciences — someone who has thought hard about the metaphysics of computation and about the ethics of moral status — and help me turn a half-formed idea into that proposal.

The half-formed idea. The debate about machine sentience is conducted as a search for the right computational property: integration, global broadcast, recurrent feedback, higher-order representation, and so on. I suspect the search is misdirected, not because these properties are unimportant but because computational properties are attributed to a system only relative to a level of description, and nothing in the physics of the system fixes which description is the right one. If that is so, then the question of which computations matter morally is underdetermined until we say who is entitled to choose the description and on what grounds, and the moral question may have to be answered at the level of our descriptive practices rather than at the level of the system's intrinsic properties. I want to know whether that thought can be made precise, and whether it leads anywhere other than the deflationary conclusion that any answer is as good as any other — which I take to be unacceptable, and which I also suspect is false. A second strand I would like to braid in: even granting a metaphysical answer, all our actual attributions of sentience are made from the outside, on the basis of evidence, under uncertainty, so the operative question for practice is what we are licensed to do when the metaphysics cannot be settled. I do not know whether these are one project or two, and I would like help deciding.

Please think carefully before you write, and begin with a brief brainstorming pass — a short paragraph per candidate, no more — setting out two or three ways of framing a single tractable research question here. For each, say what the question would be, what sort of finding would count as an answer to it, and how likely it is to be settled in a year of part-time work by someone with my background. Then commit to the one you would defend, say why the others are weaker, and write the proposal itself.

The proposal should run to roughly two pages (900–1,200 words) and should contain, in whatever order makes the strongest case: a precise statement of the research question, precise enough that it has a determinate answer and I could recognize that answer if I found it; an account of why the existing positions leave it open, described as positions rather than as the views of named individuals; the method — what I would actually do with the year, month by month, including what I would read, what I would write, and how I would tell whether an argument I had constructed was any good; why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives, in particular why not simply survey the candidate markers of sentience and adjudicate between them, and why not treat the whole matter as an empirical question for the neurosciences; what a successful outcome would look like, and what would count as failure — I would rather have an honest failure condition than a promise of a paper; and a realistic assessment of scope, bearing in mind that I am retired, working part-time, and will spend the first months simply getting up to speed. Where the project turns on a distinction, state the distinction explicitly and say what is lost without it.

Please include no bibliography, reading list, or citations; I will add those myself, and I would rather see the positions described than labeled with names I cannot check. Avoid leaning on particular titles or dates, and if you want to invoke a specific argument or thought experiment, describe it in your own words. Write in a sober first-person register suitable for a foundation that reads a great deal of science and is mildly suspicious of philosophy that never touches anything. If, as you work, you conclude that the project is not worth doing in this form, say so and propose a better question rather than proceeding politely. Quality of reasoning matters here more than polish.
