I'm building the final for my upper-level seminar on the philosophy of biology, and the last chunk of the course is on whether "life" picks out anything real or is just a word we wave at a pile of different phenomena. I want one essay question that actually makes them think, and I want to write it so it can't be answered by reciting. Last year I got thirty blue books that all said the same four things: viruses are the classic borderline case, prions are weird, mules are sterile, and maybe someday we'll have a definition that covers everything. So this year I'm seeding the cases myself, in the question, and making them run the definitions through material they've never seen. The three families of definitions they'll have in their heads from class are these: the metabolic/organizational one, where a living thing is a system that builds and repairs its own parts and maintains its own boundary; the evolutionary one, where what's alive is whatever belongs to a lineage capable of evolution by natural selection; and the working formula astrobiologists keep recycling, life as a chemical system that keeps itself going and is capable of Darwinian evolution. I want those three to collide on my cases.

Here's what I'm seeding. Case one, from a subglacial lake: a membrane-bound packet of chemistry a few microns across that takes in dissolved ions and simple carbon compounds, runs a gradient across its own membrane, and spends that energy building its own lipids and its own catalysts and patching itself when it's damaged. It's been kept in culture for years and it just keeps going. There's no genome and no template of any kind in it — the whole thing runs as a collectively autocatalytic network — so when a current shears one in half, both halves carry on as before. What it doesn't do is hand anything down: the halves come out compositionally the same as what they came from, and there's no heritable variation for selection to get a grip on. Case two, from a hydrothermal pore: a naked self-replicating RNA-ish molecule with no membrane, no metabolism and no internal organization worth the name; it just catalyzes its own copying out of raw nucleotides in the pore water, copies itself sloppily, and its variants compete for the same feedstock, so after a few thousand generations you've got a lineage that is plainly adapting to the pore. I also want a sub-question about an ordinary sterile individual of an ordinary species, because I suspect most of them will not notice what the evolutionary definition does to that case and I want to see who does.

So: write the exam question, then a model answer of around 700 words, then a marking scheme. Twenty marks, seventy-five minutes, closed book, and the scheme should be built on five must-hit moves at four marks each, with a line for each saying what a B answer does instead of the move. I'd like the question to force a verdict from each of the three definitions on each of the two cases, to make them say which of the three they'd actually keep and why, and to make them take a position on whether the word is a natural kind, a cluster of loosely related features, or just a stipulation we set for a purpose — and then defend it against the obvious objection. For what it's worth I genuinely don't know which way I land on case one, so I'd rather the model answer plant a flag than split the difference. Keep names out of the model answer entirely — no citations, no bibliography, and nothing where a scholar's name is doing the work the argument should be doing. Same for the scheme. One more thing: I'm pasting this into a plain-text email to my co-instructor, so no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables anywhere in the response — continuous prose for the question, the answer and the scheme, the way you'd write a long letter. Oh, and tell me at the end which single stipulation in my two cases is doing the most work, and what happens to the whole picture if I quietly change it.
