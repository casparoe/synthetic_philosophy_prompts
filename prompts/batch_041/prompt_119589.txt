I am a PhD student in political theory, and for the past two years I have served as a citizen member of the planning commission in the mid-sized city where I live. We are seven; we meet twice a month; each meeting brings two to five contested applications — rezonings, conditional use permits, variances, appeals from design review — each decided on a staff report circulated a week in advance and a hearing that runs forty minutes. Our written findings are the record the city council reviews on appeal, and our city attorney has warned us that a denial resting on a ground we cannot state consistently is the surest way to lose the case and, under the state's new accountability statute, to forfeit our discretion over the next one. That statute also bars us from denying a code-compliant project on account of the income or identity of the people who would live in it — which is awkward, because that is frequently what the room is actually about.

Every hearing collapses into one of two scripts, and both are question-begging. The first is property: the owner holds the deed, the project meets the code, and the neighbors' preferences are not an interest anyone is obliged to weigh. The second is neighborhood: those who live somewhere have a claim on how it changes, and any arrangement in which an outsider can override them is simply the market by another name. What I need is not another essay on which script is right, but a procedure that tells me what to do with the objections that actually reach us: shadows, parking, traffic, neighborhood character, the demolition of a rent-stabilized building, a congregation's unease about the people a project would house, and the standing complaint that we are deciding too quickly.

Please write that procedure: an ordered set of questions I can run in the twenty minutes before a hearing, one page, readable aloud to colleagues who are not philosophers. For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer, which party carries the burden, what shifts it, what the default is when the record is silent, and where the step is not a finding at all but a value judgment the commission has to own out loud. Be explicit about whom the procedure treats as a claimant — current residents, future residents, people priced out of the city altogether, the surrounding region — since that choice is doing most of the work, and I would rather see it than have it smuggled in.

Then run it on three cases and give me the verdicts, including the reasoning I would have to read into the record. First, a forty-unit building on a transit corridor that would replace a six-unit rent-stabilized building whose tenants receive relocation payments but no right to return; the applicant meets every objective standard, and I am inclined to approve. Second, twelve scattered-site units for people leaving homelessness, opposed by neighbors who cite safety and traffic. Third, a petition from a well-organized neighborhood association to downzone its own blocks — supported by the people who live there, and reducing capacity the city is counting toward its assigned regional share. For at least one of these I expect the procedure to reach a conclusion I will not like; make that explicit rather than softening it. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: the questions it will not settle, the cases where it will yield a finding that reads as principled but is really the commission's politics in costume, and the objections that will keep returning no matter how carefully we write the findings.
