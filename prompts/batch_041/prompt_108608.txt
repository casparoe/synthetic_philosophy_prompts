I'm building a little collection of fiction that tries to dramatize ideas from the philosophy of probability, and I want you to write me one short story for it.

The idea I want it to carry is Keynes's distinction between the probability of an argument and its "weight." If I'm remembering A Treatise on Probability (1921) right, his point is roughly this: two arguments can hand you the same probability for some outcome while differing enormously in how much relevant evidence stands behind them, and new evidence can push the probability either way but always thickens the basis. So an identical-looking number might rest on a mountain or on a hunch. I dont want a lecture on that, I want it *felt*. And I might be getting some of the details slightly off, so go with the spirit of it and dont let the story hinge on me being exactly right about Keynes.

Dont set it in a seminar room. Set it somewhere the distinction actually bites — where a person has to stake money, reputation, or lives on a number. Some options, take whichever serves the story or invent your own: a marine reinsurance syndicate in the 1920s writing policies nobody can price, a modern catastrophe-risk desk underwriting a brand-new kind of flood cover, a small mutual insurer in a town that has only ever had one bad fire. Concretely, I want:

- Somebody who insists on pricing not just the probability but the *weight* — refusing, say, to write a policy that is plainly expected-profit-positive because the evidence under the loss estimate is thin — and who looks like a coward or a fool to everyone who only sees the number.
- At least one scene where two characters arrive at the same probability from totally different evidence bases and disagree about what to do, and you make the reader feel why both are being reasonable.
- A moment where the protagonist genuinely can't rank two risks — where neither "more likely" nor "less likely" nor "about as likely" feels right, and they have to act anyway. (There's a bit in the Treatise where Keynes can't decide whether to bother with an umbrella; dont name-check it, just use that feeling.)
- One small invented piece of machinery: the firm keeps a ledger where every risk gets two entries — the number, and the basis, meaning how much relevant knowledge sits underneath it. Make that concrete and a little strange.

Keep it under about 1,500 words. Casual prose, dialogue welcome, no moralizing at the end.
