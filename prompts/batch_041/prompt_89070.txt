I hold a cluster of views about wild animal suffering and want to know how exposed it is. First, suffering in nature matters morally in proportion to the number of sentient lives it involves, not to how intense any single animal's suffering is, and the unit of accounting is the whole life rather than the life-year, so a life cut short still counts as a full loss. Second, because r-selected species produce enormous numbers of short-lived young, most of whom die before reproducing, nature's suffering is dominated by them; naturalness carries no independent moral weight; and so it could be permissible, even required, to reduce the reproductive output of such species even at the cost of there being fewer total animal lives. What would count against this cluster? Give me the specific empirical findings, conceptual problems, and population-ethical commitments it stands or falls with, not just the familiar worries. Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a committed defender would concede, and judge honestly whether the position has been insulated from disconfirmation by hedging about insect sentience or about our inability to measure wild suffering. Tell me what would change your mind about your verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
