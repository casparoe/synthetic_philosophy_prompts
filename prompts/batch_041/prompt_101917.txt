Write a sestina in the voice of Mary on the first day after she leaves the room. Draw the six end-words from the vocabulary she already commanded inside it — terms such as wavelength, diagram, measure, room, word, and a verb of knowing — so that the poem never introduces a word she did not already possess, and the only thing that changes from stanza to stanza is their order. The form should do the argumentative work: a sestina permutes a fixed set of words without adding any, which is exactly the physicalist's reading of her release, that she gains no new fact but a new arrangement of the old ones; yet the same permutation can be read the other way, as the dualist's claim that the rearrangement is itself the new thing, and the poem must not pretend to adjudicate between them. Let the envoi be the moment the color actually arrives, so that the release is felt as a final reordering of the same six words rather than announced, and keep the abstract metaphysical vocabulary out of the poem itself — no "qualia," no "physical facts," no "propositional knowledge" — so that a reader who knows the knowledge argument can watch the old-fact/new-fact dispute get enacted in the form while a reader who has never heard of it can still follow a person meeting a color for the first time. It should read as attentive rather than triumphant: she is not discovering a new world so much as noticing something about the one she already knew. Standard sestina — six six-line stanzas and a three-line envoi, thirty-nine lines.
