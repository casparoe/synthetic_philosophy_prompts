I am in my final year of medical school, and something has been bothering me since I began my clinical placements that none of my teachers seems to find strange. In the anaesthetic room, consciousness is handled as a quantity with a dial. The anaesthetist watches a number the monitor derives from the EEG, watches the concentration of the agent being given, watches movement and blood pressure, and keeps the patient below a threshold that is supposed to guarantee that nobody is at home; when a patient afterwards reports having been aware during the operation, the episode is treated as a failure of measurement rather than as evidence that a threshold was the wrong sort of idea to begin with — and some of the consultants I have watched barely glance at the monitor at all, dosing instead by clinical signs. One floor down, the neurologist does something quite different: she has to answer a yes-or-no question about a patient who has been diagnosed as unaware, and she answers it with behaviour — a blink to command, eyes that follow a face — or, when the behaviour is silent, with a scanner, asking the patient to imagine an activity and looking for the pattern a healthy volunteer would produce. And in the pain clinic I was told on my first morning that the patient's own report is the measurement, and that there is no other. A friend who reads philosophy says that what I keep circling is the hard problem: that nothing you learn about what a brain is doing tells you what the creature whose brain it is is experiencing.

What I want is not an explanation of consciousness but an account of what this corner of medicine presupposes about it. Take the practice as it actually is, rather than as the textbook idealises it, and reconstruct the view of consciousness it needs in order to make sense. Does it require that being conscious be a matter of what the system does, reports or later remembers, so that the residue the hard problem points at makes no practical difference? Does it require that consciousness be a single property that comes in degrees along one axis, or that it be a threshold state a monitor can locate, and are those two commitments really the same? Does it require that a third-person test can settle a first-person fact, and if so, what does it assume about the relations between report, behaviour and experience? Does it require that consciousness be something a brain or a species has, rather than something an individual has at a time and may lose and regain? And does the moral warrant of the whole arrangement — that we may cut into someone who has not consented because there is no one there to consent — depend on that view holding?

Then do three further things. Separate what the practice strictly needs from what it merely sits comfortably with: a functionalist, an identity theorist, someone who thinks experience is exhausted by higher-order representation, a panpsychist, and someone who thinks the hard problem shows we can never know anything about other minds — which of these could license the same practice unchanged, and which does the practice quietly rule out? Say where the practice is underdetermined, and where its parts pull against one another: the anaesthetist's dial, the neurologist's yes-or-no, the pain nurse's deference to the report. Which of the three is doing the real work, and could the other two be brought into line with it without changing what clinicians actually do? Finally, tell me what would have to change if the presupposition were replaced — if we thought, say, that a patient could be conscious with nothing reportable going on and no memory of it afterwards, or that awareness shades off by degrees with no threshold anywhere to be found. What would the anaesthetist, the neurologist and the pain nurse each have to do differently, and what would be left of the argument that it is permissible to operate on someone who is unconscious?
