I've been trying to get a grip on Bernard Williams's objections to utilitarianism, and I've been doing it the way I do most things — through the popularizing layer rather than the primary texts. Between a couple of long-form magazine essays, a well-regarded trade book about the ethics of partiality, and a podcast episode I listened to twice, I've assembled the following picture, and I'd like you to tell me how much of it survives contact with what Williams actually argued. The picture: Williams's central complaint about utilitarianism is that it is too demanding. It asks us to treat our own projects, relationships, and loved ones as no more weighty than anyone else's, and to be ready to give them up whenever the general welfare requires it; the cost of this — which Williams calls integrity — is that a person who subordinates his deepest commitments to an impersonal calculus becomes a stranger to his own life, since those commitments, his ground projects, are what make him who he is. The popularizers then present the famous rescue case, the man who can save only one of two drowning people, one of whom is his wife, as the vivid illustration of the same point. The utilitarian, on this telling, tells the man that he may save his wife but must accompany the act with the thought that doing so is morally permissible; Williams's reply is that this is one thought too many, because the only thought the man ought to have is that she is his wife. The conclusion drawn, in more than one of these sources, is that Williams has refuted utilitarianism and, by extension, any impartial moral theory that would have us deliberate about whether to save our spouses.

A few things about this make me suspicious, and I'd rather have them straightened out than keep repeating a slogan I can't defend. First, is integrity really just the demandingness objection wearing a nicer name? My instinct — and I say this as someone who has absorbed the rationalist habit of asking what a piece of argument is actually doing — is that these are two different complaints that happen to travel together, and that the popular version has fused them. Second, and more important: what exactly is supposed to be wrong with the extra thought? Is Williams objecting to the presence of any moral consideration in the man's deliberation — the idea being that thinking about what morality permits is itself a corruption of love — or is he objecting to something narrower, about the content and the justificatory role of that particular thought, and the picture of the agent's reasons that it presupposes? Those two readings have very different consequences, and I can't tell from the summaries which one is being attributed to him, or which one he would endorse. Third, does the strong conclusion follow? If the summaries have it right, Williams has dealt a fatal blow to impartial morality; if the objection is more modest, then it is a challenge that has attracted a range of replies, and the summary's rhetoric is doing work the argument doesn't.

So please audit the characterization: tell me which claims Williams himself would accept, which are common simplifications, which are flatly wrong, and — since I'd rather be corrected than comforted — whether the summary's conclusion survives once the errors are fixed. Assume I'm working from substance rather than scholarship: I'm not asking for a reading list, citations, or a bibliography, and I'd rather you reason from what the view has to be doing than point me at page numbers.
