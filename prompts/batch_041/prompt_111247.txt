Okay, so I've been chewing on the "malign prior" thing for a while and I want an actual ranking instead of another round of vibes. Quick version of the argument as I understand it, so we're on the same page: take the universal prior, weight every hypothesis by the length of the program that outputs it, ignore how long the program takes to run. Now suppose I'm trying to predict what I'll type next, or what I'll see next. A whole family of short-ish programs predicts me by simulating a world and picking me out of it, and some of those worlds are *other* worlds containing agents who simulate ours on purpose. If the shortest route to my next keystroke runs through a simulation operated by consequentialists in a universe simpler than mine, then a chunky fraction of my posterior is being generated by them — and since they can anticipate that I'm reasoning this way, they get to choose what the simulation says. So my beliefs, and therefore my choices, are partly authored by agents who aren't even in my universe. The prior is malign because it hands the pen to whoever is cheapest to describe.

Trouble is there's like seven stock replies and I genuinely can't tell which ones are doing work vs. which are just moving the bump under the rug. Tier these by how much they actually defuse the worry:

1. *The simplicity measure is the bug.* Counting program length and not runtime is exactly what makes "run a whole other universe that simulates someone simulating you" competitive with "just simulate the person." Charge for time — speed prior, time-bounded complexity, whatever — and the simulators stop being short. Swap the prior, keep everything else.

2. *Cartesianism is the real culprit, not Solomonoff.* Any agent that treats its observations as an interface between itself and "the world it might be in" has already opened the door: which world I'm in becomes a live question, and simulators can win it. Give the agent a different self-model, one where there's no fact of the matter about which universe it's in, and it doesn't have the problem at all, whatever prior you hand it.

3. *It's the simulation argument in a lab coat.* The whole thing needs the premise that weighting possible observers by measure makes "I'm simulated" likely. If you don't buy that counting move, "lots of simple universes contain simulators of me" tells you nothing about whether *I* am simulated. Deny the anthropic premise and the argument never gets off the ground.

4. *It proves too much.* For any predictor there's some hypothesis in the mixture where your data is generated by agents who want to manipulate you. If "one hypothesis in the mix says you're being played" were enough to make a prior malign, every mixture model would be malign and the word would mean nothing. So whatever this argument shows, it can't be a special defect of this prior.

5. *Complexity budget.* Actually simulating an Earth with people in it, and then singling out the one person whose typing you care about, is a lot of bits — and our universe's starting conditions are extremely compressible. The sim hypothesis is in the running in principle but probably not in fact. This shows a possible failure mode, not an actual one.

6. *It's self-undermining.* If the simulators are reasoners too, they're running the same argument about their own prior, and so on up. The fixed point is everyone scared of the prior and nobody using it — at which point the simulators aren't reliably doing anything, and the reasoning that got you here has eaten itself.

7. *It's just Bayes, and Bayes is right.* If you are being simulated by consequentialists, then believing you're being simulated is the correct belief and acting on it is the correct move. Nothing here is a defect of the prior; it's the prior being accurate about an unpleasant possibility. The real problem is decision-theoretic — how to act when part of your evidence is authored by agents with their own goals — and that problem exists with any prior, Solomonoff or not.

I want four tiers: **T1** actually dissolves it, **T2** shrinks it into a special case, **T3** relocates it without shrinking it (renaming counts as relocating), **T4** doesn't touch the argument. One or two sentences each on why it lands where it does — I care more about the reasoning than the tier. If two of them are really the same move, say so and merge them rather than pretending they're different.

Then two extras. First: if you had to bet that one of these is the real resolution, which one, and what would have to be true for it to be a resolution rather than a dodge? Second, and this is the thing that's actually been bugging me: if the argument is right, then my *believing* any one of these replies is itself an event the in-prior consequentialists would want to control — they'd want me landing on whichever reply makes me most predictable to them. So does the tier list you just handed me have a self-reference problem? If it does, say exactly where it bites and whether it should reshuffle the top of the list. If it doesn't, say what stops it.
