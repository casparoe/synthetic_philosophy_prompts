I'm in debate club and one of my friends has gotten really into this thing he calls a "commitment race" — he says it's from Cold War game theory, some guy who wrote about burning bridges. The way he tells it: when two sides are in a standoff, each one is trying to be the first to lock itself in, burn its own boats, make a move it can't take back, because once you've done that the other side is the one who has to fold. He says it's the same logic as a general burning the bridge behind his own army, or a kid in the playground going "I'm not moving, you move." And he's turned it into a rule he applies to everything now: in any real standoff, whoever commits first wins, because the side that hasn't committed always has more to lose by holding out than by backing off. Last week he used it on the Falklands war, which we did in history class, and said Argentina should have won since they committed first by invading. I couldn't tell him what was wrong with that and it's been bugging me for days. I know the basic story — Argentina invades in April 1982, Britain sends a task force halfway around the world within days, there's a ton of shuttle diplomacy in between, and it ends in June with Argentina surrendering — but I don't want the story retold. I want the war actually run through his theory.

So treat the Falklands as a test case for the commitment-race idea, and go in both directions. Set it up properly first: who counts as a player, what each side's actual commitment moves were, which of those were real commitments that genuinely took options off the table versus just loud signals that cost nothing, what each side thought the other's commitment was worth, and whether there was even a race here or just two governments doing expensive things at each other. Then the interesting part. Where does the theory actually earn its keep — is there anything it explains that a plain "they misread each other" doesn't? And where does it break? Does it get the Argentina-goes-first prediction wrong, and if so, is that because his rule is wrong, or because the case isn't really a commitment race in the first place? I have a feeling he's sliding between "commits first" and "commits hardest" without noticing, but I want to know if that's the actual diagnosis or if I'm just making up a distinction to win an arguement. Also be honest about the stuff the theory has nothing to say about: the British had a clock on them because of the southern winter, there were mediators offering both sides off-ramps the whole time, and neither side was a single player with one set of preferences. Tell me what a better model would be if this one doesn't fit, and what the case ends up showing about the concept itself.
