I want to practise a position I have only recently come to take seriously, against a competent opponent rather than a straw man. Please play a committed reductionist: a physicist by training who holds that every regularity in chemistry, biology, and the special sciences is in principle derivable from the microphysical laws together with the boundary conditions, and that talk of emergence is either a harmless label for our present ignorance of the derivation or a piece of obscurantism. Be genuinely formidable; concede local points where they are owed, but never surrender the central claim. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to one substantial paragraph so that it feels like an exchange rather than a lecture. My opening move is this. In the theory of critical phenomena, systems whose microscopic constituents and interactions differ wildly — a magnet, a fluid, a binary alloy — exhibit near their critical points the same macroscopic behaviour, the same exponents and the same scaling functions, and this is accounted for by the renormalization group: as one coarse-grains, the microscopic details flow to a common fixed point and are washed out. The macroscopic regularity is therefore not merely derivable from the microphysics; it is insensitive to it, and one and the same macro-law is realized by indefinitely many incompatible micro-laws. If a higher-level generalization can be true no matter which of countless microphysical stories happens to obtain, in what sense is it nothing over and above the microphysics, and does your derivability claim not become a promissory note that no one could ever cash?
