Okay, here's a thing that's been nagging at me since I retired. I did 35 years as a field biologist and now I lead birding and wildlife trips overseas — small groups, six to ten people, two or three trips a year. And because I'm nominally in charge, everyone asks me the local drill. Do we tip the driver? Is that a line we're supposed to be in, or do we just crowd the counter like everybody else? Is it okay to cross mid-block here? Shoes off or no? And I've noticed I give different answers trip to trip, and I honestly dont know if I'm being consistent or just going along with whatever the loudest client thinks.

I'd like an actual method instead of vibes, because it isn't just me — my clients copy whatever I do, and sometimes a local guide's livelihood rides on it. So what I want is a decision procedure I can run in my head in about a minute, on the spot, for this question: when is a local everyday convention — tipping, queueing, jaywalking, shoes, haggling, whatever — something I'm obligated to follow as a visitor, versus something I can politely not bother with, versus something I should refuse even though everyone around me is doing it? I usually can't look anything up (no signal, don't speak the language), so it has to run on what I can see and guess.

Build it out of whatever the philosophy of convention and moral obligation actually has that's usable — the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure flat-out says follow it or don't, and where it stops and hands the call back to me because it's a values question and not a fact question. I don't need a reading list or citations, just the machinery. There are two distinctions I suspect matter but can't keep seperate, so tell me if they really do: the difference between a convention that's basically an arbitrary coordination point (which side of the sidewalk we walk on) and one that's carrying real moral weight underneath (tipping standing in for a wage), and whether my being a guest — here for a week and then gone — changes what I owe versus what a resident owes.

Then run it on three cases so I can watch it work. (1) Tipping: a country where guides expect it and it's part of how they eat, and another where offering it is taken as an insult. (2) Jaywalking: the crosswalk is 200 feet away, it's technically illegal, and every local is crossing right here. (3) A local custom I find distasteful — say a seating arrangement, or a "facilitation payment" that's just how business gets done there and everybody treats as normal. I want at least one of the three to come out a way I won't like, and I want you to tell me straight where this procedure is going to misfire or just can't decide.
