Ok so some context: I'm a philosophy prof (metaethics, mostly) and for the last few years I've also sat on the clinical ethics committee at the teaching hospital attached to our med school. We get maybe four or five consults a month — someone on the team pages us, we get 45 minutes in a room with the chart and whoever bothered to show up, and we have to hand the chair something in writing that he signs off on. Our current method is embarassing: it's basically the four principles everyone memorized in med school (autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice) plus a lot of throat-clearing, and then we all vote. Meanwhile I've been teaching the particularist line in my grad seminar — Dancy and co., the idea that there are no defensible moral principles at all, that what a feature counts for depends on the whole case it's embedded in — and it's kind of wrecked me, because a good chunk of our consults are exactly the cases where the principles point one way and the situation, looked at properly, seems to point another. A colleague floated something at a workshop that I keep chewing on: she says the way to have your cake and eat it is to treat principles as reminders rather than rulers — they don't decide anything, they just make you notice a feature you'd otherwise have walked past. I want to know if that can actually be turned into something my committee can run, or if it's just a nice slogan.

So: write me a decision procedure for the committee, grounded in whatever the particularism/generalism literature actually establishes (not the cartoon version where particularists are just people who hate rules), but usable by clinicians who have never read any of it and have 45 minutes and an incomplete chart. The questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; which steps are just there to make us slow down; and — this part matters — the points where the procedure has to stop and hand the decision to a person, because the thing left over is a values call and not a fact we could look up. It should survive the cases where we can't get the patient's story (unconscious patient, no family, no advance directive) and the cases where the patient's stated wish and their actual situation pull apart. Then run it on three consults: (1) a competent adult refusing a standard, effective treatment on religious grounds, with the team pushing hard to override; (2) a family asking us to withhold the diagnosis from the patient, which is normal where they're from; (3) a patient who's been verbally abusive to the nursing staff and wants to refuse something, where I'll be honest that my gut is not neutral about him and I want the procedure to catch me doing that, even if what it tells me is something I won't like. Be straight with me about where this thing will misfire and what it simply can't do — I'd rather have a procedure with a visible hole in it than one that pretends to cover everything.

Format it like this, with these section headings in this order: start with a section called Bottom line, three sentences max, saying whether the reminders-not-rulers thing survives contact with an actual committee and what the biggest caveat is. Then The tension, about 150 words, on what the particularist is actually claiming and what a procedure would have to give up to be particularist-compatible. Then The procedure, numbered steps, roughly 600 words, each step with what counts as a sufficient answer and a flag on whether it's doing work or just slowing us down. Then Stops and hand-offs, about 200 words, on the places the procedure must end in a human decision. Then the three worked cases, about 200 words each, each ending with what the procedure says and whether that's a verdict or a hand-off. Then What this can't do, about 150 words. And at the very end, a Cheat sheet I could laminate — the whole thing in under ten lines, phrased for a surgeon who's already annoyed at me.
