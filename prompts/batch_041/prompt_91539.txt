You are a philosopher of science with a strong working knowledge of two literatures that rarely talk to each other: the philosophy of mechanistic explanation, and the methodology of mechanistic interpretability in machine learning. I am a postdoctoral researcher in an interdisciplinary lab, and I need you to turn a half-formed worry of mine into a proposal I can submit for a six-month internal pilot grant. Think carefully before you write. Begin with a short brainstorming section — three or four candidate ways of individuating mechanisms, each stated in a sentence or two, and what each would say about one concrete anomaly — and only then converge on the single criterion you would build the project around. Then write the proposal itself, roughly 1,800 words, in the first person plural, for a panel containing one philosopher of science and one machine-learning engineer.

The half-formed idea is this. In the lab we say, more or less interchangeably, that one model is "running the same mechanism" as another; that a new failure is "the same circuit misfiring under a different input distribution"; and that we have found "a genuinely new mechanism". These cannot all be trading on the same criterion of sameness, and which criterion we tacitly adopt decides what we are entitled to claim afterwards. The interesting question is not mechanism individuation in general but its consequence for anomaly detection. When a system produces an output we did not expect, what has to be true for the right answer to be "the known mechanism, under new conditions" rather than "a different mechanism that merely overlaps with it"? Candidates you might consider include: sameness of component parts and their activities; sameness of causal role or input-output profile; sameness under a stated equivalence of interventions; and sameness of explanatory function for a stated audience. Note where these come apart, and note that the anomaly case is precisely where they are most likely to.

The proposal should do all of the following. State the research question so that it has a determinate answer — a reader should be able to say what would settle it. Explain why the existing answers to the general individuation question leave this narrower question open, describing positions in terms of their arguments rather than their provenance; do not produce a bibliography, reading list or citations, since I will supply references myself. Set out the method — what I would actually do, week by week across six months — and defend that method against the obvious alternatives (a survey of practitioners, a benchmark, a formal treatment of causal graphs), saying honestly which of those would be better and why I am not doing it. Include at least one worked anomaly case, described concretely enough to be argued about; if you need an example that you are not certain of, construct a schematic one and label it as schematic rather than presenting it as an established finding. Say what a successful result looks like as a deliverable — a decision procedure, a taxonomy with worked cases, a checklist for the lab — and what would count as the project failing. Say what evidence would force a change of mind. And close with an honest assessment of what could make this too big for six months, and what you would cut first.

Throughout, treat the lab's own vocabulary as data to be interrogated, not as a theory to be formalised. Do not assume that the philosophy literature and the interpretability literature are describing the same thing when they both say "mechanism"; part of the value of the proposal is in saying where the translation fails.
