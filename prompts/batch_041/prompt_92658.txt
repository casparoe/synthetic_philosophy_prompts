I'm commissioning a short story for an anthology about institutions that end up talking about themselves, and I'd like it built on a premise I've worked out myself rather than on a familiar AI-governance or surveillance plot. Please don't reach for the stock versions of this.

The setting is a mid-sized city that has run for two generations on a system in which elections settle ends and markets settle means. Voters fix a single scalar measure of collective welfare; for any contested policy, the city asks whether that measure would be higher in five years with the policy than without it; where the relevant conditional market gives a confident answer, the policy is adopted. The measure itself has an odd history. It began as an ordinary composite of surveys, but a generation ago the city replaced the survey with a perpetual contract: the official welfare index is simply the market price of a claim that pays out according to the official welfare index a year later. It is, in other words, a number that measures nothing except the market's expectation of the number next year.

Write the story of the year the loop closes. Somewhere in the machinery — a clearing house, a resolution office, an audit bureau — someone notices a consequence the designers did not intend and has to decide what to do about it before a scheduled vote. You may choose which consequence to dramatize. Among the ones I've turned over and would accept: that the index has decoupled from every underlying statistic and can now be moved by belief alone; that a conditional market on the question of whether abolishing the index would leave the index higher is unanswerable, because the market's answer determines which branch anyone will ever observe; that the resolution office is being asked to certify a counterfactual no one can see. Pick one and follow it.

Keep the mechanism accurate. The distinction between voting on ends and betting on means, the counterfactual character of the bets, and the fact that only one branch of any conditional is ever realized should all be right — a reader who knows this material should recognize the apparatus rather than wince at it. Invent the city, its officials, and the index's name; use no real place names, no dates, no real people, and quote nothing. No one should deliver a lecture on the theory: whatever the reader learns about the system should come from what characters do, what they trade, and what they discover they cannot decide. Include at least one scene in which a price and a payout are concrete, and give at least one character a personal stake in the outcome that is not a matter of principle. End on a specific act or image rather than a summary of the problem; I want the difficulty left standing rather than dissolved in the final paragraph. Roughly 2,500 words.

Begin with the story itself. A title is fine, but no preface, no framing note, and no commentary afterward.
