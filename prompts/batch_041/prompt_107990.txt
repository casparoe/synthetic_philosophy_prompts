Okay, so the mid-90s hoax where a physicist got a cultural-studies journal to publish a deliberately nonsensical parody paper about quantum gravity and hermeneutics, then announced it was a hoax and everyone had a laugh at the humanities' expense — people still trot it out as proof of the Weinberg/Hawking line that philosophy of science is useless to actual scientists. Look, they published a physicist making fun of them, therefore the philosophers have nothing to tell physics.

Treat that episode as a test case for the claim, running it in both directions. First, what did the hoax actually show? I think it's a bait-and-switch, and I want the targets pried apart: (1) philosophy of science as a discipline, (2) the philosophical commitments already doing work inside ordinary scientific practice whether scientists admit it or not, (3) the cultural-studies / science-studies crowd that was the real target, (4) whatever the editors thought they were assessing when they ran it. Which of those is the episode actually evidence about, which does it leave untouched, and which one does the physicists' complaint really need in order to land?

Then the reverse direction: what does the episode show about the claim itself? What bugs me is that a hoax is a strange kind of argument — it works by violating the norms of the practice it's attacking, so it seems to need an account of why those norms matter in the first place, and I don't see where that account comes from if not from philosophy of science. Does that rescue the discipline or is it just a tu quoque? And is there a weaker version of the physicists' claim — something like, this particular style of theorizing about science is worthless — that the episode genuinely does support, and if so, does it survive the fact that the journal obviously never checked the physics with anyone who knew it?

Finish by saying where the episode runs out: what it can't settle either way.
