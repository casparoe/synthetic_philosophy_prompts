I'm teaching a session on free will and rational choice in a first-year seminar, and I want to pair Buridan's ass with Stoppard's Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead. Give me a reading of the play through the problem of choice without preference — and I need it honest in both directions, so tell me where the play is really about this and where it isnt.

Quick setup so we're on the same page (correct me if I've got any of it wrong): the play opens with the two of them betting on coin flips, Rosencrantz calls heads and wins ninety-two in a row. Rosencrantz couldnt care less, and it's Guildenstern who starts to come apart, wondering whether theyre inside some un- or supernatural force. The donkey is the old puzzle: an ass exactly between two identical piles of hay, no reason to prefer either, so it starves. I know it's called Buridan's ass even though I've read that Buridan never actually used an ass — his own version, in the commentary on Aristotle's On the Heavens, is a dog, and the man-equally-hungry-and-thirsty version goes back to Aristotle, where it shows up in a strange arguement about why the earth stays put. Al-Ghazali has two identical dates and thinks free will breaks the tie; Leibniz thinks we're never truly tied because of tiny unconscious preferences we dont notice. So there's a tradition here and I'd like the reading to know it exists without drowning in it.

What I want:
- where the play actually stages the problem, and where it stages something else that people just label Buridan. Is the coin-toss scene about preference at all, or is it about probability — an epistemic puzzle dressed up as a practical one? Does that distinction matter for the reading?
- the two of them as the two haystacks: they cant tell themselves apart, nobody else can either, and they end the same way. Does that analogy hold, or is it too cute?
- the rigged coin. Later the Player's coin turns up under his foot and the whole random tie-breaker turns out to be a trick. What does that do to the idea that a coin toss is how a rational agent chooses without preference? Is the play saying our tie-breakers are always secretly loaded, or that we just pretend the choice was ours?
- the determinism sitting under all of it: theyre minor characters in someone else's play and their deaths are already in the script. Doesn't that make the no-preference puzzle moot — the worry isnt that they cant choose, it's that choosing does nothing? Tell me whether that undercuts the Buridan reading or is the point of the play.
- and the part I actually care about: what does the play give us that the argument cant? Is there a scene that shows something about picking without a reason — the phenomenology of it, the anxiety, the way people perform having reasons — that Ghazali and Leibniz just assert?

End by saying where the play resists the reading and you have to admit it isnt really about this, and whether the play offers a solution to the puzzle or only a mood. No quotes needed, I know the text.
