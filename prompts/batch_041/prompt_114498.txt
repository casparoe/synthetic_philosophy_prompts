I sit on a state parole board — roughly four hearings a week, 45 minutes each, and at the end of every one I have to fill in a standard form with a section headed "Offender's acceptance of responsibility," which the board chair then signs off on. The trouble is I got dragged into an ethics reading group and we spent two sessions on Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument" (i think that's what he calls it): you're responsible for what you do only if you're responsible for the way you are; you're responsible for the way you are only if you're responsible for how you came to be that way; and that last bit would need you to have chosen your own character before you had any character to choose with, so the chain never bottoms out and nobody is ultimately responsible for anything. I can't shake the feeling that this is basically right and also that I'm supposed to walk into a hearing room and decide something anyway. A colleague on the board keeps saying we only ever need to answer "was it him?", never "was it his?", and I half agree but I genuinely don't know where the line falls, and when I'm staring at the form at 4:30 on a Thursday I need something more than a shrug.

So what I want from you is a decision procedure — a checklist I can actually run in the ten or fifteen minutes I have before I write the section, with the questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a flat no, and — this is the part I care about most — where it runs out of things to tell me and hands the call back to me because what's left is a value judgment and not a fact about the case. Keep two things apart that my colleague keeps collapsing: responsibility for the act, and responsibility for the self that performed the act. And give me a separate step for the "could he have done otherwise in the same situation" idea, because I suspect "same situation" is doing an enormous amount of hidden work in these hearings and I want the procedure to say that out loud rather than smuggle it past me. Constraints: I have the file and the transcript but not time to re-read either, the chair will not accept "we couldn't decide," and whatever I write has to be defensible if a victim's family reads it. Be straight with me about which steps are just the argument wearing a costume, and mark clearly where someone who thinks the whole Strawson line is wrong — a compatibilist, or someone who thinks the regress proves less than it looks like it proves — would say the procedure has already gone off the rails. I want the settled parts separated from the genuinely contested ones; don't hand me a contested premise dressed up as the consensus, because I'll repeat it in a hearing and it'll be somebody's liberty.

Then run it, start to finish, on three cases I've actually had or could have: a guy who was nineteen when he did an armed robbery, is now thirty-four, has done every program offered, no write-ups in six years, and says all the right things about taking responsibility — where I expect the procedure to tell me I can't release him *for the reason I want to*; a woman whose offense happened during an untreated psychotic episode, where the regress makes it feel like there's no one left to hold responsible at all and yet the hearing still has to end in a recommendation; and one where I think the procedure is going to give me an answer I won't like, either because it zeroes out the responsibility factor in a case that disgusts me, or because it tells me the whole box on the form is unfillable and that every release decision has to be argued on risk grounds alone. If that last one is where it lands, don't soften it for me — I'd rather find out now than at the podium. No citations or reading list, I'll chase those down myself if I need them.
