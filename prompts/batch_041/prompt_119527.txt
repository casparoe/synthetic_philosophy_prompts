John Dewey died in 1952, well before the emergence of the deliberative-democratic institutions now usually called citizens' assemblies or deliberative mini-publics: bodies of roughly one hundred citizens selected by lot rather than by election or self-selection, convened for a fixed period by a legislature or an executive, furnished with expert testimony and professional facilitation, and asked to deliberate on a single contested question before issuing recommendations that carry no legal force. The Citizens' Assembly on Electoral Reform in British Columbia, which met in 2004 and recommended replacing the province's voting system, and Ireland's Citizens' Assembly, established in 2016 with ninety-nine randomly selected members to take up questions such as abortion law and climate policy, are the standard reference points; advocates of the general idea sometimes describe it as lottocratic.

Suppose Dewey had lived long enough to observe these experiments, and suppose he had been asked whether they fulfill or betray his account of democracy as a shared way of life and of social inquiry as experimental. I would like a substantive, argued prediction rather than a hedge, and I would like you to work through at least the following:

1. Would a randomly selected mini-public count as a public in his sense — a body constituted by the shared perception of the indirect consequences of transactions — or do its selection procedure, its temporary existence, and its commissioned mandate make it a different kind of thing altogether?

2. Does the procedure amount to inquiry as he understood it? Dewey held that inquiry begins in a genuinely problematic situation that the inquirers themselves undergo, and that its results are warranted only insofar as they are carried forward and tested in further experience. Mini-publics are handed a question that others have framed, and their conclusions bind no one. Does that disqualify the procedure, or only constrain what it can be expected to achieve?

3. Which justification would have moved him? Many proponents defend mini-publics instrumentally — better-informed opinion, less polarization, more legitimate decisions — whereas Dewey is often read as valuing democracy as growth and shared experience rather than as a device for producing good outcomes. Would he have accepted the instrumental case, or rejected it as a category mistake?

4. Does the small scale of these bodies answer or evade his diagnosis, in The Public and Its Problems (1927), that the public had been eclipsed and that genuine communication was threatened by the scale and complexity of modern society?

5. Does his rejection of Lippmann's technocratic alternative commit him to opposing these institutions, or to welcoming them as an instance of organized intelligence that remains accountable to a wider public?

Weigh against all of this his characteristic treatment of democratic deficits as remediable only by more democracy. Conclude with a clear verdict on which way the balance falls, and say where the question is anachronistic or where his commitments genuinely underdetermine an answer. Please write in American English, and please do not include a bibliography or reading list.
