I need to write a document for a workshop and I want you to be a philosopher of mind who knows both the Dennett stuff about the intentional stance and the current mess about whether language models "believe" anything. Think it through carefully before you write: I'd rather you spend the first chunk of your answer brainstorming the candidate cruxes and deciding which ones are real, then draft the actual document.

The setup: two people who've been arguing for two years about whether we should say that a language model believes things.

Side 1: ascribing beliefs and desires to a model is fine and often the best thing to do. It's Dennett's intentional stance pointed at a new target, and if mentalistic vocabulary buys you prediction and explanation of what the system does, that's the only currency the stance ever traded in.

Side 2: the vocabulary is systematically misleading here. The model isn't in the business of representing how things are; it's producing text that looks like the output of something that is. "Belief" is on loan from a theory of a creature with a persisting, world-directed, action-guiding state, and in this case the loan never gets repaid.

What I want is the joint statement they could both sign after a genuinely good-faith adversarial collaboration. Not a verdict — a document. Concretely:

- a numbered list of claims both sides can sign, including about what belief-talk is for here (prediction? explanation? licensing demands for consistency, sincerity, or blame? something else), and about what would count as evidence either way;
- a disagreements table: each row a claim, with a column for how each side would state its own position in wording it would actually accept, so that neither column is a strawman and neither is a weaselly middle;
- the cruxes underneath — the sub-questions where settling one would move at least one side — and for each crux, the kind of result both sides agree would bear on it, plus what each of them predicts about it. I want at least one crux that's an empirical/interpretability question (say, whether there are stable, causally efficacious internal representations playing a belief-ish role across contexts) and at least one that's conceptual.

Three things I specifically want you to grapple with, because I think they're where this gets interesting and where the usual exchange of intuitions goes nowhere:

(a) wether the right comparison class is the thermostat / chess-program case, where the stance is openly instrumental and the "as if" is a feature rather than an embarrassment, or the human case, where the ascription seems to get its grip from the fact that the behaviour is produced by states with a certain causal role — and whether models break the analogy in a new way, since they're trained on the behavioural output of the very creatures the stance was originally built for;

(b) fragmentation. People who work on human belief have argued for a while that belief ascription tolerates a lot of local inconsistency and context-dependence, so the system that "believes P here and not-P there" may not be the clean disanalogy everyone treats it as. Does that help side 2 or side 1? Don't let either side have it for free;

(c) how much of this is a fight about the word. If it turns out that they agree about the internals and the dispositions and disagree only about whether to spend the word "belief" on it, say so plainly and tell me which part is verbal.

Also, if the whole thing turns out to make no difference to anything anyone does — evals, interpretability targets, model welfare — say that too, rather than manufacturing stakes.

Finally, please be careful to seperate what's actually settled among people who know this material from what's genuinely contested. I don't want a confident synthesis presented as though everyone agrees, and where you're unsure whether something is consensus or just one camp's line, flag it rather than smoothing it over.
