I am a high school student and the organizer of my school's philosophy club. In a few weeks I am running a session on the paradox of tragedy: the puzzle of why we voluntarily seek out films, novels, songs, and paintings that make us sad, afraid, or grief-stricken, and why we so often describe those experiences as good ones. Most of the students who will attend have read nothing on the subject, so I want the handout to sort them by what they actually believe rather than by which famous position they happen to have heard of. Please design a decision tree for that handout.

Some specifications:

1. Every node must be phrased as a question about a concrete case or a plainly stated claim, answerable by someone who does not know any of the labels. No node may take the form "do you think the pleasure is compensatory?" or otherwise presuppose a term of art.

2. The tree must turn on the following three distinctions, arranged in whatever order produces the cleanest structure:
   (a) whether the sadness, fear, or grief felt in response to a work is the same state as its real-life counterpart, or a distinct state that merely resembles it;
   (b) whether the good in the experience lies in the painful emotion itself, in something that accompanies or results from it, or in the experience taken as a whole;
   (c) whether the good is a pleasure at all — that is, whether the experience is valued for how it feels or for some non-hedonic reason, such as understanding something, finding it meaningful, or being the kind of person who can be moved by it.

3. Add at most two further branch questions of your own devising, and say in one sentence each what the tree would lose without them.

4. Include at least the following cases, and any others you need: a student who cries at a film about a family falling apart and immediately wants to watch it again; a student who says she is glad to have watched a documentary about a historical atrocity but did not enjoy it and would never watch it again; a student who plays the same sad song on repeat for an hour while doing homework; a student who puts on sad music while already sad and reports that it makes her feel better rather than worse; a student who calls a bleak novel with no redemption the best thing he has ever read; a student who writes sad songs himself; someone who cries at a wedding video but would deny that this has anything to do with art; and a student who has seen the same tragedy five times and cries at it every time.

5. Give the tree between eight and twelve leaves. For each leaf, supply a one-sentence statement of the position in ordinary language; the label it usually goes by, if it has one, with no author names or citations required; and one sentence naming its most awkward commitment — the case or implication the position handles least well.

6. After the tree, list the combinations of answers that no leaf occupies, and say of each whether you take the view to be incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Then flag any pairs of leaves the tree cannot separate, and state what further question would be needed to separate them.

7. The handout, tree and notes together, should run to roughly 900 to 1,100 words.
