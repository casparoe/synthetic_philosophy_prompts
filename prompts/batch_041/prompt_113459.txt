Write a dialogue between two philosophers who both hold that moral status turns on having interests, but disagree about what having interests requires. The first defends the stake condition: a system can fare well or badly only if it is a self-maintaining organization with something to lose, so that some of its states are valenced for it. The second defends the self-model condition: a system has interests once it represents itself as a subject whose states can go better or worse, and a purely predictive system with no homeostasis and no body could in principle do this. Have them work through a case neither view was built for: a system that is continuous within a single run but discontinuous across runs, instantiated afresh each time with nothing carried forward. Does the absence of a subject persisting from one run to the next make welfare talk incoherent, or does it merely relocate the subject to the run, or to the specious present within the run, and is any of that a difference that matters morally rather than a fact about implementation? They should also press each other on what follows from our not knowing which condition is correct: whether that uncertainty gives us reason to count possible digital minds at some nonzero weight in our moral deliberations, and how one could argue for a weight rather than simply assert one. Neither should be a strawman, and each should answer the other's strongest formulation of their point.
