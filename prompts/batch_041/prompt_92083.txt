I'm a second-year law student taking a jurisprudence seminar that's cross-listed with the philosophy department, and I keep running into something in my torts and criminal law courses that I can't get straight. Both courses teach that cause-in-fact is a plain counterfactual matter — would the harm have happened anyway if the defendant had done otherwise? — and that this factual question gets settled before the judge ever reaches the separate, normative question of whether the defendant owed a duty. Applied to failures to act, though, the counterfactual test seems to work just as well as it does for actions: but for the lifeguard's failure to swim out, the swimmer would have lived. What puzzles me is that the law is ruthlessly asymmetric about exactly these cases. A stranger who watches a child drown and does nothing is not liable, and when I ask why, I get told both that he had no duty to act and that his failure didn't cause the death, as though those were one point rather than two. A parent who watches the same child drown is guilty of homicide. A doctor who turns off a ventilator at the family's request is said not to have caused the patient's death even though the patient would have lived on for some time had the machine stayed on, while a doctor who injects a lethal dose plainly has caused it. And in every case where an omission does count as a cause, it's because some duty — a special relationship, an undertaking, a statute — supplied the missing ingredient, so the causal question and the duty question never quite come apart. A classmate in the seminar insists this is all beside the point because absences simply cannot stand in causal relations: there is nothing there to be a relatum, and the counterfactual test only makes it look otherwise. I don't know whether to believe her, and I don't know what the doctrine would look like if she's right.

What I want from you is the picture of causation that this whole apparatus presupposes, reconstructed as carefully as you can. Does the law assume that omissions are not causes at all and that it is only speaking loosely when it calls them causes, with all the real work done by duty? Does it assume instead that omissions are causes in just the sense actions are, so that the duty requirement is an extra, independent filter laid on top of an otherwise neutral causal judgment? Or does it assume something I find more interesting and harder to assess — that causation is itself partly normative or context-dependent, so that which condition counts as the cause of an outcome is picked out against a background of what we expect people to do, in which case the duty requirement is not a separate filter at all but is already inside the causal judgment? Tell me which of these the practice actually commits you to, which it merely fits comfortably with, and where it is genuinely underdetermined among them; if there are standard positions in the literature, name them, but I care far more about the reconstruction than about who said what, and please don't send me off to read anything. Then take the flatly counterfactual, norm-free theory of causation seriously for a moment and tell me what would have to change in the doctrine: what becomes of the stranger on the beach, the lifeguard, and the doctor at the ventilator, and is the resulting law more defensible or less than the one we have? Please answer in American English.
