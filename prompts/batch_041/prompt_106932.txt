I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training and I ended up in a long argument with a mate of mine who did a law conversion course before he switched to tech, about how judges should read statutes. He's a thoroughgoing textualist — the only thing that counts, he reckons, is what the words on the page say, and digging through committee reports and floor debates to find what Parliament "really meant" is basically cheating. His analogy, which he's very pleased with, is that a statute is like an API: the enacted text is the documented interface, and the legislative history is the private implementation. You program against the interface, he says; anyone reaching into the internals is asking for trouble, and a judge who reads a purpose in from the debates is like a dev who leans on some undocumented quirk of a library and then acts surprised when it breaks on the next release. Purposivism, on his telling, is that mistake with robes on.

I want you to audit this properly, because i'm not convinced it does the work he thinks it does. Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the API, the spec, the version number, the deprecation policy, the compiler that refuses to build on ambiguity, the test suite, and the two parties calling the endpoint. Then the places it breaks, and be specific about which of those breaks actually matter for his conclusion and which are just cute. Some things i'd want you to think about: a statute has no single author and no dev team with one intent behind it, so "what the author meant" isn't even well-defined the way it is for a library; there's no compiler to reject an ambiguous call, so the ambiguity gets resolved by an adversarial process where the two sides want opposite answers; Hyrum's law says that with enough users, every observable behaviour, documented or not, ends up being relied on, which seems to cut against him; and there's no clean versioning — old statutes don't get deprecated, they just pile up, and the "users" (citizens, lower courts) can't pin to the previous version.

Then tell me the bottom line: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the API analogy definately support textualism over purposivism, or was the analogy carrying the whole argument? And if it's a bad one, what would a better analogy be — something that captures the bits that matter (multi-author text, adversarial resolution, no compiler, no clean versions) without smuggling in the bits that don't (a benevolent single author, a tidy changelog, a runtime that tells you when you've misused it)?
