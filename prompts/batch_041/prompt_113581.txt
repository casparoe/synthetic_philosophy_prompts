Ok so I'm a second-year PhD student (sociology, theory track), and I keep ending up in arguments about methodological individualism with people who got their picture of it from a one-page cheat sheet that circulates in my department — no author, no date, half the first-years study off it — and I'm fairly sure the sheet is doing damage. Here's the gist, close to how it reads:

"Methodological individualism is the view that only individuals are real; talk of states, markets, classes, and norms is just shorthand for facts about individuals. A proper social explanation therefore has to derive every social outcome from the beliefs and desires of the individuals involved, with nothing left over. Holism is the opposite position: society is a thing over and above its members, with its own consciousness and causal powers, which is why Durkheim insisted that social facts be explained by other social facts. The debate was basically settled by the fact that only individuals act — collectives can't have intentions — so holism is refuted, and MI is now the default in economics; the main holdouts are sociologists who need society to be real in order to justify their politics. Finally, MI is itself a normative commitment: it says we should explain outcomes as products of individual choice, which is why it goes hand in hand with laissez-faire."

I'm inclined to think the last bit is just guilt by association (wasn't Elster both an MI guy and a Marxist?), and I suspect the "only individuals act" line is doing something fishy, but I can't say where the rest goes wrong — and I don't want to just tell people the sheet is bad, I want to be able to point at which sentences are bad and why. So: go through it claim by claim and seperate them into (a) stuff a careful philosopher of social science would accept, (b) defensible-but-crude formulations a specialist would wince at but could live with, and (c) things that are just wrong or that quietly fuse two positions that the debate keeps apart. For each, tell me what a defender of the position being described would actually object to, not just what's true. Two things I definately want nailed down: does the "only individuals act" argument refute holism, or does it just relocate the dispute; and does methodological individualism carry any normative/political commitment, or is the laissez-faire connection an accident of who happened to be arguing for it. And if you fixed all the errors, would anything the sheet concludes survive? My hunch is that it lands half-right for entirely the wrong reasons, but tell me if that's wrong.
