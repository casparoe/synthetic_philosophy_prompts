For several years now I have been carrying around a picture of the self that I met in an essay I read long ago and have never been able to set down, and I would like a poem built on it rather than about it. The picture is this: that there is no small person or object inside us answering to the word I; that what we call a self is instead something like the center of gravity of a life's stories, the point that everything said by a person and about a person hangs together around. A center of gravity is not a part of the body and cannot be put your finger on as a piece of one (a doughnut's center of gravity falls in the hole, where there is no doughnut at all), and yet it is not a fiction: it has a definite location, it shifts when weight is added or redistributed, and other people's retellings of you can put it somewhere you would never have put it yourself. Strip away the tellings and I do not believe anything is left over; but I also do not think a self is made of stories the way a wall is made of bricks, and I do not want a poem that settles that question for me.

What I am asking for is a sestina of the ordinary kind — six stanzas of six lines, unrhymed, and a three-line envoi — and I want the form to be doing the thinking rather than decorating it. I mean to read it aloud at a memorial, so let the six end-words be plain and concrete things of the sort a family keeps mentioning about someone in that room. Let each rotation put them in genuinely new company, so that the same six items become a different story depending on where they fall, and let at least two of the tellings contradict each other, the way a family's do. In the envoi, gather all six words — three at the ends of lines, three worked into the middle of them — so that the whole pattern is briefly visible as one thing, which is as near as the poem should come to saying what a self is. Keep it in the first person plural, the voice of the people in the room, and never let it use the first person singular, since the person the poem is about is precisely what all that telling is about, and I do not want the poem claiming to stand inside them. Let none of the words self, narrative, center, gravity, identity, or soul appear; if the poem needs them it has not done the work. Someone who holds the view I have described should find the poem accurate, and someone who has never heard of it should still find it a good poem about a room full of people telling the same six stories and getting a person out of them.
