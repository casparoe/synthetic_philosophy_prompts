I have spent more years than I care to admit in the comment sections where the arguments about self-locating belief play out, and I have watched the same pattern repeat: someone raises the Sleeping Beauty case, the thread splits into camps over which prior a waking subject ought to hold, and everyone talks past everyone else about what it even means to say that one of many copies is "you." The fiction I have found that touches this material tends to stage the textbook case and then let a character announce the correct answer, which drains all the interest out of it. I want a story that treats the puzzle as live.

Write a short story of roughly 1,800 to 2,400 words, in American English, built around the following setup, which you should establish clearly enough that a reader who has never encountered any of this can follow it.

A clinic offers what it calls a redundancy service. A client is scanned once and then suspended; afterward, a random process that the client cannot observe decides how many instances of the client's pattern to instantiate. If the process comes up one way, a single instance is woken; if it comes up the other way, many instances — suppose a thousand — are woken. Every instance wakes with the client's memories up to the moment of scanning and no memory of anything after, and no instance can tell from the inside whether it is the sole survivor or one of a thousand. The waking protagonist learns the rule but not the outcome.

Now give the protagonist a decision with real stakes: she can authorize an irreversible action — a costly signal, say, or the release of a resource that is shared across all instances — whose benefit would accrue to every instance that exists, if any do. Whether the action is worth taking turns on her credence about how many instances there are, and on how she weighs her own existence as evidence about that count. Build in a second character, another instance she can communicate with, who reasons from the opposing prior and who should be given a genuinely strong case rather than a caricature. And make the machinery stranger than a plain coin: include a credible rumor, which the two characters dispute, that the random process is not random at all but is instead fixed by a simulation of what the waking instance will decide, so that instances are created in proportion to how they choose — which would make the protagonist's present choice partly responsible for how many of her there are.

The story should satisfy a few conditions. The self-locating uncertainty must be load-bearing: the plot cannot work if you replace the protagonist with someone who simply knows how many copies exist. The opposing views must each get their best argument, with the disagreement turning on something real rather than on one character being careless. The story should not settle the underlying puzzle by fiat or tack on a moral that declares one side the winner; let the tension stand. And let the human stakes — continuity of memory, the queasy possibility of not being the original, the pull of loyalty toward beings who may or may not be other people — carry as much weight as the reasoning.

Keep the story free of footnotes, citations, or appeals to named literature; I want it to stand on its own as fiction.
