I am co-teaching a graduate seminar on effective altruism this term, and a colleague who works on the ethics of philanthropy has drafted the section on moral trade for our shared syllabus. Before I put her draft in front of students, I would like a careful audit of the summary it contains, because I have reservations about it that I have not yet managed to pin down precisely.

Her summary reads as follows:

"Moral trade is the practice of two parties who disagree about values agreeing to advance each other's preferred causes instead of their own: you care about animal welfare and I care about global poverty, so I donate to your preferred charity and you donate to mine. Each party ends up with more of what she values than she would have had alone, so the arrangement is a Pareto improvement, and this is precisely why deep moral disagreement, far from being an obstacle to cooperation, is what makes cooperation profitable in the first place. The mechanism is comparative advantage, exactly as in ordinary trade: the gains appear whenever the two parties value the two causes in different proportions — indeed, whenever each party cares more about the other's cause than about her own. Nothing in the arrangement requires either party to revise her moral views, which shows that we can secure the benefits of moral agreement without anyone converging on a shared morality. The obstacles, accordingly, are not ethical but psychological: people are attached to their own causes and slow to perceive the gains. Moral trade is thus the natural solution to the collective-action problems that moral disagreement creates, and its logic is the same as that of buying carbon offsets for one's flights."

I would like you to go through this claim by claim and tell me what a philosopher working on moral trade would accept as stated, what is a defensible simplification, and what is simply mistaken. Three things interest me in particular. First, whether the account of where the gains from trade come from is correct, or whether it has quietly put in place a condition that is either too weak or too strong. Second, whether the concluding claim — that moral trade solves the collective-action problems of moral disagreement — follows from anything true in the earlier claims, or whether it inflates a much more modest thesis. Third, which objections to moral trade the summary leaves out, stated as objections that a serious defender would have to answer rather than as mere practical obstacles. Where the summary is wrong, I would also like to see what a corrected version would say, so that I can decide whether to rewrite the section myself.
