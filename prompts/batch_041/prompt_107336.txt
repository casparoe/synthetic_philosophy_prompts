Ok so I've been chewing on this pattern I keep running into — call it a commitment race — where two sides are in some dispute and each of them can lock itself into a position (publicly, or by spending money, or by making a promise they'd lose face breaking) in a way that's hard to walk back, and the whole point is to be the one who locks in first, because whoever moves second has to react to an already-frozen opponent. Standard story: precommitment is a weapon, the ability to commit helps you, and being the first to burn your boats is an advantage.

I want a case that runs the other way. Construct me a thought experiment where two parties are in a commitment race and winning the race is actually a trap — the one who manages to commit first ends up worse off than if they'd just stayed flexible. Constraints: keep it realistic (two small businesses, a divorcing couple spliting a house, two departments fighting over a budget, a landlord and a tenant — that kind of scale, not a nuclear standoff or anything sci-fi), no perfect predictors or mind-reading, no outside authority that can just enforce an agreement for them, and the irreversibility has to be genuinely irreversible, not merely expensive to undo. Give me the scenario in two paragraphs max, and don't worry about citing anything — just reason it out.

Then walk me through it: for each side, what exactly is the commitment device, why does timing matter (i.e. why does it matter that you go first and not just that you go at all), and what are the payoffs such that both are still tempted to jump even though the winner eats the loss. Point to the one feature that turns the race into a trap — the thing that flips first-mover advantage into first-mover curse — and tell me what happens to the whole case if I remove that feature. Does it collapse back into the boring 'precommitment is good' story, or does something else break?

Last thing: steelman the other side. If someone thinks precommitment is basically always advantageous, what's their best line of attack on my case — do they say the lock-in isn't really irreversible, or that a side deal could still be struck, or what? And does their attack actually work here, or is it just wishful thinking?
