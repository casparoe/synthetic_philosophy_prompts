I'm a novelist — literary fiction mostly, though my last book had a speculative streak — and I'm deep in planning for a new one. The premise: somewhere in the near future, a mid-sized insurance company (I'm calling it Prosperity Mutual, still deciding) has adopted, as official policy, something a consultant sold them as "Causal Hygiene." The rule is that only physical causes count for paying out claims — if your broken leg has a sufficient physical/neural cause, then your decision to go skiing can't be cited as a cause of it, because that would be double-counting, and the company has a hard rule against double-counting. Nobody there thinks this is strange; the mental stuff is still logged, still discussed in meetings, it just doesn't *do* anything. That's the world of the book. One of the "found documents" I want to include is meant to be funny, and I need help writing it.

So here's what I'm asking: write, in-universe, a deadpan internal FAQ / staff-handbook entry for the company's "Causal Closure Compliance" desk. Maybe 500–700 words, numbered Q&As, written by some poor HR person who has fully absorbed the party line and finds it completely reasonable. The comedy has to come out of the actual machinery of the exclusion argument, not jokes bolted on top: causal closure of the physical, the ban on systematic overdetermination, the fork between identifying the mental with the physical (in which case, as one of my characters puts it, you were never a separate thing to begin with) or letting it be excluded, and what happens to reasons, intentions, and blame once you've gone down that road. I want a philosopher of mind to read this and laugh in recognition rather than wince, so get the moves right and let the absurdity live entirely in the flat bureaucratic tone — don't exaggerate the philosophy itself, it's already doing enough work. It should stay legible to a reader who's never heard any of this (my agent is definately one of those people).

Treat yourself as a philosopher of mind with a good comic ear, and think it through before you start drafting — brainstorm the five or six places where this kind of argument is most vulnerable to a bureaucracy taking it literally, because that's where the best material is. A couple of things I'd love worked in if they fit: an employee grievance from a woman passed over for promotion who wants to claim her *intention* to do good work as the cause of the good work; and a footnote or sidebar about the "epiphenomenal consultants" the firm hires for big meetings — present, paid, causally inert. Keep it deadpan, no winking, no scare quotes around the metaphysics, and no citations or references to real books (it's a fictional company's internal doc, it wouldn't have them anyway). Thanks — this is the tone I'm chasing for the whole novel, so if it lands I'll probably ask you for alot more of these.
