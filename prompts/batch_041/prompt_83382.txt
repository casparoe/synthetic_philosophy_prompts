I am a teaching assistant on a second-year undergraduate metaphysics course, and I am working through the final examination. The question reproduced below was worth 20 of the 100 marks, and students were advised to spend about twenty-five minutes on it. I would like an assessment of one script that is detailed enough to be returned to the student and to withstand an appeal against the mark.

Question.

A bronze statue and the lump of bronze of which it is made appear to occupy exactly the same place at exactly the same time and to be composed of exactly the same matter. Yet they also appear to differ: the lump existed before the sculptor began work, and it would survive the statue's being melted down, whereas the statue would not; the statue is essentially a statue, whereas the lump is only accidentally one. (a) Set out the argument these observations generate against the identity of the statue and the lump. (b) Explain and evaluate at least two responses to that argument. (c) Explain the objection that a constitution view cannot say what grounds the difference between the two objects, and say whether the objection can be met. (d) State which response you think is best, and why.

Answer.

Let S be the statue and L the lump. S and L coincide: every part of S is a part of L and every part of L is a part of S, and there is no region of space occupied by one and not the other. If they were distinct we should expect to find some difference in their parts or in their whereabouts, and there is none; so far as their material situation goes, there is just one thing there.

They nevertheless appear to differ in their properties. The bronze was cast as a lump some years before the sculptor began work, so L existed before S did. L will also outlast S, since the bronze can be melted down and recast, and a statue cannot survive that. And L is only accidentally a statue, since it might have been made into a bowl instead, whereas S is essentially a statue and could not have been anything else.

By Leibniz's Law, if S = L then every property of S is a property of L and conversely. Since L has the property of having existed before the sculptor began and S does not, S is not L. (Leibniz's Law is the converse of the principle of the identity of indiscernibles, so the two principles stand or fall together.) Identity is therefore too strong a relation to hold between the statue and the lump, and we need something weaker.

The standard suggestion is constitution: L constitutes S, and constitution is not identity. Constitution is the relation between a material object and the matter that makes it up; it is irreflexive and asymmetric. The constituted object inherits its mass, shape and location from the constituting matter but not its modal and historical properties, which is how S and L can differ in the latter while agreeing in the former. This also captures the asymmetry of dependence: S could not exist without L, but L could exist without S.

Eliminativism says that there are no statues, only lumps of bronze arranged statue-wise. This dissolves the problem by denying that there are two things there. But it is very hard to believe. We count statues, insure them and put them in galleries; if there are no statues, a great deal of what we say about art is false, and the view saves a puzzle at the cost of the obvious.

Four-dimensionalism says that S and L are distinct objects, because L has temporal parts that S lacks, namely the parts of the bronze's career that precede the sculpting. They are not identical, since identity is identity of the whole four-dimensional extent, but they share a temporal part at every moment at which S exists, which is why they appear to coincide. This is elegant, and it has the advantage that the modal differences can be handled by counterpart theory rather than by brute essentialist facts.

Deflationism says that there is only one object here, which we may describe either as a statue or as a lump, the difference lying in our concepts rather than in reality. This is really the same strategy as four-dimensionalism: both avoid the puzzle by refusing to multiply objects, and both locate the difference in how the situation is described rather than in the situation itself.

I favour the constitution view. It preserves the existence of statues, avoids the ontological extravagance of four-dimensionalism, and does justice to the dependence of the statue on the bronze. The grounding objection asks what makes S essentially a statue and L only accidentally one, given that S and L are microphysically identical. I think this can be answered: the modal difference is grounded in the physical facts about the two objects' histories, since the bronze's career includes the casting of the lump and S's does not, and histories are themselves physical facts. So no non-physical ingredient is needed, and the constitution view is the only one that respects both our ordinary counting practices and the priority of the bronze to the statue.
