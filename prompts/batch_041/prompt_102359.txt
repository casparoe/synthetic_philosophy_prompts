I am a novelist a third of the way into a long book about a woman who leaves a marriage, and my difficulty is one of method: I keep writing scenes in which she announces what she wants and I do not believe her, or in which what she does and what she says diverge, and my editor returns the pages with the same question in the margin. I want a procedure I can run scene by scene, before drafting rather than after, built on the economist's treatment of preference as revealed in choice rather than reported in speech. First fix the terms, since I keep sliding between three things I might mean by what she wants — what she would choose, what she would endorse on reflection, and what would be good for her. Then say what a choice must have been like to reveal anything: which alternatives were genuinely available, what each cost her, whether the same options come round twice. Say what I may conclude when her choices cycle rather than settle — incompleteness, a change of mind, a want I have not yet named — since I must not treat every contradiction as depth. And say when to stop inferring and write from the inside, because the interiority this whole picture treats as dispensable is what my book is for: give me a rule for the case where her avowed and revealed wants disagree, one that distinguishes self-deception, a change of mind, and my own wish that she want something else. Set out the steps in the order I should run them, mark the ones that are irreducibly my judgment — who decides which choices count as costly, and which choices I stage at all — and name the failure mode in which the procedure merely confirms the character my plot requires. I have no economics and my editor has less; revisions are expensive. Run it on three scenes: she refuses an offer she has spent a chapter asking for; she pays for something she has called worthless; and she decides to leave — where I suspect the procedure will tell me she does not want to leave, and I would rather it did not.
