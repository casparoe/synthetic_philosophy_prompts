I am a high school student in an ethics elective, and I am trying to work out what I actually believe about cases in which one person's burden is weighed against the much smaller burdens of a great many people. As I understand Scanlon's contractualism, the test is not a matter of adding up benefits and burdens across everyone; it asks whether some single person could reasonably object to living under a principle that permitted the action, with each person's objection considered on its own. Going by my own reactions, I seem to accept some aggregation and to reject other aggregation, and I would like to know whether there is a version of the test that fits me.

My verdicts:

1. Cutting off one person's hand so that ten million people each avoid an hour of nausea: not permissible.
2. Requiring one person to spend three days with a bad cold so that ten million people each avoid a day of bad flu: permissible.
3. Two swimmers are drowning, one on the left and five on the right, and I can save either group with equal ease: saving the five is permissible.
4. Approving a vaccine that will give one person in a million a permanent tremor, while everyone else avoids an illness that would keep them in bed for two days: permissible.
5. A rule that gives each of ten million people a one-in-ten-million chance of losing a hand, in exchange for all ten million each avoiding an hour of nausea -- the same expected numbers as in case 1, but spread as a risk over everyone rather than concentrated on one person: I keep changing my mind.

I suspect the difference between cases 1 and 2 has something to do with whether the burden on the one person is of roughly the same size as the burden on each of the many, but I cannot tell whether that is a principled line or merely a description of my reactions. Please tell me whether a single principle gets all of these verdicts, and if so, state it precisely enough that I can apply it to cases I have not listed. In particular, say what it implies about case 5, give its verdict on one further case of your own choosing, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and whether I should give that verdict up.
