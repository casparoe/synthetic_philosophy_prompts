I am a high school student, and I have been trying to work out how we ought to judge historical figures such as Jefferson, Churchill, and Columbus. In a history class discussion I found myself defending a particular version of the so-called man-of-his-time defence, and I would like you to tell me what would count against it.

The version I hold is roughly this: a person from the past is fairly blamed for an act only if the moral objection to that act was genuinely available to them — only if someone in their time and place could have put the objection in terms they could have understood and had some reason to accept. Where the objection depends on moral discoveries that had not yet been made, blame seems misplaced. I do not intend this as a blanket excuse; I intend it as a claim about what it is fair to hold someone responsible for.

Please tell me what would count against this position, ranked from the considerations that would be fatal to it to those that would be merely awkward. I would particularly like to know: what sorts of historical facts show that the objection really was available to a given figure, and how much weight should the existence of dissenting contemporaries carry — especially those who were marginalised or ignored at the time? Can the position distinguish someone who genuinely could not have known better from someone who arranged not to know, and what evidence would settle that? Does the position, taken seriously, prove too much, exonerating nearly everyone and making any moral criticism of the past impossible? And are there internal tensions — for instance, could a figure be condemned by the standards they themselves professed, or by the standards of the community they claimed to belong to? I am also unsure what to make of the thought that, if our own moral outlook is shaped by our time, this position may undercut our standing to judge anyone at all; I would like to know whether that is a genuine consequence of the view or a confusion.

Finally, I would like an honest assessment of whether the position I have described is a substantive moral claim or whether, once stated carefully, it has been qualified into something nearly empty — and, if the latter, whether that is a defect or simply what any reasonable position of this kind must look like. Where the defence's own defenders would concede a point, I would like to hear that too, not only the critics' side.
