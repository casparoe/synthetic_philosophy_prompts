Two nights ago a friend and I argued about pacifism until nearly dawn, and the objection he left me with was not the familiar one. He is not a pacifist. He thinks the position is either vacuous or dishonest, and he put it like this: when our network stages a sit-in at a weapons plant and a worker must choose between driving over a grandmother and losing his job, we have not renounced force at all — we have merely found a way to apply it without touching anyone, and to keep our hands clean by letting the other man's conscience do the work. He also claimed that the single word hides two different doctrines, one that forbids the act whatever comes of it and one that forbids producing the harms of war, and that these two disagree precisely in the cases that matter to me. He ended by saying that a doctrine which cannot tell me whether to authorize a particular action on a particular Saturday is not a doctrine but a mood, and I could not answer him with anything I would be willing to write down.

I coordinate training and tactics for a small nonviolent direct-action network, and I am the person who, most weeks, either clears or refuses an action before it happens. The decisions recur, I have perhaps half an hour per action, I have no one on call with a philosophy degree, and my calls go to a five-person steering committee that can overturn me. The stakes are real: people get arrested, occasionally get hurt, and the network's claim to be nonviolent is the entire basis of its standing with the communities we work in. I want to turn pacifism from a position I hold into a procedure my team can run.

Please write that procedure. It should be a sequence of questions to ask in order, with a clear statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure stops and refuses the action, and the points at which it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of judgment or value rather than fact. Ground it in what the serious thinking about nonviolence and the ethics of force actually establishes, not in slogans; I do not need a reading list or citations, and I would rather you reason from the arguments themselves. The procedure has to take a position on the distinctions my friend and I could not settle: between violence and coercion; between what we do, what we allow, and what we provoke; between refusing to initiate force and refusing to answer force in kind; and between our responsibility for our own members and for bystanders and opponents who never chose to be in the story. If the honest answer is that pacifism yields different procedures depending on which of the two doctrines you adopt, then say so, choose the reading that survives contact with the cases rather than the one that sounds best, and tell me what I give up by choosing it.

Then run the procedure on three actions we actually face: a road-blocking sit-in at a munitions depot, whose point is to make the plant's workers and the police choose between clearing us and being seen to hurt us; a request from our members to physically restrain a counter-protester who is beating one of us; and whether to accept logistical help from a nearby faction that has smashed windows and burned a police car. At least one of your answers should be one I will not like, and you should say which of the three the procedure handles worst and why. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do — the cases where it will misfire, the distinctions it will flatten, and the point at which I should stop pretending that following it makes me a pacifist rather than merely a careful organizer.
