For a working session of a population-ethics reading group I run, I want a one-page classification instrument rather than an essay: a decision tree that takes a reader from a few plain commitments about how populations may be compared to a named position in population axiology. I need the tree built from the logical space first and from the names second, so please phrase every internal node as a question a reader can answer without already knowing which view it leads to, and make the questions bite on concrete comparisons rather than on labels. Use at least the following cases as the material of the nodes: a comparison between a small, very well-off population and an astronomically larger population whose lives are only barely worth living; a "mere addition" comparison in which one outcome contains everyone in the other plus a further group with positive but low welfare; a same-number comparison that varies only how welfare is distributed; a case where an added life is worth living to the person living it but leaves everyone else no better off and some slightly worse off; a case of choosing between outcomes whose members are entirely different people; and one case in which the two populations are made of beings whose psychologies are so different that it is unclear whether their welfare levels are comparable at all. I would also like at least one axis that is not the usual additivity-versus-not axis, so please include a branch on whether the value of one person's life may depend on the rest of the population (their relative standing, the fact of being in a minority, or their life falling above or below some population-level threshold) rather than being fixed by that life alone, and a branch on whether the sheer number of lives, or the variety of lives, is treated as a good in its own right over and above the welfare contained in them.

The leaves I need to be able to reach are: totalism; a critical-level view on which lives below some positive welfare level count against the total; an average view; a variable-value view in which the weight given to adding lives falls as the population grows; a narrow person-affecting view that counts only effects on people who would exist either way; a wider person-affecting view that also counts as good the existence of people whose lives are worth living; a view on which bringing a life into existence is never itself a benefit though it may be a harm, the asymmetry-shaped leaf; and a view that refuses a complete ranking and holds some of these comparisons to be on a par or not comparable at all. If your tree can reach a leaf only by combining answers that no one would actually give, keep the combination in the tree, mark it 'unoccupied', and add one clause on whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Two positions my group keeps conflating are the critical-level view and the variable-value view, and I suspect at least one pair of leaves in your tree will be indistinguishable given the cases above; flag any such pair explicitly rather than inventing a question to separate them, and say what further case would be needed. Deliver it as a single tree with the nodes numbered and the leaves labelled, followed by a short note on the two or three places where the tree's structure encodes a substantive assumption rather than a neutral logical split.
