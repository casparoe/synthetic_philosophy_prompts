I am assembling a reader for a graduate seminar on prediction, confirmation, and the long run, and I would like you to write a piece of sustained satire for it — the kind of thing students can read as a case study rather than merely as a joke. The target is not Malthus himself, who is long dead and whose argument deserves a better hearing than it usually gets, but the practice that has grown up around him: the vindication of a failed long-run prediction by the claim that it was not wrong, only early. Write the annual letter from the director of an invented forecasting institute to the members who fund it, an institution that has been predicting a resource catastrophe since the early nineteenth century and has been unfulfilled in every decade since. Roughly eight hundred words, in the director's voice, as continuous prose: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. The house style of the institute's correspondence is plain paragraphs, and I would like the piece to read like a real document rather than a sketch.

The director must not be a fool or a fraud. He is a competent reasoner who has inherited and extended a real apparatus of auxiliary hypotheses, each of which had an independent motivation when it was introduced and each of which now does the work of moving the date. Give the institute an internal vocabulary that does that work for it, and let the reader see the mechanism without the director seeing it: for instance, a house distinction between a revision, which alters the mechanism and so does not count against the forecast, and a deferral, which merely alters the date and does, paired with a ledger in which every past failure has been retroactively reclassified as a revision. A recursive definition of the long run, as any horizon beyond the point at which the forecast has so far failed, would be welcome too. Keep the jargon invented; do not name any real scholar, school, or book, and do not let the letter turn into a lecture on philosophy of science.

The comedy should come from the epistemology rather than the prose. I want the reader to feel the precise moment at which the claim that the forecast was right but early stops being a claim about the world and becomes a rule for reading the institute's own record, and I want that moment to arrive through the director's own argument rather than through a narrator's wink. Include at least one passage in which he is simply right: the mechanism he identified is still operating, or the objection he is answering is a bad one. The piece should have a sting, and the reader should not be allowed to settle into the comfortable view that only cranks make long-run predictions. Put words in no one's mouth but your invented characters': no quotations from real texts, and no attributions of doctrine to named real people.

By the end, without anyone stating it, the reader should be left holding the real question — what it would take for such a forecast to be falsified at all, and whether the difference between this institute and a respectable forecaster is anything more than the quality of the manners.
