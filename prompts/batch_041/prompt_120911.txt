I am writing a novel, and I have reached a scene I would rather not fake. My protagonist is a behavioural economist who has spent twenty years on intertemporal choice, and at a long dinner a man who has had rather too much of her wine asks her to say something amusing about her own subject. She owes the table a joke, and I want it to be one that only a reader who has genuinely thought about weakness of will would find funny — the laugh should come from the structure of the thing, not from the word "procrastination."

Here is the material she has to work with, and I would like the joke built out of it rather than out of deadline humour. On her account, weakness of will is not a failure of strength. It is what happens when an agent's preferences are not stable as the reward approaches: when discounting is steep over the near term and gentle thereafter — the shape the field calls hyperbolic — she prefers the larger, later good while both are distant, and the smaller, sooner good the moment the sooner one is within reach, and each preference is sincerely held at the moment it is held. She therefore denies that she is one will that keeps losing. She is a series of temporally indexed selves, none of whom ever occupies the same moment as another, each of whom can veto the rest, and none of whom can negotiate. She is equally fond of two corollaries. The first is that any device a person builds to bind her future self must itself be binding on the person who built it, and can only be so if it is made of something other than her own will — a lock, a friend, a bank, a promise made loudly in company; the tower of such devices either rises forever or comes to rest on something that does not share her problem. The second is the less consoling one: the discounter who foresees his own defection may end up worse off than the one who is surprised by it, since he alone can see that he will never finish, and so declines to begin. That contrast — the one who is surprised every time and the one who has arranged his whole life around a defection he can see coming — is the one she cares most about, and I suspect it is where the joke is.

Give me the joke she tells: four to eight sentences of prose, the sort of thing a clever, slightly tired woman can deliver across a table without losing the room. It should be funny to people who know this literature and merely pleasant to everyone else, and it must not be mistakable for a joke told by someone who has never opened a book on the subject. The punchline should turn on the actual machinery — reversal under declining discount rates, the difference between the surprised and the foreseeing, or the regress of self-binding devices — and not on the general fact that people put things off. It should not need names, footnotes, or citations; she is talking, not lecturing. One clean joke, please, rather than a set of variations, and a single sentence afterwards telling me which piece of machinery you made the punchline turn on.
