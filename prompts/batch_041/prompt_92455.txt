Here is a comparison I want worked out rather than summarized. Two familiar claims say that a measured regularity stops holding once you act on it. The first is Goodhart's law: an indicator that tracks some quantity well enough while it is merely being watched tends to lose that tracking power once it is made a target, a score, or a basis for reward. The second is the Lucas critique: the relationships estimated from historical economic data are not structural but are summaries of how agents behaved under the policy regime that generated the data, so a change of regime changes the relationships and undermines forecasts built on them.

I want these two compared under a distinction I have not seen drawn cleanly, and I would like you to treat the distinction itself as up for assessment rather than grant it. Call the first kind of breakdown strategic invalidation: the indicator stays a faithful description of the world until agents begin to optimize against it, and their optimizing is what breaks it. Call the second structural invalidation: the indicator was never describing a stable object, because the object was itself a response to the regime in place, so the breakdown follows from the measure's own presuppositions rather than from anyone's gaming. My question is whether Goodhart's law is best read as a case of the first and the Lucas critique as a case of the second, and whether that division survives scrutiny -- in particular whether Goodhart's law also admits a structural version, in which the indicator's validity erodes with no strategic agent involved, and whether the Lucas critique also admits a strategic version, in which the agents' response to the new rule, rather than the rule's effect on the coefficients, does the damage. Where the two overlap, say what the overlap predicts that neither claim predicts alone; where they come apart, say what observation would tell them apart.

Then consider a test case I supply, so that we are both working from the same facts rather than from your choice of example: a hospital publishes its risk-adjusted surgical mortality rates, and within a few years the published rates improve while the underlying outcomes, measured independently, do not. Treat this as a case and say which of the two diagnoses it favors, and what further fact would have to hold for the other diagnosis to win.
