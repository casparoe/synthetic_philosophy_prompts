I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and in the course of reporting I keep colliding with a single sentence: this is "the best of all possible worlds," a phrase routinely credited to Leibniz though I have never read the work it comes from. In one week I heard it put to two opposite uses. At a public panel on the problem of evil, a theologian produced it as a way of stopping the objection in its tracks — the thought being that if there is a God, then whatever world he actualized must be the best one available, so the sheer quantity of suffering we observe cannot count as evidence against him. A few days later, a physicist on a different stage cited the same sentence as a punchline, the reductio of armchair optimism, and the audience laughed. Neither speaker defined a single term, and I suspect they were not asserting the same proposition at all.

What I want from you is a disambiguation, not a defense or a refutation. Lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise claims that this one sentence could be making, ordered from the nearly empty to the nearly incredible, and for each one give me a verdict: is it a triviality that anyone would accept once it is spelled out, a substantive claim that is defensible, a claim that is genuinely contentious, or a claim that is simply false? I am interested especially in the joints where the readings come apart — how "best" is being measured and over what domain, whether it ranges over a global optimum or over particular events, and what notion of "possible" is in play (logically consistent, jointly realizable, or physically available). I also want to know which reading each of my two speakers was most likely reaching for, and, more pointedly, which reading — if any — would actually do the work the theologian needed it to do, namely neutralize the problem of evil rather than merely restate it. If the sentence cannot bear that weight under any reading, say so plainly and tell me what stronger principle he would have needed instead.

Please keep it to prose plus the numbered menu, written so that I can carry it in a notebook and check it against the next person who quotes the line at me. Do not send me to sources, and do not pad it with the history of the idea; I want the anatomy of the claim.
