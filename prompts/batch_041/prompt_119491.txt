I've been going down a cold fusion rabbit hole — Pons and Fleischmann, Utah, March '89, press conference before the paper was even out — and I want to use it as a test case for underdetermination of theory by evidence, the Duhem-Quine kind, except I dont want the tidy 'and thats why replication matters' ending. Setup as I understand it: heavy-water electrolysis with palladium electrodes; they claim the cell puts out more heat than goes in, plus a trickle of neutrons and gammas; the heat numbers sit right at the edge of what the calorimetry can resolve; the neutron counts are nowhere near what d-d fusion would give for that much heat, so whatever's making the heat isn't the reaction they say it is; some labs get positives, more get nothing, and the positives keep insisting you need the right palladium, the right loading, the right cell; eventually the DOE panel and most of the field say there's nothing there and it gets filed under pathological science.

So: take the evidence as of mid-1989 and tell me whether it genuinely underdetermined the choice between 'new nuclear process' and 'calorimetry error plus wishful thinking' — same evidence, both hypotheses live — or whether the underdetermination framing just lets one side off the hook for something they'd already botched. Map the auxiliaries on each side and say which ones are carrying the weight. Then say where underdetermination runs out: the neutron deficit looks less like neutral evidence than like evidence that actively contradicts the claim, so is this even an underdetermination case, or is it people not updating? If the experimenter's regress helps — you can't know whether an experiment was competent until you know whether the effect is real — use it, and tell me how the circle actually got broken, and whether the break was evidential or just social.

And the part I actually care about: what does cold fusion tell us about underdetermination itself? That it's true but toothless, because the community converged anyway on non-evidential grounds? That the strong version is false because the evidence was never balanced? Or that it's a case the philosophers didn't anticipate, where holism doubles as a shield — the same move that saves real science from premature refutation also saves garbage? And say where your own reasoning is most likely to fail and what would move you: if a clean lab got reproducible excess heat with the same neutron deficit, or the '89 calorimetry turned out to have been sound, how much does your answer change?
