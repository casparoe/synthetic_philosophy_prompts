I am not a philosopher by training and I have never studied the subject formally, but I have been thinking about consciousness on my own for several years, and I have arrived — reluctantly — at a position I can state plainly. Experiences are real: there is something it is like to see the color of a ripe tomato, or to feel a headache coming on. But experiences do nothing. Whatever causes my arm to move, my mouth to speak, or my fingers to type is a physical process in my brain; the feeling that accompanies it is a by-product. Present, perhaps the most important thing in the world, and yet causally inert. If my experiences were subtracted from the universe and everything physical were left exactly as it is, every physical event would unfold just as it does now, and I would write precisely the sentences I am writing. I gather this position has a name — epiphenomenalism — though I came to it before I knew that.

I would like you to treat it as a position to be attacked rather than defended. What would count against it? I want a map of its exposure: the observations, arguments, thought experiments, and conceivable findings that tell against it, sorted by how damaging they would be, from the ones that should make me give the view up to the ones that are merely uncomfortable. Along the way, distinguish between the objections a committed epiphenomenalist would themselves admit are serious and the ones they would wave away as missing the point. Tell me what my view predicts that its rivals do not — what I am sticking my neck out for — and which results in other areas, say about causation or about what makes a belief reliable, it stands or falls with. I am not asking to be pointed at literature; I want the arguments themselves.

I am especially troubled by one thing, which may be the whole problem in miniature. I arrived at this position by reflecting on my own experiences and reasoning about them. But if experiences are causally inert, then no experience of mine played any part in producing my belief that experiences are causally inert. That belief was produced by brain processes, and it happens to be about the very things those processes also produce. That looks like a systematic, reliable, truth-tracking coincidence between a class of causally idle events and the beliefs that correctly describe them. I want to know whether this is as serious as it feels. Can the position explain the reliability of our beliefs about our own experiences without quietly reintroducing causation running from experience to belief, and without simply declaring the agreement a brute fact? Does it help that the physical state which causes the belief also causes the experience — does that make the correlation less miraculous, or does it merely relocate the mystery? Does the same difficulty arise for my belief in other people's experiences, or for the trust I place in introspection generally?

I am also unsure whether my position is a distinct position at all. Someone might tell me that the experience just is the intrinsic character of the brain state that does the causing, or that what I call the feeling is simply that brain process as it appears from the inside. I cannot tell whether that is a restatement of my view, a rival to it, or a way of dissolving it. If it is a rival, tell me what would decide between them; if it is a restatement, tell me whether anything is left of the claim that experiences do no work.

One consequence of the view bothers me more than I expected. If I am right, a creature physically identical to me but with no inner life whatsoever would behave identically — would wince, would write these very words, would insist it was in pain. Is that a consequence I should simply accept, or is it a reason to doubt the view? I would like it treated as a consequence to be weighed rather than a puzzle to be enjoyed.

Finally, I would like an honest verdict on whether a view of this shape can be refuted at all. It seems to have a way of absorbing every report: whatever I say about my experiences can be attributed to the physical state that produced both the report and the experience. If that is right, say so, and say whether it is a defect in the view or simply a feature of any view about the relation between mind and body. If it is a defect, describe what a better-positioned version of the view would look like — and tell me whether that version is still mine.
