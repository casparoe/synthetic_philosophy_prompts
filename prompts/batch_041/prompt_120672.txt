I have never studied philosophy, but for a few years I have been keeping a notebook of cases in which someone carries on with something because of what they have already put into it, and my reactions to them will not settle into a pattern. I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, is doing the work in my judgements, what it says about cases I have not written down, and which of my judgements I ought to abandon. I am not after a survey of what other people have said about this; I want the principle itself, argued for against my cases.

The cases, roughly as they stand in the notebook:

A man three-quarters of the way through a large and expensive restaurant meal, no longer hungry and no longer enjoying it, who finishes it because he has paid for it. Plainly irrational; I feel no pull the other way.

Someone who sits through the second half of a film he is not enjoying because the ticket is bought and the first half is behind him. Irrational as well, but it strikes me as a smaller offence than the meal, and I suspect that is only because the time left is short rather than because the past counts for anything.

A man who cannot bring himself to cancel a magazine subscription he has not read for two years, because cancelling would mean those two years of unopened issues were wasted. Irrational, and I notice that the waste is exactly the same whether he cancels or not.

A company that keeps funding a failing product because of what it has already spent on it, and a government that keeps a half-built bridge going for the same reason. Both irrational.

A government that keeps a war going partly because of the soldiers already lost. This does not feel like the others, and I resist calling it irrational, though I cannot say what the difference is.

A man who promised to run a marathon in exchange for sponsorship and now wants to stop. I think he should carry on, and I think the money already promised is part of the reason.

A donor who keeps giving to a charity that has squandered his earlier gifts, because stopping would mean those gifts were wasted. Irrational.

A woman who has spent twelve years building a boat in her garden, no longer wants the boat, and carries on regardless. I would not call her simply irrational, though I would not call her rational either; it seems to me that the twelve years are now part of what the boat is.

A town that has held the same festival for two hundred years and keeps it going though hardly anyone enjoys it. I am inclined to say it should keep it, and I am uneasy about that inclination.

A patient who continues a painful and expensive treatment that is plainly not working, because of what he has already endured. Irrational.

The best I have come up with on my own is this: honouring a past cost is irrational when the past cost is the whole of the reason, and not irrational when the past cost has left something in the present — a promise made to others, a skill that makes success likelier now, or a relation to a thing, a place or a person that has become part of who one is. What troubles me is that I cannot always tell whether I am pointing to something genuinely present or merely dressing up the past, and I suspect I am doing the latter in the war, the boat and the festival, where I would rather be told so than humoured.

So: does one principle cover these cases, or do I need two, or none? Which verdicts does your principle force me to give up, and be direct about it. What does it say about a man who stays in a job he dislikes because of the years he has put into the pension, a family firm kept going out of duty to a dead founder, and someone who will not abandon a hobby because of the money tied up in equipment? And is there a test I could apply myself, on the spot, to tell the two sorts of case apart?

Please write your answer in British English.
