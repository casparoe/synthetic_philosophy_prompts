I spent about 35 years as a bench scientist before I retired, and the last couple of years I've been reading philosophy, mostly ethics and a bit of philosophy of language, and I've gotten stuck on something I used to think was simple. Everyone knows lying is wrong. But a lot of what I did over a long career was technically not lying, and I've started to wonder whether that's a real distinction or just a lawyer's distinction I used to make myself feel better about myself. I want you to tell me what principle, if any, actually fits my gut reactions, because I've stopped trusting my own summary of them.

Here's the view I walked in with, which I don't think survives contact with my own cases. Lying = saying something false on purpose. Misleading = saying something true in a way that gets someone to believe something false. Lying is worse. Simple. Now the cases.

- Grant renewal, pilot data had come out null, and I wrote "our preliminary results support the hypothesis." Straight false, and I knew it when I typed it. Verdict: wrong, no excuses. But honestly? Not the one that keeps me up at night.
- A journalist asked whether the compound worked in the mice. We'd seen shrinkage in three of twelve and nothing in the rest, no significant effect. I said "the early results are encouraging." Every word true. Verdict: wrong, and worse than the grant thing, even though I never said anything false.
- My worst one. A paper where I reported the experiment that worked and simply didn't mention the four that didn't. Not one false sentence in it, nothing even misleading sentence by sentence. Verdict: I feel worse about this than about the flat lie in the grant. Alot worse.
- A recommendation letter where I wrote "he is meticulous, punctual, and a pleasure to work with" and said nothing at all about the research, knowing exactly how that would read. Verdict: wrong, and sneaky in a way the grant lie somehow wasn't.
- A talk where I said "we've tried everything," meaning all the obvious things, and everyone in the room knew that's what I meant. Verdict: fine. Not remotely dishonest.
- A dying colleague asked whether he'd be back at the bench by spring and I said yes. Verdict: fine. I'd do it again tomorrow.
- The one I only understood years later: I told a student her project was promising because I genuinely believed it, and I believed it because I'd only ever looked at the runs that worked. Verdict: I did her real harm, and I still can't make myself call it a lie.

Two things I keep noticing. First, my verdicts seem to track what kind of document it was rather than what came out of my mouth — grants are advocacy and everybody knows it, papers are supposed to be reports — and I can't tell whether that's a real moral distinction or just my rationalization. Second, the cases that bother me most all seem to involve my standing as someone whose results can be taken at face value. It wasn't that I got somebody to believe one false thing, it's that I spent something.

So: is there one principle that gets all seven, or close? State it precisely and tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. Say whether the genre point is doing honest work or whether I'm just dressing up an excuse. And please invent one case I haven't listed and tell me what the principle predicts and whether I should believe it. Don't just tell me both are wrong — I want the actual difference, if there is one.

Please write your answer in British English, I've been reading a lot of British stuff lately and it's rubbed off on me.
