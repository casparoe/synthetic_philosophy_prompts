Write the joint statement that two camps in the foundations of decision theory could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the coherence arguments for expected utility maximization.

The first camp, which I will call the coherence-constraint camp, holds that the standard representation theorems for choice under risk and under uncertainty, taken together with the sure-loss and money-pump arguments, establish a genuine requirement of practical rationality: an agent whose preferences over prospects are incomplete or intransitive, or who violates the independence or sure-thing condition, is not merely idiosyncratic but defective, and can be led by a sequence of trades it accepts at every step into a position it strictly disprefers. The second camp, the representation-only camp, holds that those theorems establish only a conditional result -- that a preference relation which already satisfies the axioms can be represented as the maximization of expected utility -- and that the axioms themselves, especially completeness and independence, are not rationally compulsory; on this view the standard arguments either smuggle in what they purport to prove, or are rendered vacuous by the possibility of enriching the outcome space until any preference whatsoever satisfies them.

Produce the document such a collaboration would yield: the claims both camps can sign, concerning what the theorems do and do not establish, the status of the money-pump argument, and what would count as evidence bearing on the dispute; the precise points of remaining disagreement, each formulated in wording its own side would accept; the cruxes -- the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side; and, for each crux, a case, argument, or observation that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

Treat the following as candidate cruxes, to be argued into or out of the document rather than assumed to be genuine. First, whether the outcome-enrichment manoeuvre -- redescribing outcomes so that they encompass everything the agent cares about, including how the choice came about -- makes the representation theorem vacuous, and if so which axioms retain any content. Second, whether completeness is a requirement of rationality, an idealization, or a bookkeeping convenience, and what follows for agents whose preferences exhibit parity, incommensurability, or vagueness. Third, whether the money-pump argument is synchronic or diachronic, and whether it assumes rather than establishes that an agent cannot commit in advance, cannot rationally revise her values, or cannot be a resolute chooser who follows a plan she knows would be exploitable were she to re-optimize at every step. Fourth, whether the independence or sure-thing condition is a structural consistency condition or carries substantive content -- tested against an agent who satisfies completeness and transitivity over outcomes but whose preferences over those same outcomes shift according to whether the outcome is described as chosen by her or merely bestowed on her. Fifth, whether the coherence argument is best read as a deductive proof, as a transcendental argument about what it is to have preferences at all, or as a pragmatic warning about exploitation, and whether these readings diverge in their conclusions. Sixth, whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal -- a disagreement about what rationality or preference is to mean rather than about how any agent would behave -- and if so, which part and why.

No citations or references are wanted; the document must stand on its own arguments. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table with columns for the crux, the coherence-constraint camp's formulation, the representation-only camp's formulation, the shared evidence bearing on it, that camp's prediction, and the other camp's prediction. Close with a short section in which each camp states, in its own words, what would change its mind.
