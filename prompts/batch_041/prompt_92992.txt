Examination question:

Throughout the term we reconstructed Pascal's Wager as a decision problem under uncertainty, and we separated three distinct claims that have been advanced on its behalf.

(i) The superdominance claim: wagering is at least as good as not wagering in every state of the world, and strictly better in at least one.
(ii) The expected-value claim: wagering has infinite expected value, and therefore outranks any finite alternative, however small the probability assigned to the deity's existence.
(iii) The invulnerability claim: the wager's conclusion does not depend on any particular assignment of numerical probabilities, so an agent cannot escape it by adjusting prior credences.

Consider the following variant, which we shall call the Partitioned Wager. There are three mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive states: state C, in which the Christian God exists and consigns non-Christians to damnation; state M, in which a Muslim God exists and consigns non-Muslims to damnation; and state N, in which no deity exists. Assume that belief is not under an agent's direct voluntary control, so that the only available acts are (a) a sincere lifelong Christian practice, (b) a sincere lifelong Muslim practice, and (c) a sincere lifelong nonbelief. Each act carries a finite cost of practice, normalised to one unit, and the payoffs below already include that cost. The payoffs are:

in state C: (a) +∞, (b) −∞, (c) −∞;
in state M: (a) −∞, (b) +∞, (c) −∞;
in state N: (a) −1, (b) −1, (c) −1.

Take the agent's credences to be P(C) = 0.4, P(M) = 0.4, and P(N) = 0.2, and use the extended-real arithmetic introduced in lecture.

(a) Set out the decision matrix and compute the expected value of each act.
(b) For each of claims (i), (ii), and (iii), state whether it survives in the Partitioned Wager, and justify your verdict.
(c) An objector claims that the Partitioned Wager refutes Pascal, because it shows that wagering is a gamble one cannot win. Assess this objection.

Candidate's script:

(a) The matrix is as follows. Rows are acts, columns are states, entries are payoffs.

State C (0.4): act (a) +∞; act (b) −∞; act (c) −∞.
State M (0.4): act (a) −∞; act (b) +∞; act (c) −∞.
State N (0.2): act (a) −1; act (b) −1; act (c) −1.

Expected values. For act (a): EV(a) = 0.4(+∞) + 0.4(−∞) + 0.2(−1) = ∞ − ∞, which is undefined in the extended reals, so EV(a) is undefined. For act (b), by symmetry with (a), EV(b) is likewise undefined. For act (c): EV(c) = 0.4(−∞) + 0.4(−∞) + 0.2(−1) = −∞. So acts (a) and (b) have undefined expectations, and act (c) has expectation −∞.

(b) Claim (ii) does not survive. The wager was supposed to deliver an infinite expectation, and here the expectation of wagering is not infinite but undefined; an undefined quantity cannot be said to outrank anything, so the argument's engine does not run. This is the central lesson of the variant.

Claim (i) does not survive either. Superdominance is simply the limiting case of the expected-value claim in which the favourable state is certain, and here the favourable state for each act has probability only 0.4. Since wagering is not certain to be rewarded, it is not superdominant.

Claim (iii) does not survive. Because the expectations of (a) and (b) are undefined, the agent has no reason to prefer wagering to not wagering, and the verdict now turns on which of the three states obtains. The argument is therefore at the mercy of the credences in a way the original wager was not.

(c) The objector is right that the Partitioned Wager is a gamble one cannot win. In the original wager the agent who wagers stands to gain infinitely in the state where God exists; in the Partitioned Wager, whatever the agent does, there is some state in which the act is punished with −∞, so no act is ever safe. Since no act is safe, the wager yields no recommendation, and the many-gods problem therefore defeats Pascal.
