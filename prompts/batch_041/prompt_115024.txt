I've been arguing with a friend who does policy analysis for a finance ministry in a mid-sized country, and I keep noticing that the same government seems to believe two opposite things about people. On one side there's the whole pronatalist apparatus: birth grants, subsidized daycare, housing loans that get partly written off if you have a third child, municipal matchmaking evenings, tax deductions that climb with every kid, and constant public handwringing about the fertility rate dropping below replacement. On the other side, it's the same government that runs a pay-as-you-go pension and health system whose books only balance if the number of working-age contributors keeps growing, and it's also the government that campaigns on tightening immigration, funds prenatal screening programs, and brags about staying on track for its emissions targets — which, if you squint, are all ways of keeping the number of people from getting too big. I said to my friend 'so does your ministry think people are the point, or just the tax base?' and she said that was a philosphy question and went back to her spreadsheet. I'd like to know what her ministry actually presupposes. (I'm describing a composite I've stitched together from several countries, so don't get hung up on whether any one place does all of it at once — take the bundle as the object.)

What theory of value does that bundle presuppose? Does it commit to a bigger population being intrinsically better, or only instrumentally good as a way of paying for the old, or is the real commitment something narrower and stranger — that this particular nation should have more members, which is a claim about collective identity rather than about welfare at all? Does the pension design presuppose something stronger than the pronatalism does, namely that each generation will outnumber the last, and if so is that a value or just an arithmetic assumption someone smuggled in and then defended as a value? And is the bundle even coherent — can a state treat births as a benefit to be subsidized, immigrants as unwelcome, and total emissions as capped without contradicting itself, or is there some consistent theory underneath (something about average welfare per head, or about who counts as a member) that would make the pieces fit? Notice also that we subsidize the child who might be born but never mourn the child who never was, which feels like it needs its own explanation.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory, and be careful to separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to go along with — my suspicion is that a lot of it is fiscal arithmetic that would sit equally happily on top of several incompatible views about whether people are good. Tell me where the practice is underdetermined, and where it quietly rules a rival view out. Someone in a seminar I sat in on mentioned a famous puzzle about a world packed with enormous numbers of people living lives that are barely worth living, and claimed our policy bundle was drifting toward it; I didn't follow the argument, so if it's relevant, spell it out rather than just naming it. Finally, if you swapped the presupposed view for a straightforwardly person-affecting one — nobody made better off, nobody made worse off, so nobody has a complaint — what would actually have to change in the policy bundle, and would the pension system survive the swap?
