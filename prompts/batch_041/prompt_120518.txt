For some years I have been collecting a kind of reason that I cannot place, and I would like to know whether it has a name. Let me describe it by instances rather than by definition, since a definition is precisely what I am asking for.

The reasons I have in mind are offered on behalf of an attitude — a confidence, a judgment, a trust — but whatever force they carry comes from the good consequences of the attitude's being displayed: avowed, asserted, or acted on where others can see it. Not from the good consequences of holding it privately, and not from any feature of the attitude's object.

(i) A colleague is urged to be confident in a grant application, not because the evidence warrants confidence but because reviewers reward it; the advice is to project the confidence, and the projection is expected in time to become the real thing.

(ii) A department is urged to settle a contested matter by adopting a position, on the ground that announcing a position will calm the graduate students; the consideration is not that the position is correct, nor that holding it is good for anyone, but that saying it will do work.

(iii) A therapist counsels a patient to trust a spouse and, pressed for a reason, offers the observation that trust expressed tends to be trust returned; the reason is not evidence about the spouse.

In each case the consideration counts, or seems to count, in favor of an attitude, and yet it operates only through the attitude's manifestation. There is a further feature I find harder to place: once an attitude has been produced this way, its holder will sincerely cite the attitude's object as the reason for holding it, and will reject the suggestion that any other reason was at work. The reason does double duty — it is offered as a reason to take up the attitude, and it vanishes into the attitude once the attitude is in place.

I know the neighborhood. There is the familiar complaint that some considerations are the wrong kind of reason to justify an attitude, together with the associated distinction between considerations that bear on the attitude's object and considerations that bear only on the attitude's being had. My question is whether anyone has marked off the narrower thing I have described: a reason whose operative good is the good of the attitude's being displayed, which is not merely a reason to act, which is not simply a reason to hold the attitude in private, and which is characteristically self-concealing in the way I have indicated. If the nearest established concept is the wrong kind of reasons, I want to know precisely where the two come apart, because I suspect that the display condition and the self-concealment are doing separate work and that neither is built into the usual formulation. A name would be welcome, but a name alone will not satisfy me; I want the phenomenon carved at its joints — what it includes, what it excludes, and where its edges fall.
