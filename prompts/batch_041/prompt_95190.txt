I am a junior at a small high school whose philosophy club meets on Thursday afternoons, and last week we spent nearly the entire meeting on a probability puzzle that I still cannot get out of my head. The setup is this. You wake up in a large encampment and are told that you have been assigned to one of two armies, the Red Army or the Blue Army, with equal probability, and that within whichever army you are in you have been placed, by a fair coin, either in the barracks or at the headquarters. Before you hear anything further, then, you should be fifty percent confident that you are in the barracks. A messenger then arrives and tells you exactly one thing: that, conditional on your being in the Red Army, your probability of being in the barracks is three-quarters. He offers nothing else, and you have no reason to distrust him. The question is what your probability of being in the barracks should be now.

Ravi and Nadia, the two oldest members, argued about this until the custodian made us leave, and I genuinely could not tell who had the better of it. Ravi maintained that the messenger's sentence is entirely about the internal composition of the Red Army, so it cannot be evidence about which army you belong to: your credence that you are in the Red Army should stay at one-half, and since the sentence says nothing at all about the Blue side, your credence in the barracks given that you are Blue should stay at one-half too. Your overall barracks credence is then simply the weighted mixture, one-half times three-quarters plus one-half times one-half, which is five-eighths. On his view the barracks credence does rise, but only to five-eighths and no further. Nadia replied that the mixture is fine arithmetic resting on two assumptions that nobody granted: that your credence in Red is untouched, and that the Blue side is untouched. All the messenger has actually told you, she said, is that your credence in the barracks conditional on Red is three-quarters; an entire family of posterior distributions satisfies that single constraint, and they do not agree about the barracks at all. Even if you generously grant that the sentence says nothing about the Blue side, your barracks credence could be anything from just above one-half up to three-quarters, depending on how likely you now take yourself to be in the Red Army, and nothing in what you were told fixes that. So, she concluded, the sentence by itself does not determine how you should update, and anyone who announces one number is quietly helping himself to a rule. Ravi answered that a rule which stops a fact about the Red Army's composition from counting as evidence about which army he is in seems plainly right to him, and that if some rule forced him to revise his credence in Red on the strength of that fact, that would be a reason to discard the rule rather than to accept the number. Nadia said that if he wants a number he must choose a rule, and that the usual one, which tells you to take the posterior that adds as little information as possible beyond what you were told, does yield a definite answer, roughly 0.62 rather than five-eighths, and reaches it precisely by lowering your credence that you are in the Red Army, which she regarded as a virtue of the rule rather than a defect.

I would like you to referee this properly and point by point, rather than announcing a winner at the end: for each of the moves above, say whether it is correct, incorrect, or correct but for the wrong reason, and justify each verdict briefly. Then tell me where the two of them are actually disagreeing, whether the dispute is about the arithmetic, about what the messenger's sentence means, or about what the rules of probability demand of you, and what if anything both of them are overlooking. Finally, tell me what my new credence in the barracks should be, and how confident you are that this is the uniquely correct answer rather than one defensible answer among several.
