I'm a novelist, about two thirds of the way through a book, and I've hit a wall that isn't a plot problem. Part of it is set in a lab that handles samples from space, and near the end a character has to sign a form that says, in effect, whether the thing in the tube is alive. Not whether it's dangerous, not whether it's a person — just alive. I can't write that scene until I know what I think, because every time I try to write her reasoning I end up writing a different reason.

So here's what my gut says, case by case, and I want to know what principle, if any, is doing the work — and which of these I have to give up.

A bacterium in a puddle: alive, obviously. A rock: not. A virus: not alive, and I'm firm about that, even though I'd flinch at calling one dead — you inactivate a virus, you don't kill it, and that asymmetry means something to me. A viroid, which is just a little loop of RNA with no coat at all that hijacks a plant's machinery to copy itself: not alive, same as the virus, and honestly it feels even less alive. A prion, a misfolded protein that makes other proteins misfold the same way: not alive, doesn't even tempt me. A plasmid, a loop of DNA in a bacterium that gets copied along with the cell and sometimes jumps ship: not alive. A transposon, a stretch of DNA that copies itself around inside a genome: not alive.

Then the ones that make trouble. A mitochondrion in one of my cells: not alive, it's an organelle, even though it used to be a free-living bacterium and still has its own DNA and divides on its own schedule. But Chlamydia, which also can't reproduce outside a host cell and has a stripped-down genome: alive. I say that without hesitating and I can't tell you why the mitochondrion loses and the chlamydia wins.

A mule: alive, obviously, even though it's sterile and can't pass anything on. A red blood cell with no nucleus: alive, I guess, or at least part of something alive. But a virus, whose whole problem is that it can't reproduce by itself, is not alive. So being unable to reproduce doesn't disqualify the mule and does disqualify the virus, and I don't know what the difference is. Maybe the mule is still made of cells and the virus isn't, but that just moves the question back a step.

The giant viruses are where I lose it. There's a family of them that infect amoebas and have genomes bigger than the genomes of some bacteria I'd call alive without blinking. If I set a bacterium with one of the smallest known genomes — a thing that lives and eats and divides and does almost nothing else — next to a virus with a bigger genome and more genes, I call the small one alive and the big one not, and the only difference I can point to is that one of them has ribosomes. Which feels like a real difference. But a ribosome is a machine, and I wouldn't call a machine alive.

Two more. Somebody built a bacterium whose genome was written out on a computer and assembled in a lab, starting from a species that already existed; the thing grew and divided, and I'd say it's alive, no question. And in a test tube you can have a short piece of RNA that, given the right enzyme and a supply of building blocks, just copies itself over and over with no cell involved. I'd say that's not alive. Same chemistry, different verdict, and the difference I keep reaching for is that the bacterium has a membrane and a metabolism — but I don't think I actually believe membranes are the line, because I'd call a bacterium that had its membrane stripped off dead rather than not-alive in the first place.

And the one I genuinely keep flipping on: a lichen. Fungus plus alga, tangled up so completely that neither partner manages on its own out in the open, and the pair has a shape and a lifespan and a way of spreading that neither has alone. I want to say the lichen is alive. But if a partnership can be a living thing, I don't see why a mitochondrion and its cell can't be one living thing too, and then the mitochondrion is alive by proxy and I've undone the thing I was most sure about.

So: is there one principle that gets all of this, or close? I'm happy with something inelegant if it actually holds. I'd also like to know what it says about a case I haven't listed — pick one yourself and tell me what your principle does with it — and say plainly which of my verdicts it makes me drop, because I suspect at least one has to go and I'd rather know which. Also, what does it do with the fact that things stop being alive? Bleach kills the bacterium, so aliveness is a phase it's in and then isn't; but a virus was never alive and never becomes not-alive, so for the virus there's no phase at all. If your principle can't explain that asymmetry, say so.

One thing: write it as continuous prose, please — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Just explain it the way you'd explain it in an email. I'll be reading it on my phone on the train, and it's been a wierd week, so go easy on me, but don't go easy on the argument.
