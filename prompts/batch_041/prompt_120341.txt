I spent most of last night arguing with an old friend — a litigator with no philosophical training and very firm views — about whether it ever makes sense to say that a belief is made true by the good it does the person who holds it. He kept treating the two canonical pragmatist accounts of truth as one and the same, and I kept failing to show him why they are not, so I want a clean explanation I can carry into the next round. What I need to get straight is the real difference between Peirce's conception of truth — the opinion that inquiry is fated to converge on at the ideal end of investigation — and James's conception of truth as whatever it is expedient to believe, whatever pays off in the way of belief. Please get past the stock contrast between the long run and the short run: tell me what each account is actually a theory of, whether it offers a definition of truth or a criterion for sorting true beliefs from false ones or something closer to an account of justification, what each thinker was trying to accomplish, and why Peirce was unhappy enough with James's version that he wanted a different name for his own view. Above all I want to know whether James's account is a theory of truth at all, or only a theory of when a belief is acceptable to hold, and whether that difference is substantive or merely a matter of terminology.

Then stress-test the distinction against a kind of case I doubt either of them wrote about, and that my friend and I kept circling without resolving: self-fulfilling beliefs about one's own character and capacities — the student who believes she is the sort of person who finishes what she starts, the athlete who believes he rises to the occasion when it counts, the diarist who believes he is honest with himself. These beliefs appear to help make themselves true, and the comfort or benefit they deliver is part of the reason they are held at all. Walk me through how each pragmatist would treat such a belief: does it converge in Peirce's sense, is it true only in James's sense, or do the two accounts come apart here in a way that shows the distinction doing real work rather than just decorating a textbook page? Say plainly which account handles the case better, where each one strains, and what each theorist would have to give up to accommodate it. Keep it concrete and conceptual; I don't need a literature survey, a reading list, or citations — just the ideas laid out clearly enough that I can explain them to a stubborn lawyer over dinner. Please write in American English.
