I have been reading interpretability work from the outside, mostly through the rationalist-adjacent blogosphere, and I keep landing on verdicts about what a network genuinely represents that I suspect do not cohere. A linear probe recovers the country from a middle layer at high accuracy: I do not count that as the network representing the country, since the probe may be exploiting something merely correlated. Adding a France direction to the residual stream so the model answers as though it were about France: that I count, because the direction is doing work. An induction head whose ablation kills copying and little else: representation. A feature that fires on Golden Gate Bridge content, recovered by an unsupervised dictionary method, whose ablation selectively removes that content: representation. A feature only one supervised probe can find, that no independent probe recovers and that I cannot get to affect anything downstream: not a representation; it is a fact about my probe. A circuit story that would explain the observed behavior and its opposite equally well: not a representation; that is a just-so story. A computation that is plainly real but smeared across many polysemantic units, with no direction or feature I can point to: I still want to call it a representation, which already strains my instinct that causal role is the test.

My tentative principle is that a state represents X just when intervening on that state is what makes the behavior track X. But consider a model that leans on a token's index rather than anything about its content, where ablating that reliance changes behavior dramatically. My principle calls it a representation; my gut calls it an artifact. Is there a principle that keeps the verdicts above and absorbs this case, or which verdict must I give up? And what would the resulting principle predict for a case where two probes find two unrelated directions, each of which independently restores the behavior when the other is ablated?
