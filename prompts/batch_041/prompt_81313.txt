I am a postgraduate teaching assistant on a second-year undergraduate course on the history and philosophy of violence, and I have a stack of scripts to mark before the moderation meeting on Friday. I should be grateful if you would mark one of them for me. The question, which the students had fifty minutes to answer and for which no texts were permitted, was as follows: "Historians and social scientists sometimes claim that violence has declined over the long run. Assess that claim by working through the four questions introduced in the lectures: (i) the count question — how many violent deaths occurred in a given period; (ii) the rate question — violent deaths per head of population per year; (iii) the share question — violent deaths as a proportion of all deaths; and (iv) the constitution question — what is to be counted as a violent death in the first place. Say what each question would have to be answered with, note where the four can come apart, and conclude whether the claim that violence has declined is a claim with a fact of the matter or one that merely reflects our definitions."

The script reads as follows: "The claim that violence has declined over the long run is broadly true, provided we are careful about which of the four questions we are asking.

On the count question, the honest answer is that absolute numbers of violent deaths have not fallen. With a population of eight billion, even a low rate generates large totals, and it would be absurd to say that fewer people are killed now than in the Stone Age. This is precisely why the rate question is the one that matters. Here the evidence, drawn from archaeology and from the ethnographic record, points to a decline: non-state societies appear to have had homicide rates several times those of modern states, and the rate has fallen further since the Middle Ages, above all in Europe. I accept that these estimates rest on small and possibly unrepresentative samples, and that some of the ethnographic figures have been challenged as too high.

The share question is where I would be most careful, because it is easily confused with the rate question. They are not the same measure, but in practice they move together: a population in which violent deaths are only a small share of all deaths is a population in which the risk of dying violently is correspondingly low. If that is right, then the low share of violent deaths in the modern world simply is the fall in the rate, and no separate argument is needed.

On the constitution question, I agree that the category has shifted. Deaths from famine, from preventable disease and from poverty are now often described as violent, whereas an earlier writer would not have counted them. This makes the decline thesis hard to test, since each generation may widen the category. Because no single measure answers all four questions at once, and because the choice among them is a moral rather than an empirical matter, I do not think there is a fact of the matter about whether the world has become less violent. If, however, we fix the definition at direct killing, then the fall in the rate is real and well supported."
