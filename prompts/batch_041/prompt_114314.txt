I keep running into a pattern in arguments about whether a morally compromised artist's work can still be good, and I think I am collapsing two things that ought to be kept apart. The first is a work whose own content invites us to enjoy or endorse something wrong — the film that makes us root for the torturer. The second is a work whose content is untouched by any wrongdoing, but whose very production was wrong: looted materials, a life ruined during the years it was made. People seem to treat the first as a genuine defect in the work, and the second as a reason not to buy the thing rather than a reason the thing is worse. Then, when the artist is admired, the second kind gets quietly absorbed back in — the cruelty that produced the work becomes part of its interest, even its beauty, so that a fault in the making is read as a virtue in the made.

Is there an established concept for a fault that attaches to a work through its history rather than its content, and for the conversion of such faults into aesthetic credit once the maker is esteemed? If there is no such concept, coin the terms, define them, and say how they differ from the genetic fallacy, from the mistake of settling a work's meaning by the artist's intentions, and from the view that moral and aesthetic value are simply independent of one another. Give me the boundary cases that would test the definitions, and be clear about which of my two kinds is really the interesting one. I care less about the labels than about where the line actually falls.
