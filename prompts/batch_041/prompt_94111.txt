I direct a three-year project excavating a settlement of about forty houses, rebuilt on the same footprints over several centuries. The inhabitants left no writing and no identifiable descendants, and our evidence is entirely material: the rebuilt houses; a pit just outside the settlement holding some three thousand objects, most of them broken in a consistent and apparently intentional way and many of them burnt; and a cemetery in which a minority of graves contain goods carried from several hundred kilometres away. The funder's ethics panel has barred us from ethnographic analogy — we may not reason from any documented society, past or present, to this one — on the grounds that such reasoning smuggles in colonial assumptions about what counts as religion, property or a person. The ban removes the crutch my discipline normally leans on, and I want to know what to do with three years of work.

Which open conceptual questions about getting from objects to beliefs and institutions should we prioritise, and in what order? The kind of thing I have in mind: whether a material record of this sort underdetermines any attribution of belief; whether belief is the right target at all, rather than practice or skill; whether institutions such as hierarchy, inheritance or property can be inferred from patterns of deposition rather than quietly assumed; and what happens to our descriptions when the only vocabulary available is our own. Rank them, say which are upstream of which, which are tractable for a team with no texts, no informants and no permitted comparison, and what would move a question up or down the list. If you think the ban on analogy makes the central questions unanswerable rather than merely hard, say so and tell me what the project should be doing instead.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning; no preamble, and no restating of my question.
