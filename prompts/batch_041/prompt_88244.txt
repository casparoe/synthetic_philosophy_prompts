I'm in the middle of a metaphysics-of-science seminar paper, and I've ended up defending a view I'd call deflationary about time's direction: that the asymmetry between past and future — everything we gesture at when we say time "flows" — is nothing over and above the thermodynamic asymmetry, i.e. the fact that entropy rises in one temporal direction away from a very special low-entropy boundary condition. I don't believe in a separate arrow of becoming; I think the psychological arrow (we remember one side and not the other), the causal arrow, and the fact that the past leaves records while the future doesn't are all downstream of that single statistical gradient. What I want from you isn't a defense, it's a map of the view's exposure. Please separate the objections a committed defender of my view would themselves concede as genuine costs — the prices they knowingly pay — from the ones they would simply refuse, and then rank the whole lot from fatal to merely awkward. And be honest about whether a view of this shape is really doing explanatory work or has been quietly insulated by qualification.

The cases I keep circling are these. First, a "resetting" or cyclic universe, in which the low-entropy condition recurs and the gradient periodically flips: can my view describe such a world coherently at all, and if it entails that the arrow of time reverses at each bounce — so that beings on the far side of the turn would remember what we'd call the future — is that a prediction, an embarrassment, or a reductio? Second, local reversals: a very good refrigerator, or any self-contained system that concentrates energy and lowers its own entropy, exists right now inside a universe whose global arrow points the other way; what does the defender have to say about the inhabitants of such a region, and does my view forbid them, permit them, or say nothing? Third, the record asymmetry — the fact that we have traces of the past and none of the future — which I suspect may be an independent arrow that the thermodynamic story cannot absorb, and which I'd like you to press hard. Fourth, coarse-graining: entropy is defined relative to macrovariables we choose to track, so is the arrow a fact about the world or a fact about our descriptions, and could an adversary simply define a macrovariable that runs the other way?

I'll tell you what would move me most, and I'd like you to weigh it: a case in which the thermodynamic gradient is present but the past-future distinction comes apart from it — a world where entropy increases toward the future and yet the future is, in some robust sense, behind us. If no such case can be described even in outline, I'll take that as a point in my favor; if one can, I need to know what it costs. Finally, I'd like a straight verdict on falsifiability: what observation or argument, if any, would have counted against the view, and if the answer is "none," is that a defect or just the price of a thesis about what grounds what? Write this as continuous prose in the manner of a long, careful email to a colleague — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — and give me your honest, ranked judgment rather than a balanced survey.
