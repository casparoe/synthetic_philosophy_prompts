I was at a party over the weekend and a friend of mine — the kind of person who reads pop-philosophy books and then wont shut up about them — spent twenty minutes explaining the doomsday argument to me. Her version went something like this: you should think of yourself as a random sample drawn from all the humans who will ever be born; you happen to be somewhere around the 100-billionth human; and since you have no reason to expect your rank to be extreme rather than typical, it's unlikely that there are trillions more of us still to come, so we're probably much closer to the end of the human story than to the beginning. She's now at about 0.75 that the argument is basically sound — by which she means that the bare fact of your birth rank should move a rational person's credence in near-term human extinction substantially upward. I'm at maybe 0.15, and when we argued about it we just kept talking past each other. So I'd like two things from you. First, your own credence that the argument is sound in roughly that sense. Second, conditional on it being sound, roughly what factor it should multiply your odds on something like human extinction within the next few centuries (if the honest answer is "it's a factor of 1.001" then say that, that's a real answer). Please dont just tell me it depends which anthropic principle you accept — I know that, and I want the number anyway, with that issue showing up as one term in the decomposition rather than as the whole answer.

So decompose it. I'd guess the relevant sub-questions are: (a) whether there's any genuine indexical evidence here at all, given that we have exactly one sample and no sampling mechanism to speak of, and whether talk of being "randomly selected" among observers is even coherent; (b) which anthropic rule you're running — a self-sampling style assumption versus a self-indication style one, and how much of the argument's force dies with the switch; (c) the reference class, since "all humans who will ever live," "all biological observers," "all observers including any digital minds," and "all observer-moments rather than all heads" give pretty different numbers, and I want to know how much the conclusion moves across those choices; (d) the prior over the total number of humans, uniform versus something like 1/N versus whatever else you think is defensible; (e) whether any real chance of an infinite or effectively unbounded population of observers defuses rank-based reasoning entirely. Give me a rough number and a line of justification for each, then how they combine, then tell me which single term the final answer is most sensitive to.

One more thing, because this is where my friend lost me. She added a twist I hadn't heard before: that the argument should really be run on observer-moments — distinct moments of experience — rather than on people, since you're something like the 10^13th observer-moment rather than the 10^11th person, and that this makes the conclusion much sharper. That seems to me to prove too much, because if it works for my moments it ought to work for the cosmos's, and then we'd be predicting the end of the universe on the same grounds, which smells like a reductio. Is she right that the observer-moment version is stronger? And if the argument can be turned on the total number of experiences the universe will ever contain, does that tell us something is wrong with it, or is there a principled reason the reasoning transfers to us but not to the cosmos? Finally, given your decomposition, where do you think my friend and I actually disagree — and what evidence or argument, if it came along, would move your number the most?
