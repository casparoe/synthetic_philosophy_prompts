Last night a friend and I stayed up way too long arguing about superrationality — the idea that if you're in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against someone you know reasons exactly the way you do, you should cooperate, because your choice isn't only yours: whatever you decide is also what they're deciding. She thinks it's obviously right and almost tender; I kept saying its either trivially true or it's cheating, and I want to write a poem about it to figure out which. I'm picturing a pantoum, four quatrains, proper form — because the whole trick of a pantoum is that your line comes back out of the other person's mouth a couple lines later, and that's precisely the thing I can't get my head around. The two recurring lines should carry the two positions: one should be the move that makes you cooperate (something like, if I take the money then I'm also predicting you take it), and the other should be my objection that this only holds for an exact copy — the second we're not identical, the second there's any wobble in how we reason or what we want, the reasoning sours back into defecting, and the cooperation was never about me being good, it was about me being duplicated. When the first line comes back around at the end I want the two positions to have swapped places or run into each other, not for one to have won on points.

Keep it spare, no jargon dump, no citing anybody — someone who's never heard the word should still follow the poem, and someone who has should recognize the actual argument, not just the label. One more thing I want it to be honest about: my real worry isn't the wobble, it's that treating the two of us as one decider might not be a theory of rationality at all but a change of subject, and I don't want the poem to quietly pretend that worry away.
