I sit on the ethics committee at a mid-sized teaching hospital, and because I did a philosophy MA before med school I've somehow become the unofficial in-house philosopher — every time a case gets weird, it lands on my desk. The case that keeps recurring: someone wrote a clear, specific advance directive years ago (usually refusing a feeding tube, sometimes refusing dialysis, once something odd like "no antibiotics for pneumonia, I'd rather go"), and now they're in the moderate-to-severe dementia stage, and they seem... fine? Content, not resisting, sometimes actively enjoying their pudding. The family is split, the attending wants cover, and we improvise. We had one last month where the daughter said something that has stuck with me — "the woman in that bed isn't my mother, but the woman who wrote that directive was" — and I genuinely couldn't tell whether that was sentimental confusion or the entire crux of the problem wearing a sentimental costume.

Anyway, a small foundation has given us enough for one PhD student for three years to work on "the authority of the earlier self" in advance care planning, and I get to set the research agenda. So: what are the open conceptual questions here that actually matter most, and in what order would you put them? I want the reasoning rather than the list — for each, why it's upstream of the others, whether it's the kind of thing a philosopher can make real progress on in three years as opposed to something that just needs empirical data we don't have, and what would move it up or down the ranking. Some things I'd like you to at least consider, and feel free to call any of them dead ends: whether the earlier self's authority is grounded in autonomy, in the narrative unity of a life, in something promise-like, or in the brute persistence of that earlier person's interests; the reverse-Parfit move, where reductionism about personal identity ends up strengthening rather than weakening the case for honoring directives, because the earlier self is a distinct person whose claims don't just evaporate when the dementia sets in; whether the later self's apparent contentment is itself a symptom rather than a preference, and what that does to a framework built on respecting preferences; whether casting the directive as a Ulysses-style precommitment actually helps or just smuggles in the assumption that the earlier and later selves are the same agent; and the difference between a directive that's specific and a later self who's docile, versus a later self who repeatedly and lucidly says no. One more thing I'd like you to sort out for me, because I suspect we've been conflating them for years: which of these are really questions about what a person is, and which are really questions about what a hospital is permitted to do to one.
