So my uncle had a corpus callosotomy two years ago -- the surgery where they cut the bridge of fibers between the two halves of the brain -- for epilepsy that nothing else would touch. He's doing ok, mostly, but he's different in ways I can't quite put my finger on, and I've been reading about it ever since. Last week two people in my book group got into it about him, and I've been turning it over since. I'm not on either side, I honestly can't tell who's got the better of it. Here's roughly how it went.

Rosa: the studies show there really are two streams of experience in there, one per hemisphere. You flash a word to one side of his visual field and the hand on that side acts on it, and the hemisphere that does the talking makes up a reason on the spot, and doesn't know it's making it up. That's not a glitch, thats a second subject getting talked over. "Unity of consciousness" is something we assume, not something anyone's shown.

Dev: that's a lab trick. Ask my uncle and he says he's one person. And the disunity only shows up in split-second lateralized presentations; put him in a kitchen and he's one agent doing one thing. When something only appears under one contrived setup and vanishes everywhere else, suspect the setup.

Rosa: but you're using the talking hemisphere's testimony as the voice of the whole man, and that's the exact thing under dispute. Of course he says he's one; the side doing the talking can't see the other side. That's like asking the press secretary whether the president has a secret twin. And behaving coherently proves nothing -- two people can share a bank account, a rowing team pulls one oar each.

Dev: ok but then what would it take to show there are two? If no result could count against "two subjects," it's not a finding, it's a way of talking. And look at what they still share: one brainstem, one body, one bloodstream, one thirty-year history before the knife. Maybe the right description is one person whose parts stopped talking to each other, not two people sharing a head.

Rosa: here's a case. Suppose they train both sides to answer yes or no on their own, ask each one what it wants to watch, and they disagree. Now the nurse has to decide for him. Whichever you pick, you're either crowning one hemisphere the real person or admitting there are two. And consent gets strange fast -- who signs for the next surgery? You can't say "he consents" without already assuming the answer.

Dev: consent isn't a fact about brains, it's something we do with persons -- legal and social units, one per body. Saying a corporation can't sign a contract because it's made of people would be silly. And two streams of information processing isn't two subjects; my laptop runs twelve processes and isn't twelve.

That's about where they left it. Can you go through it move by move -- who's right at each step, where each of them overreaches, and whether any of it is just a fight about words -- then say who has the better of it overall? I'd also like to know what both of them are missing.
