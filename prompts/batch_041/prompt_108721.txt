I write literary fiction, and for the past year I have been circling an essay I am not philosopher enough to finish: whether there is a real moral difference between lying and misleading. My angle comes out of craft. A novelist never asserts anything false, strictly speaking, yet the whole apparatus of a novel is built to make a reader believe things that are not so, and to believe them in a particular order, at a particular pace, with particular facts withheld. I have come to think the standard distinction — a lie is a false assertion made with intent to deceive, a misleading statement is true but engineered to produce a false belief — is too coarse to do any work, and that the interesting line runs through three modes of misdirection rather than two. The first is the familiar one: you say what is true and let a false implication do the lying for you, the true answer to a question that was not quite the question asked. The second is structural: you assert nothing false and imply nothing false, but you arrange true material — a curated file, a selected sample, a staged room — so that the other person draws the false conclusion herself. The third is the one I care about and have never seen named: you arrange nothing at all. You leave a thing out and rely on the fact that the question which would have exposed it is a question this person will not ask. The deception is not in anything you do or say; it lives in a counterfactual, in what you would have said if pressed and in the gap you were content to leave unremarked.

The moral crux, as I see it, is that these three run in opposite directions. The first looks most like lying and is usually judged most harshly. The second is the press officer's defence and the conjuror's method, and people disagree about whether it is a lesser wrong or a worse one — lesser because nothing false was said, worse because the victim's own reasoning is conscripted into the deception. The third, my unasked-question case, seems on one reading to be barely an act at all — mere omission, and no one is obliged to volunteer everything — and on another reading to be the most contemptible of the three, since it treats another person's incuriosity as a resource to be spent, which looks less like a breach of trust than like a kind of use. I suspect the resolution has to do with consent and with whether there was any duty to speak, and that once you take that seriously the lying/misleading distinction turns out to be carrying almost none of the moral weight people assign to it. Fiction, on this view, is simply the case where the consent is explicit and the duty is absent, which is why the novelist can mislead with a clear conscience and the press officer cannot.

I would like comments on this, and I would rather have them cold than kind. Specifically: is the three-way division principled, or is it a distinction I have gerrymandered to fit my own examples, and does the third category collapse into the first or the second once you press on it (I worry that any counterfactual about what I would have said is really just an implicature in disguise)? Which of the two readings of the omission case is right, and is there a decisive argument for either? Is the closing move — that consent and the duty to inform do all the moral work, leaving the lie/mislead line idle — too quick, and if so, precisely where does it break? And is the whole thing merely the old commission/omission debate in new clothes? Tell me where the argument equivocates between misleading as an act and misleading as an outcome, which objection is fatal rather than merely awkward, and whether the central distinction earns its keep or merely restates what any reader would already have granted. No reading list, please; I want to know whether the thing stands up, not where to read more.
