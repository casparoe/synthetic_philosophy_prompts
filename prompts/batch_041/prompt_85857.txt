I've been reading way too much about societal collapse lately — Bronze Age, Maya, Rapa Nui — and I want to turn it into something funny, so heres the ask.

Write me a ~650-word internal case-review memo from a boutique consultancy that does "decline management" for polities. Call the firm Second Act Advisory. The running joke should be structural rather than gag-a-line: the firm's whole revenue model depends on collapse being a real, bookable event, but every client keeps insisting that what's happening to them is a transition, a restructuring, a pivot to a leaner model. The memo reviews four accounts, each drawn from a real case in the literature, run through deadpan corporate language:

- a Late Bronze Age palace economy — tin supply problems, a wave of "external vendors" nobody can put a name to, palace archives that just stop mid-tablet
- a Classic Maya divine lordship — the big administrative centers empty out but the client's customer base is still there, and this is the account the firm finds most frustrating because it can't be booked as a collapse
- Rapa Nui — the firm sold an "ecocide" turnaround narrative for years, and now the client is disputing the figures, insisting the real damage arrived later and from outside
- one more of your choice, or an internal account where the firm audits itself

A few things I want:

- Let the jargon do the philosophical work. Give the firm a proprietary metric or two that quietly bakes in a contested assumption — something like a complexity-yield score that only counts political centralization, so a society that keeps farming, building, and speaking its own language for another millennium still gets written off as a total loss. Don't flag the assumption, just let the numbers be weird.
- Include one line from a client, in the client's own words, that is a genuine argument from the collapse literature (paraphrase it, don't lift anything). The account manager should treat it as an excuse.
- No real scholars by name; invented colleagues, invented clients, invented quarterly figures are all fine.
- Keep it completely deadpan. Nobody in the memo should wink, and nobody should ever state the thesis. A specialist in this stuff should recognize that the author knows the terrain; someone who's never thought about it should still laugh and then feel slightly uneasy.
- End on something that leaves the reader unsure whether "collapse" names a thing that happens in the world or a genre we impose on it — without anyone saying that out loud.

Format it as an actual memo: to/from/subject line, numbered sections, maybe a short "action items" list at the end. Try to keep the total around 650 words, and don't seperate the accounts with anything more than a heading.
