I have recently become the parliamentarian of a small online association that governs itself through a Nomic-style wiki constitution. The association has no external charter or legal personality; its rules are its own creation. To prevent capture, the constitution entrenches its amendment rule: a proposal to amend the amendment rule passes by simple majority, but takes effect only if, at the next session, it also passes by two-thirds. A proposal that fails the second vote is void.

Last month a member proposed Amendment M, which would replace the amendment rule with one allowing any amendment, including an amendment to this rule, to take effect on a single simple-majority vote; it also declares all prior two-stage amendments void. M passed the first vote 55–45 and the second vote 60–40, short of two-thirds. Under the entrenched rule, M is void. A member now argues:

(1) A rule system is legally autonomous only if its ultimate rule of change is part of the system and not imposed from outside.
(2) The association is legally autonomous.
(3) Therefore its ultimate rule of change must be part of the system.
(4) If the ultimate rule of change is part of the system, then it either is unamendable or can be amended by its own procedure.
(5) If it is unamendable, then some rule stands outside the system and binds it, contradicting (1) and (2).
(6) If it can be amended by its own procedure, then it can be amended to authorize a single-session simple-majority amendment procedure.
(7) Under a single-session simple-majority procedure, any rule can be changed, including the rules protecting the association's funds and defining membership.
(8) A system in which any rule can be changed by a bare majority is not a constitution but a temporary majority's instrument.
(9) Therefore the association is either not autonomous or not constitutional.
(10) But we assumed it is both.
(11) So the association's constitution is incoherent, and Amendment M, although void under the old rule, reveals a deeper truth: no self-amending constitution can be both autonomous and stable.

I would prefer to keep (1), (2), and (8) if I can. Which step should I reject? Explain why that step looked plausible, then price the alternatives: what would I have to give up if I reject the step you name? If the argument is valid, say which premise is least costly to abandon and why. Do not appeal to facts about particular real-world constitutions or courts; I want the diagnosis in terms of the logic of self-amending rules. End with a final line exactly in the form \answer{...}, containing the number of the rejected step, a semicolon, and a one-sentence diagnosis.
