A colleague of mine in the department has a set of verdicts that look consistent on the surface and I want to know what she'll say about a new case before I put it to her. Hers: the drunk who drives home without incident is blameworthy, quite blameworthy; the drunk who hits and kills a child is in a different moral universe, manslaughter-grade; the surgeon who follows every protocol to the letter and loses the patient isn't blameworthy at all, though he'll and should carry it forever; the surgeon who skips a check and whose patient sails through is blameworthy; the painter who walks out on his family and turns out to be a genius isn't to blame; the same painter who walks out and is mediocre is, her words, a selfish little coward.

I think more than one rule fits all six, and I want them seperate. Two I can see: moral worth is settled by how things actually turn out, versus what the people around you have standing to hold against you depends on what they actually lost — and maybe a third, that the blame is real in both painter cases but success earns a credit that outweighs it.

The new case: a student lies on her med-school application, claiming a research internship she never did, edges out an equally strong applicant who goes elsewhere and does fine, and becomes a genuinely excellent, careful doctor. What does my colleague say about the lying? Which candidate rules her six verdicts actually fit, what each predicts here, which I should prefer and why, and what case would pry them apart if I put it to her next week. No need to cite anyone, I know the literature.
