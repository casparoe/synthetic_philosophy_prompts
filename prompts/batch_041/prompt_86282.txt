I write about science and ideas, mostly animal minds and the weird edges of moral philosophy, and I'm working on a feature about how far down the tree of life sentience goes. Everyone I interview says some version of "well, it's a matter of degree," and then their actual moral math comes out looking exactly like it did before, which drives me up the wall. So I want you to actually run the math, on the record, for a whole spread of cases.

Take the role of a philosopher of mind who works alongside comparative psychologists and welfare biologists — comfortable with both the neuroanatomy and the ethics, and not willing to hedge your way out of the question. Think carefully, and don't rush to the tidy answer.

Before you score anything, spend a few paragraphs thinking out loud about the fork you have to pick first. Is sentience a dimmer switch, i.e. there's a real fact of the matter and it comes in degrees, so a bee has a little of what a dog has a lot of? Or is it a threshold, where nothing morally relevant is there until some particular kind of integration clicks into place, and the "gradations" we think we see are really gradations of our confidence? Pick one, say why, and stay with it — don't drift back and forth halfway through.

Then use this rubric as given (don't swap in your own):
- welfare_capacity: 0–10. How much morally relevant good or bad experience the thing can have, if it has any. 0 = nothing there to go wrong, 10 = roughly a human adult. Magnitude, not confidence.
- confidence: 0–1. Your credence that it has any valenced experience at all, ever.
- expected_moral_weight: welfare_capacity x confidence, rounded to one decimal. Rank by this, descending.
- moral_unit: what we're actually supposed to care about — the individual, the colony, the organ, the whole system, or none. I keep getting told a bee colony is a "superorganism" and I want to know wether that changes the unit of accounting or is just a relabeling.
- tier: A (treat as a welfare subject in policy), B (take seriously, but don't build institutions on it), C (live possibility, no action implications yet), D (no serious case).
- crux: the single empirical or conceptual finding that would move your score most.

Score every one of these, even the silly ones: a honeybee; an entire honeybee colony; a wild-caught octopus; a shore crab; a lab mouse; a broiler chicken; a human fetus at 20 weeks; a human in a persistent vegetative state; a sleeping adult human in the middle of a vivid dream; a zebrafish larva five days post-fertilization; a mussel; a tick; a C. elegans nematode; a mimosa plant that folds up when you poke it; a human brain organoid in a dish showing spontaneous coordinated activity; a house thermostat; a chatbot finetuned to say "please stop, that hurts"; and a fictional character in a novel.

Where you're just going with the field's default, say so, and where the default feels unearned, flag it. Don't bother with citations — I can't check them and I don't want the piece leaning on stuff neither of us has actually read.
