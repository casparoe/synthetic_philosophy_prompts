I just joined the ethics committee at the nursing home where I work (I'm a social worker, not a philosopher, so go easy on me with the jargon). Theres a pattern here that I can't get to sit still, and what I want from you is an answer to what view of a person it's secretly committed to.

Here's the pattern. A resident signed a living will eight years ago, back when he was sharp: no feeding tube, no antibiotics for pneumonia, if he's got advanced dementia and doesn't know his own family anymore. Now he's got advanced dementia. He doesn't know his wife. He also seems, honestly, fine -- he eats, he smiles at the staff, he likes the music lady. Then he gets pneumonia. Our policy says we follow the paper. Nobody asks him. And if he'd never written anything down we'd treat him without a second thought, because he isn't suffering and nothing says otherwise. Meanwhile the man two doors down who still has capacity refuses his dialysis and we don't dream of overriding him.

So which one is the patient? It looks like we're saying the competent man is the real one and the man in the bed is... a kind of caretaker of him? Or is it not a claim about identity at all -- just a rule that the earlier decision was the only competently made one, so it wins on competence grounds and not on identity grounds? I want you to tell me which parts of what we do actually need the identity claim and which don't. If you could run the whole thing with no view at all about whether it's the same person, just a view about who was competent when, would the bedside look any different? Could we still say no to the pneumonia?

Two seperate things that I think are the same puzzle but that people treat totally differently. Some of our psych patients do a psychiatric advance directive -- they write down, while they're well, that if they go manic again they want to be hospitalized, basically pre-consenting to be locked up. I've been told clinics often won't act on those, because you supposedly can't consent in advance to being detained, which is the exact opposite of what we do with the living will. And then theres the guy who signs himself onto the casino's self-exclusion list and comes back a year later asking to be taken off it, and nobody lets him, and I think in some places he can't even keep his winnings. Why does the paper beat the man in one case and not in the other? And nobody will enforce a contract where I agree to sell my kidney, or to become somebody's servant if I lose a bet -- so it definately can't just be that any earlier self can bind a later one.

Tell me what theory of the person (or of autonomy, or of who counts as the decision-maker) our practice presupposes, what it merely fits with, and what would have to change if we dropped the idea that the earlier self has authority over the later one. If you think the practice is fine as it is but needs a different justification than the one people usually give it, say that too.
