I chair the ethics advisory panel of a mid-sized conservation charity that manages a few thousand hectares of woodland and wetland. Our founding deed commits us to protecting and restoring native wildlife populations, and for twenty years the panel has read that as a strong presumption of non-intervention in ecological processes: we manage habitat and control invasive species at the boundary, but we do not interfere in what happens among the animals themselves. Next week the panel must decide whether to approve a pilot intervention that would break with that presumption, and the trustees have asked for a decision rather than a discussion, so I need to circulate a written opinion beforehand.

The case is a haemorrhagic disease that has reached the deer herd on our largest reserve, apparently carried in by midges from a neighbouring estate. Our veterinary adviser estimates that it kills roughly half of those infected, but only after a week or two of fever and lameness during which the animals are visibly distressed and barely able to feed; most of the dead are this year's fawns, and the survivors seem to recover. The proposal on the table is a cull of visibly symptomatic animals, carried out by our own staff, on the grounds that it is the only practical way to shorten individual suffering and that we already cull deer for population management. Our head warden opposes it: the outbreak may be self-limiting and burn out within a season, a cull aimed at the symptomatic will not reduce the total burden of disease, and the reserve's deer are the main prey base for a recovering pine marten population whose return was the point of the last decade of our work. A trustee who favours the cull has circulated a note urging the panel to adopt what he calls a principle of parity of causes -- that we should not discount a harm merely because its cause is natural, so if we would act against this suffering when a human caused it, we must act here unless we can name a relevant difference -- while another trustee insists that a natural disease is simply not our business in the way a poisoned river would be.

Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate. It should recommend a definite course of action with a time frame -- approve the cull as proposed, approve a modified version, or decline and say what we would need to see to reconsider -- and give reasons that our warden, our veterinary adviser, and both trustees could each actually engage with, rather than restating the trust's charitable purpose. It should treat the strongest consideration on the side it comes down against as honestly as the side it favours, and it should engage the parity-of-causes proposal directly rather than ignoring it. Take a position and defend it rather than laying out the options; where the recommendation turns on a contested assumption -- about whether it is worse to be killed by a disease than by us, or how much weight a merely possible future recovery of the martens should carry against present suffering -- name the assumption and say how confident you are in it, and say where you think a reasonable colleague could disagree with you without being irrational.
