Write a mock leader of 600–700 words for an invented London daily in 1858, in the voice of a career official of the East India Company — a man who has spent thirty years drafting despatches from the Company's London office and who is now fighting the bill that will transfer India to the Crown. He is a liberal utilitarian, entirely sincere, and the piece must be written without a wink: he argues that a people not yet fit for self-government may legitimately be ruled despotically for their own improvement, that the Company is the instrument best fitted to that improvement, and that government from Westminster would be government by men who have never read a despatch and know nothing of the country. (Mill himself spent his working life in that office, rising to head of the Examiners' Office before India passed to the Crown in 1858; the speaker, however, is an invented figure, not Mill, and Mill should not be named or quoted in the piece — the reader should recognize the position unaided.) Every claim the official makes must be one a Millian liberal could actually make, and he must never catch himself out: the discomfort should come from his consistency rather than from a slip into cynicism or hypocrisy. Let him arrive, in good faith, at a proposal the reader will recoil from — an improvement test that cannot be failed but cannot be passed either, a schedule of tutelage with no terminus — and let him count the happiness of the governed in a ledger where they never quite appear as line items. Keep the diction period-plausible and the irony deadpan throughout: a reader who knows the history should find the satire fair, and a reader who does not should still feel the argument turn over in his hands.
