Take on the role of someone who both translates poetry and thinks hard about it — a translator who's spent years doing constrained / Oulipo-adjacent stuff and has also read alot of the theory — and before you react, brainstorm for yourself: list the ways someone might translate a poem that has a real constraint baked into it, and what each choice gives up. Then comment on my essay below. I mostly want to know where it's wrong.

Here's the essay I've got so far.

**The Load-Bearing Constraint**

Hofstadter's big book on translation — the one built around a little Renaissance French get-well poem by Clément Marot, the one he nicknames after its opening words — runs that tiny poem through dozens of English versions, and the whole drama is that no two of them keep the same things. One keeps the rhyme and loses the meter, one keeps the syllable count and turns the sense to mush, one keeps the sense and abandons the form entirely and becomes a different, flatter thing. His point, as I read it, is that 'the poem' isn't one object with one set of properties; its a bundle of demands, and every translation is a bet about which demand is the load-bearing one.

I think he's right, but I want to push it further, and this is where I need help. I write constrained stuff myself — a sequence of short poems where the first letters of the lines spell out a name, plus a strict rhyme scheme and a fixed meter. A friend asked me to render one into Spanish. And I found that the acrostic and the rhyme scheme and the meter are not three constraints of the same kind. Meter and rhyme are *felt* — a reader who never counts syllables still feels the thump. The acrostic is *invisible* unless you go looking for it; a reader who never scans down the left margin gets a perfectly good poem and misses the whole device. So when the Spanish words for the hidden name don't line up, I have a hierarchy: I'll break meter, I'll cheat the rhyme, but I'll fight to keep the acrostic, because the acrostic is what makes the poem *this* poem and not a greeting card. Except — and here's my worry — is that just my vanity as the author talking? I know the acrostic is there; a translator who didn't might reasonably decide the rhyme is the soul of it and the acrostic is a private joke.

Which leads to the claim I actually want tested: that 'the form' of a constrained work is never a single thing but a stack of constraints, and translation is the process of deciding which layer is structural and which is decoration — and that this decision is not discoverable in the original, it's *made* by the translator. If that's true, then the original poem was never fully determinate either; it was always a family of possible readings, and the translator just picks one and commits. The 'untranslatable' bit, the residue that won't cross over, is exactly the spot where that choice gets exposed.

Am I fooling myself? It feels profound at 2am and obvious at noon. Specifically: (a) is this just the banal 'translation is hard, all translation is interpretation' point wearing a nicer coat, or is there something real in the layered-constraint picture? (b) are there constraints that are genuinely non-negotiable, where breaking them means you've simply failed to translate rather than made a different valid choice — and if so, what makes those different in kind? (c) I suspect I'm smuggling in a confusion between preserving the *form* and preserving the *effect on a reader who knows the form is there* — those come apart, and I can't tell if my acrostic argument survives once I seperate them. Point me at the weakest joint and give me the strongest objection, not a polite one.
