I sit on my city's Commemoration and Naming Committee; I am the philosopher on it, which in practice means I am expected to say what the disagreement is actually about. On Thursday we vote on Wexford Avenue, named in 1911 for the merchant who donated the land for the public library (the library still bears his name; our remit covers streets only). The deed, digitized this spring, bound the library's trustees to members of his own congregation until 1965, and the same collection shows that the gift was funded by a business that paid its workers in scrip redeemable only at his store. The residents' association, mostly renters and recent arrivals, has petitioned against any change, arguing that renaming would mean new addresses, leases and documents for people who never chose the honor and cannot absorb the cost; descendants of the millworkers have petitioned for removal. One member proposes that we stop treating commemorations as permanent: her draft policy would make every commemoration a revocable loan of the public realm, granted for a fixed term and attached to a specific achievement rather than to a whole person, renewable only through an explicit public process.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote: a clear recommendation on the avenue, with a timeline and an assignment of who does what, and a reasoned position on the loan proposal, whose author wants it to settle this case. Give the strongest case for the side you do not take as its holders would put it — the committee is split, and the dissenters need to recognize themselves in your summary. Do not pad it with the standard statue arguments; I need the reasoning that decides this case. No citations.
