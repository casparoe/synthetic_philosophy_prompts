In Hofstadter's careenium thought experiment, a vast billiard-table-like machine contains countless tiny magnetic balls that careen around, colliding and clumping into larger, stable configurations. Zooming out, one can describe these higher-level configurations as symbols: patterns that persist, move, interact with one another, and appear to track features of the world outside the machine, much as concepts might be realized in the neural substrate of a brain. This raises a question Hofstadter poses as which level is doing the shoving: do the mindless balls at the bottom level push everything around, with the symbols being mere bookkeeping, or do the symbols themselves exert a kind of causal grip on the balls that realize them?

Please explain the conceptual issue at stake. Specifically: (1) What exactly is being claimed when someone says the higher-level symbols push the substrate around, and what would make that claim true or false rather than a mere choice of description? (2) How does this connect to the general problem of downward causation and to the causal-exclusion worry — that if every effect of a symbol is fully accounted for by the balls that realize it, the symbol is left with no work of its own to do? (3) Does the careenium analogy illuminate this problem, or does it quietly presuppose an answer to it? In addressing these, distinguish the sense in which the symbol level might be causally efficacious from the sense in which it is merely causally relevant or explanatory, and say what multiple realizability (one symbol realized by different ball-configurations that yield the same downstream effects) does to the argument.

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who have thought carefully about emergence, levels of description, and causation, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. Write in American English.
