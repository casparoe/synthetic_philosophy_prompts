Last night a friend and I argued for the better part of four hours about presentism and eternalism — she holds that the tensed picture is a projection of creatures like us onto a reality whose only temporal facts are the tenseless ordering ones, and I kept insisting that something is genuinely lost if you drop the passage from future to present to past — and she finished by staking out a third position I still cannot classify: that the tensed facts belonging to different moments are each of them real, but cannot be gathered into one consistent description of what there is, so that the demand for a single standpoint from which everything can be asserted together is itself the error. She says this is neither presentism nor the block view. I would like to be able to reply with something better than "that sounds like eternalism with extra steps," but I have to admit that I cannot follow the argument that is supposed to be the ancestor of the whole dispute, so I have spent the evening re-reading it. I am an economics student who wandered into a metaphysics reading group, so I need the reconstruction done slowly, with the technical vocabulary defined as it comes up.

Here is the stretch that keeps defeating me, from McTaggart's 1908 paper "The Unreality of Time": "For the sake of brevity I shall speak of the series of positions running from the far past through the near past to the present, and then from the present to the near future and the far future, as the A series. The series of positions which runs from earlier to later I shall call the B series. [. . .] I believe, however, that this would be a mistake, and that the distinction of past, present and future is as essential to time as the distinction of earlier and later, while in a certain sense, as we shall see, it may be regarded as more fundamental than the distinction of earlier and later. [. . .] It would, I suppose, be universally admitted that time involves change. [. . .] A universe in which nothing whatever changed (including the thoughts of the conscious beings in it) would be a timeless universe. If, then, a B series without an A series can constitute time, change must be possible without an A series. Let us suppose that the distinction of past, present and future does not apply to reality. Can change apply to reality? What is it that changes? Could we say that, in a time which formed a B series but not an A series, the change consisted in the fact that an event ceased to be an event, while another event began to be an event? If this were the case, we should certainly have got a change. But this is impossible. An event can never cease to be an event. It can never get out of any time series in which it once is."

What I want is a patient walk through this passage, not a survey of what has been written about it. First, lay the reasoning out as an explicit chain of steps, stating the conclusion of each, and tell me which single step is doing the real work — I can see that "time involves change" is a premise and that a changeless universe is being ruled out, but I lose the thread somewhere around "what is it that changes?" Second, explain what the candidate answer about an event ceasing to be an event is a stand-in for: who, if anyone, actually holds that view, and why he treats it as the only option open to someone who denies the A series. If he is attacking a position nobody holds, say so plainly and tell me what he would have needed to argue against instead. Third, say whether the usual reply — that change is a matter of things bearing different properties at different times, not of events entering or leaving the series — is genuinely excluded by anything in this passage, or whether he quietly assumes that events rather than things are the bearers of change; and if he does assume it, whether there is any independent reason he might have had for doing so. Fourth, explain the claim that the tensed distinction may be regarded as "more fundamental than the distinction of earlier and later" — "in a certain sense" — and tell me what that sense is, because I cannot see how the tensed distinction could be prior to the tenseless one when the tenseless one seems to be what fixes the order the tensed one runs along. Fifth, tell me how much of his larger case this stretch has to carry: if I grant everything in it, what remains for him to prove, and if I reject it, could the rest of his argument survive without it? Finally, take the view my friend ended up with — all the tensed facts real, no single consistent whole — and tell me whether it rescues the A series, merely relocates the difficulty, or collapses into the view she says it is not; and give me the sharpest single question to put to her next time. No reading list, no citations, no suggestions for further reading: I have the paper in front of me and no library access, and what I need is the reasoning itself.
