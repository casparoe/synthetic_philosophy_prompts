I teach a final-year seminar on perception — about a dozen students, half philosophy, half cog sci — and I've got one essay slot left on the final. It's on cognitive penetration: whether what you believe, expect or want can actually change what you see. Problem is every past paper I've set on this turned into the same essay, i.e. the modularity camp says early vision is sealed off, the anti-modularity camp says here are the effects, everyone cites the same handful of illusions and the same banana-looks-yellower study, and I learn nothing about whether the student has thought about it. So the question can't be "what is cognitive penetration" or "is vision modular". I dont want a recap of the slugfest.

The crux I want is that three things get collapsed into one. There's an architectural claim (the mind is or isn't built so that this can happen), an evidential claim (the lab findings are or aren't really penetration, rather than attention, memory, or subjects reporting what they think you want to hear — the recent "it's all confounds" wave), and a normative claim (whether penetration would make perception better or worse as a source of justification). Most students swap one for another halfway through without noticing. I also want them to separate penetration of perceptual content — the banana literally looks yellower — from penetration of how vividly or insistently something is given, where the content is arguably the same but it's shouting at you. The literature runs those together and I want to see who can pull them apart.

Give them a case they can't have rehearsed. Use this one, in the question: two subjects, identical retinal input, one has been told the object is a ripe avocado, the other that it's a convincing plastic one. Their discrimination performance is indistinguishable — same matching, same same/different, same thresholds — but only the first says the green "looks ripe", reports it as more saturated, more alive. Ask what would have to be true for that to count as penetration, whether the discrimination data is evidence against it or beside the point, and what, if anything, turns on the answer for whether perception can justify belief.

Deliverables: the essay question itself (90 minutes, closed book, they can bring one page of notes); a model answer of around 700 words; and a marking scheme out of 20 built on five must-hit points. For each point, one line on how candidates typically blow it. Then a separate short list — three or four items — of moves that look sophisticated but should earn nothing, e.g. trotting out the Müller-Lyer persistence argument as if it settled anything. And I want the scheme to name, in a line each, the move that separates a first from a 2:1, because I've got a stack of B answers that are perfectly well-organised and say nothing. No reading list or citations needed anywhere, in the question or the model answer.
