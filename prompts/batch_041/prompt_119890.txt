I'm putting together the final for my upper-level seminar on the epistemology of disagreement — we read the usual cast (Elga, Christensen, Kelly, van Inwagen) plus a couple of papers on higher-order evidence — and I've been trying to write one essay question that students can't answer by just reciting the two positions. Every year I get thirty papers that say conciliationists think you should meet in the middle and steadfasters think you shouldn't, and then nothing else. I want a question that corners them somewhere the labels don't help. I was arguing about this with a colleague over coffee and we couldn't settle it, which is either ironic or just annoying.

Here's the corner I've been circling, and I'd like you to build the question around it (sharpen it or swap the details if you can make it bite harder): A and B are colleagues who share all the first-order evidence about some question Q and are, we stipulate, equally reliable at first-order reasoning about Q. They disagree about Q. But they also disagree about disagreement itself: A holds that when two equally reliable people disagree, each should move substantially toward the other; B holds that each should stay put. Now ask what A should do about Q. The obvious conciliationist answer is that A moves toward B about Q. But B is also A's peer on the second-order question — the question of what to do when peers disagree — so by A's own lights A should move toward B about conciliationism too. And the more A concedes there, the less she's entitled to the very principle that told her to move about Q in the first place. The view looks like it eats itself. I want the question to force them to say whether there's a non-question-begging way out — a principled reason the two disagreements aren't on a par, or a reason the regress is benign, or a flat admission that the view really is self-undermining here, or an argument that the setup is rigged and the puzzle dissolves.

So: write the exam question itself (90-minute in-class essay, closed book, and I'd expect a strong answer to run around 1200 words), a model answer of comparable length, and a marking scheme that seperates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each has to contain — and, on the marking side, what the typical weak answers look like (the ones that just restate the two views, or that think 'A should just trust her own reasoning' counts as a solution). Please don't build the marking scheme around citing anyone by name; I don't want to reward name-dropping and half of them will botch the attributions anyway. And since this is a case I'm inventing rather than something from the literature, have the model answer open with a note on how confident it is that this is a fair way to frame the actual debate rather than a puzzle that collapses on inspection, and flag the single point in the answer it's least sure about.
