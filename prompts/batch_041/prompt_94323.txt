I want to think about the wave of Buddhist self-immolations that started with Thich Quang Duc burning himself in a Saigon street in 1963, during the protests against the Diem regime, and I want to run the episode through Buddhist ethics and then through consequentialism, in both directions.

The facts I care about, roughly: a monk sits down in a busy street, is doused in gasoline and sets himself alight, stays upright and dies; the photos go round the world; other monks and nuns follow; the crackdown and the international outrage feed into the coup against Diem later that year. So the question is what each framework makes of the act itself, not the politics.

Buddhist side: the first precept forbids killing, and karma is usually said to turn on intention (cetana) more than on the outward act, so how does self-immolation come out here -- a straight breach of the precept, a compassionate transgression of it on the bodhisattva model, or something the precept never actually reached? Does the act being self-directed rather than other-directed change the verdict? Does the mental state (protest, compassion, despair) carry all the weight, or do the worldly consequences of the act matter to the karma story too?

Consequentialist side: run it as an intervention -- did it work, on whose ledger, and at what cost? Then the interesting part: people say Buddhist ethics is basically a form of consequentialism (character or rule consequentialism, with the good being the reduction of suffering), and others say it's a virtue or intention ethic that consequentialism badly misreads. Use the episode to say which reading fits, or to show that self-immolation is exactly the case where the two come apart.

Dont turn it into a history lesson. I want the mapping run both ways: what the theory says the monk faced and did, where it goes silent or just starts moralizing, and what the episode shows the theory can't handle.
