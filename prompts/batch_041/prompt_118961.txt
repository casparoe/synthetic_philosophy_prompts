I should like to set my graduate seminar an exercise in the epistemology of archaeology. Take the long controversy over the builders of Great Zimbabwe — the mortarless granite enclosures on the Zimbabwean plateau, the carved soapstone birds, the imported ceramics and glass that attest to Indian Ocean trade, and the absence of any writing from the society that raised the walls — together with the twentieth-century episode in which the settler government of Rhodesia pressed archaeologists to deny an African origin, and the eventual consensus that the site was built by ancestors of the Shona. Analyse that episode through the anti-foundationalist account of archaeological inference on which justification runs along a network of mutually reinforcing cables rather than a single chain anchored in theory-neutral data: no individual inference to an unobservable past need be independently secure, and objectivity lies in the tightness of the whole web of constraints rather than in any foundation. Map the disputants as epistemic agents, say what counted as evidence for each, what the account says they faced and ought to have done, and what can and cannot be inferred about the beliefs and institutions of the builders from the material record alone. Then be candid about where the account falls silent — I have in mind coercion, the question of who was permitted to count as a knower, and why agreement arrived when it did — and run the analysis the other way: does the episode vindicate the network picture, or show that it under-describes the social and political conditions under which archaeological claims are licensed? End by saying which argument or consideration would lead you to the opposite verdict, and where in your reasoning you are most likely to have gone astray. British English, please.
