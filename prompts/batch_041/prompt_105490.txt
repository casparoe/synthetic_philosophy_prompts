Okay, I've been reading about Lysenkoism and I'm now convinced it's a better test case for the James-vs-Clifford fight then the usual ones everyone recycles (the shipowner, the climber, the guy deciding whether to believe his friend is honest). So here's what I want.

Take the Lysenko affair and run it through both the ethics of belief and the will to believe. Quick shared picture so we're on the same page: Trofim Lysenko, the Soviet agronomist, pushes vernalization and the inheritance of acquired characteristics, he and his people call Mendelian genetics bourgeois and fraudulent, and in 1948 the agricultural academy makes it official -- genetics is out, effectively banned from being taught or done in the USSR. Vavilov, who actually knew the genetics, dies in a prison camp. Soviet agriculture pays for it. I'm not asking you to relitigate any of the science; take all that as given.

What I want is a case analysis that runs both directions: the theory reading the episode, and the episode testing the theory. Some things I specifically want pinned down:

1. On the Clifford side: who's the shipowner here? Clifford's whole point is that believing without sufficient evidence is wrong because it puts other people at risk, and that the believer is culpable even if it happens to turn out fine. Map that onto the Soviet case honestly -- who believed, on what evidence, and who carried the cost. But then tell me whether the harm-to-others rationale even does the work Clifford needs, because here the evidence wasn't just thin, it was being actively suppressed and the belief was being enforced. Does a principle about insufficient evidence have anything to say about evidence that's being strangled?

2. On the James side: James only licenses the will to believe for a genuine option -- live, forced, momentous -- and only where the truth can't be settled by evidence in advance of believing. Show me which of those conditions the Lysenko case does and doesn't satisfy. My gut is that it satisfies the formal ones in a creepy way: for a young Soviet biologist, accepting Lysenkoism was live, forced, and momentous. If that's right, what does it say about the criteria that terror can satisfy them? Is James's notion of a genuine option doing any work at all if the state can manufacture one?

3. The part I actually care about: where does each theory go silent? I suspect James has no room for coerced belief (his paradigm is a person free to believe or not), and Clifford has no account of how much evidence counts as sufficient when the relevant evidence is unavailable through no fault of the believer. If I'm wrong about either, say why. And if the case is really an argument for one of them over the other, say which and why -- or say it's a case neither was built to handle.

Keep it in American English, and don't bother with reading lists or citations, I can't look anything up and neither can you.
