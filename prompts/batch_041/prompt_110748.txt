I've been working from a writing-craft book a friend lent me, the chapter on dialogue, and it leans alot on a philosopher named Grice. Here's the chapter's argument, as close to the book's own words as I can remember:

"All conversation runs on an unspoken contract. The philosopher Paul Grice called it the Cooperative Principle: when we talk, we're agreeing to be informative, to be truthful, to be relevant, and to be clear. Those are his four maxims. A character who breaks one of these on purpose isn't being uncooperative — she's signalling that she means something other than what she said. That gap between the words and the meaning is what Grice called an implicature. So when your character looks out at a downpour and says 'lovely weather,' the reader understands she means the opposite; Grice's insight is that the reader does that work for herself, which is why you should trust her to. Implicatures are always cancellable — you can always tack on 'but I don't mean that' and the meaning evaporates — which is what makes them fragile, and useful. The upshot: anything a sentence conveys over and above its literal content is an implicature, so if you want your subtext to land, keep your sentences plain and let the implications do the lifting."
