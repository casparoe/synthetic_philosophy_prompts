I have been sitting in on a metaphysics reading group that keeps circling back to material constitution, and I have noticed that my gut verdicts on the cases form a pattern I cannot quite name. Let me lay out what I actually think, case by case. A sculptor takes a lump of clay and shapes it into a horse: I want to say there are two things occupying that region — the lump and the statue — that they are not identical, and that the statue depends on the lump for its existence but not the reverse. Squash the horse flat and I say the lump survives while the statue does not. Rework the same clay into a bowl and I say the lump survives and the statue is gone. But press a single extra pinch of clay onto the horse's flank and I say the statue is the same statue, merely enlarged, while the lump is now a different lump, because you have added to the matter. So a change of shape preserves the lump but destroys the statue, and a change of matter preserves the statue but destroys the lump. When a lump is split and worked into two separate figurines, I count two statues and, uncomfortably, still want to say there was one lump that no longer exists rather than two lumps that do. And when I imagine the clay minus a single molecule, I refuse to multiply lumps the way the problem of the many tells me I should, even though by the same reasoning I cheerfully multiply statues.

The pair I cannot reconcile is this. Hand me a snowball that happens to be shaped like a face and I say there is one thing there, not two — no sculpture, just snow. Put a mass-produced urinal in a gallery and call it art, and I suddenly want to say there are two things: the porcelain object and the artwork, with different persistence conditions, so that the artwork could survive being chipped in a way the object could not, or vice versa. Yet nothing physical distinguishes the snowball from the urinal except a fact about the art world, and I am not sure I want my ontology of material objects hanging on that. My tentative principle is that two material things in the same place at the same time are distinct just in case they have different persistence conditions, and that persistence conditions differ just in case one of them is essentially a form or a function rather than a portion of matter. Test that against the cases above and tell me whether it survives, or whether it collapses the snowball and the urinal into the same verdict.

What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of competing candidates stated precisely, that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, with each clause tied to the specific case that forces it. Say what each candidate predicts for cases I have not listed — a wave on the ocean, a hole in a piece of cheese, a shadow, a melody, a corporation, a robot whose parts are gradually swapped — and be honest about which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate and would force me to give up. If you think the whole framing is confused, say so and rebuild it, but do not simply hand me the textbook options; I already know those and they are exactly what fails to fit. Please answer in American English.
