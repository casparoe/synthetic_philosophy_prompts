I serve on the grants committee of a family foundation that makes about a dozen awards a year out of a permanent endowment, and I have to circulate a written recommendation before our March meeting. The single item on the docket is a request from a consortium of archivists, materials scientists, and linguists for roughly a quarter of our unrestricted endowment. They want to build and endow what they call a Millennial Archive: a self-contained, technology-independent record of basic knowledge — arithmetic, metallurgy, seed-saving, water purification, a primer in several major languages — inscribed so that it remains legible without electricity or specialist equipment for ten thousand years, together with a small permanent fund to pay for inspections every few decades and a custodian's salary. Our guidelines say we favor "durable public goods" but specify no horizon at all; the last long-horizon request we funded was a seed bank, and I was in the minority that voted against it. One of my fellow trustees has now circulated a two-page memo against the grant. His argument, as I understand it, is the doomsday argument: since you and I are in no way remarkable among all the humans who will ever have lived, finding ourselves roughly in the middle of the sequence of all humans is evidence that only about as many humans will come after us as have come before, so there is very likely nobody around in a thousand years, let alone ten thousand, and the archive would be a monument with no readers. He adds that our endowment's four-percent spending rule already concedes that future benefits count for less, so the doomsday argument only makes explicit what our investment policy assumes. He wants us to decline and move the money into near-term literacy work. I cannot tell whether he has even stated the argument correctly, and I have no idea whether, if he has, it should change our vote.

Please draft the recommendation I will circulate, addressed to the committee, that I can put my name to. It should reach a concrete bottom line — fund, decline, or fund at a specified reduced figure with conditions — say what we should require of the applicants before the next review, and give reasons the other trustees can actually argue with rather than a survey of the literature; we have no time to read, and I would rather the memo stand on its own.

Because the whole weight of the opposition rests on that one argument, the memo has to sort it out rather than wave at it. Tell me what the doomsday argument really claims and what it would take for my colleague's version to be the genuine article: which population he is sampling from, what he is assuming about how our position within it was selected, and whether the argument delivers any probability at all about readers in the year 3000 as opposed to a distribution over the total number of humans who will ever live. Then say whether an argument about the species can do the work he wants against this particular institution, given that the endowment, the languages the archive is written in, and the habit of reading are all shorter-lived than the species and each stand or fall on their own; whether the argument, if sound, would equally undermine the endowment that pays for our grants and his own confidence that our grantees will repay our investment; and whether his point about discounting is the same argument or a different one that happens to point the same way. If the argument ought to carry weight with us, say how much and why; if it ought not, say what would have to be true for it to.
