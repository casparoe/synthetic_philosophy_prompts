I am preparing a seminar on radical critiques of representative democracy, and I need a thought experiment built to order. The target is Simone Weil's claim that political parties are not merely electoral vehicles but structures that manufacture collective passion, pressure the thinking of their members, and pursue their own growth without limit; her proposed alternative is not a free-for-all of atomized individuals but a public life organized around journals, reading circles, and other non-party forms of association. I want a case that tests whether that alternative can escape the very dynamic she diagnoses. The case should be set in a realistic contemporary polity—say, a mid-sized city or a professional association—that has formally abolished parties and replaced them with a system inspired by her suggestion: competing journals, local reading circles, and, because the polity still needs to make binding decisions, a sortition-based assembly whose members are selected by lot. Constraints: the scenario must fit in one paragraph; it must not rely on a villain, a foreign plot, or a science-fictional device; the party-like symptoms must emerge from ordinary institutional incentives, not from characters who have read Weil and decided to imitate parties; and the case should include one feature that a defender of parties will say makes the comparison unfair—an external threat, a scarce resource, a media environment, or a coordination problem—and that feature should be explicitly flagged.

After the scenario, do the following. State what Weil's critique would say about the case, using her categories of collective passion, pressure on thought, and the growth imperative, but distinguish the reading you are relying on from readings that are genuinely contested among Weil scholars. Then state what a democratic pluralist would say in reply, and what a radical democrat who is sympathetic to Weil might say back. Identify the single structural feature that is doing the work—formal party label, common enemy, vote coordination, shared narrative, resource competition, or something else—and run the removal test: say what happens if that feature is removed, and whether the case collapses into an ordinary interest-group story. Finish with an honest vulnerability assessment: the strongest objection a defender of parties would raise against the case, the strongest rescue a defender of Weil could offer, and any point at which the scenario stipulates a verdict rather than generating it from the institutional logic. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
