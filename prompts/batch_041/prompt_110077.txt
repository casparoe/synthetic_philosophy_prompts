I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar on decision theory and multi-agent systems, about twenty students, one 90-minute session. I want an activity that makes them feel the force of the commitment-race dynamic — the way agents who would each prefer a negotiated division to a fight, and each prefer winning to losing, can be driven by the mere availability of binding commitments into an outcome worse for all of them than what they could have had — without my saying the words "commitment race" until the debrief. I don't want citations or a reading list; I want the design.

I have a rough mechanism in mind and I'd like you to build the activity around it, or around a better one, but not to treat mine as fixed. The pool: a fixed pot of a hundred points that three teams claim against one another; each round, teams play commitment cards of graded strength, publicly and in an order drawn at random for that round; a team's claim on the pot is set by the strongest card it has played and cannot be withdrawn without a penalty; if the total claims exceed the pot, the round collapses and every team receives a small consolation; a team may instead spend its turn proposing a division, which takes effect only if all three accept it. I would like the first half of the session played with non-binding declarations only, with binding cards introduced after a few rounds, so that the group watches a workable arrangement be destroyed by the arrival of a tool that ought to help them. Teams should have to reach internal unanimity before playing a card; I suspect that matters, and I'd like your view on whether it earns its complexity. No real money: a scoreboard, cards, and a secretariat of two or three students who record commitments and enforce the penalties.

What I want back is the full rules, the materials, a payoff scheme with the numbers behind it, a session timeline, and a debrief that maps particular moments onto the structure — why the escalation was overdetermined rather than caused by anyone's greed or confusion, what the random draw does, why a mutual preference for a deal does not by itself make a deal reachable, and what an external enforcer would and would not fix. Say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it, and be honest about which feature of the idea the game cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself in the debrief. I would also like one rule change I can make in a replay — a single dial, such as making commitments costly or reversible, or adding a fourth claimant — with your prediction of how the group's behaviour shifts and why. Finally, tell me where the design is most likely to fail as a teaching device: which step in the causal chain from the rules to the insight you would abandon first, and what you would want to see in a trial run that would change your mind about it.
