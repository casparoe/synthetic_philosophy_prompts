I'm a novelist — not an academic, never published a paper, and I'm applying for one of those year-long arts-and-humanities fellowships that basically pays you to think. They want a two-page research proposal and I've got half an idea I'd like you to turn into something I could actually hand in. Don't worry about citations or a reading list, I'll get a friend in a philosophy department to fill those in for me.

The half-formed idea: I keep noticing something when I workshop scenes. If I give the same moral dilemma to two readers — say, a person has to decide whether to divert a runaway trolley, or whether to hand over a friend hiding in the attic — and I write it one way as a bare clinical paragraph, and another way as a slow domestic scene with weather and a kitchen and somebody's bad knee, I get different answers. Same content, different container, different verdict. And then there's translation: two of my books have been translated, and the translators made choices I'd never have made, and the reviews in those languages zeroed in on completely different things than the English ones did. So my suspicion is that the big cross-cultural surveys of philosophical intuitions — the trolley stuff, the Gettier cases, the moral-luck stories — might be partly measuring narrative conventions and habits of storytelling rather than some deep difference in moral or epistemic intuition. A vignette is a tiny piece of fiction, and nobody in that literature treats the genre of the vignette as a variable that matters. That's the in-the-weeds bit I want to push on.

Turn this into a real proposal. I need: the question stated so precisely that there's a definite answer to it, some account of why the existing positions and studies leave it open (without me having to cite them, just describe the shape of the debate), and then the method — what I would actually do, month by month, for twelve months. My instinct is that the cheap and clever move is to vary the register inside a single language and culture first, where I can control everything, before spending money on translation and cross-cultural samples; say whether that's the right order and why, and what the obvious alternative approach would be and why mine beats it. Then tell me what a result would even look like — what I'd write at the end, and what finding would actually be interesting versus what would just be me confirming that readers are suggestible. And be honest about what would count as the project failing, and whether a year is enough given that I'll also be finishing a novel in the same twelve months. Assume I have basically no budget and no lab, just time, a laptop, some willing readers, and a couple of translators I can beg favors from.
