Consider a jurisdiction whose courts have adopted, as the official logic of legal proof, the paraconsistent logic LP, known as the logic of paradox. LP is three-valued: a proposition may be true, false, or both. An interpretation designates a proposition when it assigns it true or both, and an argument is valid just in case no interpretation designates every premise while failing to designate the conclusion. Three consequences matter here. LP is not explosive, so a court that records both A and not-A is not thereby committed to every conclusion whatever. LP retains the law of excluded middle, so every instance of A or not-A is designated. And because disjunction introduction is kept, disjunctive syllogism has to go: from A or B together with not-A, B does not follow; and since the material conditional is read as not-A or B, modus ponens fails with it, precisely in the cases where the antecedent is both true and false. Two further points of vocabulary matter. Paraconsistency is a property of a consequence relation rather than a thesis about truth, whereas dialetheism is the thesis that some contradictions are true. And validity in LP is designated-value preservation rather than truth preservation, so a valid argument may have a conclusion that is both true and false: valid does not mean true.

Write the practice direction by which the judges of that jurisdiction are instructed to conduct trials under LP. It should be a formal internal document, with a plausible heading, headed sections and numbered paragraphs, deadpan from beginning to end, of about 800 to 900 words. Invent the jurisdiction and its institutions; do not lean on a real state, court, statute or living person. The register should be that of an English practice direction, in British spelling and idiom.

The humour must come from the logic rather than from invention. The direction should cover, at a minimum: the standard of proof, given that a fact-finder's conclusion may be designated without being true, and given that the doubt itself may be both well founded and not; the inferences still available to advocates and those withdrawn, with a worked example of the loss of disjunctive syllogism (a disjunction established by evidence, one disjunct excluded by further evidence) and one of the loss of modus ponens (an alibi, or an expert's conditional conclusion, whose antecedent is itself both); the verdict form, and whether a defendant may be found both guilty and not guilty, with provision for how such a finding is recorded, pronounced and reported; the classes of case in which a finding of both is permitted, the drafter offering a criterion for distinguishing a genuine contradiction from a merely apparent one, reaching for self-reference, self-applicable rules and borderline vagueness, and saying something about why ordinary disputed facts are supposed not to qualify; the downstream consequences for sentence, appeal, double jeopardy and precedent, including the status of a judgment that is valid and yet false; and the law of non-contradiction, explaining how a court whose logic still validates that law can convict on the strength of an instance of it that is false.

Nothing in the document should be a consequence LP does not actually have; where the arrangements become unworkable, that must show through the drafting itself, in a proviso that undermines the rule it qualifies or a paragraph the reader must read twice, and never through commentary, aside or any character who finds the situation absurd. The document must believe in its own reasonableness, and should close by reassuring the public that little will change in practice. A reader who knows no non-classical logic should be able to follow it, since the drafter will explain the logic as he goes; a specialist should recognise every loss as real.

The target of the satire is the suggestion that paraconsistent logic is a cost-free upgrade, one in which explosion is simply dropped and ordinary reasoning carries on as before, together with the wider insouciance about contradictions that the dialetheist position invites. The piece should be a fair reductio rather than a caricature: the drafter should be competent, evidently at home with the logic, and genuinely sympathetic to the reform he is obliged to administer.
