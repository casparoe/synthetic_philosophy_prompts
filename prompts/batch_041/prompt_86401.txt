I sit on the ethics committee at a hospital, and something about how we handle deaths in the ICU doesnt sit right with me. When a family agrees to stop treatment -- pull the breathing tube, stop dialysis -- we call that letting nature take its course; it needs a form and a second signature, but nobody thinks anyone killed the patient, and our policy actually says stopping a treatment is the same as never having started it. But when a nurse doesn't notice a patient going downhill and doesn't call the rapid response team, and the patient dies, that's a failure, someone gets hauled in, and everyone says the nurse caused the death. Same non-action, opposite verdict. It gets stranger: a parent who doesn't feed their kid can be charged with murder, while a doctor who stops a feeding tube is treated as acting within their rights. And we sometimes turn the ventilator down over several hours instead of switching it off, which I've been told is partly so it doesn't look like killing.

I want to know what this whole pattern presupposes about what a cause is. Does it require believing that omissions aren't causes at all, or that they are causes but a separate story about duties explains why only some of them count? Is the pattern even internally consistent, or are the ICU rules and the criminal law pulling in different directions? Which bits of the practice actually need that view of causation and which would survive without it -- and where is the practice underdetermined, so that several rival theories would license exactly the same policies? Finally, if we swapped in a view where not-doing is just as causally real as doing, what would actually have to change in our forms, our consents, and our discipline?

Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, with no intro and no wrap-up at the end.
