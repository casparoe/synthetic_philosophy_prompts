I am a postdoc in logic, and in three weeks I have to give the department's logic-and-computation seminar a talk built around a deliberately provocative thesis. The thesis is this: the widely held view that intuitionistic logic is the logic that gets the meanings of the logical constants right — and that classical logic is at best a convenient fiction and at worst unintelligible — rests on a mistake about what a semantics is supposed to do. My argument is that the BHK-style explanations of the connectives, which constructivists treat as the touchstone of correctness, are better read as a translation manual between two proof systems than as a disclosure of the one true meaning of conjunction, disjunction, implication, and the quantifiers; and that once you see this, the classical and intuitionistic connectives are not rivals for the title of the real logic but simply different connectives, fixed by different stipulations. On this view, the constructivist's charge that classical reasoning about, say, an as-yet-unsolved Diophantine equation is contentless is not a mathematical discovery but a category mistake: the classical mathematician and the constructivist are not disagreeing about a single shared subject matter, they are talking past each other with homonymous connectives. I would like you to draft the Q&A prep sheet for the talk.

The audience is awkward in a specific way, and I want the objections phrased the way these people would actually phrase them. About half the room works in constructive mathematics and type theory: they use Martin-Löf-style type theories and proof assistants daily, they regard the BHK explanation as non-negotiable, and they are likely to hear my talk as a piece of classical triumphalism. Two of them in particular care about canonicity and normalization and will want to know what work my "translation manual" reading is supposed to do that the standard story does not already do. The other half is split. A couple of classical model theorists agree with my conclusion but think the whole debate is a fuss about nothing, and will be impatient with any argument that takes constructivism seriously enough to engage it; and one formal-verification person is perfectly happy to use classical logic and an SMT solver all day, but has a practitioner's stake in the question of what a proof's constructive content is when you extract a program from it. There is also, sitting quietly at the back, a historian of mathematics who knows Brouwer's rhetoric better than anyone in the room and may object that I am attacking a strawman version of the position.

Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, each attributed to the kind of audience member who will raise it and phrased in that person's vocabulary; order them by how much damage each would do if I fumbled it; and for each one tell me what I should concede, what I should hold, a reply that fits in two minutes, and a note on where that reply is weakest. Finish by flagging the objection I am hoping nobody asks, and say plainly whether I can afford to leave it unanswered.
