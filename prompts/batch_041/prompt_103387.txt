I teach an upper-level epistemology course and I am writing the final exam. I want a single essay question on the Meno problem — why knowledge should be more valuable than mere true belief — that cannot be answered by reciting the familiar answer from the dialogue itself. Here is the background I want the question to presuppose, stated explicitly so that a student who has read only the primary text and one secondary article can engage with it.

In the Meno, Socrates compares true beliefs to the statues of Daedalus: fine and lifelike while they last, but liable to run away unless they are tied down, and he suggests that knowledge is the tied-down, and therefore more valuable, condition. Call this the stability answer: knowledge is worth more than true belief because it is more durable and less likely to be lost.

The swamping problem, as it is usually put, runs as follows. If the value of a true belief is what ultimately matters, and the extra ingredient that turns true belief into knowledge — justification, or the reliability of the belief's aetiology — is valuable only because it tends to produce true beliefs, then once the true belief is already in hand that ingredient adds nothing: its value is swamped by the value of the truth it was meant to secure. On this argument, any account on which the distinguishing ingredient of knowledge has merely instrumental value cannot explain why knowledge is more valuable than true belief.

Now the twist I want the question built around. Suppose we discover a procedure — call it T — that can be applied from the outside to any true belief, including one produced by a lucky guess, and that renders the belief as durable and as counterfactually robust as any piece of knowledge: the agent will retain it, and would retain it under a wide range of perturbations, exactly as a knower would. Applying T requires no justification on the agent's part and does not improve the belief's pedigree; it is a purely external stabilizer — a cognitive analogue of a procedure that pins a belief in place without making it any better grounded.

The question should ask the student, in effect: given T, what is left of the claim that knowledge is more valuable than true belief? Specifically, it should force the student to separate (i) the claim that knowledge is more stable than true belief from (ii) the claim that knowledge is more valuable than true belief, and to say whether T refutes the stability answer or merely shows that stability is not what marks the difference between knowing and merely truly believing. It should then connect this to the swamping problem: if a mere true belief can be made as stable as knowledge by an external procedure, does that show the value of knowledge cannot lie in its stability, and does it leave the swamping problem intact or dissolve it? Finally, it should ask whether a reliabilist — someone who holds that a belief is knowledge just in case it is true and produced by a reliable process — can still give a satisfying answer to the Meno problem once T is on the table, and if so, where the extra value is supposed to come from.

Please write the question itself, phrased as one essay prompt with two or three lettered parts, answerable in about seventy-five minutes; a model answer of roughly nine hundred words; and a marking scheme out of thirty that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must make — for instance, the move that distinguishes the stability of a belief from its pedigree, and the move that locates the value of knowledge either in a non-instrumental property of the knowing state or in the way the whole exceeds the sum of its parts. The scheme should also list the specific mistakes that should cost marks, such as treating the T-case as a refutation of reliabilism when it is really a challenge to a stability-based answer, or confusing the swamping problem with the Meno problem.

One thing to watch. If you think the T-case collapses a distinction I am relying on — say, if "durable and counterfactually robust" cannot be specified without already smuggling in the belief's pedigree — then say so and explain why the question cannot be marked as posed, rather than producing a scheme that papers over the problem. I would rather be told the exercise is confused than receive a clean-looking answer that ignores it.
