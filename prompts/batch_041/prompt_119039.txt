I've been sitting in on a reading group about Stoicism's modern revival, and last week a disagreement broke out that I still can't sort out. A colleague of mine — a project manager who reads the popular Stoicism books and quotes them in standups — has a line he deploys whenever anyone complains about a decision handed down from above: "You can't control what happens to you, only how you respond." He used it to shut down a junior colleague who was angry about a reorganization that had moved her team and made her job substantially worse. His point was that her anger was her own doing, that the reorganization was simply not hers to have any say over, and that the only proper object of her attention was her attitude toward it. What struck me is that I couldn't tell whether he was saying something trivially true, something false, or something genuinely deep that I was failing to appreciate, and I suspect the slogan is doing several different jobs at once, only some of which would license his move.

I'd like you to lay out the precise claims that sentence could be making, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, and then give me a verdict on each. What does "control" have to mean for the first clause to come out true, and does that same sense carry over to "how you respond" — given that panic, grief, resentment, and the physiological side of anger are not obviously up to us in the way that raising one's arm is? Is the claim about direct voluntary control, about causal influence more broadly, about what is attributable to one's agency, or about what it is reasonable to care about? Is it a descriptive thesis about the limits of power, a normative thesis about where one's concern should be directed, or a claim about the metaphysics of fate and assent — the ancient Stoic view that everything is fated and yet what we do is nonetheless ours? And is the dichotomy itself a false one, since most of what people care about is partly influenceable rather than either fully controllable or fully beyond reach? For each reading, say whether it is trivial, analytic, substantive and defensible, or simply false, and say explicitly which readings would actually support my colleague's use of it — that is, which of them would justify telling someone that her anger at an institutional decision is misplaced — and which would leave his move unmade.

Two bits of the ancient text seem relevant and I'd like you to bring them in. Epictetus's Enchiridion opens by saying "Some things are in our control and others not," and then puts body, property, reputation, and command on the side of what is not in our control, while putting opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion, and one's own actions on the other side; a few chapters later it says that "Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things." I want to know whether the modern slogan is a faithful compression of that position or a distortion of it — in particular, whether the original claim is about the scope of a specific faculty (the use of impressions, or what the Stoics called what is up to us) rather than about control in the ordinary English sense, and whether the original's normative payload, that only virtue and vice are genuinely good or bad and everything else is indifferent, survives in the popular version or gets quietly dropped. Finish by telling me which reading you think the popular revival actually means in practice, and which reading the ancient text is actually committed to, if those two come apart.
