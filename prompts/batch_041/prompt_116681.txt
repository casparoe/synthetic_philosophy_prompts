I have finished a long essay — originally a lecture, now meant for publication — and I would like your comments on it before I send it anywhere. I am a novelist by trade and not a historian, and the essay makes claims about historiography that I can defend only from inside my own craft. I would rather learn now where I have overstepped than in print.

The essay is titled, provisionally, The Wheel and the Arrow. Its argument is as follows.

Every story I have written has an arrow in it. Something begins, something is complicated, something is resolved, and the resolution, when the story is any good, arrives as a kind of judgment — as though the ending were settling an account opened at the start. I used to think this was simply the shape of narrative. I now believe it is a philosophy of history that I absorbed without ever choosing it, and that it is only one of two available philosophies.

The arrow is the linear view. Its ancestors are the providential histories that read the whole of time as a single story with a beginning, a middle, and an end that retroactively reveals the meaning of the parts. On this view time is irreversible and cumulative: what comes later is not a return but an advance or a fall, and the story is going somewhere. The arrow is what makes a novel possible, because reading is itself an irreversible experience — you cannot un-read page one after page two — and the arrow is the form of irreversible experience.

The wheel is the cyclical view. Polybius, as I understand him, watched constitutions turn through a fixed sequence and come round again. Ibn Khaldun watched dynasties rise on the cohesion of a founding generation and decay through the softness of its heirs. Spengler treated cultures as organisms passing through their seasons, and Toynbee, if I have him right, treated civilizations as answering challenges until the creative minority stops being creative. Different mechanisms, one shape: recurrence without progress, a story that is always already beginning again.

Here is the difficulty that prompted the essay. I have tried three times to write a novel on the wheel, and three times I failed in the same way. The failure was not one of plotting; it was one of experience. A cycle is visible only from a vantage point that has already seen the whole turn — from the second lap. But my reader is always on the first lap, because reading is a single irreversible passage. So whenever I render a cycle I am forced to render it as a report: I need a narrator who has already gone round, or a device — a chronicle, a legend, a prophecy — that tells the reader what is coming back. The cycle is never experienced, only announced; and an announced cycle is, formally, merely an arrow that points at its own tail.

From this I want to draw a stronger conclusion than I am sure I am entitled to. I want to say that the cyclical view of history cannot be narrated at all — that it can be believed, diagrammed, and preached, but not told, and that this is a limit of the medium rather than of my talent.

I do propose one device that I think comes closest, and I want to know whether it earns its keep. I call it the turntable ending: an ending written so that it is also a beginning, its last movement completing a circuit back to the first, so that on a second reading the book becomes a different book, its ending now a premise. The cycle, on this account, exists for the rereader and not for the first reader. If the turntable ending works, then cyclical history does have a home in narrative after all — but only for the reader who reads twice. That is a strange and perhaps fatal condition, since it would mean the wheel is available only to the already-returned, which is precisely the vantage I said the reader can never occupy. So either the device rescues the wheel, or it confirms my claim. I cannot decide which, and I half suspect that this undecidability is the essay's real subject.

What I would like from you:

First, where does the argument overreach? I am fondest of the strong claim — that the wheel cannot be told — and I suspect it is also the weakest, but I would like to know what it would cost me to keep it and what it would cost me to give it up.

Second, is the turntable ending a genuine formal device, or have I simply redescribed an ordinary linear ending with an epilogue attached? If it is genuine, what distinguishes it from the mere circularity of a story that happens to end where it began?

Third, is my compressed sketch of the four figures fair, or have I flattened four distinct views into a single shape for the convenience of my argument? If they do not in fact share one shape, say where the seam is.

Fourth, is there a counterexample — a narrative that genuinely puts its reader on the second lap, so to speak, rather than merely informing them that laps exist? If there is, my strong claim falls, and I would rather hear it from you than from a reviewer.

I am not asking you to fix the prose. I am asking whether the thing I have built holds weight, and where.
