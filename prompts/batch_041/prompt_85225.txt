I recently spent several days at my sister's bedside while she was in a medically induced coma, and the experience has made me think hard about a puzzle in epistemology: how someone could know an ordinary fact and yet fail to know the denial of a skeptical scenario that the ordinary fact entails. I have been reading about Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge and the debate over epistemic closure, and I would like you to construct a thought experiment that makes the following verdicts come out cleanly, without relying on the standard brain-in-a-vat, evil-demon, or dreaming cases.

The case should be realistic — a hospital, a courtroom, a workplace, or a family situation, not science fiction — and should fit in one paragraph. In the scenario, a subject S must know some ordinary proposition p by a method that satisfies the tracking conditions (sensitivity and adherence). S must also know that p entails q, and must come to believe q by deducing it from p. But S must not know q, because S's belief in q fails the sensitivity condition: in the nearest world where q is false, S would still believe q (and would still believe p, and so would still deduce q). The failure must turn on the method-relative character of the tracking conditions — the nearest p-false world and the nearest q-false world must come apart — and not on S being irrational or careless.

After the scenario, do the check: state p, q, the method S uses for each belief, the relevant nearby worlds, and why each tracking condition gives the verdict it does. Then identify the single feature of the case that does the work, say what happens if that feature is removed (for example, if S had an independent reliable method for q, or if the nearest q-false world were an ordinary rather than a skeptical scenario), and explain how a defender of epistemic closure would attack the case. I do not need citations, reading lists, or references; just the thought experiment and the analysis.
