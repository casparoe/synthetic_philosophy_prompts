I am a retired plant physiologist — thirty-four years on the water relations of leaves — and since I stopped working I have been trying to supply the philosophical education I never got, chiefly by attending a monthly reading group that meets in a deconsecrated chapel at the edge of town. One of our members, a retired professor of philosophy whom I shall call Vera, has become the group's oracle: we put cases to her, she gives a verdict, and we write it down. Our present subject is joint action — what it is for several people to do something together, as opposed to merely doing things alongside one another — and I find the notion harder to handle than anything in my old field. In physiology we were forever asking who exactly was doing the doing; a stand of trees does not compete for light, the individual trees do. I should like to know what the equivalent discipline is here, because Vera's answers have begun to look less like verdicts than like the output of a machine whose mechanism I cannot see.

Her record, as well as I have it, is this. Two neighbours who agree to carry a sofa up to a third-floor flat, each intending that the two of them carry it, each knowing the other intends it too, each adjusting his pace to the other's: joint action. A rush-hour crowd funnelling through a station exit, every person heading home, adjusting to avoid collisions, all mutually aware: not joint action. Two people in a darkened hall who each independently begin stacking the chairs, neither aware that the other is doing anything of the kind, no arrangement between them of any sort: not joint action, though the chairs get stacked and both wanted them stacked. Neighbours who have promised one another to raise a barn, each knowing the others have promised, each feeling bound and each entitled to complain if one of them drifts off: joint action. A chorus of strangers assembled for a single evening's performance, who have never met, each singing her own part and listening hard to the others, all of them knowing the rest are doing likewise: joint action. An adult and a small child building a tower of blocks, the child placing blocks wherever she pleases and understanding nothing of the adult's plan: not joint action — the adult is building a tower and the child is playing beside him, whatever an onlooker might say. And two strangers meeting on a narrow bridge, each reading the other's intention and edging past in perfect mutual anticipation, with nothing agreed and nothing shared beyond the wish to reach the far side: not joint action.

Next month I am to put this to her. A country lane is blocked by a branch brought down in a storm; two motorists, strangers to one another, stop at the same moment, and without a word each takes an end of the branch, they lift it in time with one another, each watching and adjusting to the other's grip, and then they nod and drive on. Neither asked for help; nothing was agreed, promised, or even gestured; each would have had no complaint whatever to make had the other simply driven around the obstruction; and each, as it happens, would have let go the instant the other did. Will she say that they moved the branch together, or only that each moved half a branch at the same moment? What she says about the branch interests me less than the principle she is applying. So: which rules her earlier verdicts leave standing — whether what she is tracking is a shared intention that each party knows the other has, or a commitment the parties have made to one another, or something else I have not thought of — which rules they rule out, what each surviving rule predicts for my two motorists, which one I should expect her to be running and why, and what case I should put to her next if I want to force the difference into the open.
