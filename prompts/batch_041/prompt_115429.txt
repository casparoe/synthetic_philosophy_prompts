My father died in February, and since then I have been sitting in on a Stoicism practice group that meets above a bookshop on Tuesday nights. The woman who runs it, Ruth, has been at this for decades and is the only one there who never waves her hands; when the rest of us argue about what is and is not up to us, we mostly end up arguing about what Ruth would say. I have started keeping a list of her rulings, because I would rather learn to run her reasoning myself than keep asking her. On my own cases so far: it was up to me whether I kept replaying the thought that I should have called the ambulance a day sooner; up to me whether I told my mother what the will actually said; up to me whether the anger I feel toward my brother hardened into a standing grievance I nurse; up to me whether I show up on Tuesday nights; up to me whether I get up at six and run. It was not up to me whether my father's cancer was caught in time, whether my brother forgives me for missing the last Christmas, whether my mother is alive this time next year, or whether the agent takes my novel. And when I told her that I still flinch every time I walk past my father's study, she said the flinch is not up to me, that it is closer to a reflex than to a thought, and that what is up to me is the ten seconds after it; she said the same about the tears that ambush me when his song comes on in the supermarket.

The case I cannot settle by myself is the one that actually keeps me up: whether it is up to me that I am still grieving at all. Not the flinch, which she has already ruled on, and not any single bad afternoon, but the fact that the loss is still the thing my days are organized around and, as far as I can tell, will be next February too. The group has been no help, because half of them insist that a passion like grief is only a judgment I keep endorsing, and so of course it is up to me, while the other half insist that grief is precisely the thing no one can will away and that all you can decide is what to do while it lasts; and one man there keeps saying the whole dichotomy is a fiction, since almost nothing worth naming is either wholly ours or wholly not. I have a stake in this that has nothing to do with being right. If it is up to me, then the fact that I still cry in the car on the way home is a failure of mine, and I would like to know whether that is the correct reading of her.

So please tell me what Ruth will say about the new case, and, more than that, tell me the rule she seems to be running. Set out the rival rules that fit everything above just as well — I would rather see two or three that each survive her verdicts than a single tidy one — say what each of them predicts about my grief a year out, tell me which one I should bet on and why, and name the further case I could put to her that would most cleanly force her to choose between them.
