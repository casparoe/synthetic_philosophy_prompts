I'm giving the toast at my colleague's retirement dinner next month, and I need you to write it. She's a historian of the Braudel school — spent her whole carreer on the longue durée, the three tiers of time, the Mediterranean as a near-permanent structure, events as little more than foam on the surface of deeper currents. Here's my problem: a retirement toast is supposed to be about a person, and her own framework says individual lives and individual events barely register at that scale. I don't want to just ignore the theory, and I definitely don't want to smugly undercut her with it either. I want a toast that takes the longue durée seriously and finds some honest way to honor her from inside it — so that the view actually constrains what I can and can't say, rather than being decoration. Mixed room: about a dozen historians who will notice instantly if I fudge the tiers of time, plus her family, who won't. Five minutes, and it has to land as affection, not as a seminar.

Take the role of a philosopher of history who knows Braudel's framework cold. Before you write anything, brainstorm two or three different ways an individual could be said to matter within a longue-durée picture, then pick whichever makes for the stronger toast and write it.
