A colleague and I have been arguing for two years about epiphenomenalism, and we have agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration rather than another round of seminar sniping. Write the joint statement we should be able to sign.

I hold the epiphenomenalist line: phenomenal experiences are real, are caused by neural events, and are themselves causally inert with respect to every physical event, including the neural events that issue in our reports about them. My colleague is no eliminativist; he takes experiences to be real, to be more than the neural states that realize them, and to make a causal difference to what we think and say. He grants that nobody has a satisfying account of how experiences fit into the physical world, but holds that if experiences make no causal difference, then the fact that our judgments about them are so reliably about them becomes a coincidence, and, worse, that my assertion that experiences exist cannot be justified by the experiences it is about. He calls this the paradox of phenomenal judgment and reads it as a reductio; I read it as a consequence.

Draft the document that would let us stop talking past each other. Open with a numbered section of claims we can both sign: about what the physical sciences do and do not license, about what exactly is and is not being denied when I call experiences inert, and about which parts of our disagreement are not about experience at all. Follow it with a disagreements table. For each disagreement, give the question, my formulation in words I would accept, his formulation in words he would accept, and then the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of us. For each crux, name a case, argument, or empirical result we would both agree bears on it (a dissociation in which a subject judges accurately about a stimulus while denying any experience of it; the conceivability of a physical duplicate that behaves exactly as we do while experiencing nothing), and record in advance what each of us predicts about it, and what either of us would count as disconfirming his own prediction.

Three matters I would rather have settled than fudged. First, whether the reliability of a judgment about an experience requires the experience to be among the judgment's causes, or whether a common cause — a neural state that produces both the experience and the judgment — can do the work, and whether that turns on whether the content of the judgment is fixed by the neural state alone. Second, whether a judgment can track an experience without being caused by it, given that both may depend on a common cause, and whether that kind of counterfactual dependence is enough for the judgment to be about the experience. Third, whether any part of our dispute is merely verbal, especially the part about whether experiences can be called causes at all; I suspect that on a difference-making rather than a production account of causation, some of what he denies is something I never asserted, and I would like you to say so if that is right. Neither of us may treat as a brute fact why creatures like us form judgments about experience at all, and you should say plainly whether there is anything that would move either of us.

Write a document we could both sign and circulate: no adjudication, no tie-breaking, no middle position that neither of us holds.
