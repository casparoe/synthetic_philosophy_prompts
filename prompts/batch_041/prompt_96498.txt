I'm in my third year of a philosophy degree, taking a course on ethics, and two people in my seminar had an argument that our tutor left hanging, so I'd like your help settling it properly. The case we were discussing was a friend of ours, a pre-med student who volunteers at a hospice. He's quite open that he does it partly because it looks good on his medical school application and partly because he finds it rewarding; he readily admits he wouldn't keep it up if it did nothing for him. The disagreement was about whether his volunteering has moral worth.

One of them said it plainly doesn't. Acting for the right reason, he said, means acting from duty; if the application is what's doing the work, then the act is self-interested no matter how much good comes of it. The other said that sets an impossible bar, because real motives are always mixed, and a standard nobody could ever meet just shows the standard is wrong; she added that the requirement is only that the action be done from duty, not that duty be the sole motive in play. He replied that mixed motives are acceptable only when duty is what actually moves you, and that here it plainly isn't, since our friend says he wouldn't do it for nothing. She said that's a claim about what he would do in a situation he isn't in, not about what he is in fact doing, and that we are notoriously unreliable about such counterfactuals even in our own case. He answered that if you drop the counterfactual you lose the only thing separating acting from duty from merely acting in accordance with it, and the distinction then collapses into nothing more than doing the right thing.

I'd like you to adjudicate this move by move — who is right about which specific claim, and where each of them is overreaching — rather than telling me who wins overall. If my framing rests on a confusion (say, about whether moral worth attaches to the act, the agent, or the motive, or about whether motives can be individuated the way the argument assumes), say so plainly instead of working around it.
