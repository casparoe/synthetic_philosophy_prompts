I've been reading Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments on and off for a few months and somethings been bugging me about the impartial spectator. The way I understand it, the impartial spectator is basically the voice of the community that we've internalized. As kids we learn what the people around us approve and disapprove of, and later, when we're deciding what to do, we imagine how our conduct would look to a fair-minded onlooker -- except that onlooker is really just a tidied-up version of the crowd we grew up in. If that's right, Smith is a conventionalist. Whatever a society's spectators happen to approve of sets the standard, so a person can't really be right while everyone around them is wrong; the most you can say is that you've internalized a different audience than your neighbours did.

But then I keep running into bits that don't fit. He's plainly hostile to slavery even though the spectators around him were fine with it, and he says custom and fashion can warp our moral sentiments -- that people brought up surrounded by cruelty and injustice lose their sense of how dreadful it is. And when I try to patch this by saying the impartial spectator isn't the real crowd but an idealized one, I get a regress: I imagine how an impartial spectator would feel about my conduct, but to know whether his feelings are the right ones I'd need to imagine yet another spectator watching him, and so on. He also doesn't seem to fall back on God's commands or on a rational law of the kind his contemporaries used, so I can't see where an independent standard would even come from in his system.

So am I misreading him? Is the impartial spectator supposed to be something other than the internalized voice of my society -- and if so, what exactly is it, and what stops the whole thing from collapsing into "whatever my crowd approves is right"? I'd especially like to know which parts of this are genuinely settled about what Smith meant and which parts Smith scholars are still arguing about. Please don't hand me a confident textbook answer where the experts actually disagree, and flag it clearly if some reading I'm assuming is just one contested interpretation.
