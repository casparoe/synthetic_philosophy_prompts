I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for a long time, and there is one argument I have never been able to reconstruct in a way that satisfies me: the bootstrapping objection to using a faculty or an instrument to vouch for itself.

The version I keep running into goes like this. I put a parcel on my bathroom scale; it reads 2.4 kg; I take it, on the scale's own authority, that the parcel weighs 2.4 kg; I note that the scale's reading matched the parcel's weight; I repeat the exercise with a hundred parcels and conclude that the scale is accurate. The objection is that this is circular reasoning — I have used the scale to establish the scale's reliability — and that a circular argument transmits no warrant, so any account of justification that licenses it must be false.

I cannot find the circle. Each step is an ordinary valid inference, the premises are ones I seem entitled to, and no premise simply restates the conclusion. If the argument is defective, the defect must lie somewhere other than in its logical form, and I should like to know where. It also seems to me that the diagnosis of circularity dictates the cure: supply the missing premise — that the scale is reliable, or that I have independent grounds for trusting it, such as its giving stable readings on repeated weighings — and the argument becomes either respectable or unnecessary. Is that the right repair? If it is not, what precisely is wrong with the argument, and why does the obvious repair fail?

Please lead with your bottom line in your first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
