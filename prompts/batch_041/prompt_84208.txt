Question 3 (10 points): Epistemic contextualism and pragmatic encroachment both hold that practical factors can affect knowledge attributions, but they do so in different ways. (a) Using the bank cases, explain how each view treats the relation between practical stakes and knowledge. (b) Suppose an attributor A is in a low-stakes situation and is assessing whether subject S knows that p, where S is in a high-stakes situation and S's evidence for p is only moderate. What does the contextualist say about the truth of A's attribution "S knows that p", and what does the subject-sensitive invariantist say? (c) Does pragmatic encroachment entail epistemic contextualism? Explain why or why not.

Student answer:

Both contextualism and pragmatic encroachment agree that practical factors matter for knowledge, but they disagree about where the practical factor enters. In DeRose's bank cases, a subject is deciding whether to deposit a check on Saturday. In the low-stakes case, the check is small and there is no penalty for waiting until Monday, so the subject can truly say she knows the bank is open. In the high-stakes case, the check is for a mortgage payment and missing it would be catastrophic, so she cannot truly say she knows the bank is open. For DeRose, this is because whether the subject knows depends on how much is at stake for the subject: high stakes raise the epistemic standard the subject has to meet. Stanley's subject-sensitive invariantism also says stakes matter, but it denies that "knows" is context-sensitive. Instead, the knowledge relation itself depends on the subject's practical interests, so the word "knows" picks out the same relation in every context even though whether that relation holds varies with stakes.

(b) If A is in a low-stakes situation and S is in a high-stakes situation, the contextualist says A's utterance "S knows that p" is true, because the standards are set by A's context and A has low standards. The subject-sensitive invariantist says the same thing: since A is the one making the attribution, what matters is A's practical situation, so the attribution is true. Either way, the sentence can be true in one context and false in another.

(c) Pragmatic encroachment does entail contextualism. Once you admit that practical factors can make a difference to whether someone knows, you have to say that "knows" is context-sensitive. That is why Fantl and McGrath, who argue that you cannot know in high-stakes cases because you cannot act on the proposition, are contextualists.
