I have been arguing with a friend about Wittgenstein's On Certainty, and he has produced an argument that I can restate but cannot disarm. I would like you to tell me which step fails, and what it costs to give that step up.

He sets it out as follows.

(1) A hinge proposition is one that is exempt from doubt within the practice in which it does its work. It is held fast rather than tested; it is the scaffolding against which particular questions are raised and settled, and it is not itself one of the things that gets questioned or confirmed inside that practice.
(2) Doubt requires grounds. To doubt that p one must have some reason for thinking not-p, and one must already accept a great deal in order to have any reason at all. So there is no such thing as doubting a hinge. Doubting a hinge is not merely hard or unusual; it is not a move in the game at all.
(3) Suppose a hinge proposition could be false. Then it would be possible for those who hold it to be in error about it. But where error is possible, grounds against the proposition are possible; and where grounds are possible, doubt is possible.
(4) Therefore a hinge proposition cannot be false. Its certainty is not the ordinary certainty of a claim that happens to be very well supported; it is immunity to falsity.
(5) Now take a community whose hinges are, by our lights, false: that the earth is flat and fixed, that the dead watch over the living, that a particular book settles what the weather will do. By (1) through (4), those propositions are hinges for that community, and so cannot be false for them either.
(6) So no community's hinges can be false. Truth, for a practice, is whatever that practice holds fast.
(7) This is too much. It means that when we call a rival framework mistaken we are saying only that it is not ours; that we cannot say of our own past that we were wrong about something we once held fast; and that the difference between a science and a superstition is the difference between two scaffoldings and nothing more.

His rejoinder to the obvious objection: you will want to say that hinges can be revised, that the river bed shifts. But revision requires grounds, and by (2) there are no grounds against a hinge from within the practice; so what looks like a rational correction of a hinge can only be a conversion, and a conversion is exactly what (7) describes.

I would rather keep (1) and (2), which seem to me to be what the later Wittgenstein actually holds, than accept (7). So: which step should I reject, and why did it look as though it followed? If the culprit is (3), tell me what I now owe — in particular, how a proposition can be false, or later turn out to have been false, without ever having been doubtable within the practice that held it, and how its falsity makes itself felt. If it is (1) or (2), tell me what remains of the hinge picture once the premise is gone. And if the argument is in fact valid, say which premise is cheapest to give up and what that concession costs the rest of the view.
