I chair the public statements committee of my professional association in philosophy, and I need the written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's vote. One member has moved that we declare that a state's eighteen-month campaign against an ethnic minority — mass killings, destruction of villages and places of worship, forced displacement of the survivors, and internal planning documents that describe the group as an obstacle to be removed — constitutes genocide. Our charter permits us to speak on matters of public concern, but it requires that any legal term of art we invoke be defensible, and the association has twice before declined to name atrocities. A rival motion would abandon the yes-or-no question for a graded internal vocabulary: three tiers of severity with stated thresholds, on the theory that a body like ours can responsibly mark what is occurring without purporting to establish criminal intent.

Please draft the memo. Recommend one course of action with a date certain for whatever we adopt, deciding between the two motions or explaining how they should be combined. It should be explicit about how far our finding may rest on inference from a pattern of conduct rather than from an avowed plan, and about whether the category reaches the destruction of a group's culture and institutions or stays confined to the Convention's enumerated acts and protected groups. Give the losing side its strongest hearing — the objection that a scholarly finding of genocide cheapens a category built for courts and summons obligations we cannot discharge, weighed against the claim that withholding the word repeats the failure it was coined to prevent.
