For roughly a year a reading group I sit in on has been going around the same argument about interpersonal utility comparisons, and I would like a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than another round of it. The positions are stable and, as far as I can tell, fully known to both sides. One camp holds that comparisons of welfare across persons are not merely difficult to measure but lack truth-conditions, because each person's utility is fixed only up to a monotone transformation and no fact about the world selects a common scale; on this view the apparent comparisons we make in cost-benefit analysis, health allocation, or any claim that one policy leaves someone worse off than another would have, are either disguised value judgments or disguised appeals to something other than utility. The other camp grants the representation problem and still insists that we cannot do normative economics without such comparisons, that the demand for them is not a lapse of rigour but a condition of saying anything about who is owed what, and that the first camp has mistaken a demand for a particular kind of cardinal unit for a demand for comparability as such.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not a conclusion: the two sides may not be disagreeing about a fact or a formalism at all. It may be that the word comparability is being predicated of different things — of the existence of a shared measuring rod on one reading, of the meaningfulness of a comparative welfare claim on another — so that each side's central argument is sound against the other's target and neither touches the other's claim. An alternative diagnosis, which I would want weighed against the first rather than folded into it, is that one side is making a claim about measurement (a formal and epistemic matter) while the other is making a claim about the admissibility of value judgments within positive science (a methodological and normative matter), and that these never meet because they are not answers to the same question.

I would like you to do four things. First, diagnose the structure of the disagreement, engaging what each side actually claims rather than the labels they wear, and say plainly whether it is verbal, substantive, or two questions under one heading. Second, assess whether the standard taxonomy that crosses measurability (ordinal versus cardinal) with comparability (none, level, unit, full) dissolves the impasse or merely relocates it — in particular, whether the practical comparisons the second camp needs might require only ordinal level comparability, in which case the first camp's objection may be aimed at something narrower than it supposes. Third, identify whether a hidden formal result or empirical question is doing the real work, and, if so, what it is. Fourth, and given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a theorem, a change of question, or an argument that none of these should be expected, and why.
