I'm a first-year PhD student and I've somehow ended up running the reading group for the causation unit, which has turned into a slow-motion trainwreck. It started with the dumbest possible example: my flatmate's basil plant died while he was away and I was supposed to water it, he says I killed it, I say the plant died of drought and I didn't do anything. Two months later four of us are still going at it every Thursday and nobody has moved an inch. One camp keeps insisting that a cause has to be a positive event or state that makes a difference to the outcome -- absences aren't in the world, so "I didn't water it" can't be the cause, at most it's something we mention when we're handing out blame. The other camp keeps insisting that's just a prejudice in favour of the visual: delete the omission and the plant lives, so of course it's part of the causal history, and the only reason it feels different is that we're smuggling in a picture of causation as pushing or transferring. Same papers, same moves, endless restatement. My own hypothesis is that it isn't really a disagreement about causation at all but about what the relata are supposed to be -- events vs. facts vs. something contrastive -- and that once you fix that, the dispute either dissolves or shrinks to a narrow technical question. But I might be rationalizing. And here's the thing that nags at me: all our cases are nice and punctate, one moment where someone could have pulled a lever and didn't. I want to know whether the stall survives when the omission isn't a missing act at a time but the absence of a whole pattern -- a regulator that never builds a safety culture over twenty years and a plant eventually explodes. There's no single non-act to point at there, and my hunch is that the two camps start to come apart in ways that show which one is really doing metaphysics and which is really doing explanatory practice.

So: be a philosopher of causation and action who knows this literature well and is willing to give a deflationary answer if that's the honest one. Think it through before committing -- first sketch the candidate diagnoses (a verbal dispute about the word 'cause'; a disagreement about relata; two sides answering different questions, one metaphysical, one about causal selection and blame; a hidden empirical or formal issue), then say which actually fits what each side says. Don't give me a survey of the literature, I've read it. I want the diagnosis and your reasons for it, whether my relata hypothesis is right or just a rationalization, whether the twenty-year-pattern case changes anything, and what would count as progress here -- a distinction, a formal result, a change of question -- or a straight answer that none should be expected and why. 500 words max, and lead with your verdict in the first two sentences.
