I do cost-benefit modelling for a small consultancy that advises city councils, mostly transport stuff. We've got one on the go right now about a congestion charge, and the model is full of assumptions that everyone involved, us included, knows are false: that drivers respond smoothly and rationally to the price, that nobody reroutes, that the revenue gets spent as if it were the council's own money, that kind of thing. My boss brushes it off with the old line about judging a model by its predictions rather than its assumptions -- I think it traces back to Friedman. Fair enough.

But I've started noticing that our false assumptions arent all doing the same job. Some are false in a direction that flatters the scheme (assuming drivers react a lot to the price), and some are false in a direction that damns it (assuming nobody shifts to driving at night once the charge is off). And I can't just net them out, because they're not independent -- the same simplification that makes drivers rational is what makes them react so much in the first place, so the two errors move together rather than cancelling.

So what I actually want from you: please describe the most important implications of this distinction -- between false assumptions that fix the *direction* of a result and false assumptions that only fix its *size* -- for how we should be presenting model output to a council that is going to treat one number as the truth. Does Friedman's idea even bite here, given we can't check predictions against data for a policy that hasn't been tried yet, or does it only do its work where there's an out-of-sample test to appeal to? And what does the distinction imply for how much hedging is honest, and for whether its ever legitimate to deliberately pick the assumption that pushes the model toward the conclusion you privately think is right?
