For most of my working life I was a geologist, and since retiring I have been sitting in on philosophy seminars; I am now preparing a teaching instrument for a mixed group of scientists and philosophers who talk past one another whenever the conversation turns to what the cross-cultural variation in moral judgment shows about whether morality is objective. The familiar standoff between realism and relativism strikes me as far too coarse, and I suspect the real action lies in the steps in between, which nobody in the room seems to have laid out in order. In particular I want the tree to turn on whether the variation is genuine rather than an artifact of translation or of divergent moral vocabularies; whether the parties are disagreeing about the same moral questions or about which questions count as moral ones; whether the divergence lies in people's verdicts on particular cases or in the considerations they will accept as reasons at all; whether the spread is between cultures or within them, since a finding that within-culture disagreement is as wide as between-culture disagreement would alter the shape of the argument entirely; and whether the supposed undermining of moral belief is meant to run through an evolutionary account of where our moral sense comes from or through a merely sociological account of upbringing.

Please turn this into a decision tree, with every branch node phrased as a plain question that a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and every leaf a named position. I need at least the following leaves to appear: a robust realism that accepts the variation and treats large swathes of other cultures' moral thought as error; metaethical relativism; the view that the variation is real but shallow because all cultures converge on some thin core; a view that locates the variation in who is admitted to the moral community rather than in the rules governing those admitted; a non-cognitivist or quasi-realist position on which the empirical findings are largely beside the point because moral utterances are not truth-apt to begin with; an evolutionary debunking position; and the deflationary view that objectivity is a matter of what the reasons are and not of what anyone believes, so the variation is evidentially irrelevant. Give each leaf one sentence naming its most awkward commitment.

Three further things would make this genuinely useful to me. First, mark every node as either conceptual or empirical, and wherever an empirical node depends on a contested or simply uncollected finding, say so, because I want to see where the tree is hanging on facts we do not yet have. Second, where a branch is left unoccupied, diagnose the gap — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored — rather than quietly omitting it. Third, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, since I would rather know that the instrument is blunt at a given point than be told it is not. Eight to twelve nodes and roughly eight leaves would suit a single handout, and it should stand on its own for someone who has read none of the literature.
