I'm taking a course on social choice theory and have to write a case study, so I'd like your help analysing the 2011 United Kingdom referendum on the Alternative Vote through the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem. Since you can't look these things up, here are the facts you'll need: the referendum was held on 5 May 2011 and asked whether first-past-the-post should be replaced by AV, a single-winner system in which voters rank the candidates, the lowest-placed candidate is eliminated, and their ballots are transferred, until someone has a majority; the proposal was rejected by 67.9 per cent to 32.1 per cent on a turnout of 42.2 per cent. The Yes side's central claim was that AV would end tactical voting, because a voter could put their genuine first preference first without wasting the vote; the No side argued that AV was complicated and could hand victory to the least popular candidate. As I understand it, the theorem says that any ordinal voting rule with at least three possible outcomes that is not dictatorial is manipulable — Dummett and Farquharson conjectured it, and Gibbard and Satterthwaite proved it in the 1970s — and it is usually glossed as showing that no voting rule escapes strategic voting. What I want is a disciplined two-way analysis rather than that gloss. First, does the theorem really entail that AV could not deliver the reform its supporters promised, and what, if anything, does it say about the first-past-the-post system that was retained? Second, what does the theorem not say — how much manipulation it licenses, which strategies it predicts (mere compromise, or burying a rival, or the specific openings that elimination rules create and plurality rule does not), and whether matters change once we drop the assumption of an unrestricted domain: if real party preferences are roughly single-peaked on a left–right axis, is there a strategy-proof rule available that neither campaign had any reason to mention? Third, the referendum itself was a binary choice, so say what that does to the argument — the theorem's two-outcome escape clause seems to leave the Yes side's central claim untouched by the very result they were invoking. Finally, tell me what the episode shows about the theorem in the other direction: evidence that it has no practical bite for a choice between two real institutions, or that it was mis-sold to the public. Please answer in British English, and stay disciplined: where the theorem is silent, say so rather than stretching it.
