I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about anekāntavāda, and I came away convinced that the standard defense of it is wrong in an interesting way, so I would like you to argue for the position I arrived at rather than simply summarize the doctrine.

Here is the position. The seven-fold predication associated with syādvāda — the series that runs through syād asti, syād nāsti, syād asti-nāsti, syād avaktavya, and the three combinations of the last with the first two — is usually read as a ladder of truth-values: each predication is supposed to say something about how a thing is, and the qualifier syāt is treated as a hedge that softens each claim into a partial truth. I want you to argue that this reading is a mistake, and that the seven predications are better understood as an indexed family of assertions, where the index is a standpoint (naya) rather than a degree of truth. On this reading, syāt does not weaken the copula; it specifies the respect in which the copula holds, so that the same sentence can be true under one standpoint and false under another without either standpoint being privileged. The seven-fold list is then a canonical enumeration of the ways a predicate can be indexed: to a thing's own nature, to what it is not, to both at once, and to the limit at which the two cannot be jointly expressed in a single act of speech.

Then I want you to press the consequence my friend kept resisting. If the predications are indexed by standpoints, then anekāntavāda owes us a criterion for individuating standpoints — for saying when two disputants occupy the same naya and when they occupy different ones. Argue that no such criterion is available from within the doctrine, because any criterion would itself have to be stated from a standpoint and would therefore be indexed too; yet without it, the doctrine cannot distinguish genuine many-sidedness from the trivial observation that different people say different things. So argue that anekāntavāda faces a dilemma: either it concedes a standpoint-neutral meta-standpoint from which nayas can be counted and compared, which contradicts its non-absolutism, or it accepts that standpoint-individuation is itself standpoint-relative, which threatens to make the seven-fold scheme inexpressible as a scheme. Then argue that the second horn is the one a Jain should take, and that taking it does not destroy the doctrine but relocates its point: anekāntavāda becomes not a thesis about truth but a discipline of not mistaking one's own index for the absence of an index.

I want the argument to engage the actual mechanics of the seven predications, not just the slogan about many-sidedness, and to be honest about where the view becomes hard to state without sounding as though it is secretly claiming a view from nowhere. Do not treat the kevalin's omniscience as a deus ex machina that resolves the dilemma; if you mention it, explain why it does not help with the standpoint problem for anyone who is not a kevalin.
