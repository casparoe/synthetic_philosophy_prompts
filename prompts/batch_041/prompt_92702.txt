I recently had to give up a belief I had held with complete confidence for more than a decade — I would have said without hesitation that I knew it — and a single piece of new information dismantled it in an afternoon. Since then I have been unable to decide whether I had been in possession of knowledge all along or merely stubborn, and that is presumably why I have become fixated on the dogmatism paradox: the argument that because knowledge is factive, any evidence that turns up against a proposition one knows must be misleading, so the knower may rationally set it aside. Two people in my reading group have been circling this for weeks and I cannot referee them. Mira argues that if she knows that p, then any future datum E that appears to tell against p is, by her own lights, misleading — p is true and she knows it — so when E arrives she may discount it without irrationality; the point is not that E supports p but that her knowledge of p explains E away, which is simply what knowing is for. Knowing that the butler did it is knowing in advance that the alibi is a fabrication.

Hal's reply is that this may hold for a closed body of evidence but cannot be turned into a policy, because the licence to ignore E has to be available before E is seen, and to have it one would need to know that one knows p — or that one's belief is unrevisable — whereas a fallible knower can know p while being in no position to know that this is one of the cases where she does. Worse, he says, a knower who adopts the policy cannot distinguish her knowledge from mere conviction, since the two feel identical from the inside and the policy instructs her to treat counter-evidence the same way in both cases; that would make the paradox a reductio of one of its premises rather than a discovery about knowledge. He also presses my own case against her: on her account I never knew, only felt certain — yet my evidence was excellent and was defeated by something I could not have anticipated, which is what it looks like when knowledge is defeated rather than exposed as pretence. Mira answers on two fronts: my case cuts the other way, since if I did know, then what changed my mind was misleading evidence, and my present conviction that I was wrong is simply that misleading evidence doing its work; and in any case Hal conflates whether she is justified with whether she can prove to a bystander that she is. She does not need to know that she knows, and she rejects the charge of circularity, insisting that using p to explain away E is exactly what any Bayesian does when she discounts an outlier reading as noise. Hal retorts that the analogy proves too much, since the Bayesian does revise when the outlier is decisive while the paradox's knower is licensed never to revise, and that 'by my own lights E is misleading' is doing illegitimate work — her lights before E, perhaps, but not her lights once she has E. Each has now accused the other of begging the question, and I genuinely cannot tell who has the better of it.

Please adjudicate this move by move rather than declaring a winner. Add a final section, outside the table, giving the single premise on which the whole dispute turns, what each of them is missing, and who is more nearly right overall.
