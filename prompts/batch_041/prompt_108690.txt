I am convening a ninety-minute session next month for a mixed group of first-year doctoral students in philosophy and economics. The philosophers have read the standard post-war analytic literature on weakness of will; the economists arrive with the first-year sequence on intertemporal choice and the quasi-hyperbolic model of present bias; almost nobody has read in the other tradition, and nearly everyone assumes the two literatures are talking about the same thing. The session is on whether they are. Please do not explain akrasia or discounting to me — I know the material and I need a teaching plan, not a summary. What I want first is the order in which the ideas must be introduced, with the dependencies made explicit: what has to be secured before the distinction between acting against one's better judgment and having a better judgment that reverses over time can even be stated without equivocation; whether the discounting model should enter before or after the group has a firm grip on akrasia, and why; where the distinction between the agent who knows her own future bias and the agent who does not belongs in the sequence, given that this is the point at which the two traditions most nearly touch and also where they most sharply part; and at what stage, if at all, the difference between akrasia and mere weakness of will should be drawn, since I suspect half the room is using the two interchangeably and I would rather correct that deliberately than let it dissolve.

Second, the carrying example. I need one case that does the whole job, and I want to know what it must have and what it must lack. My own instinct is to build the session around a case in which nothing goes wrong at the moment of action: a woman who at six in the evening sincerely judges that the best thing is to work until nine and then go to the gym, who at eight sincerely judges that the best thing is to go home, and who, from the sofa at half past nine, judges that the gym was the best thing after all. Nobody in that story acts against her better judgment at the time she acts, and yet something has evidently gone wrong; I want the plan to say precisely what, and to adjudicate whether the correct diagnosis is akrasia, weakness of will, or neither. A colleague insists that we should simply use Ulysses and the sirens and be done with it, and I have a nagging suspicion that this is the wrong example, because he never loses the argument with himself — but I would rather you settle the question than assume my answer. If my sofa case does not survive scrutiny, say so and supply the example that does, with reasons.

Third, the misconceptions, and the checks. I expect the group to collapse weakness of will into temptation or impulsivity; to hear 'hyperbolic discounting' as a normative verdict rather than a descriptive model; and to infer from the universality of present bias that the behaviour it describes is therefore rational, or at least excusable. Predict the ones I have not thought of, and for each tell me not merely that it should be addressed but at which point in the sequence it should be headed off, and why that point rather than another. Then give me a single question I can put to the room at the end that would show whether they have actually got the distinction or merely learned its vocabulary, and tell me what I should refuse to explain at this level — I have my own views about the formal apparatus and about the neuroscience, but I want yours — and how to say so without leaving the group feeling short-changed. Assume no assigned reading and give me no bibliography; the plan has to stand on its own. Throughout, take a definite line: where the literature is genuinely contested, concede it in a sentence and then commit to a choice with your reasons, rather than laying out the options and leaving the selection to me. Think the dependency structure through before you begin writing; open with the ideas in the order you would teach them; then give me the plan in prose, written as someone at home in both the philosophy of action and the economics of intertemporal choice.
