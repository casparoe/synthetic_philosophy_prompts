I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of physics, and I've been asked to bring a position paper to next week's session of our probability-and-cosmology reading group — the organiser wants each of us to defend something rather than summarise the literature, so I've drafted the short essay below. I'd like your comments on it. What I most want to know is whether the central claim survives scrutiny: I argue that the long-running dispute between the self-sampling and self-indication assumptions is not really a dispute about which rule of inference is correct, but about the unit across which credences are calibrated, and that once you see this the two assumptions stop competing and start answering different questions. I suspect this may be a truce that only looks stable from where I'm standing, so I'd also like you to tell me flatly where the essay is mistaken — if I've misdescribed one of the assumptions, attributed to it a consequence it doesn't have, or got a number wrong, say so without softening it. And if there's an obvious objection I haven't anticipated, I would much rather hear it from you now than from the audience on the day.

Here is the draft.

**1. The case.** A machine will flip a fair coin. If it lands heads, the machine creates exactly one person, in a room. If it lands tails, it creates one thousand people, each in an identical room, each with identical memories and identical knowledge of the setup — including knowledge of this very paragraph. You are one of the people in a room. Nothing available to you distinguishes your situation from that of any other person the machine may have made.

**2. Two rules, two answers.** The self-sampling assumption tells you to reason as though you were a randomly selected member of the relevant set of observers. Since your being in a room is guaranteed on either outcome, learning that you are in a room tells you nothing, and your credence that the coin landed heads stays at one half. The self-indication assumption tells you to weight a hypothesis by how many observers it contains, on the grounds that a hypothesis generating more observers supplies more places for you to have turned out to be. Tails supplies a thousand times as many places, so your credence in heads falls to about one in a thousand and one.

**3. What the disagreement is usually taken to be.** Standardly this is treated as a disagreement about which rule is correct: one of the two must be wrong, since they cannot both be right about the same question. The self-indication assumption is defended by the observation that it is the rule that lets you win money, and attacked by the observation that it allows a single person's indexical evidence to swamp a mountain of ordinary empirical evidence — in the cosmological version, a theory that posits vastly more observers is favoured at absurd odds on no physical grounds at all. The self-sampling assumption is defended by its modesty in that cosmological case, and attacked for licensing the conclusion that we are probably near the end of human history, and for needing a reference class it cannot supply from within.

**4. My claim.** I want to suggest that both sides are right, and that the appearance of contradiction comes from asking the two rules to be calibrated across different populations. Take the credence of one half in heads and score it two ways. Score per observer: consider every person the machine ever creates, ask whether the coin landed heads in that person's world, and penalise the credence accordingly. A credence of one half is then badly miscalibrated, because only about one person in a thousand and one is in a heads-world, and the rule that says one half takes a large penalty on each of the thousand people who were told tails. The self-indication answer, one in a thousand and one, is almost perfectly calibrated under this scoring. Now score per world instead: run the experiment many times and score the credence against the actual coin each time. The self-indication answer is then catastrophic, and one half is perfect. So the self-indication assumption is the calibrated rule for the population of observers, the self-sampling assumption is the calibrated rule for the population of worlds, and the real question is which population credences are answerable to.

**5. Consequences.** If this is right, the argument that the self-indication assumption wins because it wins bets is not an argument that it gives the correct credence; it is an argument that our practical interests are indexed to observers rather than to worlds, which is why a bet placed separately by each of a thousand people is a thousand bets and why the aggregate comes out that way. Likewise, the argument that the self-sampling assumption is right because it refuses to let indexical evidence swamp cosmology is really the claim that our scientific interests are indexed to worlds. This also suggests a repair for the worst defect of the self-sampling assumption, namely that it is undefined until a reference class is fixed: if credences are scored over observers, the reference class simply is the set of observers over whose membership you are prepared to be scored. I think that is circular, but I also think it is the same circularity that afflicts any calibration-based account of credence, and so not a special defect of the self-sampling assumption.

**6. The worry I cannot dispel.** I can hear the objection: credence is credence, and I cannot hold one half and one in a thousand and one at the same time. My reply is that I do not hold them at once; I hold one of them, and which one I hold depends on what the credence is for. I am not satisfied with this reply.

Please give me your assessment in no more than 800 words. Open with a two- or three-sentence verdict on whether the central claim of section 4 survives, then go point by point through the sections, labelling each of your remarks as correct, mistaken, or in need of qualification, and finish by naming the single strongest objection to the essay — including, if you think the truce in section 4 collapses, exactly where it collapses.
