I host a small podcast on applied epistemology, and next month's guest is a philosopher who defends a deliberately awkward position: that the standard remedy for echo chambers — flooding them with opposing information — is not merely ineffective but often counterproductive, and that what needs repairing is not what people are exposed to or believe but whom they trust to interpret evidence. She also argues that the familiar contrast between epistemic bubbles (a deficit of exposure) and echo chambers (a deficit of trust in outsiders) collapses in practice, because any community that has a shared method for disqualifying outsiders ends up with both deficits at once. Draft eight to ten interview questions that press her on the substance rather than let her restate the thesis. I want at least one question each on (a) the mechanism by which exposure is supposed to backfire, and whether her account can stand without the correction-backfire findings that have proved hard to replicate; (b) how she distinguishes a healthy trust structure from a manipulated one without appealing to whether the beliefs it produces are true, which looks circular; and (c) what is left of the bubble/chamber distinction if the two really converge. For each of those three, give the reply she is most likely to reach for and the follow-up that should come after it. Close with a question about what evidence would count against her view and one about what a teacher or a platform moderator should do differently on Monday. Begin with the questions themselves, with no preface. After them, add a brief note on how confident you are that your follow-ups match the answers she would actually give, and name the single question you are least sure lands.
