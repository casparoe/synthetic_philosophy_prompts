I have just been appointed to a municipal Automated Systems Accountability Panel. The panel does not award damages or decide legal liability; it issues public findings on who, if anyone, is blameworthy when an autonomous or semi-autonomous system causes harm. It has a standing rule that it never finds a system itself blameworthy, only persons or institutions. Its docket so far is as follows.

Case 1. A highway car with conditional automation and a driver-monitoring system. The driver had disabled the monitoring with a weighted object and was watching a film. The car failed to brake for a stopped truck. The panel found the driver primarily blameworthy and the manufacturer secondarily blameworthy for a monitoring design that could be defeated so easily.

Case 2. A geofenced campus shuttle with no safety attendant. It failed to see a pedestrian because its sensor fusion treated a reflective jacket as background. The panel found the manufacturer solely blameworthy, noting that the campus had no reason to anticipate the defect and that the vehicle was within its approved operating domain.

Case 3. A fleet of sidewalk delivery robots supervised by one remote operator per ten robots. An operator missed a low-battery alert while attending to another robot; a robot stopped on a curb ramp and a pedestrian tripped over it. The panel found the operating company blameworthy for understaffing and expressly declined to blame the remote operator.

Case 4. A farm robot whose owner had disabled a speed limiter to finish harvest before a storm. The robot struck a worker. The panel found the owner blameworthy and the manufacturer not blameworthy.

Case 5. A city bus with an automated braking system. The city had failed to trim a tree that obscured a sensor. The bus failed to brake for a child. The panel found the city blameworthy and the manufacturer not blameworthy, because the system performed as specified in the conditions it was told to expect.

Case 6. An autonomous drone used by a fire department to survey a wildfire. It flew outside its approved altitude and collided with a helicopter. The panel found the fire department blameworthy for the flight plan and the software vendor not blameworthy, even though the vendor's geofencing module had a known bug that the fire department had not been told about. One member dissented, arguing that the vendor's nondisclosure made it blameworthy.

The new case is this. A public hospital deploys a vendor-built autonomous sepsis detection and antibiotic ordering system. The system assigns a low priority to a patient with atypical sepsis because its training data underrepresented the patient's autoimmune condition. The hospital protocol makes the system's score the primary trigger for escalation. A nurse sees the patient deteriorating but does not override the score because the protocol treats an override as an exceptional event requiring attending physician approval, and the attending is covering forty patients and does not examine the patient until after the delay. The patient dies. The vendor contract says the hospital is responsible for clinical oversight. The hospital protocol says the vendor is responsible for algorithmic validity. The regulator approved the system, and the underrepresentation was not disclosed in the labeling.

I need you to predict the panel's finding. First, identify the candidate rules that fit the prior docket, and say what each predicts for the new case. Second, give your best guess about which rule the panel is actually running and why. Third, identify which prior case is the closest analog and which further case would best tell the competing rules apart. Fourth, if the docket genuinely underdetermines the new case, say what principle the panel would be adopting if it blames the vendor, what principle it would be adopting if it blames the hospital, and what principle it would be adopting if it blames the nurse or the attending physician. I am less interested in the abstract question whether this is a responsibility gap than in what this panel will do with its own precedents.
