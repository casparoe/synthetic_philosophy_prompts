So I had a big stupid argument with my friend last night about Norway's boardroom quota and I still can't say exactly what it is that bugs me about it, or whether it bugs me at all. The thing itself is easy to state: back in 2003 Norway passed a law that boards of public limited companies have to be at least 40% women (and correspondingly not more than 60% of one sex), it took effect around 2006 with a couple years' grace, and the stick behind it is that a company that won't comply can basically be refused registration or dissolved, which is a wild penalty. My friend's position is that it's just discrimination with the sign flipped: fairness means you pick the best person for the seat and ignore what's between their legs, full stop. I kept saying it's more complicated than that but I couldn't actually defend myself, which is annoying.

What I want from you is to work out what that law *presupposes* -- what does the practice only make sense under. I don't have the philosophy vocab for this so bear with me, but here's the stuff I keep bumping into:

- It cares about the outcome (who ends up on the board) and not at all about the process (whether the search was fair). Does that mean it presupposes that a fair process is not enough, i.e. that you can't get there from here by just not discriminating? Or is it compatible with thinking the process *is* what matters and this is just a crude instrument?
- A board's legal job is to look after the company -- it's a fiduciary body, not a parliament. So making it mirror the population seems to assume it's also *representing* somebody. My friend actually made this point and I think it's his best one. Does the law need boards to be representative, or can it just be about the members' own competence and the quota is a fix for biased hiring?
- It's gender and only gender, and it's boards and not the pipeline of executives or the CEO. Why draw the line there? What does that say about the assumed *cause* of women being missing -- past discrimination, present bias in who gets asked, networks, or something about preferences?
- And is a woman appointed under this quota a *representative* of women on the board, or just a person who happens to be a woman who got the seat? Those seem like totally different pictures of what the seat is.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory for me, and then be honest about the seams. Where is the practice *strictly* committed to something, and where could several different theories all license the same law? I suspect you can read it either as a corrective-justice thing (we're fixing a specific historical wrong) or as a diversity-is-good-in-itself thing (balanced boards are just better/more legitimate), and those two pull in really different directions about who should get the seats and whether the quota should ever end. Tell me which one the practice actually needs, if it needs either, and what would have to change in the law if you swapped the presupposed view for the other. And at the end, settle the fight for me -- is my friend right that this is just flipped discrimination, or does the law have an answer to him that doesn't depend on him secretly already agreeing?
