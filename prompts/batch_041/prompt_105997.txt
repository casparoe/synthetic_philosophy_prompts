Ok so I'm a science journalist and I've been doing a bunch of reporting around idealization in modelling — climate models, econ models, the whole business of models being useful even when they're wrong — and I keep running into an argument that a modeller put to me, and I'm fairly sure it proves way too much, though I cant quite put my finger on where it slips. Here it is, tidied up:

(1) The assumptions of a model are idealizations — frictionless planes, point masses, agents with perfect information. Taken literally, they're false of the actual world.
(2) The F-twist line (Friedman) is that this is fine and irrelevant: we judge a theory by how well it predicts, not by whether its assumptions are realistic.
(3) So a theory doesn't have to be committed to its assumptions describing anything real.
(4) If it isn't committed to them describing anything, then trading its assumptions for any others that spit out the same predictions doesn't change what the theory says about the world.
(5) So two models that predict identically are the same model for scientific purposes, no matter how different or daft their assumptions.
(6) That can't be right — a model of the economy built on utility-maximising agents and one built on agents flipping coins are not the same theory even if they happen to fit the same data, and we'd obviously prefer the first.

He says (6) just shows the F-twist is a dead end and that assumptions matter after all. My instinct is that something between (2) and (4) is doing a slide, but I can't name it. Which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost — i.e. if I want to keep the spirit of (2), what am I actually committing myself to? I'd rather keep (2) than (6) if I have to choose, but tell me the bill.

And please seperate for me what's actually settled here from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this stuff — I don't want a confident answer that's really just one camp's line dressed up as the consensus.
