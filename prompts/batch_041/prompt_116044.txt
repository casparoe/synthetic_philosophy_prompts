I've been asked to run the opening "framing" session at a small alignment workshop for graduate students in computer science and philosophy, and I want to begin from a taxonomy a colleague and I sketched rather than from the usual list of doom scenarios. Our cut is five-way: (1) the specification problem — turning a vague human intent into something a system can actually optimise against; (2) the pursuit problem — getting a system that has the right specification to optimise that rather than an accidental proxy; (3) the robustness problem — keeping behaviour aligned as capability, horizon, and deployment scale increase; (4) the verification problem — determining from the outside whether the first three have been achieved; and (5) the political problem — deciding whose intent gets specified, given that there is no single "we" whose preferences could be written down. Please rate each of the five on the share of the total difficulty of "the alignment problem" that it carries — give me a number for each, e.g. 0.3, with the five summing to 1 — and then arrange the five into tiers (three tiers is fine) by how much a decade of ordinary research effort would visibly reduce that sub-problem, as opposed to merely generating literature about it. One sentence per item explaining why it lands where it does.

Then do two things that matter more to me than the prose. First, tell me which, if any, of the five you regard as a relabelling of another rather than a distinct problem; if two collapse into one, merge them and re-normalise the fractions so they still sum to 1. If you think the taxonomy is cut in the wrong place — that the real joints lie elsewhere — then re-cut it yourself and give me fractions and tiers on your own cut instead, saying plainly what you changed and why. Second, pick the single sub-problem you would bet on turning out to be illusory: one that would dissolve rather than be solved once the others are dealt with, and explain what would dissolve it. The audience is mixed, so avoid jargon; no citations needed, and the numbers matter more to me than the style.
