I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of biology, joint with cognitive science (long story), and I've been volun-told to run next month's session of our reading group. The topic is teleology without minds — the idea that there might be genuine reasons in the world even where nothing is doing any reasoning. I know the literature on functions and representation well enough, and so does everyone in the room, so please don't hand me citations, a reading list, or a bibliography; a model that can't check anything would just be guessing at titles and years anyway, and that isn't what I need. What I want is the logical space drawn up on one page: a decision tree that a room full of grad students can walk down, each answering for themselves, and land on a named position about whether there are reasons without reasoners. Start from a commitment I can assume almost everyone shares — something like: natural selection produces traits that look designed even though nothing designed them, and that appearance of design is not simply an illusion — and then branch only on questions that actually split the positions. Think hard about what those questions are before you commit to any of them: is the for-ness of a trait fixed by its selection history or by what it currently contributes to a system; does anything have to represent the goal for the rationale to be about something, or can the aboutness ride on the selection process itself; does the position allow a trait to malfunction, or is it all just statistics about typical performance; and is the rationale there whether or not any interpreter, actual or possible, could read it off. Phrase every node as a question someone can answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to (no are-you-a-Millikan-style-etiological-functionalist nodes), and put the positions at the leaves, including at least these: the view that the rationale is real but genuinely free-floating; the view that functions are constituted by selection history; the view that function is just a causal role in a system; the as-if or instrumentalist view that there's no fact of the matter; the eliminativist view that the whole vocabulary should go; and the neo-Aristotelian view that life-form teleology is real and not grounded in selection at all. If some leaf needs a name and doesn't have one, say so and coin one.

Test the tree against the cases I think pull the positions apart, and tell me if any of them don't: a slime mould that leaves food behind in a pattern that amounts to solving a maze; a bacterium running and tumbling up a sugar gradient; a river that takes the shortest route to the sea (this one's my favourite, I suspect it breaks several views at once); a feather selected for warmth that gets co-opted for flight, where I'm never sure whether the rationale is the old one, the new one, or both; a molecule that by sheer chance folds into a shape that happens to catalyse something, with no history and no interpreter; an immune system that seems to identify a pathogen; and a chatbot fine-tuned on human approval that behaves as if it wants to be liked, where the gradient descent is doing something that looks a lot like reasoning. For each leaf give the position's name (or "unnamed"), one sentence on the most awkward commitment it has to swallow, and which of my cases it handles worst. Also flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually seperate — I'd rather see these two are empirically identical, they differ only in what grounds the normativity written down than have the tree pretend to distinguish them — and mark any combination of answers that nobody occupies, with a one-line diagnosis of why (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored). Keep it to roughly eight to ten leaves and no more than about six branch nodes; if it can't be done that small without collapsing real distinctions, tell me that instead of quietly trimming.

Last thing: I'd much rather you push back than be polite. If the way I've set this up is confused — if, say, the free-floating option isn't a stable location in the space but just a way station between two others, or if some of the positions I listed are really the same view under different descriptions — say so plainly and tell me what you'd do instead. Where you're confident about a branch, say so; where you're stipulating or guessing at what a view has to say about one of my cases, mark it as such. Before you draw anything, spend some space brainstorming the candidate distinctions and the candidate positions, and only then lay out the tree — I want to see the working, not just the chart. And sorry in advance: I know the case list is a bit of a grab-bag, and I've probably run together two things I shouldn't have, so feel free to reorder or swap cases if a different set carves the space better.
