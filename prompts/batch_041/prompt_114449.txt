my best friend is getting married next month, to her girlfriend of nine years, and i'm giving the toast. she asked me for one thing specifically: don't do the love-is-love bit, and don't do the bit where the ceremony gets treated as a formality laid on top of a relationship that was already basically a marriage. she said she wants someone to say something true about what marriage is actually for. i said sure, easy.

now i'm stuck, because a real answer has to survive the actual room. her grandmother believes, sincerely and with no malice at all, that marriage is fundamentally for having and raising kids, and thinks this wedding is lovely but not quite the thing itself. two of their closest friends think marriage is a reactionary institution and that queer people campaigning to get into it is a bit embarrassing, and they're all sitting at the same table. and one of their oldest friends asked me a question last week that i can't shake: whats the difference between what they're doing on saturday and two best friends promising to stick together forever? he wasn't being snarky, he genuinely wanted to know what the promise adds.

so: five minutes, warm, ideally funny, no lecture and no campaign speech. it has to take an actual position on what marriage is for — one that doesn't need children to make sense of it, one that doesn't require the couple to be just like anyone else, and one that gives a real answer to the friend's question, or else says honestly what it is that marriage does that even a very good friendship can't. i don't want to hedge my way out of it, but i also can't have the grandmother or the cynics feeling like they're being argued with at a wedding.

last thing: when i asked them why they were doing it, they said they wanted to be bound — that saying it out loud in front of all of us makes a reason for staying that neither of them could make for herself alone, and that they wanted that kind of reason. use that if it helps.
