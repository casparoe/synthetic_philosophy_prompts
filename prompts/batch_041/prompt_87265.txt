For about a year now, a reading group I sit in on has been going around in circles over Newcomb's problem, and I have started to think the circling is the more interesting phenomenon. We all accept the setup: a predictor of high reliability has already placed a sum in the opaque box or not, the placement is finished before you choose, and you may take the opaque box alone or both boxes. The two-boxers say that taking both is dominant, since the contents are already fixed and your choice cannot reach back to change them, so you should take the extra visible sum whatever the opaque box holds. The one-boxers say that taking the opaque box alone is what the reliable predictor has already foreseen, so it is the choice that makes you rich, and any theory of rationality that counsels you to ignore that is a theory that reliably loses. Every argument on both sides is known to everyone in the room by heart, and in a year nobody has shifted an inch.

We have tried to break the deadlock by altering the problem rather than re-arguing it, and I want you to tell me whether the alterations expose the real fault line or merely paper over it. In one variant the predictor is not a black box with a quoted hit rate but a complete simulation of your own deliberation that finishes just before yours does, so that the prediction is, in a sense, another run of the very decision procedure you are about to execute; half of us find that this makes one-boxing transparently correct and half find that it changes nothing, which is precisely the same split we had before, so it may be doing no work at all. My own suspicion, offered as a hypothesis rather than a conclusion, is that the two camps are not answering the same question: the two-boxers are answering which act is rational given the world as it stands with the prediction already settled, while the one-boxers are answering which policy or deliberative procedure is rational given that your deliberation is itself one of the things being predicted. If that is right, then part of the dispute is verbal, turning on whether rational choice attaches to acts or to policies; but I doubt it is only verbal, because the sides also seem to disagree about whether the counterfactual in which you take both boxes is one in which the prediction would have differed, and that looks like a genuine disagreement about causation rather than about words.

So please diagnose the impasse rather than adjudicate the puzzle: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, a disagreement about the metaphysics of counterfactuals, a disagreement about whether a predictor of the relevant kind is even possible, or two different questions sharing one name? I would like a committed verdict, with reasons drawn from what each side actually says, rather than a neutral inventory of the options, and I would like you to say what would count as progress given your diagnosis, whether that is a distinction, a formal result, or a change of question. Write your answer in British English.
