I spent most of last night in a long argument with a friend about why fiction moves us, and I would like you to take on the role of a philosopher of art and mind — someone thoroughly at home in the literature on the so-called paradox of fiction — and help me work out what really separates our two positions. Her view is that there is no genuine puzzle here: being swept up by a novel or a film is just like screaming on a roller coaster or flinching in a haunted house. You know perfectly well that you are safe, the whole thing is engineered for thrills, and yet you scream; so, she concludes, of course we can be moved by events we know never happened, and to call that a paradox is a confusion. I suspect the analogy is carrying more weight than it can bear, and may be smuggling in the very thing that was supposed to be explained. Please think this through carefully, and begin by brainstorming the candidate correspondences before you commit to any of them. Then lay the mapping out explicitly — what plays the role of the ride, the danger, the knowledge that one is safe, and the fear — and tell me where it breaks down: which features of the roller-coaster case have no counterpart in our engagement with fiction, and which of those gaps actually matter for her conclusion rather than being idle disanalogies. I am especially interested in whether the fear on the ride is directed at the same kind of object as our sadness or fear for a fictional character, and whether belief plays the same role in each. End by saying plainly whether her "no paradox" conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and, if it does not, what the residual puzzle really is.
