I'm a doctoral student in formal epistemology, and for the department's winter review I've been asked to contribute something performable rather than another slide deck. I want a poem for two voices about Aumann's agreement theorem, and I want the form to carry the argument, because the misreading I keep running into is a claim about conversation — that rational agents who learn each other's credences are somehow compelled to converge, and that whoever still dissents is simply being irrational. What I'd like is a piece that can be performed as a round in the musical sense. Please write it as a single sequence of lines that both voices utter, with the second voice entering one line behind the first, so that at every moment one voice is saying what the other said a moment ago, and with the two of them deliberately ending together on the final line — the leader holding back a beat so that the follower arrives on it too — so that the poem closes in unison rather than in sequence. Since every adjacent pair of lines will be heard simultaneously as well as successively, each line has to be written to bear being spoken over the line before it. Neither voice is ever given words of its own: on the theorem's own account, whatever separated them was never in what they were saying.

What I want the form to make audible is the difference between the mutual knowledge the two singers begin with — each has heard the other's estimate, and that is all — and the common knowledge the theorem actually requires, in which the posteriors themselves are common knowledge, so that each knows that the other knows that the first knows, without end. The poem should let a listener hear why that nesting is not a rhetorical escalation but a fixed point, and why what closes the round is the shared prior rather than anyone's persuasiveness: the disagreement dissolves rather than being won, and the theorem says nothing so strong as that argument is irresistible. Then append a short coda, marked as an alternative ending, in which the round cannot close at all: give one voice a single line the other cannot echo, and stop the piece there, so that a reader can see without a gloss what the common prior is doing and how little the theorem has left to say when it fails. Three or four minutes of reading time; accuracy matters more than prettiness, and I would much rather a referee who works on this nodded than winced.
