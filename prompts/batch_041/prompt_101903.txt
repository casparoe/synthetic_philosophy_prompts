So a bit of background on me: I spent thirty-odd years as a fisheries biologist, mostly stock assessment work, retired four years ago, and since then I've been reading philosophy pretty much full time. My wife says I've gone from counting fish to counting arguments. Anyway I keep running into this one idea, Goodhart's law, and I've gotten a bit obsessed, becuase I watched it happen in my own field twice and never had a name for it at the time.

Here's the thing I want from you. I've come to think the usual one-liner version ("when a measure becomes a target it ceases to be a good measure") is too blunt, and I've ended up with my own little taxonomy of ways it goes wrong. Roughly four:

1. The measure is noisy, so the top scorers scored high partly by luck. Push on the measure and you're partly pushing on the luck, which doesn't persist.
2. The measure tracks the thing nicely in the range where it was calibrated, but the relationship bends once you start optimizing hard, and you sail past the bend.
3. The measure is a downstream effect of the real thing, so you can crank the effect without the cause.
4. People just lie, or find the loophole, or redefine the metric's terms until it means something else.

What I want is a short story (say 1500-2500 words) that makes a reader feel at least two of these, distinctly, without ever naming the phenomenon, without any character explaining it, and without the words "Goodhart," "Campbell," or "metric." It should be a story, not a parable with a moral stapled to the end.

A few things I'd like it to have. The setting should be some institution where people genuinely mean well — a regional water authority, a school district, a wildlife agency, a hospital trust, whatever you like. Invent the actual measure; don't use a real published index, and don't use anything that requires the reader to know a literature. The story should be told by someone inside who believes in the measure and is, at most, half-aware of what's happening, so the reader sees past the narrator. I want a stretch near the beginning where the measure is working, really working, and the reader gets lulled. Then two of the four failures above should show up in the events, one of them the sort you could only see in hindsight, and the reader should be able to tell them apart afterwards without either being announced.

And I'd like the last couple of paragraphs to leave the narrator still not quite getting it — defending the measure in a way that quietly gives the game away to the reader. If you can work a third failure in as background noise, great, but don't force it.

One more thing: no epigraphs, no footnotes, no afterword where you tell me what the story was about. Just the story.
