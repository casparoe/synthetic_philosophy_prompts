Ok so I'm a novelist, which means I spend my life inventing people who do things, and I've got an argument stuck in my head that I can't get out — half of it is about a friend, half of it is about the book I'm 200 pages into, and I want a philosopher's answer rather than a shrug. So put on your philosopher hat and think carefully before you answer: start by telling me what the disagreement actually is underneath the surface one, then what you'd say.

The friend is Dermot. Session drummer, brilliant, plays like he's inventing it as he goes, and he'll tell you at length that he never plays the same thing twice. Except I've watched him alot now and he does. Bass player drops a beat — same little lift, every time. Singer comes in early — same fill. And once at a wedding the sound guy killed the whole band for eight bars by accident, and Dermot just kept playing, exactly what he'd have played if he could hear everyone, as if the silence was the song. Didn't miss a note. Also didn't notice anything was wrong for about ten seconds. Afterwards he said "I felt it, I just didn't have anywhere else to go."

Which is a great line and its been eating me. Because what I think he described is: he's got a big tree of responses with a lot of branches, but push him off the tree and he doesn't grow a new one, he just keeps producing the branch he was on. I said this to him and he said everyone's like that, and he has a name for it — the forty-move problem. He reckons every person alive, me included, has about forty moves and just cycles them, and my imagination is the same thing as his, only my moves are sentences.

So the argument we keep having: I say but you can notice. You just noticed — I told you what happened, you went red, you'll play differently next week. He says noticing is just another move, and embarrassment is a move, and changing is a move, and you can get caught in a loop at that level too; and if you can't, prove it. And I dont have an answer. I only have the feeling that there's a difference between him and a wind-up toy, and I can't say where it lives.

What I want from you. First, tell me what this argument actually is — I'm fairly sure it's an old one and I want to know what's really at stake and what the standard moves are. Second, is Dermot right that any answer I give just gets eaten? Is there a version of "he can step back from his own routine" that doesn't collapse into infinite regress, or into just one more rule? And is there any test at all, or does "he's just looping" only ever describe him from the outside — so that anyone I stare at long enough, including me, starts to look like a machine going round? Third, the novel problem: I've got a character who's meant to read as free, and lately every scene I write for her looks like a mechanism working through its branches. What should I actually be putting on the page — if anything — that separates a person who's genuinely open from one who just has a bigger repertoire? Don't send me to a reading list, I can't get to a library this month and I want your thinking, not homework. And please no "it's a mystery in the end."
