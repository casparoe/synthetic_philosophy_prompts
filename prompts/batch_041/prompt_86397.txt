I am trying to work out what I actually believe about the identity of indiscernibles by attending to my verdicts on particular cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them. I will set out the verdicts, then a principle I have been tempted by, then two cases I cannot decide.

First, some vocabulary, because part of my difficulty is that "discernible" is doing several jobs at once. Two things are absolutely discernible if some property that each has on its own separates them. They are relationally discernible if some relation to other things separates them: each is a mile from a sphere, but only one is a mile from a cube. A friend has told me there is a third notion, which she called weak discernibility: a pair is weakly discernible when some irreflexive relation holds between them, so that each bears it to the other and to nothing else — being at opposite ends of a diameter, or having opposite spin. I do not know whether weak discernibility should count as a difference, and I suspect the whole question turns on it.

My verdicts:

1. Max Black's two iron spheres, at a distance from each other, in an otherwise empty universe: genuinely two spheres. Not one sphere in two places, not a single object counted twice.

2. The swap: a world exactly like that one but with the two spheres exchanged is not a second world. There is no further fact about which sphere is which.

3. "Being identical with sphere A" would discern the two, and so would "being the sphere nearer the origin of the coordinate grid I have just drawn." Both are cheats, and I do not count them.

4. Add a pebble, off centre, so that one sphere is nearer it than the other. I count that as a real relational difference. But I notice that in a furnished universe almost any two things will differ in some relational property of this kind, which makes the relational version of the principle nearly vacuous.

5. Two spheres intrinsically identical but with different histories — one cast from a particular ingot that was then destroyed, the other copied from it — strike me as discernible; and then it strikes me that this is the same cheat as (3), since the only difference is which one the history attaches to.

6. An infinite lattice: identical spheres, evenly spaced, forever, no boundary, nothing else. I want to say there are infinitely many. But every sphere has exactly the same relational profile as every other, and the world looks the same from each; I cannot see what makes the count infinite rather than one sphere repeated. Here the swap thought from (2) becomes dizzying.

7. Two electrons sharing a spatial state and differing in spin: two particles, and what makes them two is not any property of either alone but the irreflexive spin relation — so weak discernibility suffices for me here. Two photons in exactly the same mode: I want to say two, and I cannot name anything at all that makes them two.

The tension I cannot dissolve: (1), (6) and (7) push me to say that distinctness requires no discernibility whatever, so that two things can be distinct while sharing every property, weak relations included; (2) pushes me to say there is no fact about which is which, so nothing in the world changes when they are exchanged. I want both. My tentative principle is therefore this: the identity of indiscernibles is false in every version, but anti-haecceitism is true; the distinctness of objects is not grounded in any difference between them, and yet there are no worlds differing only by a permutation of indiscernible objects.

Is that coherent, or does it collapse — on one side into "there is only one sphere," on the other into haecceitism? If it can be held, state it as precisely as you can, clause by clause, giving for each clause the case that forces it. Then say what the resulting principle implies for two cases I have not decided. The first is the strongest version of Black's case: two identical spheres in a finite closed space with no boundary and no distinguished location, so that no absolute position or distance from a centre is available to either. The second moves the problem from objects to times: an eternal, perfectly periodic universe, with no first moment and no boundary, in which the entire configuration recurs exactly — is the recurring configuration a second occurrence or the same one? Finally, tell me honestly which of my seven verdicts the principle cannot keep.
