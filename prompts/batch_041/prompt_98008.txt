Two of my friends got into an argument about a case, and neither would give ground, so I would like you to sort out who is right about what. The case: a reinforcement-learning system plays a complex video game at a level no human reaches, and no one can extract from it any rule, recipe, or proposition that it is representing; it simply outputs actions. Dana said this is a clean case of knowing how without knowing that: the system can do the thing and cannot be credited with knowing any fact about how to do it, so the distinction between practical skill and propositional knowledge is real and irreducible. Marcus said it shows nothing of the sort: if the system reliably gets the result, then something in it must carry information that, if articulated, would be a rule, so its knowledge is propositional knowledge held in a practical rather than a theoretical way, and the old regress argument — that if knowing how were knowing a rule, applying the rule would itself require knowing how, and so on — only bites if you insist the rule be consciously recited. Dana answered that "if articulated" is doing all the work: any successful behaviour can be described by an observer after the fact, but the observer's description is not the agent's knowledge, and by that standard a thermostat knows that the room is cold. Marcus replied that the thermostat is beside the point, because the difference is that the system's state is usable in action, and a state that guides action correctly just is knowledge of a proposition. Dana said that move redefines knowledge so that nothing could ever fail to have it, which makes the dispute verbal. Please adjudicate point by point: which moves are right, which are mistaken, where the disagreement is substantive rather than merely about words, what both of them are missing, and who has the better of it overall.
