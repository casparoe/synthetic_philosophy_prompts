I'm a novelist, and my new book is a three-generation family thing about a family whose money comes from a massacre their great-grandfather's militia carried out. Next month I've been asked to give a talk at a university seminar about the thinking behind it, which was a mistake on my part, because now I have to actually defend a position rather than just read a chapter.

The position, roughly: Jaspers' four-way split of guilt (criminal — you broke a law and a court can try you; political — the deeds of the state, borne by all citizens, dealt with through liability and atonement rather than through a court; moral — your own acts, judged by your own conscience; metaphysical — the guilt of being alive when others were killed, which no earthly tribunal can touch) is usually read as a way of *denying* collective guilt. I think it gives you a way of saying yes and no at once. Moral guilt is agent-indexed, so it dies with the agent, and my characters cannot be morally guilty of a massacre they didn't do. But political guilt isn't agent-indexed in the same way: it attaches to being part of a polity that still exists and hasn't repudiated the deed, and I want to say it can be inherited the way a debt is inherited — as liability, not as guilt. And then there's a third thing the novel is really about, which isn't guilt at all: inherited shame, the response you have to what your lot did because you're in solidarity with them, which wants concealment rather than repair.

What I need from you is the Q&A prep. The room is roughly: four or five historians of modern Europe, who are allergic to importing a German post-war framework into a non-European case and will go after the analogy rather than the ethics; one legal philosopher who thinks collective liability is a category error and will want to know who exactly the obligor is; one postcolonial theorist who'll say the whole guilt frame is a bourgeois substitute for material reparations and that shame is the most self-indulgent version of that substitution; and two literary critics who'll ask whether a novel can render inherited guilt at all without turning the victims into scenery for the inheritor's moral education.

Give it to me as a table, ranked by how much damage each objection would do if I fumbled it (worst first), with these columns: rank; who raises it; the objection in their words and their vocabulary; what it actually hits (my reading of Jaspers, my extension of him to descendants, or my use of shame); how likely it is (low/medium/high); my two-minute reply; and where the reply is soft — the bit I should concede instead of defending. Then below the table, one short paragraph under the heading The One I'm Dreading, which is the question I'm hoping nobody in the room thinks to ask.

No citations or reading lists please, they're no use to me here, just the arguements. And please don't let the historians' objection be the generic one about not being able to inherit guilt — I know that one already. I need the seperate version they'd actually put to a novelist who's borrowing Jaspers.
