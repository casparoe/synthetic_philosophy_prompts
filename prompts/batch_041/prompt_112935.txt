I am a PhD student in ethics, and I hold a position whose exposure I want mapped. Moral uncertainty is not a distinctive practical problem: when I am unsure which moral theory is correct, I should assign credences across the candidate theories, treat the values they assign as measuring one common quantity, and act so as to maximize expected choiceworthiness, just as I maximize expected value under empirical uncertainty — the decision-theoretic machinery transfers without loss. Tell me what counts against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with the internal objections kept apart from the external ones. Internal: that the common scale is definable only up to an arbitrary choice of unit and zero point, so the recommendation rests on a convention that no theory supplies; that the set of theories over which credence is spread is individuated by our accidental taxonomy rather than by anything in the subject matter; that a minuscule credence in a demanding theory swamps everything else; that the position, applied to itself, licenses its own partial abandonment. External: that it presupposes moral claims are the kind of thing one can hold credences in at all, or that a hedged course of action is one that every candidate theory rejects. Say which of these its defenders would themselves concede and which they would dismiss as a misunderstanding; whether the analogy to unknown rather than merely uncertain probabilities — ambiguity rather than risk — has real force here or is a confusion; and whether any version survives without collapsing into the trivial claim that one should do what one thinks most likely to be right, and if the collapse is inevitable, whether that is a defect or a sign the position was never distinctive. I am most persuaded by the scale-indeterminacy problem, so weight it accordingly.
