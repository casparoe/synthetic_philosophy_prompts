Friend A: dreams are experiences, obviously -- when you dream you're being chased, you really are afraid, you really do see the thing chasing you; the fact that none of it is real doesn't matter, because a hallucination is still an experience and nobody goes around saying hallucinations aren't experiences. Friend B: that's exactly the confusion, dreaming isn't an experience at all -- when you wake up and say "I dreamt I was being chased," you're not reporting something you went through, you're telling a story, and the story gets assembled in the seconds after you wake. The tell, she said, is that dream reports are wildly unreliable and shift depending on when you're woken, so there's no settled fact about what you "experienced." A: that can't be right, because lucid dreamers know they're dreaming and can deliberately look around, even do arithmetic mid-dream, and in sleep labs people woken out of REM report stuff that seems to be happening right then rather than invented afterward. B: lucid dreams are still only ever described after the fact, so they prove nothing, and even if some scrap of experience happens, that doesn't mean the whole elaborate chase scene you report is that scrap -- you might get a two-second flash and your waking brain spins a movie around it. A: but then you're committed to saying every dream report is a confabulation, which is a massive claim, and worse you're saying dreaming isn't even a mental state, which is absurd, it's not like you're unconscious the whole night. B: fine, call it a disposition to confabulate stories about the night, that's still a mental thing. Then she threw in this one: suppose we could scan your brain and show that the chase-narrative only shows up in the activity right after you're woken, and during the REM itself it's just noise -- would A still say he'd "experienced" the chase? A said yes, definately, because he remembers it and it felt like it was happening, and memory is our only access to any of it anyway. B said that's circular.
