I have been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for well over a decade, long enough to have absorbed the functionalist picture of mind — the view that a mind is a matter of organization rather than of stuff, so that if the functional relations are right, the thinking is there, whatever the substrate happens to be made of — without ever having had to defend it in an argument where anything was at stake. Last month, at a meetup, a friend who knows I hold this view put the following to me, and I have not been able to find the loose thread since. He took the Einstein's-brain thought experiment I first met in The Mind's I and ran it out into this.

(1) Einstein's brain contained finitely many neurons, each in one of finitely many relevant states, and the next state of the whole was fixed by its present state together with its inputs. So it was a finite-state machine — an unthinkably large one, but a finite one.
(2) Any finite-state machine is completely specified by its transition table: the list that says, for each state and each input, which state comes next.
(3) A transition table is just a finite pattern of marks. So there is a book, call it B, whose pages, read in the right order, are exactly the transition table of Einstein's brain.
(4) Anything that matches that table, and is given the corresponding inputs, will produce exactly the outputs Einstein's brain produced: the same words, in the same order, in answer to the same questions.
(5) And matching outputs is all any of us ever has in the case of another person. Nobody has been inside anyone else's head; the evidence that my colleague is conscious is entirely that she says and does the right things.
(6) So B has as good a claim to be conscious as my friend does, and, when the input is a question, as good a claim to be answering it.
(7) In fact B need not even be written down. The transition table is a mathematical object, and mathematical objects do not wait to be inscribed. If the table is the thing that matters, then nothing about Einstein's mentality ever depended on Einstein's brain.

I would most like to keep (5), and I am prepared to keep (1) through (3) as well, if I must. It is (6) I cannot accept, and (7) strikes me as an absurdity the argument has no right to reach. I keep feeling that the trouble lies upstream of (6), where I am not looking, but I cannot say where. So I would like a diagnosis at the level of the steps: which one is the mistake, why did it look innocent, and what does the repair cost? I have already been told that this is only the Chinese room in a new coat, and that the book no more understands than the room does; that may be so, but it does not tell me which of my seven lines to cross out, and I want the accounting done on the lines. One constraint on any repair: I am not willing to save (5) by making mind depend on carbon. If your fix would equally demote a silicon machine that passed every behavioural test we could devise, or would make the difference between the minded and the mindless a matter of brute chemistry, then say so up front and tell me what that concession costs elsewhere, because I would rather see the price than take the discount.
