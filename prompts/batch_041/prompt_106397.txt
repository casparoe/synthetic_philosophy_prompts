I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, and I've somehow been drafted into writing the take-home exam for a grad seminar I'm guest-teaching on social choice (the regular instructor is on leave and owes me a favour, don't ask). I want exactly one essay question on the discursive dilemma / judgment aggregation, pitched so it can't be knocked off by someone who's memorised the three-judge doctrinal paradox and the line that majority voting can go inconsistent once the propositions are logically connected. The students have seen the basics; what I actually want to test is whether they can think with the framework instead of reciting it.

So here's the case I want the question built on, straight out of my own newsroom. Five of us on an editorial board had to settle three things about a story: (P) whether a whistleblower's central claim checked out, (Q) whether running the story would be fair to the company it names, and (R) whether we should run it — where our standing rule was that we run it only if P and Q. We voted on all three and got a majority for P, a majority for Q, and a majority against R, which nobody was happy about. Then a colleague said this is just Condorcet's paradox in a suit; a second said we should have voted only on P and Q and let R follow automatically; a third said we should have voted only on R; and then it got worse, because someone pointed out that the rule "run only if P and Q" was itself something we could have put to a vote, so which propositions count as the premises isn't handed down from on high. I want all of that in the question.

Please write the exam question (something a strong student could do in about 90 minutes), a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme with the specific moves that seperate an A from a B — itemise the marks out of 20, and give me a one-liner for each of the main places students go wrong (e.g. treating this as Arrow's theorem in disguise, or assuming premise-based voting is simply the "more correct" procedure). Don't fuss about citations or a reading list, I only care about the reasoning. One more thing: at the end of the model answer, have it say how confident it is in what it's claiming and flag the single point it's least sure about — I'm nervous about grading a topic I only half know, so I'd rather the standard advertise its own soft spots.
