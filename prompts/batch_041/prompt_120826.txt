Ok so I just spent like three hours arguing with my friend Nadia about whether the people who commit mass atrocities are basically ordinary people dropped into an extreme situation, or whether you need something extra — ideology, a culture that's already primed for it, some particular kind of person — to explain them. I came out of it on the situational side, and I want you to try to knock me off it.

Here's my position, roughly. The ordinary men picture is basically right. Take Reserve Police Battalion 101: middle-aged working-class guys from Hamburg, not SS fanatics, and at Józefów the commander made the offer that anyone who didn't feel up to it could step out, and fewer than a dozen out of roughly five hundred did. Put Milgram and the Stanford prison study behind that and the moral is that the situation — authority, peers, routinization, the slow escalation where by the time it's mass murder you're already in too deep — does most of the explanatory work, while character, ideology and pre-existing hatred do much less than we'd like to beleive. Goldhagen's book is literally called Hitler's Willing Executioners, and his line, that these men were already eliminationist antisemites, feels to me like it's mostly there to make the reader feel better about the distance between them and us.

So: what would count against this? I want the real ledger, not a both-sides shrug. Which findings, cases, cross-case comparisons, reanalyses or conceptual arguments should actually move me, ranked from the ones that would be close to fatal down to the ones that are merely awkward? What do people who hold my view already concede counts against it — not just what its critics say? And have I insulated the thing: if every new factor (prior participation in roundups, careerism, ideological framing, self-selection into the unit in the first place, the fact that the men weren't randomly assigned to anything) just gets absorbed as part of the situation, then what's left that could disconfirm it, and is that a real problem or just what any broad explanatory framework looks like?

Being honest about what would move me most: comparative evidence, where the pressures look the same but participation rates come out very different across units or countries, or a case where the killers clearly weren't just carried along. Also tell me if the stuff I'm leaning on is shakier than I think. I have a vague memory that people went back through the Milgram tapes and the blind-obedience reading is contested, and that the Stanford prison study has been taken apart (guards coached, whole thing closer to a performance), and I think there's an argument that the situation gets defined after the fact by how people behaved, which smells circular. If any of that is wrong, say so.

And please seperate what's actually settled from what's genuinely contested among the historians and psychologists who know this material — don't sell me a contested reading as the consensus. No need for a bibliography, I'm not going to chase footnotes.
