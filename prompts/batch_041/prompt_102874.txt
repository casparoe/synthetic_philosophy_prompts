I'm trying to get my head around Aristotle's account of money in Nicomachean Ethics Book V — the stretch on exchange and money — and I keep going in circles. You're a classicist who's spent years on Aristotle's ethics and on ancient economic thought, so please help me think it through rather than just summarising the usual secondary literature.

Here's the argument as I've reconstructed it in my own words (so please dont treat my phrasing as Aristotle's): exchange is a kind of justice and has to be proportional — a house can't simply equal a pair of shoes, there has to be some ratio between them. But for a ratio to exist the items have to be commensurable in some single measure, and by nature they aren't, since they differ so much in kind. So money comes in as a measure, by convention (nomos) rather than nature. Money also makes exchange possible across time, because it works like a guarantee or pledge that you can get what you need later. But then he also says that what really holds exchange together is need or demand (chreia), and that need is the true measure — yet need itself isn't commensurable by nature, which is why money exists as a conventional stand-in for it.

What I can't untangle: does the pledge idea do any independent work, or is it just money-as-measure restated for the time dimension? And where does all this leave him on whether exchange value is natural or conventional? I keep feeling he wants value to rest on real human need but then admits need has no natural unit, so money ends up constituting the measure rather than representing one — but I'm not sure if that's the right reading or if I'm just imposing modern economics on him.

Give me a committed reading, not a survey: take a side on the natural-vs-conventional question, say what work you think the pledge is doing, and flag where you're least confident. Start by sketching the two or three main ways this gets read, then commit to one.
