I have a tentative principle about when a strike is justified, and I would like it tested against a set of verdicts I hold. The principle turns on a distinction I find myself drawing between a strike's addressee and its target. The addressee is the party the strikers mean to move by withholding their labor; the target is the party who in fact bears the cost. My hypothesis is that a strike is justified exactly when its addressee is the party with whom the strikers have their dispute — normally their own employer — and that it does not matter, in itself, who the target is.

Here is what I think about particular cases.

First, nurses at a hospital strike over staffing ratios. Elective surgeries are postponed and some patients' recoveries are delayed. I think the strike is justified.

Second, workers at a parcel depot refuse to handle parcels originating from a different company whose own employees are on strike over pay. Their employer has done nothing to them; they mean to squeeze the other company by disrupting its shipments, and they are costing their own employer money in the process. I think this is justified.

Third, a union at a manufacturer strikes to demand that the company stop sourcing from a country whose government is committing atrocities; the workers say they will not be made complicit in it. I think this is justified.

Fourth, a union strikes to demand that the employer dismiss a particular worker — one who kept working during the last strike and is now, the union says, poison in the workplace. I think this is not justified, even though the employer could simply refuse the demand.

Fifth, a union strikes to demand that the government raise the national minimum wage. The employer cannot grant the demand; the strike is meant to inconvenience the government through the employer. I think this is not justified.

Sixth, a group of workers walks out without their union's authorization and in breach of a no-strike clause the union itself negotiated, after the grievance procedure failed to address a supervisor's harassment. I think this is justified.

Seventh, workers at a firm on the edge of insolvency strike for a raise; the strike will very likely tip the firm into liquidation and cost everyone their jobs. I think it is still justified.

My addressee principle appears to deliver my verdicts on the first, third, fifth, sixth, and seventh cases. But it seems to give the wrong answer twice, and in opposite directions. In the second case the addressee is not the strikers' own employer, so the principle condemns the sympathy action — yet I think it is justified. In the fourth case the addressee is exactly the employer, so the principle permits the strike — yet I think it is not. So the distinction, as I have stated it, is either too crude to be repaired or in need of a clause I have not found.

I would like you to do three things.

First, say whether the addressee/target distinction survives at all. If it can be repaired, state the repaired principle as precisely as you can, clause by clause, and name for each clause the case that forces it. If it cannot be repaired, say what should replace it.

Second, tell me what the best principle you can state says about four cases I have not listed: picketers physically blocking replacement workers from entering the premises; individual workers crossing a lawful picket line because they cannot afford to lose the pay; a union striking against a proposed merger that would leave its own members' terms untouched but would cost jobs at another plant; and a lockout by the employer, which is the mirror image of a strike and about which I have no verdict at all.

Third, tell me which of my seven verdicts the best principle cannot keep, and whether dropping that verdict costs less than complicating the principle in order to preserve it.

I am especially unsure about the second case set against the fifth: both are strikes whose grievance lies elsewhere, and I want to know what, if anything, distinguishes them besides my sympathy for one cause and not the other.
