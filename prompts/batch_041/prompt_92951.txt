I got into an arguement at a friend's birthday thing last weekend about believing things you don't really have evidence for, and I've been chewing on it since. Context: I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training at all, my only exposure is one ethics elective in college that I mostly slept through. I know there's some old debate about this — one side saying it's always wrong to believe anything without sufficient evidence, the other saying there are cases where you're entitled to just go ahead and believe — but I don't remember who said what and honestly I don't care about the history. I want to know what the right rule is. So here's me going case by case on my gut, and I'd like you to tell me what principle (if any) actually fits these, and which of my verdicts I have to give up to get a principle that isn't a mess.

1. A landlord I know vaguely never gets the wiring checked in his building, tells himself it's old but fine, a fire happens, someone dies. Not fine, and I don't think it matters that he sincerely believed it.
2. A friend of mine has a chronic illness with a rough prognosis. She believes she's going to get better. It keeps her going, she sticks with her treatment, she's doing better than the doctors expected. Fine — more than fine, I'd say it's good, and I'd be annoyed at anyone telling her to be realistic.
3. Same friend, same belief-shape, except now it's her kid and she's decided he doesn't need the treatment the doctors want, because she believes it'll work out. Not fine. And it bugs me that this has the same shape as #2 and the only difference I can point to is who's carrying the risk.
4. A juror who says "I just know he did it." Not fine.
5. Me. I'm behind on a project at work, no real evidence I'll land it, and I believe I will, mainly because believing it is the only way I keep working on it. Feels fine, maybe even necessary — and I notice it looks a lot like #1.
6. Same belief, except I've talked two friends into quitting their jobs to join the project. Now it feels not fine, even though the belief and the evidence are identical to #5.
7. My uncle trusts his wife completely. No evidence she's ever been unfaithful, no reason to suspect it, he never checks anything. I don't just think that's OK — I think demanding proof there would itself be the bad thing. So this is a case where the no-evidence belief feels not just allowed but sort of required.
8. Same uncle, except there's a hotel receipt in the drawer and her stories don't line up, and he's decided he won't look at any of it. Not fine. Same belief content as #7, though I guess what changed is what he's doing with evidence he already has.
9. My grandfather believed he'd see his wife again after he died. Nothing to check, nobody hurt, and I can't bring myself to call it bad, but it also doesn't feel like #7 where believing is required. It feels like a third category I don't have a name for.

My half-baked rule is this: you can believe whatever you want as long as (a) you're the one who pays if you're wrong, and (b) you're not ignoring evidence you've already got in hand. That seems to get #1 (fails a), #3 (fails a), #6 (fails a), #8 (fails b), and it lets #2, #5, #7, #9 through. But it makes #5 sound merely tolerated when I actually think it's kind of essential, it doesn't tell me why #2 is admirable rather than just permitted, and #7 seems to be about something else entirely. Also, at work we call code "load-bearing" if a ton of other stuff sits on top of it, so if it's wrong the whole thing comes down — and I keep wanting to say some beliefs are load-bearing like that. The landlord's belief about the wiring was load-bearing for a whole building; my #5 belief is load-bearing for my entire quarter. Maybe the evidence bar should scale with how much rests on the belief, rather than with who eats the loss. Does that hold up, or is it just "important" with extra words? Either way, give me the principle or small set of candidates that gets as many of these as possible, say precisely what each clause is doing and which case forces it, tell me honestly which of my nine verdicts it can't accommodate, and then tell me what it predicts for the case that I think breaks my rule anyway: a doctor who's sure her patient will pull through and treats him on that basis, where she doesn't eat the cost if she's wrong, he does, and yet it seems clearly fine to me.
