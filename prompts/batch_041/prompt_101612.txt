For the last few months I've been trying to assemble a defensible view of the future, and I've reached the point where I want an outside audit before I commit any of it to paper. Here is the package as it currently stands. (1) I'm a classical logician: every meaningful declarative sentence is either true or false, and I won't allow a third value or a truth-value gap — not even for sentences about what hasn't happened yet. (2) I hold that truth is timeless: if a sentence is true at all, it is true at every time, and to say that it "will be true later" is only another way of saying that it is true now. (3) I accept a truthmaker principle: whenever a sentence is true, there is something in the world — some fact or feature of reality — in virtue of which it is true, so that truth never floats free of what there is. (4) I hold that the future is genuinely open: it is not the case that everything that will happen is already settled or already "there." (5) I am emphatically not a fatalist; deliberation, effort, and warning people off bad outcomes all strike me as perfectly sensible, and any view that rendered them idle I would treat as refuted by that alone. (6) And I want to keep all of this without the standard escape hatches: I'm not religious, so an appeal to God's foreknowledge is not available to me; I refuse to carve out a special exemption to bivalence for future-tensed sentences; and I won't posit a shadowy domain of "future facts," or a privileged line already drawn through what hasn't happened, because that looks to me like quietly denying (4).

Here is what worries me. Take the old case of a sea battle tomorrow, or, if you prefer, the claim that it will rain here tomorrow. By (1) that claim is either true or false. By (2) it is already true, or already false, as of now. By (3), if it is true, something must make it true — yet the rain does not exist yet, so either its truthmaker is some present fact that already guarantees the rain, in which case the rain is settled and (4) is false, or there is a truth with no truthmaker, in which case (3) is false. A friend pressed the point on me this way: if it is already true that it will rain, then the rain is as much a part of reality as yesterday's rain is, differing only in where it sits on the timeline; and since I take myself to be unable to alter yesterday, I should not think I can alter a tomorrow that is equally real. I cannot locate the flaw in that, but I also cannot bring myself to accept the conclusion.

So: which of these six commitments actually pull against one another, and which only appear to? I suspect at least one of my stated worries is spurious, though I cannot tell which, and I would rather you tell me than have me assume. Order the genuine tensions by how much they would cost me to repair, say for each what exactly would have to give and what else that concession drags down with it, and then tell me which single revision would buy the most consistency for the least loss — including, if you think it is available, a weakened version of one of the six that preserves its spirit while dropping its letter. Please don't answer that it all depends on one's theory of time; I want to know what follows from the package as stated.
