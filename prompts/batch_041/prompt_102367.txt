Suppose a team is building a proof assistant whose trusted kernel is meant to be as small as possible: the kernel fixes a short list of inference rules, and every other rule of inference must be derived as a theorem inside the system. A reviewer objects that this cannot deliver the guarantee the team advertises. Invoking Carroll's tortoise, the reviewer argues that the kernel's own rules can never be justified from within the system, so the supposedly minimal "trusted base" is simply trust under another name, and the design's central selling point collapses.

Please describe the most important implications of the tortoise regress for this dispute. I want you to work out what the regress does and does not establish. Does it show that any finite trusted kernel is arbitrary, or only that justification must at some point give way to the acceptance of a rule rather than the acceptance of a further premise? How does it bear on the distinction between taking a rule as something to be applied and taking it as one more proposition to be believed? What follows for the practical question of where a system — or a person — should stop demanding further justification, and what makes a stopping point principled rather than merely convenient?

Also address the objection that the regress is a philosopher's puzzle with no bearing on engineering, and be explicit about where the analogy to the tortoise breaks down: in particular, whether a machine that mechanically applies its rules is in the same epistemic position as an agent who must first be persuaded to apply them.
