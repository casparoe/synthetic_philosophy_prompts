I am in a graduate seminar on distributive justice, and I have spent the last few weeks arguing with a friend who is a committed utilitarian. He cheerfully grants that the leveling-down objection is fatal to egalitarianism: if equality is valuable in itself, then an outcome in which the better off are pulled down and no one is raised is in one respect better, and that is supposed to be absurd. His new claim, though, is that the leveling-down objection proves far too much — that it does not stop at egalitarianism, and that once stated carefully it leaves aggregate maximization as the only survivor. I cannot accept that conclusion, but I have not been able to locate the misstep, and I would like you to diagnose it rather than simply reassure me.

His argument runs as follows. (1) A view is refuted by the leveling-down objection exactly when it implies that there is something morally to be said for an outcome in which some people are worse off and no one is better off. (2) Egalitarianism implies this: because it treats a more equal distribution as better in itself, it treats the outcome in which two people sit at 5 and 5 as better in one respect than the outcome in which they sit at 5 and 10, even though the person at 10 has lost and no one has gained. (3) What licenses step (2) is that egalitarianism treats the relation between people's levels — the shape of the distribution — as something of intrinsic value. (4) But prioritarianism treats the relation between people's levels as intrinsically valuable too. Its defining claim is that the moral weight of a person's welfare depends on where that person stands relative to others, so that the worse off you are, the more your benefit is worth; that is to say, the shape of the distribution is what carries intrinsic moral significance. (5) So prioritarianism, like egalitarianism, treats a more equal pattern as better in one respect, whether or not anyone gains. (6) Hence prioritarianism implies that there is something to be said for leveling down. (7) By step (1), prioritarianism is refuted by the leveling-down objection. (8) Therefore no view that assigns any intrinsic significance to the distribution of welfare survives, and only pure aggregate maximization does.

Which step should he give up, and what does giving it up cost him? I would rather keep prioritarianism and keep the leveling-down objection against egalitarianism than accept his conclusion, so I am especially interested in whether those two commitments can be held together, and if so at what price — including whether step (1) is even the right way to state what the objection shows, and whether a view can be condemned merely for ranking a leveled-down outcome as better in one respect while still ranking it as worse overall. Explain why the step you reject looked plausible, and then finish with a final line beginning Verdict: that names the step and gives your diagnosis in a single sentence.
