I'm a second-year PhD student in ethnomusicology and I've somehow ended up on the selection panel for a small municipal arts grant — the kind that hands out three thousand dollars to local artists, so the stakes are low but the arguing is not. Our next meeting is Thursday and I need to actually make a case rather than sit there nodding.

Here's the setup. We adopted a pilot rule this year: every applicant submits, alongside the work, a short note saying what feeling they were trying to put into it. Reviewers then score the work on whether that feeling actually came through, and we're supposed to weight that above technical skill and originality. The director's idea was to stop us from funding slick, empty stuff. I have recieved alot of grief about this rule already.

Three finalists:

A photographer, technically stunning, who shoots decaying industrial sites. In his note he says he was after grief and elegy. In a podcast interview last year he said he has no particular attachment to the buildings and mostly does it because it pays and he likes the process. He's not hiding this, it's the first thing on his website.

A retired bus driver who made a shaky nine-minute phone video of his late wife's garden over the last summer of her life. No editing, bad sound. Everyone who watches it cries, including two of my fellow panelists, who then said they didn't trust their own reactions and wanted to recuse themselves.

A composer with a forty-minute sound installation that, honestly, requires about an hour of training to hear what's happening in it. The three people on the panel who've done the training say it's the best thing on the list by a mile. Everyone else was bored out of their minds in under five minutes.

The fights we're already having: one trustee says difficulty is itself a mark of seriousness and that if we fund the bus driver we're officially saying craft doesn't matter. Another says public money can't go to a piece that only trained people can even perceive properly. And I'm stuck on our own rule — the photographer is at least honest about not caring. Does his honesty count as sincerity, or does it disqualify him entirely? Also, can a work be completely sincere and still fail as art? Because I think that's what the video is and I can't tell if that's an argument for funding it or against.

What I want from you: act as a philosopher of art who actually knows the tradition here and isn't afraid to be blunt. Start by brainstorming the different ways this situation can be read — what's really being disagreed about, underneath the grant-committee stuff — and then commit. Don't give me a menu of positions and leave me to pick. Tell me which application I should back, tell me whether to defend the feeling-note rule or quietly bury it, and give me the reasons in a form I can actually say out loud at a meeting. If the whole framework my panel is using is confused, say that directly. And if you land somewhere I'm going to hate, land there anyway. I don't need citations or a reading list, I need an argument.
