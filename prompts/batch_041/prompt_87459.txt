I retired a few years back after 35 years doing experimental neuroscience — mostly single-unit recordings in visual cortex, back when we spent alot of grant money arguing about whether a neuron that fires for a vertical edge is really "representing" the edge or just correlated with it. So you can imagine my amusement, now that I've drifted into philosophy of mind and started reading the machine-learning interpretability papers, to find the exact same fight happening all over again, just with matrices instead of spikes.

Two friends of mine won't stop going at it. One is convinced the interpretability crowd is discovering the real internal features and circuits of these networks — that when a sparse autoencoder pulls out a clean feature, or a linear probe decodes a concept, or a steering vector flips a behavior, that's evidence the network genuinely represents the thing, in whatever sense I used to mean it about a neuron. The other thinks it's all a useful fiction: the network is just a big pile of weights, and "features" and "circuits" are descriptions we impose because they help us predict, not things the network actually has. They've been emailing for months and mostly talking past each other, and I'd like to referee by making them write the document they should've written together. So: draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the representational realist and the deflationist about neural network interpretability. I want the claims both can sign — about what the weights and activations actually are, about what would even count as evidence one way or the other, about which parts of the dispute might just be about words. Then the precise disagreements, each phrased the way that side itself would want it put, so neither feels strawmanned. Then the cruxes — the sub-questions where settling things would actually move somebody — and I have a hunch two of them are these: does it matter whether the decoded thing plays a causal role in the network's behavior (so an ablation or steering test counts for something) versus merely being decodable; and does the fact that the same activations can be carved into many different, equally faithful feature-bases (superposition, non-identifiability, whatever you want to call it) actually undermine realism, or is it just a fact realism has to live with. For each crux, give a concrete case or experiment both sides would agree bears on it, and write down what each side predicts it'll show, in advance. And if you decide part of their fight is purely verbal, say which part and why. Anyways, keep it in a format I can actually hand to both of them without either storming off — maybe a numbered set of agreed claims up top, then a table for the disagreements and cruxes.
