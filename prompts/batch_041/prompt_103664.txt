Me and two friends have a running argument that I can't let go of. One of them works in marketing and talks about "Gen Z" and "Boomers" the way my dad talks about the weather -- as if these were things you can just look up, like the boiling point of water. The other one is a bit of a pedant and keeps saying "that's just people in their twenties, they've always been like that," and then the first one says "no, it's different now because of phones," and I genuinely can't tell who's winning. I don't have any background in this stuff, I just read a lot of comment sections. What bugs me is that everyone seems to agree these labels pick out real groups with real traits, and yet nobody can tell me who got to draw the lines, or why 1996 makes you one thing and 1997 makes you another, or how you'd even tell whether something is a fact about a "generation" or a fact about being young during a bad economy. So I want to see the whole thing taken seriously, in writing, by someone who is clearly way too confident about it.

Here's what I want: a parody press release, Onion-style, from a made-up market-research firm announcing that it has formally identified and named a brand-new generation -- they're very proud of it, there's a rollout, there's a launch. Give it a headline, a dateline, roughly 500-600 words, and the usual furniture: a triumphant quote from the firm's in-house demographer, one from a rival who thinks they split the group wrong, and a boilerplate "About Us" paragraph at the bottom. All the humor should come from the actual mechanics of classifying people by birth year -- the cutoffs that are chosen and then treated as discovered, the fight between the people who want fewer, bigger generations and the ones who want more, smaller ones, the idea that you could have a type specimen or a generation go extinct or get merged with another one, the fact that the same person ends up in two different generations depending on which chart you're looking at, and the quiet possibility that whatever the firm is calling a generational trait is really just an age thing or a bad-year thing that will look identical in every cohort that lives through it. Don't ever come out and say that the whole category is fake, and don't wink -- stay in character as the firm, deadpan, no jokes about how silly marketing people are. It should be funny to someone who's thought about how you'd even measure a cohort effect and still readable to someone who hasn't. Don't cite any real studies or real people, obviously -- I know you can't look stuff up, so just invent the names and the fake numbers and keep them consistent. American English please.
