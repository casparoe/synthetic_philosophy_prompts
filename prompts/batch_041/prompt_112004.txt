Write a dialogue between two philosophers, each fully conversant with Frege's logical and philosophical work, on whether the antisemitic and nationalist diary he kept in 1924—published only in 1994—bears on the interpretation of that work. One philosopher argues that the diary is biographically disturbing but philosophically inert: the merits of Frege's accounts of sense, reference, and the foundations of arithmetic are settled by argument, not by the author's politics, and treating the diary as hermeneutically relevant risks the genetic fallacy. The other argues that the diary is not merely external to the philosophy: Frege's conception of logic as a realm of objective, timeless truths, his hostility to psychologism, and his sharp separation of the logical from the empirical may themselves be shaped by a political sensibility that prized purity, hierarchy, and impersonality, so that refusing to read the two together is a way of protecting the philosophy from its own history. Let each philosopher press the other on the strongest version of their position—the first should specify what could legitimately count as biography bearing on doctrine, and the second should explain how a political origin could impugn a philosophical argument without simply committing the genetic fallacy. The dialogue should contain real back-and-forth and should not end in agreement unless the argument genuinely earns it.
