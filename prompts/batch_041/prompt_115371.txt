I'm a philosophy PhD student working on theories of punishment, and I'm writing a chapter that uses historical cases as stress tests for the retributivism/consequentialism divide, because I'm tired of the trolley-problem-style examples everyone reaches for. The case I've landed on is the British handling of the leaders of the 1916 Easter Rising. After the surrender the leaders were court-martialled in secret and shot in batches at Kilmainham over the first ten days of May, with several hundred people dead from the week's fighting, and the standard story is that the whole thing backfired: instead of cowing the country it radicalised it, the men became martyrs almost instantly, and by 1918 the political map of Ireland had flipped completely. I know the chronology, so please don't spend the answer re-telling it, and don't bother with citations or reading lists, I'm not going to check them, I want the argument. Act as an expert in the philosophy of punishment and take this slowly. Start with a short brainstorm in which you pick the strongest version of each side rather than the straw man, then do the mapping: who is actually the punisher here (General Maxwell? the cabinet? the Crown?), what the live options in the room were (shoot all of them, shoot only the seven signatories, commute the rest, transport them, intern without trial and shoot nobody), what each theory says the right answer was and why, and -- this is the part I care about -- which features of the episode each theory simply cannot see.

Two things I keep running into. First, the men wanted it. The blood-sacrifice idea usually credited to Pearse -- that a rising which failed, and the executions that followed, would do more for Ireland than any victory -- was clearly in the air among the people who planned the thing, so from the offender's side the punishment wasn't a cost, it was the point. Both theories seem to assume the offender is trying to get away; what happens when he isn't? A retributivist will want to say the offender's attitude is irrelevant to what he deserves, which I find attractive, but then I can't see why the hard treatment still counts as a burden -- can you burden a man by killing him if he thinks he's buying Ireland's freedom with his life? And a consequentialist has to face the fact that the killing recruited for his side, so the punishment produced the opposite of what it was for. Second, the meaning problem. There's a family of views, expressive or communicative ones, that say punishment is partly a matter of saying something: denouncing the act, addressing the offender as someone who can answer for what he did. That seems to need an audience that hears the message the way it was sent, and in Dublin the audience took the state's sentence and re-wrote it into a different sentence entirely. The hunger strikers make this even stranger -- MacSwiney, the Lord Mayor of Cork, starving himself to death in Brixton in 1920, or the men in 1981 -- the prisoner takes the punishment over and decides what it means, and the state can only stand there.

So work it in both directions. From theory to case: what does each view recommend ex ante, and be honest about whether a well-informed consequentialist had any way to see the backlash coming -- if he did, the executions weren't bad luck, they were a blunder, and if he didn't, I want to know what that does to the theory's claim to guide action. From case to theory: where does each view break? The willing martyr; the offender who authors the meaning of his own punishment; the selectivity (why the signatories, and not MacNeill, who tried to call the whole thing off?); and the secrecy and speed of the courts-martial, which I take to be a specific problem for retributivism, since desert without a real hearing is just a sentence. End with a verdict: is this an anomaly the theories can absorb with some patching, or evidence that both were built for offenders who lose and have nothing to say about ones who win? Flag the questions you think are genuinely open instead of pretending to close them. Its the centrepiece of a chapter, so I'd rather have your real reasoning than a tidy answer.
