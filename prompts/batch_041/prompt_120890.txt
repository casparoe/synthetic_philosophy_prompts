I've fallen down a rabbit hole on the historical Jesus and I'd like a real diagnosis, not a survey of the positions. You're a philosopher of history who's spent alot of time on methodology — think carefully, and brainstorm for a bit before you start writing.

Here's what I can't get past. The field runs on "criteria of authenticity": a saying is more likely to go back to Jesus if it would've embarrassed the early church (his baptism by John, his execution as a criminal), if it shows up in multiple independent sources, if it's unlike both the Judaism and the Christianity around it, if it coheres with the rest. Everyone has known for decades that these are leaky — embarrassment can be invented, dissimilarity selects for whatever makes him hardest to place in his own world, and the criteria aren't independent of one another, so stacking them like independent lines of evidence is bogus. Then there's another camp that says the question is wrong: stop asking which sayings are authentic and ask how memories of him formed and got retold.

Why hasnt this resolved? Is it a substantive disagreement, a hidden one about priors, or two groups answering different questions under one name? My working hypothesis, held loosely: mostly the third — one side is doing source criticism on a first-century individual, the other is doing cultural history of memory, and "what did he actually say" isn't even a well-formed question for the second project. But I also suspect a genuine, maybe resolvable dispute is hiding underneath: the criteria are basically likelihood ratios, and nobody in the debate will state a prior, so "authentic" quietly comes to mean "survives my background assumptions about what a Galilean peasant would say."

Steelman both sides, tell me which parts of the clash are verbal and which are bedrock, and end with what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal point about how the criteria can and can't be combined, or a reason the question can't be settled at all.
