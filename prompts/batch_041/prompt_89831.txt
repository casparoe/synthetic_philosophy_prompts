I'm building up to writing my own expressivist semantics, and before I start I want the full spec of what the thing has to do — the criteria, not a survey of who said what. So: give me the desiderata an account of moral language has to meet, each with the case that motivates it. Some I already know I'm on the hook for — 'lying is wrong' has to mean the same thing when it sits unasserted in 'if lying is wrong then getting your brother to lie is wrong' as when it stands alone; the modus ponens has to come out valid for someone with no opinion about lying; 'lying is wrong' and 'lying isn't wrong' have to be contradictories and not just two different attitudes colliding; and it has to make sense of us calling these things true and arguing about them. Fine, those are the price of entry.

What I actually want is the rest of the list, including the ones that aren't usually written down. Here's the kind of thing that keeps tripping me up and that I'd want a requirement for: 'I wonder whether lying is wrong' — nobody's expressing an attitude there, but the clause seems to mean what it always means. 'Lying is probably wrong' — what is 'probably' doing to an attitude? 'Either lying is wrong or the sign is misprinted' — one disjunct is a norm, the other is about ink. Someone who says all the right sentences and then adds 'but I couldn't care less' — is she using the words with our meaning and just cold, or is she using different words? And 'I take that back' after having said it.

For each requirement, give me the case behind it and say whether it's non-negotiable or a prejudice of one camp. Then the part I care about most: which subset of these can't be had together, and which single requirement does the most to sink expressivism. Brief scorecard at the end of how the standard moves (quasi-realism, hybrid views, minimalism about truth) fare against the list — keep that short, the criteria are the deliverable. No reading list.
