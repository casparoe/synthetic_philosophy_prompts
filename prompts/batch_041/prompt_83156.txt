Here's the question I asked: Define the difference between what I called bare nothing (the absence of any entities, properties, laws, or spacetime) and physical nothing (a state with no particles but with fields, laws, or spacetime). Then assess the claim that quantum cosmology has shown that the universe came from nothing. In your answer, explain why the distinction matters for the question of why there is something rather than nothing, and consider the objection that a physical nothing is still something.

And here's the answer I'm looking at:

The question of why there is something rather than nothing is often confused with the question of how the universe began. The first is not about a temporal first event; it is about why there is any reality at all. The second is a question for cosmology. The distinction between bare nothing and physical nothing helps here. Bare nothing would be the absence of everything: no particles, no fields, no laws, no space, no time. Physical nothing is more like an empty state within a physical theory: no particles, but fields, laws, maybe spacetime. The quantum vacuum is a physical nothing, not a bare nothing. It has fluctuations and energy, so it is not literally nothing.

Some popular accounts say quantum fluctuations show the universe came from nothing. That is misleading if by nothing they mean bare nothing. The vacuum is a structured entity governed by laws, so it is already something. But I think the distinction does not make as much difference as it seems. The vacuum is the most basic state physics can describe, and if we can show that it is unstable and produces particles, then we have shown that something can come from nothing in the only sense physics can make precise. The remaining question, why there is a vacuum at all, is not empirically testable. So it is not a genuine question. It is a pseudo-question, like asking what is north of the North Pole.

One might object that this just pushes the problem back. The vacuum and its laws need an explanation. The principle of sufficient reason would demand an explanation of the vacuum, but we do not have to accept that principle. We can say the vacuum is brute. So the real lesson is that the question why there is something rather than nothing is either a scientific question about the early universe, which cosmology can answer, or a confused question that dissolves once we stop assuming that everything needs an explanation.
