For a living I design authentication and zero-knowledge protocols, so my working vocabulary is that of provers and verifiers, commitments and openings, witnesses, transcripts, soundness and completeness, and the distinction between proving that a statement holds and revealing why it holds; I also spend a good deal of time on remote attestation, in which a machine convinces a distant party that it is in a particular state by exhibiting a chain of trust rooted in hardware rather than by being inspected directly. I would like you to take the classical positions on the problem of other minds and restate each of them in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can. Take them one at a time: the argument from analogy, the appeal to behavioural criteria, the inference-to-the-best-explanation line, the simulation or empathy route, and the deflationary view that the demand for a proof is itself the error. For each, tell me what translates cleanly, what has to be distorted in order to fit, and what finds no expression at all in my terms — in particular, what becomes of the asymmetry between my knowledge of my own case and my knowledge of yours, and of the thought that another's experience is something I can at best be assured of and never verify. Then give me the residue report: is there anything the philosophers are genuinely disagreeing about that has no counterpart in protocols — anything that turns on first-person givenness, on whether the claim that another is conscious is even the sort of statement one could put to a verifier, or on whether there is a root of trust for experience at all? And tell me whether that residue marks a real limitation of my vocabulary or a place where the original debate was less well-defined than it looked.
