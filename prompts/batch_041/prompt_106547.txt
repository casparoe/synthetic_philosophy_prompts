I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, working mainly on metaethics and the theory of normativity, and my undergraduate alma mater — a small liberal arts college — has invited me to give this spring's commencement address. The audience will be a few hundred people: the graduating class, their families, and a faculty who are mostly not philosophers. What I want to say, and the reason I keep getting stuck, both come out of a problem I spend my working life on, the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem. The idea is roughly this: there seem to be two very different ways a consideration can bear on an attitude such as a belief, a hope, an admiration, or a feeling of self-trust. One is that the consideration bears on whether the attitude fits its object — evidence that things will go well is a reason for hope in the ordinary sense; the other is that the consideration bears on the benefits of simply having the attitude — that hoping will steady you, that believing in yourself will help you succeed. The second sort of consideration feels like a reason, and in a way it plainly can move us, but there is a strong intuition that it is the wrong kind of reason: that it does not speak to whether the attitude is correct or warranted at all, and that we are confused when we treat it as if it did. The trouble is that the genre I have been handed runs almost entirely on the second sort of consideration. Believe in yourself because it will help you succeed; be kind because it pays; hold on to hope because hope is good for your health; study the humanities because they will make you interesting — every one of those offers a reason that concerns the usefulness of the attitude rather than its fittingness, and I am fairly sure that at least some of them are precisely the considerations that should not count.

So please write me the address. I do not want to stand up, announce the problem, and sit down again; I want to actually give these people something to hold on to, and to do it without smuggling the wrong-kind reasons back in under prettier language. Constraints: it has to land for the philosophy faculty in the room, who will recognize exactly what I am up to, and for the relatives who have never heard of any of this; it must be an address rather than a lecture, and it should not use the term of art or any jargon. The problem should genuinely constrain the speech — you should be able to feel it ruling moves in and out, not merely decorating the opening. I would like the prudential it-will-pay-off register avoided entirely, and the grounds you offer for hope, self-trust, and a love of learning should be reasons that actually fit the world; where those run out, I would rather you be honest about the gap than paper over it. About eight hundred words, pitched for the ear rather than the page. Afterward, in a short paragraph, tell me which sentence or move is doing the real work of holding the tension together — or of honestly failing to — and what a critic who thinks I have simply been evasive would say back to me.
