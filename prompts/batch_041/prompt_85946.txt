ok so I'm a 3rd-year PhD student in philosophy, working mostly on social choice and distributive justice, and I've somehow aggreed to run the 90-minute session for our interdisciplinary political theory reading group next month. About 12 people, mix of philosophy and econ grad students, and they've all seen the veil of ignorance before, so I can't just do the Rawls song and dance and call it a day. The topic I got handed is Harsanyi's aggregation theorem.

Here's my problem with it. If I stand up and walk them through the axioms and then the representation result, everyone nods, everyone writes down "social welfare = weighted sum of individual utilities" in their notebook, and nobody actually feels why anyone would believe it. Written on a slide it looks like a conjuring trick; felt from the inside it's supposed to look like common sense. I want them to arrive at the utilitarian conclusion themselves, out of a game, before the name Harsanyi is ever said out loud.

So what I want from you is an activity, runnable in 90 minutes with 12 people, where the rules do the work and I mostly shut up. Rough shape of what I'm after, but improve on it if you see something better:

1. They're behind some kind of veil -- they don't know which of N positions in a made-up society they'll end up in -- and they have to pick between a few policies or constitutions. I want the incentive structure such that any halfway sensible play gets them ranking the options by average expected payoff, i.e. they reconstruct expected-utility aggregation without being told that's what they're doing.

2. Then I want a trap, and specifically I want to hit the ex ante / ex post distinction. Give me two policies where each person's individual prospect is identical -- say everyone faces the same 50/50 gamble -- but in one of them the gamble resolves the same way for everybody and in the other it resolves differently for different people. Ranked by expected utility per person those two come out exactly tied, and I want them to feel the pull of saying the second one is nonetheless worse, that it's unfair, while being unable to point to where in their own rules the unfairness was supposed to enter. That gap is the whole session.

Things I care about:

- No lecture up front, and ideally they don't hear "utility" used as a technical term until the debrief. Also no "so this is basically Harsanyi" reveal at the halfway mark -- save it.
- The payoffs have to be actually runnable: points, fake money, chocolates, whatever, but no real money, and it needs to survive a room of grad students who will try to game it. If they game it, that's fine, I want to know how.
- I want at least one role or rule that lets the Rawlsian objection get voiced inside the game rather than by me afterwards -- someone who refuses the "it's just a lottery, maximize expected value" framing and insists on planning for the worst case. I want to see whether the game makes that refusal look unreasonable or whether it can't.
- Equal weights: my hunch is my setup hands them equal weights for free, since I'll have made everyone equally likely to be anyone. Is there a cheap way to make that assumption visible and contestable rather than baked in? If there isn't, say so.
- Debrief should map what happened onto the actual moving parts of the theorem -- which rule they leaned on where, which assumption they never actually agreed to, where the fairness intuition got squeezed out -- and be honest about what the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself.
- Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. Grad students, so I'm expecting at least one person to argue the veil is incoherent, one to refuse to play, and one to crack the whole thing in four minutes and get bored.
