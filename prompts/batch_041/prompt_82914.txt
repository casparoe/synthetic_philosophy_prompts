My friend runs a bioethics seminar and I've been sitting in on it mostly to argue with her, and I've started keeping a list of her verdicts on enhancement cases because they've gotten oddly consistent. Growth hormone for a kid whose pituitary doesn't work: fine, she doesn't even file it under enhancement. Same drug for a kid who's just short with nothing wrong with him: no, and she gets visibly irritated when students defend it. Modafinil for narcolepsy: fine. Modafinil for a healthy surgical resident on eighty-hour weeks: yes — that one surprised the room, and her whole argument was about the patients, not the resident. Cochlear implants, laser eye surgery: fine, "don't be silly, that's medicine." EPO for a pro cyclist: no. A hypothetical safe pill that lifted everyone's IQ ten points: yes. The same pill, but only your kid can get it: "that's a different question," which is definately not an answer. An antidepressant for someone grieving a dead dog who isn't depressed: no, but tentatively. A cheap drug with no side effects that cuts your need for sleep to four hours: "ask me next week," which is her tell for "my rule doesn't reach this." An adult who wants a memory-boosting drug: fine; the same drug given by parents to their willing fourteen-year-old: no. And she approved embryo selection against a severe early-onset disease but said no to a heritable edit that would prevent the exact same disease in the same family, and when I pointed out that looked inconsistent she said the difference was "the grandkids," not the kid.

Next week's case is a one-shot gene therapy given at birth, irreversible, that raises baseline muscle mass and speeds recovery from injury, no known downside, offered free to every family through the national health service, and yes, it passes to the child's own kids. Predict her verdict, tell me what rules her record is actually consistent with — I can see at least a treatment-versus-enhancement line reading, a reading where she only ever cares about welfare and knock-on effects on third parties, and one classmate's hypothesis that the real variable is whether the advantage is positional, i.e. whether it's only worth something because other people lack it — then say what each of those predicts about the new case, which one I should actually bet on, and what single case I should bring up next week to force her to choose between them.
