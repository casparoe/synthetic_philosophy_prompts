I work for a city council in a mid-sized city and we've somehow ended up committed to building a standing citizens' assembly -- not a one-off consultation, an actual permanent body that meets in cycles, gets a question from us, deliberates, and hands back a recommendation the council then votes on. My job is to write the operating manual: how members get drawn, how long they sit, how they're replaced, when we're allowed to deviate from a plain lottery, what we do when people decline. We have no statistician on staff, the clerk has to be able to run the thing, and the city attorney will read whatever I write before the council does. I've become a convert to the whole idea -- the fairness case (everyone gets an equal chance to hold power, not just an equal vote), the epistemic case (a random draw brings cognitive diversity that a self-selected room of the usual suspects never has), the fact that nobody in the room has to fundraise -- but i genuinely can't see how to get from any of that to rules a hostile councellor can't call rigged.

So: turn the case for sortition into a decision procedure I can actually follow. Ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a hard no, and where it stops and says "this one is a value call, get a human to sign off." The things I keep getting stuck on and need the procedure to resolve: which variables, if any, we stratify on when we draw, given that you can't stratify on everything without turning the lottery into a quota system; whether what actually matters is census-fidelity or diversity of *relevant* perspectives, and how that changes the answer; whether we recruit by address or let people sign up, given that volunteers are systematically more civically minded and more opinionated than the population; what to do about attrition when a third of the seats turn over mid-cycle; whether paying members changes who accepts and therefore corrupts the draw; who gets to frame the question and write the briefing materials, since that's where the real power sits; and whether the body should be able to bind us or only advise.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show your work: (1) a proposal to run the draw as an open public sign-up, which we know will produce roughly three men for every woman who volunteers; (2) a councillor's motion to reserve eight of sixty seats for one particular group; (3) my own favourite, making the recommendations binding. I'll warn you I'm rooting for (3), and I'd rather you told me straight if the theory doesn't get us there.

Don't give me citations or a reading list -- I can't look anything up right now and it would just be guessing. Cap it at 1200 words, I'd much rather have a tight procedure than an essay. And tell me what would change your mind about it: which argument or consideration would make you reorder or drop one of the steps, and where you think the procedure is most likely to break down in real use.
