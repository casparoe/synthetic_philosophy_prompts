After thirty-four years as a volcanologist — most of them spent producing the long-horizon eruption forecasts that civil-defence agencies in my country are obliged to take seriously — I have retired and, somewhat to my own surprise, enrolled in a philosophy programme. I have lately been reading about what I am told is called the reference class problem, and I find that it describes with unnerving precision a dispute I have had with a former colleague for most of my career, though neither of us had the vocabulary for it at the time. Here is the case, stripped of identifiers. A large caldera that I monitored for two decades has produced one eruption in its 8,000-year tephra record; the global population of comparable arc volcanoes yields a recurrence rate of roughly one event per 3,000 years per volcano; a physics-based model of the chamber's current pressurisation state, run with the parameters my colleague and I both trust, implies a considerably higher rate; and the volcano has recently shown a precursor pattern that in the instrumental record has preceded eruption in a small but non-trivial fraction of cases. Depending on which class one uses, the thirty-year probability of an eruption ranges from about 0.03 to about 0.35. My former colleague insists that the number is whatever the correct class yields, and that our task is simply to find it; I have come to think that we are choosing a class rather than discovering one, and that the choice is answerable only to the purposes the forecast serves. I would therefore like your credence in a claim I have tried to state precisely enough to be false: for a singular physical event of this kind, there is a unique, mind-independent fact of the matter about the correct reference class, and hence about the objective probability of the event — a fact that holds irrespective of who is asking, for what decision, and under what loss function.

Please give me that credence, decomposed. The decomposition should include, at a minimum: whether objective single-case chances attach to macroscopic geophysical events at all, or whether chance-talk is at bottom frequency-talk over populations; conditional on such chances existing, whether the world itself supplies a natural partition into classes — some homogeneity or exchangeability structure — rather than merely tolerating many admissible partitions; conditional on several being admissible, whether any inquirer-independent selection rule (the narrowest class with reliable statistics, say, or the rule that maximises expected accuracy across candidate classes) amounts to more than a pragmatic convention; and the probability that the right account of what I was doing all those years is a decision-theoretic one, in which the probability is an agent-relative credence and the reference class is simply part of the model rather than a feature of the world. One further term belongs in the sum, and it concerns a proposal of my colleague's that I have not encountered anywhere in the literature: she holds what she calls the same-mechanism requirement, on which a class is admissible only if membership is fixed by the causal mechanism that will actually produce or fail to produce the event, rather than by any feature observable beforehand. Assess that requirement as well, and give me a number for the probability that it succeeds in singling out a unique class in cases such as mine. For the response I would like the following sections, in this order: (1) "Bottom line", two or three sentences leading with your credence in the stated claim; (2) "The terms", a table giving each sub-question, its probability, and one line of justification, with any terms you think I have omitted added; (3) "How the terms combine", no more than 150 words showing how the sub-probabilities generate your number and whether you are treating them as independent; (4) "The same-mechanism requirement", roughly 200 words; (5) "The weakest term and what would move it", roughly 200 words, naming the term you are least confident in, the direction in which you suspect it errs, and the specific evidence or argument that would shift your number most.
