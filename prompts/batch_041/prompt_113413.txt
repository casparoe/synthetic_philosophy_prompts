I have been in a decision theory reading group for the better part of a year, and our sessions on counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail keep ending in the same place: everyone can state both problems, everyone agrees about what each standard decision theory recommends, and yet the group splits cleanly into people who think the payer is being played for a sucker and people who think the non-payer is leaving money on the table. I would like a diagnosis of that impasse rather than another round of it.

Here is the shape of the two problems as we have been using them. In counterfactual mugging, Omega, a perfect predictor, flips a fair coin. If it lands heads, Omega pays you $10,000 on the condition that it predicted you would have paid had the coin landed tails. If it lands tails, Omega asks you for $100 and nothing further happens either way. Paying on tails costs $100 and has no causal bearing on the coin; declining costs nothing, but Omega's prediction means that a person who would decline never collects the $10,000 in the heads world. In XOR blackmail, Omega sends you a message whose content is a biconditional: the message is sent if and only if your house has been infested with termites or you pay Omega $1,000, but not both. You have received the message. A termite infestation would cost you $1,000,000. If you are the sort who pays, then receiving the message tells you that the XOR condition was satisfied by the payment rather than by the termites; if you are the sort who declines, receiving the message tells you that it was satisfied by the termites. Paying $1,000 does not cause termites to appear or disappear.

The positions in the group are these. Causal decision theory says decline in both cases, because the act of paying is not a cause of the coin, the prediction, or the termites. Evidential decision theory and the various updateless and functional approaches say pay in both, or at least pay in XOR blackmail, because the act is evidence about which branch of the setup you are in and, on the updateless story, because the decision procedure that pays wins across the branches rather than within the branch you happen to occupy. Everyone in the group agrees that if you could pre-commit in advance, you should commit to pay; the fight is entirely about what to do when you have not pre-committed and the coin has already landed or the message is already in your hand. A few members think the recurrence of the scenario matters — that the toy cases are meant to model an agent whose environment repeatedly poses such choices, and that once you say which kind of environment you are in, the disagreement dissolves. Others think that is a dodge, because the question was supposed to be about a single unrepeatable choice.

What I want is a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a substantive disagreement about which acts are rational, in which case I want to know which premise the two sides differ on and whether either side has an argument the other should accept? Is it a verbal dispute about what the word 'rational' or 'ought' is doing, with the causal theorists evaluating acts and the evidential and updateless theorists evaluating something else under the same name? Is it a disagreement about the object of evaluation — acts, policies, decision procedures, or whole agents — so that the two sides are answering different questions and only appear to conflict? Is it a disagreement about whether the problems are well-posed at all, given that the predictor, the biconditional, and the counterfactual branch may not pick out a single determinate decision problem? Or is it, as I half suspect, a clash of bedrock intuitions about whether an act that cannot causally change anything can nonetheless be made correct by what it reveals or by what it commits you to — an intuition neither side can argue for from neutral ground?

I have a hypothesis I would like tested rather than confirmed: that the real disagreement is about whether decision theory is in the business of evaluating token acts in the situation the agent is actually in, or of selecting a procedure that does well across the class of situations the agent might be in, and that once that question is separated out, most of the heat goes out of the cases themselves. If that is right, say so and say what follows; if it is wrong, tell me what the actual fault line is.

The deliverable is the diagnosis, supported by what each side actually says about these two cases and by the structural features of the cases themselves, together with an honest account of what would count as progress given the diagnosis — a formal result, a distinction that separates the readings, a change in the question, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the disagreement bottoms out in intuitions. I do not want a survey of decision theories or a verdict on which one is correct. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature, and do not present a contested view as though it were the consensus. Write in American English.
