I am a policy fellow, and next month I present to a joint committee of both Houses a paper arguing that England should give up establishment in favour of a levy. Under the scheme I propose, any registered religious or philosophical body — humanist associations included — may have the state's revenue offices collect a surcharge on the income tax of its self-declared members, with departure effected by a declaration to a civil authority rather than to the body itself, as in the German arrangement. A fixed share of all receipts is then pooled and redistributed per capita across every registered body, so that a community of a few thousand is not starved by its own smallness. My thesis is that establishment and the levy are two ways of financing the same public good, that England has drifted into the worst features of each, and that only the levy can be extended to Muslims, Hindus and the non-religious without privileging anyone.

The committee is five people. An Anglican bishop for whom establishment is a ministry to the nation rather than a privilege of his church. A secularist who holds that state collection of religious dues is a subsidy whatever it is called. A comparative lawyer who knows the German scheme's real pathologies — the exit waves, the collection machinery, the way it entrenches the majority church — and who will note that France is officially secular everywhere except Alsace-Moselle, where ministers of the four recognised faiths (Catholic, Lutheran, Reformed, Jewish) remain on the state payroll. A Treasury economist. And a Muslim community organiser who is wary of machinery built for churches.

Draft my question-period prep: the objections each of these five will actually raise, in that person's own vocabulary, ordered by how much damage they do to my case, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is softest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
