I am a novelist, midway through a book whose premise has turned out to be a philosophical problem as much as a narrative one. My protagonist dies about two-thirds of the way in, and the final third is told in a first person that is no longer hers alone: her voice persists, thinned and divided, in the minds of the five or six people who knew her best — in their memories, their borrowed turns of phrase, the way they now finish her sentences — and I want the reader to feel, not merely be told, that the I narrating is still in some sense her. My editor is enthusiastic and has an argument she deploys whenever I falter. A self, she says, is like a flame. One candle lights another, and then another; the second flame is as real as the first, and the first has lost nothing in the lighting. What is a person, on this picture, but a pattern that can kindle itself elsewhere? Nothing tragic and nothing dubious follows for my ending: my protagonist's flame has simply been passed to other wicks. She adds that Douglas Hofstadter makes a related claim — that a self is not sealed inside one skull, and that each of us carries partial and degraded versions of the selves of the people we have loved.

I suspect the argument is carrying more weight than it can bear, and that I have been letting it carry my book. I would like you to audit it.

Please take the role of a philosopher of mind specializing in personal identity, with a side interest in how such arguments are put to use outside philosophy, and write to a serious non-specialist. I am not asking for a reading list; I want the argument itself, from you. Before you answer, spend a few sentences thinking on the page about what work the analogy is being asked to do here, since my editor and I may want different conclusions from it: she wants reassurance that the ending is coherent, whereas I want to be able to write the final third as though the narrator is the same subject of experience and not merely a family resemblance. Flag anywhere you are unsure of Hofstadter's actual position; where that happens, argue from the version of it I have described rather than guessing at his text.

Then work through the following.

1. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — a table is welcome — mapping each element of the candle-and-flame picture (the flame, the wick, the wax, the act of lighting, the darkness before and after, the heat, the light) onto whatever element of the mind it is meant to stand for. Where the picture supplies no plausible counterpart, say so rather than inventing one.

2. Identify where the mapping breaks, and sort the breaks by whether they matter to the particular conclusion my editor is drawing. A disanalogy irrelevant to that conclusion should be marked as irrelevant; I do not want an inventory of everything a flame lacks.

3. Say what the picture quietly imports. I am especially interested in whether it assumes the answer to the question I am actually asking — whether what persists is the same self or only another instance of the same type — and whether it presupposes a view about what a self is (a pattern, a process, a thing with a point of view) that my book needs to earn rather than assume.

4. Say plainly which of two conclusions the analogy can support, if either: that the distributed self is numerically the same I that died, or that something real but numerically new continues. Then assess whether Hofstadter's view — which, as I understand it, treats a self's presence in other minds as a matter of degree, depending on how rich and how faithful the internal model is — helps my case or is obscured by the all-or-nothing flame.

5. If a better analogy could rescue the argument, propose one (a melody, a river, a legal precedent, a role in a play, a word's meaning spread across a whole community of speakers, or something of your own) and say what the replacement buys and what it still cannot deliver. If no analogy can carry the weight, say that too, and tell me what would have to be argued directly instead.

A final, narrower question, because it is the one that will change my sentences: on the strongest defensible version of the picture, what may the narrator legitimately say about herself in the final third? If the honest answer is that she can write we but not I, that is a different novel, and I would rather learn it now.
