I teach a first-year ethics section and I'm sick of the numbers-count debate turning into everyone just paraphrasing Taurek and then going back to whatever they already believed. So I want to hand out a flowchart that actually sorts people by what they think, not by which name they drop. Can you build it?

Here's the setup. Everyone starts in the same place: they agree that every person's life matters equally, that saving someone is a serious good, and that in the stock case you can save either one person or five, all strangers, all equally healthy, no other differences, and anyone you don't save dies. From there I want a tree where every branch is a single question about a concrete case or a single claim, phrased so a student can answer it without knowing what position it leads to. No "do you believe in aggregation" type questions — they have to answer about the case.

I want the first split to be about whether the bare fact that saving five saves more people is, by itself, any kind of reason at all to save the five over the one. Then force people apart on things like: whether that reason (if there is one) is about more lives, more claims satisfied, more welfare, or just more people getting the equal treatment they're owed; whether numbers are a tiebreaker you fall back on when nothing else separates the options or a reason that's always in play; whether it matters that you'd be required to save the five or merely allowed to; and whether the numbers do their work before the fact (everyone gets an equal chance) or only after the fact (you save the five). One branch should be the split between someone who'd flip a coin or save whomever and someone who'd give each side a proportional chance — those get lumped together all the time and I want them seperate.

Please include a couple of cases that aren't the usual five-vs-one, so the tree catches people whose answers to the stock case are just memorized. Use (a) a case where one person needs the whole dose of an antidote and five people each need a fifth of it, so more lives and more good come apart, and (b) a case where the five have already been told they'll be rescued and the one hasn't, so there's a promise or expectation in the mix. Whatever you do with those, make sure they earn their place as branch nodes and aren't just decoration.

I want at least these to show up as leaves, and it's fine if a couple of them land on the same leaf — if they do, say so: the pure "more lives is just more good" view; the "more claims satisfied, and that's not the same as adding up welfare" view; the "numbers only break ties" view; the "you're permitted to save either, flip a coin if you like" view; the "give everyone an equal chance" view; the "you're required to save the five, but not for aggregative reasons" view; and the "numbers don't constrain what you may do but do affect what you should feel bad about afterwards" view. Aim for something like six to eight branching questions total.

Then, seperate from the leaves, I want the combinations of answers that no position actually occupies — say which ones those are and, for each, whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because it's coherent but no one would want it, or just because nobody's bothered.

Please structure it like this:
- "The setup" — one short paragraph fixing the starting case and the ground rules, under 150 words.
- "The tree" — the actual flowchart. Write each branch as a numbered node with its question, and label each edge with the answer it takes, down to the leaves. This should be the bulk of it.
- "The leaves" — a list, each leaf named and given one sentence on the most awkward thing it has to say. One sentence each.
- "Empty rooms" — the unoccupied answer-combinations, each with a one-line diagnosis of why it's empty.
- "What the tree can't tell apart" — any positions that come out at the same leaf or that the questions as written don't separate, with a note on what extra question would separate them.

Keep it plain, no citations, and assume the reader has never heard any of these names. Don't worry about being exhaustive on every position ever defended — I'd rather have a clean tree that misses a few than a sprawling one that sorts nobody.
