You are a philosopher specializing in normative ethics and value theory, with particular expertise in the structure of deontological constraints and the distinction between agent-relative and agent-neutral reasons. I am a PhD student writing on the foundations of deontology, and I would like your help diagnosing an argument I keep reconstructing in my own notes and cannot decide whether to fear. Please begin by brainstorming privately: map the main ways one might relate deontological constraints to agent-neutral value, mark the points at which each step below might be resisted, and only then commit to a diagnosis and defend it in full. I would rather have one well-argued diagnosis than a survey of every position on offer.

Here is the argument. Call the constraint it attacks the prohibition on killing one to save five.

(1) It is morally wrong to kill one innocent person in order to save five others from being killed.
(2) Such a killing is wrong because it treats the one as a mere means: it makes his death an instrument for the good of others, and thereby violates the inviolability that persons possess.
(3) If the wrongness of a killing consists in the violation of a person's inviolability, then a killing is, in that very respect, a bad thing -- the sort of thing it is better for there to be less of.
(4) If killings are bad in that respect, then a world containing five killings is, in that respect, worse than a world containing one.
(5) When you can bring about either of two outcomes, and one is worse than the other in a relevant respect and better in no other respect, you ought to bring about the better one.
(6) Therefore, when you can save five from being killed only by killing one, you ought to kill the one.
(7) But (6) contradicts (1).

I would most like to keep (1) and (2); without them the view is not deontological at all. What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a rescue. Tell me which step or steps I should reject, and be precise about whether the trouble lies in a step that is invalid as stated or in a premise that is true but that I cannot afford to hold alongside the others. Then explain why the offending step looked compelling in the first place -- what makes it read like plain good sense -- and what it costs to give it up.

I have two suspicions I want you to test rather than assume. The first is that premises (3) and (4) trade on an equivocation between the sense in which an act is wrong and the sense in which a state of affairs is bad, and that the inference from (2) to (3) only goes through under a reading of "inviolability" on which violations are commensurable and aggregable across persons -- an assumption that may be doing the real work and that I may not be entitled to. The second is that the genuine pressure point is (5), and that rejecting it commits me to nothing worse than a restriction on an otherwise plausible maximizing principle. Think carefully, and argue for your conclusion rather than cataloguing the alternatives.
