ok so I'm giving the student commencement speech at my college next month — small liberal arts school, like 500 of us graduating, families in folding chairs on the lawn, whole faculty up front. I'm a philosophy major and I just finished a seminar on infinity that has completly broken my ability to write this thing. Every draft comes out as the standard speech: you're all unique, you're going to change the world, the future belongs to you. I don't think I believe any of it anymore, and I definately can't say it with my seminar professor sitting in the third row and my grandma flying in (she's already told me to keep it short and not depressing).

The seminar was about what happens to ethics if the universe turns out to be infinite. The argument we spent two weeks on goes like this: if space really does go on forever and matter is roughly evenly spread, then there are infinitely many people, so the total amount of good and bad in the universe is infinite. And then adding any finite amount to it doesn't change it — a world where infinitely many people each have slightly better lives is still just infinite, same as before. So if the point of a life is to add something to the total, our contribution isn't small, it's exactly zero. Second thing: in an infinite universe there'd also be infinitely many copies of every one of us somewhere out there, which makes the you're-the-only-you line pretty literally false. And the one that actually keeps me up: if there's even a tiny chance of something infinitely good or infinitely bad hanging on what we do, that chance should swamp every finite consideration, including everything I want to say to my classmates about the choices in front of them.

What I want from you is the actual five-minute speech. It has to be honest — I'm not willing to stand up there and say things I think are false just because it's graduation — but it also can't be a shrug (nothing matters, so do whatever; my professor would wince). Warm, funny at least once, it has to land for parents and grandparents who've never heard any of this, and it can't turn into a lecture. One more wrinkle: I speak right after the class president, whose whole speech is about how we're the generation that's going to fix everything, and I don't want to undercut her. And I need to be able to defend every sentence of it in seminar next week, so don't paper over the problem with something vague about how life is a mystery.
