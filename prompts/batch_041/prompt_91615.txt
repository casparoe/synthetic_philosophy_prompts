I'm chairing my department's hiring committee this year, and I got roped onto this regional consortium of department chairs too — it meets quarterly and I'm the new person, so of course I got handed the write-up. Here's the mess.

Two years ago the consortium (about a dozen departments in our region, all competing for the same PhDs) adopted a voluntary rule: no exploding offers. Meaning if we make someone an offer, it stays open at least three weeks, and nobody pressures a candidate to accept in 48 hours. The idea was to stop the whole thing where everyone makes offers in a panic and candidates get squeezed. It mostly worked the first year. Then this cycle, one of the bigger departments — I won't say which — made offers that expired in 72 hours, got their top two candidates, and then shrugged and said the rule was never binding anyway. I lost a candidate I'd been courting for months to a 48-hour ultimatum from a department I won't name. Two other chairs told me privately they're planning to do the same next cycle, because why be the sucker.

So now the consortium wants a decision memo from me for the next meeting. The options people are floating: (1) actually enforce it — some kind of penalty, maybe a fine or losing consortium privileges; (2) drop the rule entirely and let everyone compete; (3) some third thing, like a rule that if any member makes an offer, other members get notified and can match, which sounds insane to me but a few people like it.

What I want from you is the memo I'll circulate. I care less about "is defecting bad" (obviously it's bad, that's why we made the rule) and more about whether this thing we've built is actually the right kind of fix — is this a case where the honest move is to enforce harder, or is enforcement just going to fall apart anyway, or is the whole "no exploding offers" agreement maybe not even the thing we should be protecting? A friend of mine keeps saying the real problem is that we're all competing for a fixed pool of candidates and no rule fixes that, and I half believe her. Also, honestly, the candidate-side view is barely in this: candidates might actually like exploding offers if it means they get resolved fast, and I don't know if we're "protecting" them or just protecting ourselves from having to move fast.

Please write it so I can put it in front of the chairs. Use these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

- Bottom line (about 100 words) — what I'm recommending, up front.
- What happened (about 150 words) — the situation, plainly, for chairs who weren't there.
- What kind of trap is this, actually (about 350 words) — I want the memo to actually think about the structure: why each department's best move on its own is to defect, whether that's the same as everyone being worse off, and whether this is a genuine dilemma or just a coordination failure where we all want the same thing and keep missing it.
- Why my recommendation is the right call (about 350 words).
- The strongest case against me (about 250 words) — steelman it, don't strawman it. My friend's point about the fixed pool should get a real hearing.
- Motion for the chairs to vote on (about 150 words) — concrete, with a timeline and who does what.

Keep it readable, not a journal article. Assume the chairs know the situation but not any of the theory, so if you use a term like "coordination failure" or "race to the bottom" or whatever, explain what you mean by it in the sentence.
