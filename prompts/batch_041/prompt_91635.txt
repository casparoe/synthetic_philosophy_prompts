Ok so this is a half-formed thing I've been chewing on for a while and I'd like to know if it has a proper name, because right now I keep calling it "the two doors that look the same" and that's obviously not it.

The shape of it: an agent goes through a sequence of decision points, and because of how their memory works they can't tell the points apart from the inside — the first one and the second one present identically. So whatever rule they settle on, they're going to run that same rule at both. Fine so far. The part I care about is that the best rule often tells them to do, at this point, the thing that looks worse from here. And not for the usual long-run-averaging reason. It's that "here" is two places wearing one face, so the rule has to be decent in both roles rather than optimal in the one you happen to be standing in. The version I've run into is about a driver who can't remember which exit he's passed, but I'm fairly sure the driver is just the poster child and the general shape is something bigger.

Three things I can't fit into that picture:

(a) Suppose you could buy a cheap reminder of which point you're at — but only at the moment of deciding, and you can't un-know it once it's offered. The reminder can leave you worse off than not having it at all. So this is information that's bad to have. I know there's literature about preferring not to know things, but usually that's about the content being upsetting, or about commitment, and here it seems to be about the structure of the situation rather than what you learn.

(b) Make the forgetting lopsided. Say at the first point they're dead certain they're at the first point, but at the second point they're genuinely unsure which one they're at. Same phenomenon, or does it collapse into something more like playing a game against your own future self?

(c) Two agents doing this at the same time, each one's move touching the other's payoff. Now my rule for me has to be a rule for someone who is also guessing about me. I dont think it just stacks.

So: is there an established name for the general thing, or is it always handled case by case? And I don't want just the label. I want the edges — which of (a), (b), (c) are the same phenomenon and which are neighbours, and what the closest established ideas are and exactly where each one falls short of what I've described. If nothing quite fits, coin something and define it, including a couple of cases that should fall outside it.

Can you give it back to me as a JSON object with these fields:

- "established_name": the standard term if there is one, else null
- "verdict": one of "exact match", "partial match", "no match"
- "closest_established_concepts": a list, each an object with "name", "what_it_gets_right", "where_it_falls_short"
- "proposed_name": your coinage, or null if you think an existing term covers it
- "definition": one or two sentences, precise enough to exclude the nearest neighbours
- "variants": a list over a, b and c, each an object with "variant", "same_phenomenon" (true or false), and "why"
- "boundary_cases": two or three cases that should NOT count as this phenomenon, each with "case" and "why_not"
- "confidence": one of "high", "medium", "low", plus "what_would_change_it" as a single sentence
