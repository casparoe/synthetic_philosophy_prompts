I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology, and I've reconstructed an argument from my notes that I can no longer defuse. I'd like a committed diagnosis rather than a survey of positions.

(1) An agent's credences are defective if a bookie can assemble a set of transactions, each of which the agent's own credences give her no reason to refuse, that leave her worse off whatever happens.
(2) If her credence in p is x in (0,1), then a ticket paying 1 if p is, by her own lights, exactly fairly priced at x: she can cite nothing in her credences to refuse either to buy it at x or to sell it at x.
(3) Let p be a logical truth she has not established — a long tautology, say — and let her credence in p be 0.99. By (2) she will sell a ticket on p at 0.99: she collects 0.99 and undertakes to pay 1 if p. Since p holds, she pays 1 and is out 0.01 with certainty.
(4) So any agent who is less than certain of a logical truth is defective.
(5) No finite agent is certain of every logical truth.
(6) So every finite agent is defective, which is absurd; and the defect has nothing to do with her evidence or her values, only with which proofs she has happened to carry out.
(7) So one of (1) through (5) is false.

I'd rather not surrender (2), since the betting interpretation is the only thing that makes credence operational for me, and I'm not prepared to surrender (5), since I am not logically omniscient and don't think rationality requires me to be. So I take the damage to be in (1), (3), or (4), but I can't see what each repair would cost me. Tell me which premise to give up, why that one rather than the others, and what I lose elsewhere by giving it up — in particular, whether a repaired version of the argument still constrains anyone, or whether it collapses into the harmless claim that incoherent credences are incoherent.
