I'm in a slightly odd spot here, so bear with me. I spent thirty-odd years as a combustion chemist — burner design, flame chemistry, the sort of work where you spend a decade learning precisely how a flame eats its way through a fuel — and I've just retired, and to fill the gap I've signed up to an evening class in medieval philosophy. We finish al-Ghazali's Incoherence of the Philosophers next Thursday and I've been lying awake over it. The chapter that's got me is the one on causality, the seventeenth, the one with the cotton and the fire, where he says the fire doesn't do the burning. As I read him, he isn't denying that we watch one thing follow another; he's saying that's all we ever watch, and that the necessity the philosophers tuck in behind it isn't something anyone has observed or could demonstrate. The burning is brought about on the occasion of the contact; the fire is a real occasion and not nothing; but the link itself isn't a necessary one. What we have instead is a habit — God's habit, steady as anything, steady enough that we've built an entire chemistry on top of it — but a habit and not a bond. I notice he leaves it open whether it's done directly or through some intermediary, and I rather like that he doesn't shut that door. Our tutor mentioned Hume and everyone got very comfortable and started nodding, and I don't want a Hume poem; nor do I want a sermon about divine power. The thing only bites if the fire stays in the picture and you take the claim as strongly as he takes it.

So: what I'd like is a poem I can read out at the end of that last session, and I have a form in mind. A sestina — thirty-nine lines, six six-line stanzas and the little three-line envoi — with the six end-words being fire, cotton, habit, occasion, witness, again (that order in the first stanza, then wherever the form takes them; witness is in there for the poor soul standing at the side, who only ever gets the conjunction). The reason I want a sestina and not a sonnet is that the form can do the work for me: those six words keep coming back in an order that looks as if it were fixed by law, and yet nothing at all travels from one stanza to the next — every return is a fresh act, and the poem can't help showing that a pattern and a necessity are two different things. And the envoi, where all six words get crammed into three lines, is the moment you step back and see the habit whole, so please don't let that last stanza dribble away into something vague. I'd like someone in the group who's read the chapter to find it right — fire still the occasion, the pattern dependable, the necessity missing — and someone who's never heard of any of this to find it a good poem about fire and cotton.

Practical things. British English spelling and idiom please (I've been here all my life, but the last thing I wrote was a report for a Texan contractor and my head's in a mess). It'll be read aloud, so it has to work by ear. No epigraph and no lines from the translation — I'd rather hear you. No dates or titles or names in it either, and definately no Arabic terms; i know there's a word for the habit business and I've forgotten it, which is probably a sign it shouldn't be in the poem. And keep it out of the pulpit and out of the seminar room: no argument in verse, no winks at the reader, no tidy resolution.
