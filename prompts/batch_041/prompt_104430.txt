I've been arguing with a friend in a cognitive science reading group about sphexishness for the better part of a year, and since neither of us has moved an inch I would like a diagnosis of the dispute rather than another round of it. The phenomenon, as it is usually told: a female digger wasp provisions her burrow with a paralysed cricket, leaves the prey at the threshold, and goes inside to inspect the nest; if you nudge the cricket a few inches away while she is in there, she will retrieve it, drag it back to the threshold, and — because her behavioural sequence has been reset — inspect the nest again, and will do this as many times as you care to repeat the trick. We both know that the anecdote has been challenged by later fieldwork suggesting more flexibility than the story allows, and that the persistence of the example in the literature may owe more to its rhetorical convenience than to its accuracy. That does not seem to matter to either of us, which is itself part of what I want explained. My friend takes the wasp to show that a system can be exhaustively explained by its mechanism, holds that 'sphexish' names a real and important category, and thinks human freedom is interesting precisely because we are not that. He has a further move I cannot decide about: he says a system can be flexible within a fixed repertoire and still be sphexish at the level above — flexibility that is itself a routine — and that this is where the real work gets done. I think 'sphexishness' names a difference of degree rather than of kind, that the wasp's loop and my own habits differ only in how easily the loop is broken, and that if you push the notion of a mechanism hard enough it swallows everything, including the very flexibility we are using to define it.

So: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about how the word 'sphexish' ought to be used, or are we answering two different questions under one name — he a question about what an explanation has to do, I a question about whether anything is free — without noticing? If it is substantive, is it an empirical question about insect cognition that better fieldwork could settle, a conceptual question about whether any behaviour can be non-mechanical, or a clash of starting intuitions that neither of us can argue for? My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not as a conclusion, is that we both inherit an unexamined assumption that 'mechanical' and 'flexible' are exclusive, and that the interesting philosophical move is to deny it — but I would much rather you told me the hypothesis is wrong than agreed with me politely.

Given your diagnosis, tell me what, if anything, would count as progress here: a distinction, an experiment, a formal result about what counts as a mechanism, a redefinition of the term, or a reason to think the question should be dropped altogether rather than answered. Please commit to one diagnosis and defend it against the obvious alternatives, rather than laying out the possibilities and leaving me to choose; where the diagnosis depends on a contested reading of the wasp literature or of the notion of a mechanism, say so plainly and tell me how much rests on it.
