A friend and I spent most of last weekend arguing about secession — whether a region inside a consolidated state can ever have a right to leave, and whether that right is legal or merely moral — and the argument kept circling back to a text neither of us could settle. I have been reading Vattel's The Law of Nations in the eighteenth-century English translation, and in the early pages of Book I I keep running into three passages that seem to pull in different directions. First: "Every nation that governs itself, under what form soever, without dependence on any foreign power, is a Sovereign State". Second, on unions: "Finally, several sovereign and independent states may unite themselves together by a perpetual confederacy, without ceasing to be, each individually, a perfect state. They will together constitute a federal republic: their joint deliberations will not impair the sovereignty of each member, though they may, in certain respects, put some restraint on the exercise of it, in virtue of voluntary engagements. A person does not cease to be free and independent, when he is obliged to fulfil engagements which he has voluntarily contracted." And third, on the other side: "But a people that has passed under the dominion of another is no longer a state, and can no longer avail itself directly of the law of nations. Such were the nations and kingdoms which the Romans rendered subject to their empire; the generality even of those whom they honoured with the name of friends and allies no longer formed real states."

What I want is help reading this as a connected argument rather than as a grab-bag of maxims. Specifically: what work is "no longer a state" doing in the third passage — is it a moral verdict, a legal consequence, or a sociological observation about which entities the law of nations can address at all? And how does that square with the second passage, where union is described both as perpetual and as founded on voluntary engagements, with the analogy to a free person who remains free while bound by promises he has made? My friend thinks the analogy is decisive in favour of a right of withdrawal, since a promise made in freedom cannot extinguish the freedom that made it; I think "perpetual" is doing real work and that Vattel may simply be describing the dissolution of a body politic as something that happens to a state rather than something a part may claim as a right. I would like you to lay out the possible readings of this trio of passages — including one in which secession appears only as a factual event the law of nations registers after the fact, one in which the constituent part retains a right it can reactivate, and one in which sovereignty is alienated at union and cannot be reclaimed — and say which reading the text best supports and what an interpreter would have to import from outside it to reach the others. Then, briefly, tell me how far this eighteenth-century framework can be stretched to illuminate the disputes I actually care about: a union originally formed by an agreement between two parliaments, a region whose autonomy statute sits inside a constitution that declares the nation indivisible, and a province whose highest court was once asked whether it could secede unilaterally. I am not looking for citations or a reading list; I want the conceptual map and a judgement about where the text runs out and the philosophy has to take over.
