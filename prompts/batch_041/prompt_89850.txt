I'm helping a small foundation design a three-year program for roughly six researchers — half philosophers of mind, half physicists or philosophers of physics — on the ethical implications of panpsychism and pan-proto-experientialism, with the specific aim of asking whether anything in fundamental physics can ground claims about suffering. Take as fixed background that the truth of panpsychism will not be settled within the funding period; the question is what to do given that it might be. Which open questions in this area most deserve the program's effort, and in what order?

Treat at least these as candidates, and add any I've missed: (a) the valence problem — what would distinguish physical states that feel good or bad from those that feel nothing, and whether valence is an extra physical property, a functional role, or a confusion; (b) the aggregation problem — how welfare sums across vast numbers of micro-subjects with no obvious boundaries or common scale, and whether countless tiny subjects can outweigh a few large ones; (c) the constraint question — whether symmetries and scale-free features of fundamental physics bear at all on which processes could carry valence, or whether physics is simply silent on the matter; and (d) the upshot question — whether any human action could affect micro-level suffering, so that the inquiry has practical consequences rather than being purely theoretical.

I want the reasoning more than the list: for each question, why it is upstream of the others, whether it is tractable for philosophers rather than physicists, how neglected it is relative to mainstream consciousness ethics, and what a development over the next three years would move it up or down the ranking. Where the ordering turns on a contested assumption, identify the assumption and say what would happen to the ranking if it failed.
