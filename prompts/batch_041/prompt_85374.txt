Next month I'm giving a work-in-progress talk at the joint seminar that our computational neuroscience group runs with the theory people down the hall. The room is about thirty people, and I know it well enough to be nervous about it: mostly lab scientists who fit encoding and decoding models to spike trains and fMRI data and who use the word computation the way other people use the word movement; three or four who work on attractor dynamics and low-dimensional state spaces and are openly allergic to representational talk; and one philosopher of science who comes because her office is next door and who will ask the only question in the room that is actually a philosophical one. This is a group that is impatient with philosophy that doesn't change what anybody does on Monday, and several of them have told me over coffee that my topic is a fight about a word. My thesis is that the claim that the brain is a computer is either trivially true or, in the sense the labs actually intend, unsupported. The argument I plan to run is this: any physical system at all can be described as implementing any computation you like, given a sufficiently patient mapping from physical states onto computational states, so the bare claim needs a further condition before it says anything; I propose that a system genuinely computes only when there is a mechanism whose function it is to carry information, and when the mapping from physical states to computational states is fixed by that mechanism's function rather than by the convenience of whoever is doing the describing; on that criterion the evidence does not settle whether brains compute, and a good deal of what gets called computational explanation is really a claim about how well our models predict, which I am happy to grant. I should be clear that I am not out to take anyone's models away — I build them myself — I just want the word to be doing some work.

Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, not the ones I would get at a philosophy of mind conference, ordered by how much damage they do to my position, each phrased the way the person who holds it would phrase it — I want to be able to hear who is talking before they finish the sentence — each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. I expect the dynamics people to say that if I am right then representation is the real culprit and my information-carrying function quietly lets it back in through the side door, and I expect the modellers to say that they never meant anything metaphysical by the word and that I am picking a fight with a figure of speech; tell me which of those has teeth and which I can afford to be gracious about. Tell me what to concede up front so that the room stops suspecting I am a closet vitalist, which single reply has to land or the whole talk is over, and the question I am hoping nobody asks.
