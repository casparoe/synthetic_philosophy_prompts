I want to pose a question about criminal law's voluntary-act requirement and its selective reception of neuroscience, and I want an answer that reconstructs an implicit theory rather than summarizing the free-will debate.

Consider two features of criminal practice. First, the law generally will not convict a defendant for a bodily movement that is not an act in the relevant sense: a reflex, a muscle spasm, a movement during an epileptic seizure, sleepwalking, or an automatism is treated as not the defendant's doing at all, and the defendant is acquitted without any need to establish that he lacked a general capacity for self-control or that he could not have done otherwise. Second, courts in a number of jurisdictions now admit neuroscientific evidence — a frontal-lobe tumor, evidence of reduced prefrontal gray matter, an abnormal scan — in mitigation at sentencing, and occasionally on the question of culpability itself, while those same courts continue to reject the broader claim, associated with Libet's readiness-potential experiments and with physicalist accounts of action, that conscious willing never initiates anything and therefore that no one is ever responsible.

These two features appear to sit uneasily together, and I want to know what the practice is committed to. What conception of the voluntary act, and of the relation between conscious intention and responsibility, does it presuppose? Does it require that the act be consciously initiated, or only that the movement be attributable to the agent's practical agency in some thinner sense? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out — for instance, that responsibility is a matter of character rather than of occurrent willing, or that it is a matter of statistical normality rather than of metaphysics? Why should a localizable lesion exculpate or mitigate when the universal facts of neural causation do not? Is the special treatment of brain abnormality consistent with the treatment of automatism, or do the two limbs of the practice presuppose different and incompatible theories of action? And I want you to distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with: tell me where it is underdetermined, so that several theories of action and responsibility would license the same results.

Finally, tell me what would have to change in doctrine if the presupposed view were replaced — first by a strict conscious-initiation requirement, then by a thoroughgoing eliminativism about the conscious will.

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of action, criminal-law theorists, and neuroscientists, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
