Having spent thirty-five years doing field ecology before I retired, and having spent the last several years reading philosophy of science with more enthusiasm than discipline, I have agreed to give a seminar next month to a joint session of our biostatistics group and the philosophy of science reading group, and I would like your help preparing for the discussion that follows. My thesis is a deflationary one: that the so-called replication crisis is not, at bottom, a frequentist failing that a wholesale migration to Bayesian methods would cure, and that the familiar frequentist-versus-Bayesian quarrel is largely orthogonal to the mechanisms that actually generate non-replicating results — forking paths through a garden of defensible analyses, stopping rules settled after a peek at the data, selective reporting, and the latitude to define the outcome once the numbers are in. My worry, which I will present as a claim to be defended rather than a settled matter, is that these same mechanisms reappear in Bayesian dress: a prior chosen with some awareness of the data, a Bayes factor reported for the single comparison that happened to look impressive, and optional stopping excused by the assurance that the posterior does not depend on when you stopped — an assurance that holds only when the prior and the model were genuinely fixed in advance, which in ordinary practice they rarely are. I also mean to lean on the observation that a result significant at the conventional threshold frequently amounts to only modest evidence once it is expressed as a likelihood ratio, so that the frequentist ritual and the Bayesian ritual are alike capable of dressing weak evidence as strong.

The audience, not the argument, is what worries me. There will be two committed Bayesians who can derive the standard default Bayes factors in their sleep and who understand the paradoxes surrounding diffuse priors and point nulls far better than I do; two unrepentant Neyman-Pearson frequentists who will hear the whole talk as an attack on the only framework that ever controlled error rates; and a larger bloc of empirically minded philosophers of science who are sympathetic to my deflationary line but who will want to know what a working scientist is actually supposed to do on Monday morning. Write me the preparation sheet I need for the question period. I want seven or eight objections, ordered by how likely they are combined with how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that particular interlocutor would actually phrase it — the Bayesians in the vocabulary of priors, marginal likelihoods, and coherence; the frequentists in the vocabulary of Type I error, power, and the sampling model; the philosophers in the vocabulary of underdetermination and evidence. For each objection, give me a two-minute spoken reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front before anyone asks, which single reply has to land for the talk to survive, and — the one I am quietly dreading — the objection I am hoping nobody raises. Please draft everything in American English.
