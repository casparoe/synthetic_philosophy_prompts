I sit on the patient-care quality committee of a rehabilitation hospital as the family and community representative; I have no clinical or academic training, but since my father's long illness I have been reading on my own about a strange condition called pain asymbolia, in which people clearly feel pain — they can point to it, rate it, and describe its character — and yet are not bothered by it, sometimes laughing or joking while it is being inflicted. That has left me unsure what exactly it is about pain that we are supposed to be relieving, and now the committee has to decide something concrete. Our physicians want to adopt, for a small group of patients with intractable chronic pain, a treatment that would leave the sensation untouched while removing the distress, so that the patient still feels the pain and still says so, but no longer minds it. The medical director calls this the proper aim of pain medicine; two family advisers on the committee call it silencing patients rather than helping them, and insist that a pain that no longer hurts is still a pain, and still a harm. I have to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting. Please draft that memo for me: a clear recommendation we can vote on, a time frame, and the conditions I should attach — how we monitor these patients, what consent has to cover, who decides if the patient later reports that the pain "is still there." Argue the bottom line rather than asserting it, and treat the objection of the two family advisers as the serious position it is, not a misunderstanding to be corrected.
