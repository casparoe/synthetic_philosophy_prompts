I've got completely stuck on Book 1, chapter 10, where he's trying to work out how big the Trojan expedition really was and starts wondering what some future observer would make of Sparta if it were ever emptied out. He says: "For I suppose if Lacedaemon were to become desolate, and the temples and the foundations of the public buildings were left, that as time went on there would be a strong disposition with posterity to refuse to accept her fame as a true exponent of her power." Then the line that really trips me up: "Whereas, if Athens were to suffer the same misfortune, I suppose that any inference from the appearance presented to the eye would make her power to have been twice as great as it is." And out of this he concludes that "no exact observer would therefore feel justified in rejecting the estimate given by the poets and by tradition of the magnitude of the armament."

So my question is about the argument, not the Greek. A friend of mine does contract archaeology and she's always going on about preservation bias, about how what survives is not a sample of what existed, and I thought I recognised that here — but then he does something that seems to go the other way, using the poverty of the physical remains as a reason to trust the poets and the tradition instead. That feels like it might be a trick: if ruins can under-represent a city and also over-represent a city, then apparently no look at the ruins can ever count against a large claim, and you get to keep whatever the tradition told you. Is that a fair way to read him, or is there a principled version of what he's up to that I'm missing?
