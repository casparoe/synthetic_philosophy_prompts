You are a political theorist with a specialization in Renaissance political thought and a working familiarity with the philosophical literature on argument by analogy. I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on Machiavelli's conceptions of fortune and virtù, and I need a careful audit of an analogy that a colleague of mine treats as decisive. Please work through this step by step. Begin with a short brainstorming pass in which you set out, in your own words, what the analogy claims and what argumentative work it is being asked to do; then produce the audit proper; then give your verdict. I am not asking for a survey of the scholarly literature, and I do not want a bibliography or a reading list — I want your own analysis, and where you are unsure of a textual detail you should say so rather than invent one.

Shared background, so that we are working from the same materials. Machiavelli treats fortune as a real and substantial force in human affairs, but not an omnipotent one; against it he sets virtù, the cluster of qualities — skill, nerve, decisiveness, the capacity to read a situation and act in it — that he credits in the founders of states and in the princes who succeed. He also distinguishes, or at least moves between, fortuna and occasione: the occasion, the opening that circumstance presents, which the great founders are said to have been given by fortune and which their virtù consisted in seizing. There is an older iconographic tradition, revived in the Renaissance, in which Occasion is figured with a lock of hair falling over her face and a bald patch behind her head, so that she can be caught as she approaches and never once she has passed; Machiavelli's insistence that a prince act decisively when fortune offers him an opening is often read against that background.

My colleague's argument runs as follows. In the chapter where Machiavelli addresses fortune most directly, he says that fortune is the arbiter of one-half of our actions and leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less, and he compares her to "one of those raging rivers, which when in flood overflows the plains, sweeping away trees and buildings," a river that cannot be stopped but can be managed, provided that men build defenses and barriers while the weather is fair, so that when the waters rise again they may pass away by canal and their force be neither so unrestrained nor so dangerous. From this my colleague concludes that Machiavelli's considered position is that fortune is not a genuine rival to virtù but a hazard to be engineered against: virtù, properly understood, is foresight plus durable investment in defenses, and the deepest lesson of the chapter is institutional — build the levees in good time, and the flood becomes an inconvenience rather than a catastrophe. On this reading, the closing passages, in which fortune is personified as a woman who must be beaten and coerced if she is to be kept in hand, are rhetorical surplus: they belong to the same chapter as the river but not to its argument, and they should not be allowed to govern the interpretation.

Audit this, addressing at least the following.

1. The correspondence table. Set out explicitly what plays the role of fortune, of the river in its ordinary state, of the flood, of the plain and everything on it, of the dikes and embankments, of the fair weather in which the works must be built, of the engineer who builds them, and of virtù. Where a term on Machiavelli's side has no counterpart on the river's side, say so rather than forcing one.

2. Where the mapping holds. Identify the correspondences that are doing genuine work, and explain what they get right that a reader without the image might miss.

3. Where it breaks. I care about the disanalogies that bear on the conclusion my colleague draws, not about an exhaustive inventory of the ways rivers differ from politics. Among the things worth considering: whether a fortune characterized chiefly by unpredictability can be prepared against by construction, since the builder of dikes must know in advance where and when the water will come; whether building the dikes is itself an exercise of virtù, and what that does to the picture of fortune and virtù dividing the field between them; whether a fixed defense answers a threat that changes shape, and what a breached dike leaves behind; whether the analogy can accommodate the adversarial and quasi-erotic quality of Machiavelli's fortune at its most personal; and what the image silently assumes about the inhabitants of the plain, who appear in it as terrain rather than as agents whose loyalty and resentment are among the things a prince must manage. Be explicit about which of these disanalogies are load-bearing for my colleague's conclusion and which are merely decorative.

4. The woman passages. Given that they stand in the same chapter as the river, is the dismissal of them licensed by the analogy, or merely convenient for the institutional reading? If the analogy cannot accommodate them, say what it would take to accommodate them, and whether that price is worth paying.

5. Verdict. Does the institutional reading survive the audit; does it survive only in a weakened or relocated form; or does the river image turn out to be carrying the conclusion rather than supporting it? If you think the conclusion is right but for reasons the analogy does not supply, say so plainly and name those reasons. Finally, if the river will not do the work my colleague wants from it, sketch the analogy you would put in its place, and say what the replacement buys and what it costs.

A closing note on what I want from the answer: a mapping that is stated rather than gestured at, a clear separation between the failures that matter and the failures that do not, and a verdict willing to be unflattering to my colleague or to me.
