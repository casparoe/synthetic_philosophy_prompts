I have no academic training, but I have been thinking on my own about how an identity statement can tell you something, and before I read any more about it I want the specification first. Not a theory — the list of requirements any theory in this area would have to meet. Give me the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the facts about how names actually work that it has to fit, each with the consideration that puts it on the list; say which of the requirements are genuine and which are only the commitments of one side; and flag any pair that cannot be held together.

The cases I keep coming back to, and would want covered: two names for the same thing learned in separate settings, where learning that they name one thing is a real discovery (a river that the people upstream call the Long Water and the people downstream call the Black River, and someone walks its whole length); a name with no bearer at all, used for decades in a family for a grandmother who turns out to have been invented; one name carried by two different people; a name used confidently on nothing but other people's say-so, by someone who could not say who it names; a name that outlives the thing it named, still used as though the thing were there; and a true identity that is a discovery to one person and nothing to another, since the fact that startles me when I recognise myself in an old newspaper story is a fact an archivist has known for years.

I am fairly sure something has to give here, and I want to know what. A theory that says the meaning of a sentence is shared by everyone who understands it seems to sit badly with the requirement that learning that the two names name one river differs in meaning from learning that the river is itself. If that pair really cannot be reconciled, say which one should be surrendered, and tell me which single requirement does the most to rule out the obvious theories.
