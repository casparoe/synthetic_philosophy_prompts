Ok so I'm suppossed to present my take on moral worth at a seminar next week and I want to check it for cracks first, because I'm fairly sure the pieces don't all sit together but I genuinely can't see where the fault line is. Here's the pile:

(1) An action has moral worth when it's done for the right reasons, and the right reasons are the things that actually make the action right — the features of the situation, not whatever the agent happens to believe about them.

(2) You don't need the motive of duty for moral worth. Acting out of love or sympathy for someone can be fully worthy — honestly, I'd say worth more than grinding it out because it's your duty.

(3) But you can't earn moral worth by accident. The agent has to at least implicitly see that what they're doing is right, otherwise the good result is just luck and they get no credit.

(4) The shopkeeper who's honest only because it's good for business gets zero moral worth for it. That one seems airtight to me.

(5) Moral worth is what makes an agent praiseworthy, and praise ought to track what's actually in the agent's control — their quality of will, not facts they couldn't have known.

(6) Take someone who helps a person hide from the authorities while sincerely believing they're doing something wrong, purely because they can't stand to see them hurt. I want to say that action has full moral worth.

So: which of these are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and for the real ones, what's the cheapest thing I'd have to give up and what would it cost me? Order them by how bad the break is rather than listing everything as equally broken. 350 words max.
