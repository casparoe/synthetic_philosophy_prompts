My sister is a patrol officer in a mid-sized city, and through her I got invited to give the commencement address at this years police academy graduation — 34 recruits, most of them from the same neighborhoods they'll be patrolling, plus their families, the chief, a couple of city council members, and my sister's entire shift sitting up front watching me like hawks. I teach philosophy at the community college and I've spent the last four years on our city's civilian review board, and I came off that board alot more conflicted than I went on. The chief knows I'm not a cheerleader; thats apparently why he asked. He wants twelve minutes, and he asked me not to make it about policy, which I agreed to mostly because I have no idea what else I'd do with twelve minutes.

Here's the thing I keep circling, and I want the speech to actually do something with it rather than gesture at it. There seem to be two very different claims that get run together in every argument about policing. The first is: sometimes people hurt other people, somebody has to be able to physically stop them, and that somebody can't be a committee. The second is: therefore we need a permanent, armed, uniformed, unionized profession with its own internal culture and loyalty and a standing legal monopoly on legitimate force. I think the first is close to undeniable and the second doesn't follow from it at all — and the second is the one the institution needs to be true in order to go on existing in its current shape, which is exactly why it never gets separated out. Almost every reform argument and almost every defund-or-abolish argument I've heard treats the two as one claim. What I need is something I can actually say out loud, in that room, that's true, and that doesn't either (a) flatter them into thinking the institution is basically fine and they personally are the good apples, or (b) tell them the thing they just spent six months of their lives training for is a moral catastrophe. I'm not interested in a speech that works by keeping the hard part offstage.

So write the twelve minutes. It has to land as an actual commencement address — warm, concrete, aimed at particular people, with at least one stretch that's about them and not about my doubts — and there's a moment at the end where families pin the badges on, so whatever you do has to leave room for that. The recruits should be able to hear it without being humiliated, and the review-board people in the room (there will be three) should be able to tell I haven't sold out. You can use my sister; she's the reason I'm conflicted and she'll know it if I pretend otherwise. Don't resolve the philosophical question for me, I haven't resolved it, but the speech should make it obvious that the question is real, and be honest about which parts I'm not willing to say to a room full of people who are about to be handed guns. Also please don't put words in the mouths of real people — no invented MLK quotes. If you need a line from somebody, make the somebody up, or use something I'd plausibly say myself.
