Next month I'm giving a paper to our ethics reading group and I need a Q&A prep sheet. The group is mostly Kant people -- two of them work on the Doctrine of Virtue, one is finishing a thesis on imperfect duties -- plus one act-consequentialist who enjoys playing the spoiler. You know the supererogation literature cold, so put on that hat, start by brainstorming what this particular room actually cares about, and then build me the sheet.

My thesis: supererogation is real, but we keep looking for it in the wrong place. The heroic cases -- the person who throws herself on the grenade, the one who gives up a career to care for a stranger -- are required once the stakes are what they are; calling that "beyond duty" just flatters us. The real supererogatory acts are the small-change ones: the extra ten minutes of patience, the kindness no one would have faulted you for skipping. What makes them supererogatory isn't that they're extraordinarily good, it's that they lie beyond what could reasonably be demanded of you. (I've been calling it the small-change view.)

Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this room, phrased the way they'd phrase them, ordered by how much damage they do, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is weakest. Tell me what to concede up front, what the one thing I have to land is, and give me the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
