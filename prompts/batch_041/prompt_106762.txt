I'm in a master's program in phil of science but my undergrad was econ, and I've somehow ended up as the person running the first session of a decision theory reading group that's mostly CS and econ grad students. Last time we burned two hours going in circles on Newcomb-type cases, and I'm now fairly sure the problem was that everyone was answering the question "one box or two?" while actually disagreeing about something else entirely. So I want to open with a diagnostic instrument instead of a paper: please write me a decision tree (or a flowchart, whatever renders more clearly as plain text) that takes a reader from a few commitments they can actually state about themselves to a named position on how you should choose under uncertainty. Build it from the logical space first — the branch questions and the combinations of answers they allow — and only then drop the names onto the leaves, so it doesn't just reproduce the standard list of -isms. I want the branch nodes to be things like: what is the object of rational evaluation in the first place (a single act, a rule or policy, or a whole decision procedure/algorithm)? When you evaluate an act, do you hold the rest of the world fixed including your own deliberation, treat your choice as evidence about how the world is, or treat it as one instance of a pattern that a theory should endorse across worlds where the same procedure gets run? Does the theory have to be usable by an agent who can't fully compute the consequences of its own output, or is it allowed to be an idealization no bounded agent could ever execute? And what kind of counterfactual is doing the work when you ask what would have happened if you'd chosen otherwise — causal, evidential, or something more like a logical fact about what an idealized version of your procedure returns? Phrase every node as a question someone could answer about their own view without already knowing which position it leads to; no node should be "so, do you think the answer is causal decision theory?".

For the leaves, I need at least these to show up: the act-focused causal view, the act-focused evidential view, the view that takes the whole decision procedure as the object (the one that says you should commit to the procedure that does best in the counterfactual branches where the prediction about you was wrong), and the bounded/computational view that says the real problem is that agents can't evaluate their own outputs, so "the rational act" is a fiction and the subject should really be what algorithms can achieve. Also include the deflationary leaf: the position that all the classic puzzle cases are artifacts of under-described problem statements, so the one-boxer and the two-boxer are answering different questions and neither of them is wrong. Please embed the cases in the instrument rather than naming them and assuming I'll remember them — spell each one out in two or three sentences inside the question itself: the boxes and the predictor, the coin-flip gamble where the predictor only pays you in the branch where the coin came out the other way, the hitchhiker who only gets picked up if the driver thinks he'll pay on arrival, the one-shot prisoner's dilemma where the other player is a copy of you, and at least one mundane everyday case where the act-focused views agree so the whole quiz isn't weird cases. Also throw in one where you can see the contents of the box before choosing, and one where the thing predicting you is a copy of your own source code running on a slower machine. 8 to 11 questions total, two or three answer options each, plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves — I definately want the key to be mechanical (each answer adds points to one or two leaves) rather than a hand-wave.

Two things I care about a lot. First, I suspect that "one-boxing" is shared by three or four quite different positions, so if the tree can't separate them, say so explicitly: tell me which pairs of leaves come out indistinguishable on the cases I've listed and what kind of case would seperate them, or admit there isn't one. Second, for the combinations of answers that no leaf occupies, tell me briefly whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just nobody's bothered to defend it — e.g. I'm curious whether anyone holds an evidential view that's about acts but also denies that your choice is evidence about anything upstream. Don't give me citations or a reading list, I'll add those myself and I'd rather you not guess at them. And at the end, don't just hand me the instrument and walk away — tell me which leaf you'd put yourself in, why, and what you'd say to the person standing at the node next door.
