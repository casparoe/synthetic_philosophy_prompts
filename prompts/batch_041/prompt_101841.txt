A close friend and I spent the better part of a long weekend arguing about weakness of will, and we ended precisely where we began, which is the only reason I am setting this down. His claim was that the whole phenomenon — the unfinished manuscript, the broken resolution, the third drink — is nothing over and above hyperbolic discounting in philosophical dress: describe an agent as having time-inconsistent preferences and no residue remains for the word akrasia to name. My claim was that this cannot be the whole story, since the akratic agent is supposed to act against their own better judgment, and an agent whose preferences merely shift across time has not acted against anything — they have simply changed.

What made the exchange maddening was that we could not locate the disagreement. He took it to be a question about the metaphysics of the self over time, and suspected that once we settle what a person is across time — a succession of time-slices, a single temporally extended agent, a bundle of overlapping concerns — the puzzle either dissolves or turns tractable. I took that to be a red herring, and the real crux to be normative: what it is for a judgment to count as one's better judgment at all, and whether such a judgment must be all-things-considered or may be merely pro tanto. Along the way we raised a scatter of further candidates — whether the akratic act must be free and intentional in the full sense, whether precommitment devices of the Ulysses-contract sort change the analysis or merely relocate it, whether diachronic preference reversal is best modeled as a special case of bargaining among one's temporal selves — and could not agree on which of these were the load-bearing ones.

Treat this as an agenda-setting problem, not a survey. Suppose a single philosopher had three or four years and the standard conceptual toolkit — analysis, careful case work, engagement with the empirical psychology but no experiments of their own — and wanted to work on the seam between weakness of will and time-inconsistent choice. Which open questions should they actually pursue, and in what order? I care far more about the reasoning than the list: for each question, why it is upstream of the others, whether it can be settled by argument rather than data, how much of the surrounding field would move if it were answered, and what argument or evidence over the next couple of years ought to push it up or down the ranking. I am especially interested in whether the metaphysics of personal identity over time is genuinely upstream, as my friend insists, or whether it is a distraction that dissolves under scrutiny — and I would like you to say plainly which of the two of us, if either, has hold of the more promising thread.
