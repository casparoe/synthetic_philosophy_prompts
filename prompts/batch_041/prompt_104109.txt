I'm teaching a small graduate seminar this term on the ethics of truth-telling, and we've been working through Augustine's On Lying (De Mendacio) in one of the old public-domain translations — the section numbers I give below are that translation's. I came in thinking I had a clean grip on his definition of a lie, and the text has knocked it lose. I'd like help reading one stretch of it closely. The stretch is section 3, and the sentences I keep circling are these: "Wherefore, that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind. Whence also the heart of him who lies is said to be double; that is, there is a double thought: the one, of that thing which he either knows or thinks to be true and does not produce; the other, of that thing which he produces instead thereof, knowing or thinking it to be false." Then a bit later: "Whence it comes to pass, that he may say a false thing and yet not lie, if he thinks it to be so as he says although it be not so; and, that he may say a true thing, and yet lie, if he thinks it to be false and utters it for true, although in reality it be so as he utters it. For from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie." And then: "Therefore he who utters a false thing for a true, which however he opines to be true, may be called erring and rash: but he is not rightly said to lie; because he has not a double heart when he utters it, neither does he wish to deceive, but is deceived. But the fault of him who lies, is, the desire of deceiving in the uttering of his mind; whether he do deceive, in that he is believed when uttering the false thing…" (That last sentence runs on a good deal longer; I've cut it where my patience gave out.) Earlier, in section 2, he has already said: "Setting aside, therefore, jokes, which have never been accounted lies, seeing they bear with them in the tone of voice, and in the very mood of the joker a most evident indication that he means no deceit, although the thing he utters be not true".

What my students and I can't agree on is how the two things doing work in here are related. There's a mismatch condition — words that don't answer to what's in the speaker's mind, the "double heart" — and there's an intention condition — the "desire of deceiving." Is the mismatch the essence and the deceptive intent merely a symptom, or the reverse, or does he really mean both at once? He's emphatic that the deception needn't succeed, which pushes the second condition toward pure intent; but then the poor fellow who sincerely asserts a falsehood gets let off by both tests simultaneously, and that smells like a coincidence rather than a design. I've been trying to smoke him out with two seperate cases I invented for class, neither of which he discusses anywhere: (1) the transparently insincere compliment — my mother-in-law serves a cake I can't stand, I tell her its delicious, and everyone at the table including her knows perfectly well I hate it, so I'm not trying to fool anyone, I just want dinner to go smoothly; and (2) the technically true misdirection — a doctor tells a patient her scan "came back normal," which is true of that scan, knowing and intending that she'll conclude she's in the clear, when he's actually ordered a follow-up because he's worried. Under the mismatch test (1) looks like a lie and (2) looks like it isn't one; under the intent test (1) looks like it maybe isn't one and (2) looks like one. They cross over, which means either I'm misreading one of the tests or Augustine's definition is doing more work than he lets on. Please don't just hand me the usual "Augustine holds that every lie is a sin" line — I know that, and it isn't what's confusing me — and I don't need a reading list or anything like that; just your best shot at the text itself.
