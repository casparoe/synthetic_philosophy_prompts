**What makes every 'a' an 'a'?**

Put a blackletter 'a', a double-storey 'a' out of a serif, and a single-storey 'a' out of a geometric sans next to each other and nothing is shared. Not one stroke, not one curve, not one corner. The double-storey one is a bowl with a lid and a hook; the single-storey one is a circle that's been given a leg to stand on; the blackletter is a bundle of splinters that a layman would call an 'n' or a 'u' before anything else. If 'a'-ness were a shape, we'd have to give the shape up.

The usual rescue is to say: not the shape, then — the features, at the right level of abstraction. A bowl with a stroke on the right, say, or a bowl with a stem that doesn't ascend. But the level of abstraction always gets picked after the fact. Set the bar low and 'd' and 'q' and 'g' and 'b' all come through; set it high and you lose the blackletter. And the same exercise for the 'g' behaves differently, because 'g' has two utterly different standard bodies and nobody minds, whereas an 'a' with the wrong number of storeys makes people mutter. So the abstraction level isn't doing the work either; something else is.

Here's the move I want to make. 'a'-ness isn't a property of a shape, it's a property of a shape's position in a system of contrasts. A shape is an 'a' when it's the one doing what the 'a' does in that alphabet — the slot it fills is defined by everything it has to be distinguished from. Nothing inside the glyph fixes it. What fixes it is everything it isn't.

Which is where it gets fun, because that makes identity relational, and therefore movable, and the alphabet is riddled with near-collisions held apart by a hair. 'b' and 'd' and 'p' and 'q' are one shape in four orientations. A triangle is still a triangle upside down; an 'a' isn't. A whole category whose members are kept apart by which way up they happen to be printed is a strange sort of category, and it's the sort of thing that makes me think the shape was never carrying the identity in the first place.

Then there are what I've been calling orphan features. In most fonts serifs do no identity work at all — strip them off and everything's still legible. But in some geometric sans faces the capital 'I' and the lowercase 'l' are literally the same bare stem, and the only thing telling you which is which is where they sit in the word: context, not glyph. Meanwhile in other fonts a tiny flag on the '1', or a tail on the 'l', is carrying the entire burden for exactly one pair of letters and doing nothing anywhere else in the alphabet. Whether a feature is decorative or load-bearing isn't a property of the feature. It's a property of the alphabet it lives in. I think that's the strongest thing going for the view I'm pushing.

There's a real version of this. Hofstadter's group had a project called Letter Spirit, where you give the program a couple of letters drawn in a style it's never seen and it designs the rest of the alphabet so that the whole set hangs together. The interesting part is that the program has to work out which features are style and which are identity — and it can't do that from one letter, because a single letter doesn't tell you which of its own features are the ones it needs. (That sentence is doing a lot of work and I'm not sure it earns it.)

The bit I'm least sure of: if identity is entirely relational, then a font whose 'a' and its 'o' are drawn identically is a font with a collision, and the system view says there's no fact of the matter about which is which. That sounds right to me, but I can't tell whether it's a consequence of the view or just the view restated. Same problem with the cross-system case: I recognise an 'a' in a blackletter I've never seen before, and I want to say it's because I can see what it's being contrasted with — but I've only got the one letter in front of me, so where's that coming from?

And the deeper worry, which is the seperate question I keep circling: does this collapse into "a letter is whatever we agree to call a letter", with the system talk doing no real work? I don't think so, because the alphabet constrains what can be swapped and what can't. But I'd like the objection stated as strongly as it can be.
