I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I've been asked to give the toast at my advisor's retirement dinner. She is retiring after forty years of arguing that there is no Cartesian theater — no single place in the brain where everything comes together for an inner audience — and that mental life is better described by the multiple drafts model, on which contents become conscious by winning out in a competition among parallel processes rather than by being displayed on a screen. Lately she has put the point this way: a self is a center of narrative gravity, real and indispensable, but no more a physical thing inside the head than a story's protagonist is. She has asked me, only half joking, not to sum her up, since summing up assumes there was a finished thing to sum. The trouble is that a toast is exactly a summing up: it puts one character at the center and gives her life a shape. Roughly half the room will be philosophers and cognitive scientists who will notice if I smuggle the theater back in — no breakthrough moment where it all clicked, no claims about what she was really like deep down — and the other half will be her mother and cousins, who want warmth and a story they can follow. Write me a five-minute toast, in American English, that honors her without reinstating the picture she spent a career dismantling, and that lands for both halves of the room.
