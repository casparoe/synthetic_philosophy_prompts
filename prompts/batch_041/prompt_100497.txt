I run pricing for a small specialty insurer — we write the weird end of the market, event cancellation, drone operators, niche liability, that sort of thing. Team of four, and I'm the one who signs the rate, so when we disagree about a number it lands on my desk. The fight we have every single week is about the reference class. Someone pulls a broad number (all commercial aviation, all cyclists, all small contractors), someone else pulls a narrow one (this exact aircraft type, this exact city, this exact occupation), the two numbers differ by a factor of five, and we end up arguing about whose class is "the right one" until I just pick. I want to stop doing that by vibes.

So: write me a decision procedure my team can actually run, in order, before we quote. It should be the kind of thing a junior analyst can follow without having read any philosophy — keep whatever the reference-class problem actually teaches, drop the parts that don't survive being made operational, and tell me honestly where the procedure is just dressing up a judgment call. Specifically I want the ordered questions, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops and says no, and where it hands the decision to me personally because it's a values or risk-appetite call rather than a facts call.

Two things I want baked in, because they're our actual constraints. First, we have to be able to defend the class we chose to a regulator after a claim goes bad, so the procedure has to make us pick the class before we compute the number and record the alternatives we rejected and what they would have given — no peeking at the answer and then choosing the class that fits. Second, we almost never have enough data in the narrow class, so the procedure has to say what to do when the most specific class you'd like is too thin to trust, rather than just defaulting to the broad one or the narrow one.

Then run it on three cases and show your work:

1. A life/disability quote for one specific 52-year-old commercial helicopter pilot, 4,000 hours, no incidents. Broad class is all commercial pilots; narrow is helicopter pilots of that type in that region.

2. A brand-new risk we've never written: a company flying drones to inspect rooftop solar in a hail-prone metro. There is basically no loss history for this. Broad-ish proxies exist (small aircraft, roofers, general commercial liability) and they disagree a lot.

3. The one I expect to hate. A cargo-bike courier fleet in a city that just banned most cars downtown. The historical bike-accident data says cheap; the argument that the ban changes everything says expensive. I want to know what the procedure makes us do here even if the answer is "we can't responsibly price this" or "load a margin so big sales walks."

Be committed — give me one procedure with a definite ordering and defend the ordering, don't hand me a menu of approaches and let me choose. Where you genuinely can't decide between two orderings, say which you'd pick anyway and why.

Format it like this. Give me the procedure first as a table with these columns: Step number, The question to ask, What counts as a good enough answer, Where it goes if the answer isn't there (next step, stop, or hand to a human). Then, for each of the three cases, a JSON object with these fields: case (short name), classes_considered (a list, each with name, estimate, and data_size), class_chosen, rate_or_range, confidence (low/medium/high and one clause why), judgment_calls (list of the points where the procedure couldn't decide it for you), and what_would_move_it (what new evidence or data would change the choice). Finish with a short plain paragraph on what this procedure cannot do — the places it will misfire or where it's just laundering my risk appetite as math.
