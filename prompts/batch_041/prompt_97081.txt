I teach a unit on spacetime ontology in an upper-level metaphysics seminar — 14 students, mostly seniors, two master's students, one 80-minute session, and they've got enough logic to follow a proof but no differential geometry whatsoever. Every year I do the hole argument as a lecture and every year it washes over them: they nod along, somebody says it's just relabelling, and we move on with nobody actually having felt the problem. So this year I want to run it as an activity instead and let them walk into it. Design it for me: materials (I have a projector, whiteboard, index cards, a big roll of tape, and whatever's lying around the room), roles, rules, a timeline that fits the 80 minutes, and a scoring or payoff scheme that gives the group an actual reason to care which answer they end up giving rather than shrugging. The thing I want them to feel is the specific moment where they've already committed to the points of the manifold being genuine individuals — and they've committed to it casually, because it's the obviously sane thing to say — and then the relabelling inside the hole leaves them with only bad options: either the theory is indeterministic, or two descriptions that look like different worlds are secretly the same world. So the setup has to make the individualist commitment feel natural first, before anything exposes it. Don't have me stand up and announce what's coming; the rules and incentives should do the work, and I'd rather they construct the awkward choice themselves than that I hand it to them. If it helps, one seed I keep coming back to is giving them a physical "world" made of identical unmarked tokens laid out on a board with property cards attached, plus a designated sub-region that's the hole, and letting a rearrangement happen there — but take that or leave it, you know the target better than a mechanic does.

Then the debrief, which is where I think most versions of this go soft. I want the mapping from what happened in the room onto the argument, the escape routes people actually reach for and where in the activity they reach for them (the "I simply won't count relabelled worlds as different" move, the "but I'm a structuralist about the points anyway" move, the "the points aren't real, only the relations are" move), and a clear line between the escape that costs you something and the one that's just changing the subject. One thing I really care about: the activity must not collapse into the much duller point that a theory can be underdetermined by evidence. I want them to see why this is specifically about the identity of the spacetime points and not about two rival theories fitting the same data, and if your setup can't keep those two apart, tell me how to patch it — or tell me flatly that it can't be done, I'd rather know. Also be honest about what the thing can't reproduce: I'm aware the real argument is about a metric field satisfying field equations and about invariance under a whole class of transformations, not about swapping labels on a strip, so tell me which part I'll still have to supply from the front of the room. And finally, what will predictably go wrong the first time — my guess is one cluster of students will refuse to play along and declare the whole thing a language game, and I need a way to handle that in the moment that doesn't derail the debrief.
