I teach a 50-minute session in an intro philosophy of mind course, about 20 students, and I want to run an activity that makes the Knobe effect land in their gut instead of just being a quirky finding I describe. The catch: they should generate the asymmetry themselves before anyone names it, and they shouldn't realize two of the cases are the same case until the debrief.

Here's my rough idea, tell me if it works or give me something better. I hand out several short scenarios, each with a side effect the main character doesn't care about -- one where the side effect is bad (some chemical wrecks the river, chairman only cares about profit), one where it's good (same indifference, river ends up cleaner). Everyone answers, privately and fast, whether the chairman brought the side effect about on purpose, and stakes a small amount (a token they'd rather not lose) on their answer to force a gut call rather than a philosophy-student hedge. Then I have them defend their answers to a partner, tally everything on the board, and only then reveal that the pairs are structurally identical -- same indifference, same foreknowledge, only the valence flipped.

What I need from you: a runnable version with the scenario text, the staking/tally mechanics, a 50-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps each moment onto the effect -- the foreknowledge-and-indifference structure, the valence flip, and the fact that the asymmetry shows up in their own judgments and not just other people's. Tell me what predictably goes wrong when I run it (I suspect some students will just refuse to answer "on purpose" for either case, or the stakes will make them overthink and the effect will vanish), how to handle that, and be honest about what the activity can't reproduce -- I assume it can't settle whether the asymmetry reveals something about the concept of intention or is just a quirk of how people use the word "intentionally," but tell me what else it can't do. Please write it in American English. And tell me how confident you are in the claims you make about the effect, and which single point you're least sure of.
