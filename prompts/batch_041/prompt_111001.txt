I am a rising senior in high school, and I have just finished a two-week summer seminar on formal systems and logic puzzles. The seminar ends with a showcase for families, and the instructors have asked me to give the closing address. I would like you to write that address for me.

The room is mixed, and this matters. Present will be the roughly twenty students who took the seminar and who spent two weeks on this material; the two instructors; and perhaps forty parents, grandparents, and younger siblings who have never encountered any of it. The address has to be followable by the families without becoming tedious for the students, who will notice at once if the mathematics is fudged or slowed down for their benefit.

The spine of the address is the MU puzzle, which we met in a book by Douglas Hofstadter. You start with the string MI, and you may rewrite it using four rules: add a U to the end of any string ending in I; double whatever comes after the M; replace any three consecutive I's with a single U; delete any two consecutive U's. The goal is to reach MU. We worked on this for two weeks, filling a notebook with failed attempts, and it cannot be done. What settles the matter is not a cleverer attempt but an invariant: only two of the four rules alter the number of I's, and one doubles that number while the other reduces it by three, so starting from a single I the count is never a multiple of three, whereas MU has none at all. Please state this reasoning accurately; the entire address rests on it, and the students will catch any slip.

Here is the argument I want the address to make, and I would like you to build it around this rather than around the occasion.

Tell the story of the two weeks honestly: the notebook of dead ends, and the fact that on the eleventh day I was certain I had a derivation and it turned out I had applied the three-I rule to two I's. The point of the story is that from inside the system, the accurate description of our situation was never impossible but only not yet, and no amount of further searching would have changed that. What ended the search was not more searching but a statement about every string the system can ever produce, made from outside it, which the system itself cannot formulate.

Then make the distinction I care about most, and let it carry the weight of the speech, because three different things get called jumping out of the system and they are not the same. First, finding an invariant: leaving the rules untouched and identifying something they can never change, so that a conclusion follows about all possible derivations at once. Second, changing the rules, for instance adding one that yields MU in a single step: that does not solve the puzzle, it dissolves it, and it is a cheaper and quite different move, though it is what many people seem to have in mind when they urge us to think outside the box. Third, noticing that the proof of impossibility belongs to a vantage point the system cannot occupy, so that the system is silent about its own limits, which is why the puzzle is unsettling rather than merely clever.

For the ending, I do not want the customary commencement uplift, and in particular I do not want the line about always questioning the rules, because in a room containing my friends' parents it will be heard as advice to break rules, which is not what I mean and not what I think the puzzle shows. What I want instead is the honest and somewhat uncomfortable conclusion: every jump out of a system lands you inside a larger system with a blind spot of its own. The invariant argument is itself ordinary arithmetic, and arithmetic has its own MU. Jumping out relocates you; it does not deliver you to the top. I want the address to say that plainly, and still to say that the jump is worth making, without pretending it is an escape.

A few constraints. Length: 650 to 800 words, roughly five minutes spoken. Voice: first person, mine; I am seventeen, so nothing that sounds like a TED talk or a college application essay. No citations, no reading lists, no quotations, and no further names or titles beyond the single mention of the book we read. And please avoid, besides the lines I have ruled out above, any version of the real box was inside you all along.
