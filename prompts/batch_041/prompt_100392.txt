I'm a science-and-ideas journalist putting together a feature on the strange afterlife of Gödel's modal ontological proof — the argument he sketched privately in his later years and that logicians and philosophers of religion have since formalised and fought over. I want the centrepiece of the piece to be a dialogue, and I'd like you to write it.

The two speakers: one defends a Gödel-style modal ontological argument, the other rejects it. Both are first-rate modal logicians; neither is a fool or a caricature, and it would be more interesting if the critic is not a stock militant atheist but someone who grants S5 and takes the divine attributes seriously, while the defender is not simply reciting the more familiar Plantinga-style line. Please keep clear of the well-worn material — Gaunilo's island, the Kantian "existence is not a predicate" move, and generic theist-versus-atheist sparring. I want the exchange to live where the argument actually is.

In particular, I'd like the dialogue to turn on three things. First, that in the Gödelian version the possibility premise is not simply assumed but is supposed to fall out of the apparatus of positive properties together with the principle that a positive property is possibly instantiated; let the two disagree about whether that derivation does real work or merely relocates the assumption. Second, the notion of a positive property itself — whether it has enough content to be more than a placeholder, and what becomes of the argument if it doesn't. Third, the modal collapse that critics have extracted from the axioms, on which it threatens to follow that there are no contingent truths at all; have the defender face this squarely rather than change the subject, and say what a repair would take and whether the repaired proof still reaches its conclusion.

I'd also like one of them to press a line I've been mulling: that the argument is only as strong as the modal status of the divine attributes it begins from, so that anyone already willing to treat God as a rigidly designated necessary being is not being proved anything but is having a commitment unpacked. Let the other resist this.

Requirements: real back-and-forth, each responding to the actual point the other has just made, the exchange sharpening rather than repeating. Keep it readable for an intelligent general reader without watering down the logic. Have each speaker commit — I don't want a survey of positions with the reader left to choose; each should stake out a definite line and defend it, and where they reach an impasse they should say exactly which premise divides them and where each stands on it. End on a definite verdict about which move is carrying the weight, even if the two disagree about whether the argument is sound. Write in British English, around 1,200 to 1,500 words.
