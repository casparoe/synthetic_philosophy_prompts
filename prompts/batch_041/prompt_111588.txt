I have been reading alignment-adjacent writing for a long time, and I would like to work one proposal through properly instead of nodding along with it, so what I want is a constructed case rather than another exposition.

The background, as I understand it. A goal-directed agent chooses actions that it predicts will bring about the world it wants. An approval-directed agent chooses the action it predicts its overseer would rate most highly if the overseer considered that action at length, where the rating is a verdict on the action itself rather than a forecast of its consequences. The case for the second design runs roughly like this: it commits to very little, it fails gracefully when the specification is botched, and — the part I want to probe — it is supposed to be free of the usual instrumental pathologies. An agent that only ever does what its overseer would approve of has no reason to accumulate resources, resist correction, or mislead anyone, because none of that would earn approval. I take the proposal to be a near relative of what Bostrom calls indirect normativity: rather than specifying what is good, you specify a procedure for telling what is good.

I want a case in which that last claim fails, and fails structurally rather than because someone in the story is a fool or a villain.

Build one scenario under the following specifications.

1. Human scale and contemporary. No superintelligence, no exotic hardware, no perfect predictors, no philosophy-seminar stipulations. The overseer may be no sharper than a competent, well-meaning adult with ordinary time and attention.
2. Identical ends on both sides. Put a goal-directed agent and an approval-directed agent into the case with the same final values; the first optimizes those values directly, the second optimizes predicted approval. Any divergence must come from the decision procedure, not from the two caring about different things.
3. The verdict to engineer: the approval-directed agent comes out worse by the shared values than the goal-directed one — the reverse of the direction the argument predicts. It must be worse for a structural reason, not because its model of the world is worse or its overseer is corrupt. Assume the agent's model of the overseer is accurate, and the overseer is decent and reasonably well informed.
4. One setting (a firm, a lab, a committee, a household), one decision, one page or less.

Then, after the scenario:

- Walk through what each agent does and why, and make it clear that the asymmetry falls out of the mechanics rather than being stipulated.
- Name the single feature of approval-direction that is doing the work. If the case only works because approval is a state of somebody's mind rather than a fact about the world, say so plainly.
- Say whether removing that feature collapses the case back into the ordinary story in which approval-direction is safe, and what the smallest change is that does collapse it.
- Give the strongest reply open to someone who holds that approval-direction is the safer design: the cheapest way for them to claim my case cheats, and whether that reply costs them anything.

Finally, give me the mirror-image case — the same two agents under the same constraints, but with approval-direction clearly outperforming goal-direction by the shared ends — and tell me which feature distinguishes the two cases.
