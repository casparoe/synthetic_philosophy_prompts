Can you work out the rule I'm really going by? Here's my gut on a bunch of cases.

1) Two basketball players. A makes 58% of his shots overall, B makes 49%. But split it into 2-pointers and 3-pointers and B beats A from both spots. My gut: B is the better shooter and A's 58% is basically a lie caused by A only ever taking easy layups.

2) My cousin did an after-school tutoring program. Kids who did the program scored lower on the final than kids who didn't. But inside every starting-ability group (low, mid, high) the tutored kids scored higher than the untutored ones. My gut: the tutoring actually helps, and the overall number is skewed because the kids who signed up were already the struggling ones.

3) A heart drug study. Overall, people on the drug survived more often than people off it. But if you split people by whether they had a heart attack during the study, then in both groups (heart attack and no heart attack) the drug people did worse. My gut: the drug helps, and this split is a trap -- the whole point of the drug is that it stops heart attacks, so "had a heart attack" isn't a fair way to divide people, the drug partly decided who landed in which group.

See, 2 and 3 look identical on paper -- overall says one thing, both subgroups say the other -- but my gut goes opposite ways, and the only reason I can give is hand-wavy stuff about "what you split by." Is that the whole answer or is there something real underneath it? Because if the rule is just "trust the split that isn't confounded" that feels like I'm sneaking the answer in.

4) Two high schools. School P has a higher overall graduation rate than School Q, but School Q graduates a higher fraction in each of its two tracks (college-prep and general). I keep flip-flopping on this one.
