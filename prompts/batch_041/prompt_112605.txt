For most of my working life I was a quality officer at a mid-sized hospital network, and I have no training in economics or philosophy. Since retiring I have been trying to make sense of something I watched happen over and over: the moment a number became the thing we were judged by, the number stopped meaning what it had meant. I have since learned that this pattern has a name attached to it, and that it is usually told as a single joke with a single moral — people cheat the measure, the measure dies. My own experience says there are at least two different things going on, and that they are not cured the same way. One is deliberate sabotage: people find the cheapest way to move the number, and the cheapest way is not the thing you cared about. The other is quieter. Nobody cheats. The number was a stand-in for something real, and once you start pushing the system toward the extremes of that number, the connection between the stand-in and the real thing simply gives way — the measure stops tracking because the world it was measuring has been rearranged by the measuring. A hospital can hit its target readmission rate by keeping people one extra day, and everyone involved can be entirely sincere, and the number is still dead. I want a short story that lives inside that distinction instead of gesturing at it.

The story should be about an institution that tries to escape the whole problem by keeping its standard secret. Everyone knows they are being assessed; nobody knows by what. I am not interested in the leak-and-scandal plot. I am interested in the subtler consequence of that particular defense: when people know a hidden standard exists but cannot see it, they begin optimizing for the existence of the standard rather than for any of its contents. They invest in becoming the sort of person such a standard would presumably reward. They cultivate the traits that a fair-minded judge, whoever that is, would notice. Secrecy does not make the target ungameable; it makes the target unnameable, which turns out to be worse, because now the gaming has no shape and no one can be accused of it.

Please write a short story, not a parable, and not an essay with characters in it. Some requirements, because I want the story to do work rather than decorate an idea I already have:

- Choose a setting where the quality being measured is partly unobservable even in principle — something like a seed bank, a search-and-rescue service, an orchestra's internal standard, a standards body for a craft, a wildlife trust. Do not use schools, policing, hospitals, or sales quotas; those are the examples everyone reaches for and I want to see the idea somewhere new.
- Include at least one character who sincerely believes the metric is healthy because the headline level has not moved, while the trend beneath it has been quietly manufactured. Let the reader see what that character cannot.
- Include at least one character who games the hidden standard without ever intending to, simply by trying to be good in the way they imagine it would be judged. Their sincerity should be the mechanism, not a disguise for cynicism.
- At some point the institution responds to a first failure by adding a second measure alongside the first. Show what that does. I am fairly sure it does not fix anything, and I want to see why, in the story's own terms.
- Have the institution eventually reveal something — the standard, or a decoy, or a fragment — and show that the act of revealing changes the quantity being measured, so that the before-and-after numbers cannot be compared at all. The people in the story should mostly fail to notice this, or notice it too late.
- End without a moral. No narrator stepping forward to explain the lesson. If the story has a point, let it be visible in what a character does in the last scene.

Roughly fifteen hundred to two thousand words. I would rather it be sharp and specific than comprehensive; pick one institution, one or two people, and one sequence of decisions, and let the general idea be visible through them.
