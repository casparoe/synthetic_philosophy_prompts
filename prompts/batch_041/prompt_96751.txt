For several years I've been following the arguments about fairness in risk scores, and what stays with me is the set of proofs showing that a scoring rule cannot at once give equally reliable predictions across groups and equal false-positive and false-negative rates, unless the groups start out with the same base rate or the score is perfect. I am not a researcher; I chair the allocation committee of a small mutual-aid fund, where we receive far more requests than we can fund and score each request by hand against a written rubric. I would like you to lay out the most important implications of these impossibility results for a committee like mine. Does the result constrain us even though no algorithm is involved, and even when the groups we care about are ones we defined ourselves? Which of our fairness aims are genuinely ruled out, and which are only in tension and could be traded against one another? And once we grant that we cannot satisfy every criterion at once, what is left for us to decide, and how should we decide it without dressing up a value choice as a technical one?
