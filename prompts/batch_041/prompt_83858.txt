My flatmate and I spent about four hours last night arguing about expected value fanaticism and I still cant decide who won. He's a full fanatic: always take the action with the highest expected value, no matter how silly it looks — if there's a one-in-10^30 chance that wiring him fifty quid saves a googol of lives, you wire the fifty quid. I think that's deranged, but I couldn't land a clean objection on him. By the end we'd sketched five ways out, and I want you to sort them for me:

1. Cluelessness — our estimates of the distant effects and their probabilities are so shaky that the numbers going into the calculation are basically noise.
2. Paralysis — the rule can't be acted on, because there's always some option with a hair more expected value coming from some microscopic probability, so it never tells you to stop deliberating.
3. Undefined infinities — once you allow infinite or non-measurable outcomes, expected value stops being well-defined, so fanaticism can't even state its own rule.
4. Self-undermining — he has to grant some small chance that fanaticism is false, and by his own logic that small chance swamps everything, so the view refutes itself.
5. His own fix, which he's convinced is orginal — a tiny probability only counts if you can actually trace the causal route from your action to the good outcome; no route, and you treat the probability as zero.

Rank these by how much they should genuinely move a committed fanatic — tiers or 0–10 scores, up to you — with a sentence on why each lands where it does. Then tell me which one you'd actually bet on, and whether his route rule is a real fifth option or just one of the other four wearing a hat. British English please.
