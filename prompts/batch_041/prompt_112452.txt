Ok so I need an essay on the lifeboat thing — you know the picture, boat built for fifty, hundred people in the water, and the claim that hauling even one more aboard sinks everybody including the folks already sitting down, so the tough-minded answer is you keep rowing and look away. It's been used for foreign aid, for immigration, for climate. Write me an essay on whether that's a fair way to frame the problem or whether the framing has already done the work before the arguement starts, and actually land on a verdict. No both-sides-have-a-point ending, that's the one thing I don't want.

Stuff I want you to get into:

- The setup seems to assume the boat's capacity is fixed and doesn't depend on who's aboard. What if it does — the swimmers can bail, row, fish, patch the hull, and more hands means more capacity up to some point? Does the harsh conclusion flip, or just get softer? Say which and why.
- It also assumes a cliff: fifty and everyone lives, fifty-one and everyone dies. Almost nothing real works like that, it's a slope. Does the argument survive on a slope, and if it does, is it still the same argument?
- The seats are already occupied when the story opens, and the story never asks how. Does the provenance of the seats matter? Like if the people in the boat are the ones who burned the other boats, or if the water the swimmers are treading is water the passengers have been pumping out.
- Somebody has to be the one deciding who gets in. Who, and by what right? Can the argument just skip that question, or does it need an answer to get off the ground?
Then flip it on yourself: rerun the whole case where the water is rising, so capacity is shrinking no matter what anyone does, and the people in the water right now are the ones who'll be sitting in the boat later. Same verdict or a different one?
