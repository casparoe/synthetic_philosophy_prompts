I write novels, and for some years now I have also taught a fiction workshop. The next session is ninety minutes with twelve writers, in a room with movable chairs and no screen; the materials are index cards and a timer. What I want those twelve people to feel in their hands, rather than hear described, is the objection Bernard Williams presses against impartialist morality: that the man who saves the woman he loves because she is the woman he loves is then asked to add something to his motive, and that the addition is a corruption rather than a refinement. He acts; to be justified he must also be able to say that acting as he did was permitted, or required, or at any rate defensible on grounds that would hold for anyone; and the charge is that this second item does not belong in the deliberation of a person who loves her — it arrives as a foreign body. In my own trade the same thing appears as a rule of craft: a character who explains her love while she acts has stopped being a character and become a position. My writers can recite the complaint. None of them has ever had to do it.

Please design the session that makes it happen to them, and arrange it so that they arrive at the point themselves, from inside a scene, before anyone says Williams's name. I need the scenario brief (a page, of which one paragraph is the scene itself), the casting, the rules printed on cards, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief.

Constraints.

The engine is an actor's problem, not a debate. Participants must be doing something with their bodies and voices under a rule they cannot argue their way out of; argument, when it comes, should be an obstacle the exercise puts in their path rather than the substance of the exercise. One agent, one forced choice, one particular bond set against one anonymous claim. No levers, no tallies, no numbers, no trolleys, no lifeboats: the scenario must be a place and an hour in which a body does something, not a case. The rule I care most about is this — whatever general warrant the agent eventually offers must be spoken aloud in the middle of the action, in the actor's own voice, before the scene ends, so that the room can watch the warrant arrive, see where it lands and what it displaces, without anyone being told in advance that this is the point. Build in a way for the group to distinguish three things: the thought the character actually had; the thought she could have produced afterwards had someone challenged her; and the thought the moral system requires her to have as the ground of acting at all. The debrief must separate those three and say which one the objection concerns, and why naming the bond is not simply weighting a consideration more heavily than the stranger's claim. Twelve people, ninety minutes: work them in trios so that nobody performs for the whole room until the plenary.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — including anything that will turn the session into a seminar on utilitarianism — and how to handle it. And tell me honestly what the exercise cannot reproduce; in particular, whether it can show that the impartialist agent really does have the extra thought at the moment of acting, or only that he can produce it on demand once challenged. If it is only the latter, I would rather know that my exercise is staging a misunderstanding than discover it in front of twelve writers.
