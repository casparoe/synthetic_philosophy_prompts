My friend and me had a long fight last night about Hegel on war. She says the whole idea that war keeps a people from going soft and ossified is just romanticism with a system bolted on, and that anyone who takes it seriously has to swallow the metaphysics whole. I think something survives, but I can't say how much.

Give me your actual credence that this is defensible: that war can have a genuine ethical function for a state, in Hegel's sense — not merely that it sometimes has good effects, but that it's the moment in which a state's sovereignty is actualized against other states and in which finite goods like life and property are revealed as accidental, so that war can be justified as more than a necessary evil.

Decompose it. How much rides on (a) whether a people can be said to have an ethical health that war preserves, (b) whether the empirical story about long peace leading to corruption and ossification holds up, (c) whether states are the kind of thing that can have an ethical life at all, (d) whether Hegel's recognition-relation between states actually needs war as its moment of seriousness, and (e) anything else you'd add. Number and one line each, then the combination, then which term the final number is most sensitive to and what evidence or argument would move it most.

Under 350 words, and please don't just tell me it depends how you read him. I want a number.
