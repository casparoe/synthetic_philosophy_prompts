At a LessWrong meetup last month I watched a friend end an argument with a single sentence, and I still can't decide whether it was profound or merely fast. The argument concerned a mutual friend — call her R. — whose pattern is roughly this: at any given moment she prefers to keep to the plan she made for the week, and at any given moment she also prefers the deviation available right now, and the plan she made last week was itself the deviation available then. Someone at the table suggested that this showed R. was being irrational in a way that mattered, and my friend said, more or less: 'Look, anyone whose preferences loop can be pumped for money, and you can't be rational and be a money pump.' That seemed to settle it. When I pushed, he added that it makes no difference whether the agent is sophisticated about it — someone who sees the pump coming and pre-commits has simply paid the cost up front, so the loss is the same — and that the time-indexed version of R.'s case is just the same pump with the bookie standing in for her future self. Another person objected that a pump requires a counterparty willing to trade in both directions indefinitely and that real markets close; my friend replied that this was a fact about markets, not about rationality.

What I want is a menu, not a verdict. Lay out the distinct precise claims that 'you can't be rational and be a money pump' — and its usual companion, 'cyclic preferences are irrational because they can be pumped' — could be making, ordered from the trivial to the radical, and say for each whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible requirement, a contentious thesis, or simply false. I especially want the hinges made explicit: does the argument need the agent to be naive about the sequence, or merely to lack a commitment technology? Does it need her preferences to be stable across the trades — and if so, what exactly survives in the diachronic case, where the loop runs through time rather than among options available at once? Is the target preferences, or choices, or the pair? What does 'irrational' mean in each reading — exploitable, self-defeating, incoherent, or merely suboptimal for someone who does not care about the money? And for each reading, does it actually deliver what my friend needed, namely the conclusion that R., as described, is irrational in a sense that should move her or us?

Return a JSON object with exactly these fields: "slogan" (the slogan restated in its most neutral, assumption-free form); "readings", an array of five to eight objects ordered from trivial to radical, each with "label" (a short name), "precise_claim" (the thesis in one declarative sentence, no hedging), "claim_type" (one of: empirical prediction, requirement of rationality, conceptual or definitional, pragmatic advice), "status" (one of: trivial, substantive and defensible, contentious, false, not even coherent), "assumptions_needed" (an array of the assumptions that reading requires — e.g. naivety about the sequence, stability of preferences across trades, a willing counterparty, caring about the stakes at every stage), "supports_my_friends_use" (yes, no, or partly, followed by one clause of explanation), "best_case" (the strongest one-sentence defence), and "best_objection" (the strongest one-sentence objection); then "most_likely_intended" (which reading my friend probably meant); "diagnostic_question" (the single question I could put to him that would reveal which reading he is actually on); "diachronic_upshot" (what, if anything, changes when the loop runs through time rather than among simultaneous options); "verdict_on_R" (whether her pattern is irrational on any of the readings, and if so which); and "what_i_should_concede" and "what_i_should_push_back_on" (one or two sentences each). Keep every field to plain prose, cite nothing, and do not pad the list with readings that are the same thesis at different volumes.
