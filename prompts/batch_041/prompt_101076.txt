I'm preparing the closing session of a reading group on Daniel Dennett's case against qualia, and I'd like to end with a poem rather than a summary. The case I want it to work through is the one with the two coffee tasters — usually called Chase and Sanborn — who agree that the coffee no longer pleases them, but who disagree about why: one says the coffee has changed and his palate has not, the other says his palate has changed and the coffee has not, and since each would describe the shift in the drinking in the same words, nothing either of them can introspect decides between them. Write a poem of five six-line stanzas. The last line of each of the first four stanzas should be a fixed refrain, one per stanza, and the four refrains should be the four marks traditionally claimed for qualia: that they cannot be conveyed in words, that they are intrinsic rather than relational, that they are private to a single subject, and that they are known to the subject simply by being had. The fifth stanza should consist of two new lines followed by all four refrains in reverse order, so that the marks that were supposed to secure the qualia end up being exactly what makes the dispute between the tasters unresolvable. Chase and Sanborn should be the only speakers until that last stanza; a third voice, asking what observable difference the disputed difference would make, may enter only there. The philosophy should be accurate enough that someone who has read Dennett will recognize the argument, and someone who hasn't will still find the poem whole.

After the poem, under the heading Commentary, in roughly 150 words, explain the formal choices — why these refrains, why this order, what the last stanza is doing — and note where the poem takes a liberty with the material or leans on a contested reading of it. In that commentary, state how confident you are in the claims the poem makes and single out the point you are least sure of.
