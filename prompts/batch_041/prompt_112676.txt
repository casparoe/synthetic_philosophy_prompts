I'm putting together a poem to open a reading group session on memory politics, and I want to try a pantoum on how the same consolation gets recycled across lost causes — Masada, Kosovo, the Confederacy, Dunkirk, pick whichever fit. The thing I keep circling is that these myths seem to need the loss to be total: a draw or a negotiated peace is unmemorable, so the story keeps bending toward the last stand, and then the last stand gets reread as proof the cause was pure rather than just beaten. So make the pantoum's interlocking repetition carry that — the second and fourth lines coming back as the first and third of the next stanza, so one reassurance passes from a siege to a battlefield to a beach in a new mouth — and close the last stanza back onto the opening line so it feels like a trap rather than a resolution. Near the end, let the form crack once, on the line that finally admits a defeat was just a defeat. Four quatrains, no dates, no lecture; someone who's read about the Lost Cause or about Masada should recognize the move, and someone who hasn't should still feel it. Then a short section titled Notes on the choices, 150-200 words, on what you did with the repetition and why.
