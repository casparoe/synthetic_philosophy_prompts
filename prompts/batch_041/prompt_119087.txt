I'm taking a political philosophy course in which we've been reading Albert Einstein's essay "Why Socialism?" alongside more standard defences and critiques of market economies. I can follow the broad shape of his argument — that capitalism's unplanned, competitive character produces insecurity and a kind of deformation of the individual, and that a planned economy would allow people to develop their capacities more fully — but I'm stuck on what looks like an internal tension. Einstein's central complaint is that capitalist society cripples the individual by making their livelihood depend on forces they can neither control nor understand; yet the remedy he proposes requires a centralised planning authority, which would seem to concentrate exactly the kind of uncontrolled power over individual lives that he objects to. He appears to be aware of this, since near the end of the essay he raises the problem of bureaucracy becoming over-mighty and asks how individual rights can be protected against it. But he does not, as far as I can see, resolve it.

What I should like is an explanation of how Einstein's position ought to be reconstructed so that this is not simply a contradiction. Is there a reading of his view of science and of the individual — his insistence that the scientist must be free from authority, his hostility to conformity, his confidence that social arrangements can be rationally ordered — on which the planned economy emerges as a natural extension of his liberalism rather than a betrayal of it? Or does the essay in fact contain an unresolved conflict between a socialist political economy and a liberal conception of the person? I am also unsure what to make of the mechanism he proposes for protecting the individual; I believe it involves some form of democratic counterweight, but I want to understand what work that is supposed to do, and whether it is meant as a genuine solution or merely as an admission that the problem is unsolved.

Please explain the conceptual issues here rather than giving me a reading list; I am trying to understand the argument, not to survey the secondary literature. It would help if you could separate the descriptive claims about how capitalism actually operates from the normative claims about what a socialist alternative would achieve, and say which parts of the argument depend on empirical assumptions that a reader today might reasonably question. I am especially interested in whether the tension I have identified is a defect in Einstein's particular version of socialism or a structural difficulty for any view that combines a planned economy with a robust concern for individual freedom.
