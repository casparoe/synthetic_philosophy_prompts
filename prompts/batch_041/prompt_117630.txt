I run a monthly discussion group at our local library — usually about fifteen people, none of us with any training in this area, and we have ninety minutes together. For some months I have been stuck on a question I cannot settle on my own, and I would like to build a whole session around it. The question is whether history could have laws in the way that nature does — whether there is, in principle, a set of regularities from which the future of a society might be worked out, as we work out an eclipse.

Three things keep troubling me. First, whenever someone claims to have found a pattern in history — an arc, a cycle, a direction — it looks like a trend read off one particular stretch of the past and then treated as a rule that must keep holding. Second, and stranger, the moment such a prediction is made and believed, the people it concerns seem to change what they do, so that the prediction bends the very thing it was meant to foresee, sometimes making itself come true and sometimes making itself fail. Third, the future will contain discoveries and inventions that no one now alive could anticipate, and these seem to be precisely what most changes the course of events.

What I want, more than an explanation, is for the group to run into this themselves. Please design a single ninety-minute activity — a forecasting game or a structured role-play, whichever serves better — in which the participants have to predict how some social situation will unfold, and in which the rules are arranged so that the predicting and the acting interfere with one another. The feeling I am after is the difference between two sorts of claim: the kind that says what will follow if we do a particular thing, and the kind that says what is going to happen, full stop. I want them to discover, in the playing, that the first kind can be made to work while the second cannot, and to sense that the reason is not simply that the world is complicated or that they lack information. I would also like them to feel how much they want the flat prediction to succeed, and how that wanting is part of the trap. It should be a game they are genuinely trying to win, not one that announces its own lesson.

Please give me: the setting and materials; the roles or teams; how a round runs and how long each part takes; a scoring or payoff scheme that needs no real money; and a debrief that maps particular moments of the game onto the parts of the idea — the trend mistaken for a law, the prediction that alters what it predicts, and the unforeseeable discovery. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle that. And be frank about which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce, so that I know what I must supply myself.

Keep it runnable by one facilitator with nothing beyond paper and pens, and assume the participants know none of the vocabulary. They should not hear the name of the idea until the debrief.
