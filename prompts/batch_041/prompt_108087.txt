I am a novelist, and I am writing a book set in the aftermath of the Second World War, much of it inside the courtroom of one of the postwar tribunals — I am working with both Nuremberg and Tokyo. My central figure is a defence advocate, an elderly jurist of real learning, and his refrain, offered to the bench and to anyone else within earshot, is 'no crime without law' — though when he is at his most formal he gives it as nullum crimen sine lege, and sometimes, apparently interchangeably, as nulla poena sine lege. He says it when the prosecution reaches the charge of waging aggressive war; he says it over the conspiracy count; and he says it most bitterly when the court refuses to hear a word about what the victorious powers did with their own bombers and their own empires.

I know enough to suspect that this is not one claim but a family of them, and I am afraid of writing him as either a fool or a bore. So I would like you to take the maxim apart. What are the distinct precise things 'no crime without law' could be asserting, from the nearly empty to the radical? For each, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible principle, or simply false as stated. I am especially interested in the gap between the bare demand that any punishment rest on some prior legal norm, and the stronger demand that the norm have existed, been knowable, and covered this particular act before it was done; in whether the maxim is really about law at all, or about fair warning, or about the limits of what a court may do to a defeated enemy; and in whether the two Latin forms he conflates are in fact the same principle or two. I gather that at Tokyo one judge went so far as to hold the whole proceeding unlawful on grounds of this kind, and that his reasoning was taken up by others on the bench. Please tell me which of these readings would actually help my advocate — which would get the crimes-against-peace and conspiracy counts thrown out, rather than merely deplored — and which a prosecutor could grant him with a shrug and still secure a conviction. He is a serious man, and I would like him to be making a serious argument rather than a slogan in a wig.

Please write your answer in British English. I do not need references or a reading list; I want the menu of readings and your verdict on each.
