I teach a graduate seminar on historical explanation and forecasting, and I want to close the term with Asimov's Foundation read against the reference class problem. Psychohistory rests on the premise that the aggregate behaviour of populations is lawlike while individual action is not — which is exactly the claim that a singular case can be subsumed under a base rate — so I want a reading that presses on how the trilogy specifies the class. Does the Seldon Plan amount to reference class forecasting at galactic scale, treating the Empire's decline as one draw from a class of imperial declines, and is the Mule a refutation of the outside view or an indictment of the class Seldon picked? Where does the novel dramatize the gap between a statistical regularity and a prediction about a particular case, and where does it smuggle the inside view back in under another name? I am also interested in the reflexivity: the Plan seems to work partly because it is believed and acted upon, which is the sort of self-fulfilling or self-defeating dynamic that the base-rate literature tends to bracket. Where does the novel resist the analogy — is psychohistory a serious picture of the outside view, or a fantasy about what it would be like to have one? And what does Asimov see that the methodology does not: the politics of telling people their choices are statistically negligible, and what it costs to live inside a forecast. Assume I know the plot; no quotations, please.
