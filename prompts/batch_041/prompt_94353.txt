I'm in a political theory reading group and we keep circling the same question without getting anywhere: when someone on the other side of a nasty partisan argument says something that sounds like good faith, what are they actually doing? I've been reading a lot about costly signaling and the handicap principle from biology, and I've got a half-baked idea I want you to help me test. The idea is that a move only counts as a credible signal of good faith if it burns a bridge behind you -- if it raises the cost of defecting later, and the cost is paid in a currency the other side can actually see and verify. Conceding a point your own coalition will punish you for. Saying in advance what evidence would change your mind, in terms your opponents can check. Let's call this the burned-bridge thesis.

Here's the claim I want your credence on, stated as precisely as I can: In typical cross-partisan political arguments -- not formal debate, not a courtroom, just ordinary online or dinner-table arguing -- the disposition we call arguing in good faith is more accurately modeled as a costly handicap signal of non-predation than as either (a) a simple report of sincere belief or (b) a cooperative intention to seek truth together. Assume we can't read minds and only observable moves count. Give me a number between 0 and 1 for that claim, but more importantly give me the decomposition: the sub-questions it hinges on, a rough probability for each, one line of justification per term, and how they combine into the final number.

I'd expect the decomposition to include things like whether the handicap principle even transfers from biological signaling to discursive norms, whether ordinary political argument has the right payoff structure for signaling at all, whether the moves we call good faith are actually costly to fake and verifiable by the other side, whether alternative explanations (sincere belief, cheap talk, reputational bonding, plain Bayesian updating) explain the same behavior better, and whether good faith is even a natural kind or just a family-resemblance term. But add or drop terms as you see fit. Then tell me which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to, and what would move it most -- a formal model, a study, a thought experiment, or a real exchange where someone did the burned-bridge thing and it either worked or backfired. I'm not looking for a survey of the literature, just your actual reasoning with the weak points marked.
