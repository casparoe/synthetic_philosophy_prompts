Design a 75-minute session for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in moral philosophy, about twelve students, who have already met the general distinction between an act's being wrong and a person's being entitled to hold someone answerable for it. The session should be a role-play that makes the students feel the force of the standing objection — the reply that does not deny the accusation but denies the accuser's authority to make it — and then feel the counter-pressure, that this objection looks like a device by which wrongdoers exempt themselves by turning the tables on those who complain. The point must be carried by the mechanics rather than by the facilitator's framing: arrange the roles, the private information, and the incentives so that the participants generate the objection themselves, exploit it, and only afterwards see what they have done. The activity should be run as two simultaneous tables of six, each table operating its own version of the same institution, so that the debrief can compare whether the two groups converged on the same equilibrium.

Build the institution as a fictional residential cooperative with a shared budget and shared chores, and a formal grievance procedure. Each participant receives a public role (chair, treasurer, gardener, and so on), a sealed private history listing two or three things that person has themselves done which resemble the very complaints that will arise, three standing tokens, and a secret objective (protect an ally, keep the peace, have a particular member censured, never be found to have no standing). To lodge a complaint, a member spends a token; before the merits of the complaint are heard, any member may spend a token to raise a standing objection, which the table resolves by a vote on whether the complainant's history disqualifies them; if the objection succeeds, the complaint is dismissed with no finding at all on the conduct. A member found to lack standing forfeits all tokens, and tokens may be given to another member, which forces the question whether a discredited member may have a clean-handed proxy complain on their behalf. Specify the full cast, the sequence of play, the materials the facilitator must print (role cards, sealed histories, tokens, a tally sheet), a scoring scheme that needs no money, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the seventy-five minutes.

Then write the debrief, mapping particular moments of play onto the parts of the idea: the difference between a complaint's being warranted and its being one the complainant has standing to make; the several grounds on which standing is said to be lost (hypocrisy, complicity in the same wrong, complaining about a wrong that is not one's business); whether the hypocrite's blame is false, or true but unwarranted, or warranted but merely unwelcome; whether standing is forfeited permanently, recoverable, or transferable at all; and the suggestion that what is objectionable about hypocrisy is not the falsity of the accusation but a failure of moral equality between blamer and blamed. Be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce — for instance, the history and vulnerability of a real relationship between two people, and the possibility that a complaint may be entirely true and yet legitimately dismissed — and tell the facilitator what to supply in its place. Finally, state what will predictably go wrong the first time the session is run — for example, that a table votes to strip nearly everyone of standing and the procedure deadlocks, that the tokens are exhausted in the first round, that the vote becomes a popularity contest, or that one participant dominates the floor — and how the facilitator should handle each case.
