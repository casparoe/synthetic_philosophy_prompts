Ok so I'm a second-year PhD student working on rational choice theory, and there's a version of the money pump argument that I'm pretty sure proves too much, but every time I try to say exactly where it goes wrong a friend tells me I'm just refusing to bite a bullet, and I've started to lose track of whether they're right. I'm hoping you can help me get the diagnosis straight rather than just reassure me. We cleaned the argument up on a whiteboard last week and it goes like this.

(1) A set of preferences is rationally permissible only if an agent who has it can't be money-pumped — can't be led, through a sequence of trades each of which she accepts, to end up worse off by her own lights than she started. (2) With intransitive preferences — A over B, B over C, C over A — you can always be pumped: start her at C, sell her B for a penny, sell her A for a penny, sell her back C for a penny, and she's exactly where she began, a few cents lighter. (3) So intransitivity is irrational; no one's arguing with that part. (4) Now run the same trick across time instead of across a single ranking. Take someone whose ranking of the same options genuinely changes: she prefers the country house in June and the city flat in December, and when the season turns she'd trade each for the other and pay a small fee to do it. A pumper who can predict this, and who knows she can't bind herself in advance, sells her the flat every fall and the house every spring, skimming a fee each time, and she ends up back where she started having paid for the round trip. (5) By premise (1), that makes her irrational. (6) But she isn't irrational — she's just someone whose tastes shift with the seasons, and alot of people I know are like that, me included when it comes to how much I care about tenure versus free time depending on how the week went. (7) So (1) must be too strong, and there has to be a notion of exploitability that catches the intransitive case without catching her.

I'd like to keep (2) and (3), and I'd really rather keep (1) too if there's a version of it that still does the work, because without some exploitability criterion I honestly dont know what grounds the transitivity requirement in the first place. So: which step is doing the illegitimate work, why does it look fine until you push on it, and what do I have to give up to reject it? I'm especially curious whether the fix is (a) a restriction on what counts as a pump, e.g. requiring a single standpoint from which the agent ends up worse off, (b) a restriction on when a change in preferences is irrational — only when it's unresponsive to new information, or only when the later self doesn't endorse the earlier self's ranking, or something like that — or (c) giving up on exploitability as the foundation altogether and deriving transitivity some other way. Price all three for me, including what each one costs elsewhere, because I suspect whichever I pick is going to bleed into how I think about preference change generally.
