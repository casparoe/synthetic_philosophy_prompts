The epistemology reading group I run has spent the better part of a year on the norm of assertion, and we have reached the stage where every member can predict every other member's next move and nothing ever shifts. The knowledge camp holds that one may assert only what one knows, and treats our readiness to retract an assertion upon learning that the speaker merely had a true belief as the central datum. The truth camp holds that the norm is truth, and presses the intuition that a speaker who asserts a truth she does not know — a lucky guess, an unearned hunch that happens to pan out — has still said something correct, however criticizable the saying. A third contingent locates the norm in belief, or in justified belief, or in what the speaker is in a position to defend, and regards both of the above as artifacts of cases constructed to flatter a favored answer. We have cycled through the standard constructed cases — the justified false belief, the true but ungrounded guess, the assertion of something known on grounds the speaker cannot herself vouch for — and each side reads every one of them as confirmation of its own norm. I can reconstruct all three positions on demand and still cannot say which side, if any, is mistaken.

My hypothesis, offered for you to attack rather than to accept: this is not a substantive disagreement about assertion at all, but a collision of different questions travelling under one label. One camp is asking what makes a particular utterance count as an assertion in the first place — a constitutive question about the act. Another is asking when it is permissible to perform that act. Another is asking when a hearer is entitled to rely on the result. And a fourth strand may not be about assertion at all but about the norm of belief, smuggled in through the fact that assertion normally expresses belief. If that is right, the participants are not contradicting one another, and the debate cannot be won by anyone, because there is no single thing for a winner to be right about.

Diagnose it properly. Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, substantive, or several distinct questions under one name — and defend the diagnosis against the obvious objection that the camps plainly do contradict one another, since each says that the norm is this and the others say it is that. Then say what the diagnosis implies. If the impasse is a conflation, identify the distinction that would dissolve it and say what, if anything, would be lost by drawing it — in particular, whether the normative force of the original question survives being split into constitutive, permissibility, reliance, and doxastic components, or whether splitting it merely changes the subject. If instead the disagreement is substantive, name the hidden factual or methodological question doing the real work — for example, whether the intuitions that drive the knowledge camp track the norm of assertion itself or merely the pragmatics of criticizing a speaker, so that the two camps are quarrelling over what causes a judgment rather than over what the judgment is about. Finally, tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a decisive case that one side cannot absorb, a formal result, a deliberate change of question — or an argument that the debate is perfectly well posed and merely hard, so that the stalemate is exactly what we should expect and my frustration is misplaced.
