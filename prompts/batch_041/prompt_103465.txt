Draft the joint statement that a committed neo-Aristotelian virtue ethicist and a committed situationist critic of virtue ethics could both sign at the close of a good-faith adversarial collaboration. This is the constructive document the two camps should have produced — not a dialogue, not a verdict on who is right, and not a diagnosis of why the debate is stuck.

Structure it in three parts.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims, each of which both parties would defend and neither could drop without loss. Cover at least: what the empirical record on cross-situational behavioral consistency does and does not establish; the difference between the descriptive question (whether robust global character traits exist) and the normative question (whether virtue ethics stands or falls with them); the difference between a trait as a behavioral disposition, as a skill, and as an evaluative stance; the bearing of base rates and selection effects on the evidence; and what would count as a well-formed test of the disputed claims.

Second, a disagreements table. For each remaining disagreement, give a neutral statement of it, then each side's own preferred formulation, worded so that side would accept it. Mark each entry as genuine, verbal, or partly verbal; where verbal, identify the exact word or inference carrying the dispute — candidates include trait, situation, consistency, and virtue.

Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. Include at least these, and add any others you judge central: (a) whether cross-situational consistency is required for a trait to be a trait; (b) whether the rarity of virtue, if granted, defeats the situationist's evidence or merely insulates the virtue ethicist from it; (c) whether the classic obedience and helping studies bear on virtue at all, given that they concern ordinary people under pressure rather than people selected for virtue. For each crux, supply a concrete case, experiment, or dataset that both sides agree would bear on it, and record each side's prediction in advance, stated so that it could be falsified.

To keep the exercise off the standard track, treat the following stipulated study as the shared stimulus; either side may refine it, but neither may reject it as irrelevant.

The Harbor Study. Researchers assembled an experience-sampling panel in which the same participants were pinged at random over several weeks and asked about low-cost helping opportunities actually available to them at that moment. A single compassion composite showed high within-situation stability but near-zero stability across situation types, and the top decile on the composite did no better than the median on a later, separately staged, high-cost helping opportunity. A follow-up inserts a screening stage: participants are first nominated by well-acquainted peers as exceptionally compassionate, then run through the same panel.

For the Harbor Study and its follow-up, state what each side takes the results to show, what each predicts the screened follow-up will show, and what result would make each side abandon its position. Close by saying plainly which parts of the dispute the Harbor Study cannot touch, and what a joint research program would still have to run.
