I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I would like a careful explanation of a conceptual puzzle in the philosophy of mind. There is a condition described in the clinical literature as pain asymbolia, in which patients appear to feel pain without being troubled by it: they can say where a pinprick is and how intense it is, and they may even allow that it hurts, but they do not withdraw and give every sign of indifference. The natural view is that a pain which did not hurt would not be a pain at all, and I would like to know how much of that view survives contact with the case. Does the condition show that the awfulness of a pain is a separable part of it, so that a pain might hurt without being bad for the person who has it, or does it show only that the urge to escape a pain can be severed from the feeling of it?

To sharpen the question, suppose a drug reproduced the asymbolic state in an otherwise healthy person: the sensation arrives with its full intensity, quality and location, and they can still report it, but nothing about it bothers them. I want to know whether that would count as treating the pain or merely disguising it, and how to distinguish three things the case appears to run together — the sensation itself, the felt awfulness of it, and the motivation to make it stop. I am also unsure how much weight the patient's own report can carry: in saying that it hurts, are they reporting the awfulness or only the sensation, and what would settle the matter?

Please give the explanation as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, running in order from what the case establishes to what follows for whether such a state is bad for the person who has it. No introduction or summary, and no references are needed — I would rather have the argument.
