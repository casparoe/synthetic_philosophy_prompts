I have been thinking about Pascal's wager and about the St. Petersburg paradox, and a friend of mine — a deflationary sort — told me that the wager is nothing but a St. Petersburg game in theological dress, and that this settles the matter. His argument, as nearly as I can reconstruct it, runs like this: in both cases a rational agent is offered a prospect whose expected value is infinite; in both cases the infinity comes from the tail of the distribution, where ever-larger payoffs are purchased by ever-smaller probabilities; the remedy usually proposed for the game — a utility function that is concave, or bounded above — applies word for word to the wager, since the value of salvation is presumably subject to diminishing returns as well; and therefore the infinite expectation that is supposed to compel assent evaporates in the two cases together. He concludes that the wager has no more force than a fair coin that pays two dollars if the first toss is heads, four if the second, eight if the third, and so on, and that anyone moved by the one ought to be equally willing to stake their whole estate on the other.

I cannot decide whether this is an argument or a pun, and I would like you to audit it. Set out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the coin, of the doubling payoffs, of the entrance fee, of the player's budget, and of the mechanism that fixes the probabilities. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and which of the breaks matter for the use my friend is making of it. Then say what the analogy quietly imports — for instance, whether it assumes that salvation is a quantity that can be entered into a utility function at all, or that the probabilities in the wager are as determinate as those of a fair coin, or that the player is free to decline to play. Finally, give me a verdict on the conclusion — that the wager is refuted by the paradox — once the disanalogies are on the table: does it survive, or was the analogy carrying it all along? I am not asking for the standard paragraph about log utility; I want the comparison itself assessed, and, if a better one is available, what it would be.
