I keep going back and forth on whether death is bad for the person who dies, and I want a principle that fits my gut, not a summary of the debate. A friend died at 34 with a lot still ahead of him and I can't shake that something was taken from him, not just from us. But Epicurus keeps nagging me: while you're alive you're not dead, once you're dead there's no you left to be harmed, so who's the loser?

Here's what I actually think, case by case. Dying at 34 with a good life ahead: bad for him. Never having existed back in 1800: not bad for me in the slightest, I feel zero about it. Being dead for ten thousand years instead of a hundred: I can't honestly say that's worse for the dead person. Someone trashing a man's reputation with lies after he's dead: that one feels bad for him, not just for his widow. A painless death in your sleep: it's not the dying that's the problem, so either the being-dead is bad or nothing is. And a man who was going to spend his last years in agony before an early death anyway -- killing him seems to take nothing good away from him.

So is there one account that gets all of these? Deprivation nails the 34-year-old but seems to say the posthumous slander hurts nobody, and it can't tell me why I shrug at 1800 but flinch at the slander, since both are just me not existing at some time. If nothing fits, which of my verdicts has to go? And what does the account you end up with predict for cases I didn't list -- someone who dies right after the best year of his life, or someone whose life was mediocre and would've stayed mediocre?
