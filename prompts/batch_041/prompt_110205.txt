You are an expert in animal welfare science and its applied ethics, with particular experience advising on the regulation of aquaculture. I am assembling a set of teaching materials on aquatic animal welfare, and I want a dialogue that gets past the slogans this topic usually attracts. Please write a sustained, intellectually serious dialogue between two interlocutors, each presented at full strength: a precautionary-parity advocate, who holds that wherever there is credible evidence of nociception, of motivational trade-offs, and of injury-related behavioural change, the animal should be treated as a welfare subject and protected accordingly, with the burden of proof resting on those who would deny protection; and a graded-confidence skeptic, who accepts the same evidence but holds that the stringency of the rules we impose should track how confident we are that the animal has an integrated, felt experience of its own condition, and who worries that the precautionary move, applied consistently, cannot be contained.

The dialogue should be anchored in concrete practices rather than abstractions: the routine eyestalk ablation of female shrimp in hatcheries to induce spawning, the chilling of farmed fish in ice slurry as a means of stunning or killing, the boiling of crustaceans alive and the electrical stunning that some jurisdictions now require beforehand, and the broader question of which species receive method restrictions and which receive nothing. I would like the two of them to press each other on the distinctions that actually carry weight here — between nociception and pain, between reflexive avoidance and a genuine motivational trade-off, between a capacity for suffering and a capacity for the specific kinds of suffering our practices inflict — and on the comparative problem of how to weigh a shrimp's welfare against a salmon's, or either against the human interest in affordable protein. Let them also confront the design question directly: whether a tiered scheme that grants full protection to vertebrates, method restrictions to decapods and cephalopods, and nothing to insects is principled or merely a political line drawn for convenience; and whether uncertainty about sentience is better met by discounting the weight we give to an animal's welfare in proportion to our confidence, or by a threshold rule that either includes a creature fully or leaves it out. I want the skeptic forced to say what evidence would move her and the advocate forced to say where his principle stops, and I do not want either of them to dodge by talking past the other; each reply should engage the actual last point made.

Before writing the exchange, spend a few sentences setting out the strongest version of each position as you understand it, and think carefully about where the genuinely hard crux lies rather than where the familiar talking points lie. Then write the dialogue itself. Do not resolve it by fiat: end with the sharpest remaining disagreement, stated so that a reasonable reader can see exactly what would have to be settled to break the tie. Keep the contributions concrete and specific, and avoid a merely rhetorical back-and-forth in which each side simply restates its opening.
