I am a novelist at work on a book whose central figure is a mid-ranking official in a government fighting a war it has privately concluded it cannot win. What interests me is not the lying, which is easy enough to write, but something stranger: that a man can say, on the record and in public, that the war is going well, while drafting memoranda that say it is not, and can keep the two going for years without any apparent sensation of contradicting himself.

Philosophers have a name for the puzzle I keep circling. Moore's paradox is the oddity of a first-person utterance that conjoins a claim with the speaker's own disavowal of belief in that claim — that the rain is falling and that I do not believe it is. The conjunction can be true; it cannot be sincerely and coherently said. I would like you to take the Pentagon Papers episode as a case and work it through the paradox in both directions: what the paradox makes visible in the episode, and what the episode does to the paradox.

The essentials, as I understand them: a classified internal history of American decision-making in Vietnam, commissioned from inside the Defense Department, which recorded that the men prosecuting the war had long doubted what they were publicly asserting; the public optimism of those same years; the leak of that history to the press in 1971 and its publication over the government's objections; and the phrase of the period, the credibility gap, which is the public's way of holding both halves in one hand.

Four things I want pressed. First, the mapping: who is the speaker here, and what is the asserted content; who is the believer, and what does he believe? These are not obviously the same person or the same act, and I want to know whether that matters, or whether the paradox requires a single mind to attach to. Second, the diagnostic question, which is the one I care about most: the episode looks like the paradox's territory but may not be an instance of it at all, since the officials were not sincerely asserting what they disbelieved but insincerely asserting what they knew. Say precisely why that is not Moore's paradox, and what the difference shows about where the absurdity comes from — from meaning, from the norms governing assertion, or from the way a person is supposed to be transparent to herself. Third, the partition: the two halves lived in different rooms, before different audiences, in different genres, the briefing and the memorandum. What becomes of the paradox when the audience is split, and what happens when the conjunction is finally assembled by a leak and read by the public? The public can say, without absurdity, that the government asserted one thing and believed another; tell me what that asymmetry between the first person and the third reveals. Fourth, the novelist's question: if the absurdity attaches to what can be said rather than to what can be thought, then nothing prevents a man from living inside the conjunction for years. Does the episode bear that out, and what does it suggest the paradox gets wrong about interior life — and about institutions, which have no single mind to be transparent to itself?

Finish with a verdict: on the evidence of this case, what does Moore's paradox actually mark — a limit on thought, a limit on assertion, or a limit peculiar to the first-person singular — and what does the episode leave unsettled?
