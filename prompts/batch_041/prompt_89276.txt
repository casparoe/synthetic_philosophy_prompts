I'm a doctoral student in intellectual history, and I'm in the middle of a long-running argument with my advisor about whether the philosophy of history has anything to teach the practice of narrative. The text I keep returning to is Tolstoy's War and Peace — not for the battle scenes, which everyone reads, but because it strikes me as the most sustained dramatization I know of a position in that debate, and of two incompatible positions at once. Please act as a philosopher of history with real expertise on both the covering-law (deductive-nomological) model of explanation associated with Hempel and its narrativist critics — Danto on narrative sentences, Mink on narrative as a cognitive instrument, White on emplotment — and give me a careful reading of the novel through that debate. Begin by brainstorming the candidate mappings before committing to one; I would rather watch you consider and discard two or three readings than see you settle on the obvious one immediately. Reason it through scene by scene, and let the theoretical apparatus actually do work rather than decorate a plot summary.

Here is the puzzle I want you to take seriously. The second epilogue argues explicitly, at essay length, that historians' causal explanations are illusory: that Napoleon no more caused the retreat than a ship's figurehead causes the ship's motion, that the so-called great man is the least free of all the actors, and that the proper object of historical explanation is the infinitesimal aggregate of individual wills — which, if it is anything at all, is a covering-law ambition, a hunt for laws of motion. But the narrative itself does something else entirely. It explains by following particular lives — Andrei seizing the banner at Austerlitz, Kutuzov dozing through the war council at Fili while everyone else deliberates, Pierre wandering the chaos of Borodino as a tourist of his own catastrophe — and it repeatedly stages the gap between the descriptions available to the people living an event and the descriptions that event will later receive. That gap is precisely Danto's point about narrative sentences, yet the novel does something a philosophical example cannot: it makes us inhabit the earlier, descriptionless side of it. So which is War and Peace — a covering-law explanation in disguise, a narrativist one, an argument against a Collingwood-style re-enactment of the agents' own thoughts, or a fourth thing for which the debate has no name?

I want the reading to run in both directions and to be honest in both. Take the theoretical positions seriously enough to say where the novel refutes or embarrasses them, and take the novel seriously enough to say where it cheats — where, for instance, the domestic epilogue about Pierre and Natasha quietly abandons the historical question, or where Tolstoy's laws of the swarm turn out to be the covering-law model wearing a costume. Close by telling me what, if anything, the novel adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone cannot, and name the single scene you would assign to a graduate seminar to make that addition visible. Please spare me a survey of Tolstoy criticism or a bibliography; I have read the secondary literature. I want the reading itself.
