I am a novelist, and I have spent the better part of two years on a book in which a man I hold in no sympathy uses a slur against a group he despises. The word occurs only in his speech, and twice in the free indirect narration of his thoughts, where his idiom and my own sentence-making become difficult to separate. Two friends read the manuscript and argued about it at my table for an hour, and I have not been able to settle the question since. I would like you to adjudicate the argument as it was actually made. I will not tell you which of them I think is right, because I do not know.

Mara, who edits dictionaries, argued that the word's contempt is not something a speaker does with it but something it carries. On her account the derogation belongs to the meaning, and that is why it survives being reported and denied: a denial of the form he did not call her that still lands badly, and a museum audio guide reading out a slave-owner's letter in his own words still puts the contempt in the room, though no one alive is expressing it. She said that if the meaning carries it, no author can launder it by handing it to a character; the page is mine.

Dev, who teaches philosophy, said the contempt is in the act, not the word. The same syllables, he pointed out, can be used by members of the group about themselves with no insult at all; a court reporter transcribing the word is not insulting anyone; a language class pronouncing it is not doing anything to anyone; and these are not exotic exceptions but ordinary uses. If the word derogated by its meaning, those uses would derogate too, and plainly they do not. He added that Mara's view makes it impossible to render a bigot without becoming one, which no novelist with any interest in representing the world could accept.

Mara replied that reclamation is evidence for her, not for him: one can only reclaim what was a weapon, and the group is fighting over the word precisely because the contempt is attached to it. She granted that a mention is not a use and said her view concerns uses; the court reporter and the language class only mention it. Dev answered that my free indirect narration is not a mention, and neither is the audio guide, so her concession does not rescue her: in both cases someone uses the word without derogating, which is what his view predicts and hers cannot.

Mara then said that Dev's view locates the whole harm in the speaker's intention, which is the very thing that lets real cruelty pass — the joke that was only a joke, the endearment that was meant kindly — so that a hearer who cannot inspect intentions is left with nothing but the word in her ear. Dev replied that intentions are not private in the way she supposes: the conventions of a situation are public, and hearers read them off the situation rather than off anyone's heart, which is why the same word in an audiobook narrator's mouth can be a different act from the same word in mine.

Please take their exchange move by move, rating each claim from clearly mistaken through clearly right and justifying each rating briefly, rather than simply declaring a winner. Then say how your ratings bear on the four decisions I actually face: (1) the slur in my character's dialogue; (2) the same word in the free indirect narration, where the voice is partly his and partly mine; (3) an audiobook narrator who is herself a member of the group and is asked to perform the line as written; (4) whether a word can be fully reclaimed, so that the contempt is gone rather than merely disarmed. Tell me which distinction, if any, is doing the real work here — mention and use, what a word means and what a speaker does with it, or something neither of them named — and what both of them are missing.

Finally, be explicit about the limits of your own verdict: state which argument or consideration would move you to a different answer on any of these points, and say where in your reasoning you are most likely to be wrong.
