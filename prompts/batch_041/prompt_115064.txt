My friend and I have spent several long evenings arguing about whether there is any non-circular justification for trusting that the future will resemble the past, and by the end of the last one I had stopped arguing about the conclusion and started noticing the shape of his objections. I would like to know whether that shape has a name.

The pattern is this. Whatever rule I propose for getting from what has been observed to what has not — continue the regularities that have held so far, prefer the simpler of two curves that fit the data, weight recent evidence more heavily, whatever it may be — he constructs a case against it, and the construction is always the same. Suppose a world that has produced exactly the data we have, in exactly the order we have it, and that then stops conforming; my rule expects the regularities to continue, and it is wrong, while some other rule, or no rule at all, would have been right. He takes this to show that my rule rests on an assumption I cannot justify, and sums it up by saying that there is 'no free lunch'. The same move recurs elsewhere in the argument: when I say a correlation gives me some reason to expect a cause, he asks how I know the correlation is not produced by something hidden that will presently change; when I say one hypothesis fits the data better than another, he asks why fit should count for anything, since infinitely many curves pass through any finite set of points.

Two features of the pattern trouble me. The first is that the cases are built to order. They are described in terms of the very notions in dispute — regularity, conformity so far — and the recipe appears indifferent to which rule I offer: had I proposed trusting whichever regularity has failed most often, he could have run the same construction and produced a world in which that rule fails too. An objection that could not fail against any candidate, and that would have been available whatever I had said, seems to carry no information about the particular rule I actually offered, even though it is presented as a refutation. The second is that he exempts his own position. His policy — commit to nothing about the unobserved — is exposed to precisely the same construction, a world whose evidence so far is as it is and which then rewards commitment, and he does not treat that as an argument against his policy; he treats it as the sober default. He also leans on a mathematical result he calls 'no free lunch', and I am not persuaded that it says what he thinks it says: my understanding is that it concerns how methods fare on average across all conceivable problems taken together, which is not the same claim as that, for the problem in front of us, every method is as good as every other. If that is right, the theorem is being asked to do work it cannot do.

What I want is not a label but a carving. Is there an established name for the phenomenon I have described, or for the objection considered as a type? Is it simply the problem of induction in a new outfit? Is it what Goodman's grue was after? Is it the familiar point that evidence never settles which theory to accept, or that every chain of justification must stop somewhere, or that a rule has to be testable, or Popper's complaint about theories rescued by added assumptions? My impression is that it is none of these exactly, since each is about a particular obstacle to justifying a particular claim, whereas what struck me is a feature of the objection itself: it is generated by a recipe, it is guaranteed to succeed against every candidate, it can be specified only once the candidate is on the table, and for that reason it cannot discriminate. If there is no such name, give me one, define it, and mark its edges — above all, tell me what distinguishes a constructed counterexample that is a genuine strike against a rule from one that is merely this other thing. And if the conclusion is that objections of this kind are worthless, explain why that is not simply a refusal to answer the sceptic.
