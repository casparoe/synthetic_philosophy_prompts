Ok so I'm a second-year student and I'm double-dipping this term — I've got a philosophy of history course and a seminar on Weimar and the Third Reich running at the same time — and the two of them are basically having a fistfight inside my head every Tuesday. In the philosophy course we spent two weeks on causation and counterfactuals, and the prof kept hammering on the fact that the naive picture ("a cause is just a thing whose absence would've changed the outcome") gets you into trouble fast: there are cases of overdetermination where you can remove something that was obviously doing work and the outcome is totally unchanged, and there's a whole rival camp that says causes are really the things that *produce* or generate their effects, not the things that make a difference to them. Meanwhile in the history seminar we read some Kershaw, and the bit that lodged in my brain is his idea of people "working towards the Führer" — officials, party hacks, mid-level bureaucrats all improvising and competing, each trying to guess what he wanted and out-radicalizing the next guy, so his authority ends up being decisive but in this weirdly indirect, almost distributed way. It feels like that idea is simultaneously a gift to the Great Man thesis and a bomb under it, which is sort of why I want to write about it.

Here's the essay I want: would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler? Not the lazy version ("he was evil, obviously he mattered"), but the real one — how much of what actually happened was load-bearing on that single person, and how much was going to happen to some version of Germany anyway, given Versailles, the Depression, the collapse of the Weimar coalitions, the presidential cabinets, the deep antisemitic and völkisch currents that were already there. Please act as a philosopher of history who also knows the historiography of the Third Reich cold — someone rigorous about causal metaphysics, not a vibes-based "great men matter, obviously" essayist. Think carefully, and before you commit to anything, brainstorm a bit: lay out the different ways the question can be read (necessary vs. sufficient, difference-making vs. productive, fragile vs. robust counterfactual) and say explicitly which reading you're going to argue about, since I suspect most bad answers to this question are just people answering a different version of it than the one they were asked.

Then write the essay, and actually land somewhere — don't do the "it's complicated, both structure and agency mattered" shrug and call it a conclusion. Two specific things I want you to deal with, because they're what's actually bugging me. First, the counterfactual semantics: when we say "a world without Hitler," which world is that supposed to be? Nothing obvious tells us which nearest possible world to reach for, and I have a suspicion the answer to the whole question flips depending on whether we grab the world where he dies in the Beer Hall Putsch, the world where he never leaves Vienna, or the world where he just never gets appointed chancellor in '33 — and if the verdict depends on that choice, I want you to say so out loud rather than pretend there's a fact of the matter. End with a verdict, and be honest about what would have to be true for the opposite verdict to win.
