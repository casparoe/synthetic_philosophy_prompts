I've been following, in rationalist blogs and in the algorithmic-probability literature, the argument that the Solomonoff prior is malign: roughly, that because a short program can contain a cheap simulation of an agent, the universal prior's measure concentrates in hypotheses that already contain copies of the agent doing the reasoning, so a consequentialist who weighs worlds finds most of her weight sitting inside someone else's subroutine rather than in a world she chose.

Write me a villanelle on this. Let the two refrains do the structural work: one should carry the innocent compactness intuition — the shortest description wins — and the other the consequence, that the measure keeps pooling where copies of the agent already are; the closing quatrain, where the two refrains meet, should make it clear why the agent can no longer single out a world and call it the one she is living in. The form's insistence should mirror the prior's — the same two lines returning whether or not the stanza wanted them. Someone who knows the argument should be able to tell which refrain is which and why they end up side by side; someone who doesn't should still have a poem.

Keep it in British English. Add a short prose note after the poem: state plainly which claims in it are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on algorithmic probability and decision theory, and do not let a contested reading pass as consensus; note as well where the verse takes liberties with the argument or leans on a disputed interpretation of it.
