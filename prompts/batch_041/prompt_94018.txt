I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on scientific discovery and the allocation of credit, and I have grown dissatisfied with the way the canonical cases are taught. Darwin and Wallace, Newton and Leibniz — these have been worn smooth, and the question they are made to answer, who was first, strikes me as the wrong question. The case I keep returning to instead is the independent development of non-Euclidean geometry in the 1820s and 1830s. Lobachevsky and Bolyai each published a system that dispensed with the parallel postulate; Gauss, who had arrived at comparable results decades earlier and said as much in correspondence, kept his out of print, remarking privately that he feared the reception such a publication would meet. Bolyai, learning of Gauss's reaction, came to suspect — almost certainly without foundation — that his father had betrayed his work, and he complained bitterly for the rest of his life. I floated the case at a departmental workshop and a colleague objected that it is really a story about vanity and national resentment, which may be true and is not philosophically interesting. I think it is more interesting than that, and I want you to test whether I am right.

The instrument I would like you to use is a distinction I have been developing, between three achievements that the single word discovery runs together: first, being the one to entertain a result and see that it might be true; second, being the one to accept it as established, so that one would stake one's own further reasoning on it; and third, being the one to make it available to others in a form they can use, which includes publication but also communication, private or otherwise, to someone positioned to act on it. My conjecture is that priority disputes are almost always disputes about the third conducted in the rhetoric of the first or the second, and that whatever moral weight attaches to credit attaches to the third, because it is the only one of the three that changes what anyone else is able to do. Please analyze the episode through this framework rather than around it. Say which of the three achievements each of the three figures satisfies, and be candid about the evidence as you go: much of what we believe about the extent and date of Gauss's unpublished work rests on his own retrospective claims, and I would rather the analysis mark that uncertainty than resolve it by fiat. Then tell me what the framework explains and where it distorts. Does it capture what Bolyai was actually aggrieved about, or is his complaint one the distinction has no name for — something closer to the discovery that one is not alone in the world, which is not a claim about credit at all? Can it accommodate the inevitability thesis, the thought that a result of this kind was bound to be reached by someone once the surrounding mathematics was in place, or does it silently presuppose that the individual contributor is the natural unit of analysis? And is its verdict on Gauss the right one? He worked out the geometry, believed it, mentioned it to a few correspondents, and did not publish; the framework appears to convict him of something, and I am not certain what the charge would be. Close by saying what the case shows about the framework in return — whether it is a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the distinction was never designed to handle.
