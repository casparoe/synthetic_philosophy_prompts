You are a political philosopher specializing in democratic theory, and I want your considered judgment on a foundational question rather than a survey of the literature. Please reason carefully and, before committing to a verdict, spend a few lines brainstorming: spell out in your own words what each position below is actually committed to, what work it does, and what intuitive appeal it draws on.

The question is the boundary problem — how we are to determine who belongs to the demos, the body entitled to decide. I want you to compare two answers.

The first is the all-affected-interests principle: the demos should comprise everyone whose interests are affected by the decisions in question, and being affected is what grounds a claim to a say.

The second is the all-subjected principle: the demos is exactly the set of persons over whom the polity's binding, coercive rules claim authority, so that being subject to the law, rather than being affected by outcomes, is what grounds membership.

A colleague of mine adds a distinction I would like you to keep in view. She separates the authorization question — who gets to decide — from the concern question — whose interests the decision must answer to — and suggests that conflating the two is what makes the boundary problem look harder than it is. I am not sure she is right, but I would like the comparison to say whether either principle respects the distinction or collapses it.

Then run the comparison through the following cases, taking each principle in turn and stating what it implies and where it strains: (a) long-term resident non-citizens; (b) citizens of one country who are substantially affected by another country's emissions or financial decisions; (c) future generations; (d) children; and (e) a case of your own choosing that you take to be hardest for both.

There is also a circularity I want addressed explicitly: any principle that specifies the demos seems to presuppose a demos already constituted well enough to apply the principle in the first place. Say whether either principle can escape this, and whether the circularity is fatal or benign.

Finally, tell me which principle you find more defensible and why, how confident you are, and — this matters to me — what would change your mind: which argument or case would lead you to reverse your verdict, and where in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to be mistaken. Please reason from the principles themselves; I am not asking for a literature review or a reading list.
