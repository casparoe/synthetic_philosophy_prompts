I am a junior at a high school with a fairly serious philosophy club, and I have been asked to run the humor page of our student newspaper's spring issue. Our faculty advisor has insisted that the satire be informed rather than merely silly, so I would like a piece that is genuinely funny to someone who has thought carefully about the problem it mocks and merely strange to everyone else. For a club unit on decision-making under uncertainty, I have been reading about the family of views according to which the thing to do is not simply what one presently wants, but what one would want, or what an idealized, better-informed, better-reasoning counterpart of oneself would endorse, if one thought about it long enough and clearly enough. What interests me is the indirect move: specifying a normative standard by pointing at a process of improvement rather than by listing the good outcomes directly. Please do not cite any particular books, articles, or authors; I want to be able to print the piece without a bibliography, and I would rather the joke rest on the structure of the idea than on any single writer's version of it.

Please write, in the deadpan register of a corporate FAQ page — the numbered question-and-answer copy one finds on the website of a well-funded startup — an approximately 650-word in-universe document for a fictional company that sells exactly this service to ordinary people: for a monthly fee, it claims to resolve your hard decisions by consulting your idealized counterpart, the version of you that knows everything relevant and reasons perfectly. The humor should come from the genuine difficulties rather than from invented absurdities. Among them: someone must specify how the idealization is to work, and whoever specifies it is thereby smuggling in an answer; an idealized counterpart might approve of things you find monstrous, and might not approve of you; there is an obvious regress, since an idealized counterpart would presumably defer to a still-more-idealized one; the claim that a standard is what you would want if you were better is a counterfactual that cannot be checked against anything; and a counterpart ideal enough to be authoritative may well conclude that the company ought not to exist. Let the marketing copy treat each of these as a feature, in perfect earnest, and let at least one clause disclose, proudly, that the service's own terms permit it to conclude that it should be shut down. Keep the irony legible throughout — a reader should be able to tell what is being mocked without the writer ever winking — and please do not let the piece collapse into a generic complaint about artificial intelligence.
