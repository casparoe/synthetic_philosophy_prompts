I'm in a seminar on the ethics of deception and we spent most of last week on the lying/misleading distinction — the standard analysis where a lie needs a false assertion plus an intent to deceive, while misleading can be a true assertion carrying a false implicature, or selective emphasis, or technically-true hedging. It all felt pretty bloodless until I rewatched The Insider and then went down a rabbit hole on the actual 1994 congressional hearing, and got into a genuinely heated argument with my roommate about whether those tobacco CEOs lied. So I want to use that hearing as the test case, and I want the analysis to run in both directions — the theory illuminating the episode, and the episode testing the theory. I'm giving you the facts because I don't want the whole thing hanging on historical details you'd have to guess at: the heads of the major American tobacco companies testified under oath before a House subcommittee, and each of them said, in substance, that he did not believe nicotine was addictive. Meanwhile the companies' own internal research, which surfaced later in litigation, had concluded for decades that nicotine was addictive and that they were engineering cigarettes to deliver it faster. But alot of the public-facing denials weren't flat denials — they were hedged with I-believe formulations, appeals to personal opinion, caveats about what the word addiction even means, comparisons to heroin, and claims about the companies' own conduct rather than about nicotine.

So: map the actual utterances onto the taxonomy. Which are lies on the standard analysis, which are merely misleading, and — the part I care about most — what happens to the distinction when the sentence is I-believe-P rather than P? If a CEO sincerely holds some idiosyncratic definition of addiction under which nicotine doesn't qualify, then his statement about his own belief may be literally true and still produce exactly the false belief the audience was meant to walk away with. Is that a lie with extra steps, or a genuinely different act? And is there a principled place to draw the line when the hearer can't tell the difference, when the hedging is lawyer-engineered, and when the belief being reported is itself a corporate artifact rather than one person's mental state? Say where the theory goes silent here — collective assertion, the definitional dodge, the fact that perjury law tracks the lie/mislead line while the moral damage doesn't seem to.

Then run it the other way. What does this case do to the two big moral positions — the one that says lying and misleading differ only in consequences, and the one that says misleading is intrinsically worse because it recruits the hearer's own inference and lets the deceiver keep clean hands? Does the hearing support parity, since the misleading statements did at least as much damage and were harder to catch? Or does it cut the other way, because the hedges are exactly what let the speakers stay on the right side of perjury and feel innocent, which suggests the distinction is protecting the wrong people? I don't want a survey of every position in the literature; I want a disciplined mapping, an honest account of what breaks, and your own call about which framework handles the case best even if it's neither of the two I named.
