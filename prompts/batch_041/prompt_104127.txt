I have been attending a reading group on Buddhist philosophy of mind, and we keep returning to the doctrine of not-self (anattā) in a way that leaves me with an objection I cannot tell whether to treat as deep or as an artifact of my own framing. The doctrine is ordinarily summarized for us as the claim that the person is not a substance but a convenient designation — a patterned stream of physical and mental events, the five aggregates, none of which is a self and none of which owns the others — and I am told that when the Buddha was asked directly whether there is a self, he declined to answer, which I read as evasion rather than as a considered position. What I cannot get past is the combination of that denial with the tradition's insistence that actions ripen on the one who performed them: that the person reborn is neither the same as nor different from the one who died, that the whole teaching is meant to make one take responsibility for one's life, and that this is supposed to be a matter of sober fact rather than of edification.

A friend of mine, who is a thoroughgoing Parfitian about personal identity, tells me that the Buddhist account is simply the reductionist view in another vocabulary, and that the reductionist has already conceded the crucial point: once the person is only a designation, there is, strictly speaking, no person, only a succession of states. If that is right, the karmic story becomes difficult to state without embarrassment. A causal chain is real enough — one set of aggregates gives rise to the next — but a causal chain is not an agent, and for a later link in a chain to suffer because an earlier link acted looks less like justice than like punishing the wrong party; and if the answer is that the later link just is the earlier one, then a self seems to have been smuggled back in under the aggregates. The appeal to convention, which my friend derisively calls the convention shuffle, strikes me as precisely the maneuver a theory makes when it wants the credit for denying something and the credit for not denying it.

So my question is this: given that Buddhism denies the self, and given that karma requires that the one who reaps be the one who sowed, is anattā either incoherent or else a decorative restatement of a reductionism that a Western philosopher would put more cleanly — and if the Buddha really did refuse to say whether there is a self, is that not evasion rather than profundity, a refusal to take the very stand the doctrine needs him to take? If the way I have set the problem up is itself the difficulty, I would rather be told that than be handed a solution to a problem that does not exist.
