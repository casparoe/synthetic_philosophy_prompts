Assume the role of a philosopher of physics writing a short memo for a group of engineers. They want to know whether any principled thermodynamic barrier rules out a device that cools a sealed room by measuring the thermal motion of the molecules in it and opening a trapdoor only for the fast ones — that is, whether Maxwell's demon is forbidden by physics or merely by engineering.

Treat the two textbook resolutions as too quick: either the demon pays for the measurement, or it pays at least kT ln 2 per bit to erase its memory. Work instead from three claims that strain that telling.

First, erasure can be deferred indefinitely: a demon whose memory is never reset, but is eventually discarded into the environment, has relocated the entropy rather than eliminated it, and it is not obvious that the accounting must come out the same way in every version of the setup.

Second, the demon is itself a physical system governed by fluctuation theorems, so it may exploit a rare fluctuation and repay the debt later; the second law then constrains the average rather than each run.

Third, the demon need not keep a classical record at all: a device that performs a weak quantum measurement and then applies feedback can extract work while barely disturbing the measured system, and the bound that governs it involves the correlation between demon and system rather than any erasure event.

Start with a brief brainstorm of what each claim actually establishes and what it leaves open. Then describe the most important implications of the three taken together for the engineering question: where, precisely, is the cost paid, and does any of this license a perpetual-motion machine of the second kind? Close by naming the claim you would abandon first and stating what argument or experiment would change your verdict.
