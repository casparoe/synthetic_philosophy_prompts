In a discussion group I belong to, an argument broke out after a national election. One participant insisted that the country in question remains a democracy because it still holds competitive elections, oppositions campaign, and voters can and do remove governments from office. Another replied, as though the point were decisive, that democracy is more than elections, and concluded that the regime was not a democracy at all. I have since noticed the same one-liner doing quite different work in other conversations — sometimes about Hungary under Orbán, sometimes about Poland under the previous governing party, sometimes about Turkey, sometimes about anxieties in the United States — and I can no longer tell whether it expresses a genuine criterion or is simply a way of withholding the word "democracy" from regimes the speaker dislikes.

What I want is a disambiguation, not a verdict. Lay out the distinct precise claims that the sentence "democracy is more than elections" could be making, as a menu I can bring back to the group. For each reading, specify what the "more" is taken to be — a further procedural condition (rule of law, independent courts, a free press, enforceable opposition and minority rights); a condition on contestation itself (that the incumbent might really lose, and would really leave if it did); a substantive condition on outcomes (responsiveness between elections, welfare, equality); a claim about popular sovereignty, on which the people's will may be expressed through channels other than balloting; a claim about civic culture and the wider society rather than the state; or something else again — and state what each reading entails, which rival views it rules out, and whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false or self-serving.

Then tell me, for each reading, what it would imply about the four cases above, and in particular whether the criterion can be applied without collapsing into a label for regimes the speaker approves of — that is, whether the standard is fixed independently of the conclusion it is used to reach. Say which reading the participant who treated the line as decisive would have to have meant in order to get their conclusion, and which readings would not support it at all; and say which reading the people who repeat the phrase most often probably intend. Where a reading makes the slogan true by definition, or empties it of content, say so plainly.

Finally, address the question that follows any such disambiguation: once a reading is fixed, who is entitled to apply it — courts, electorates, international monitors, or theorists — and by what procedure, so that the charge of backsliding is not simply one faction's word against another's. Present the readings as a list rather than settling on one and explaining it.
