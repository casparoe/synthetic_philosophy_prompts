An old friend of mine — a historian who spent his career at a provincial university and who seemed to have read everything — once handed me a single page of notes on Fernand Braudel's idea of the longue durée. I keep it in the back of the notebook I've been filling while writing a novel set in a fictional Mediterranean port in the late sixteenth century, and it has quietly become the closest thing I have to a theory of what the book is about. His précis, as well as I can reconstruct it from memory, ran roughly like this. Braudel's great discovery was that history moves at three speeds — the flash of events, the slower oscillation of economic and social cycles, and the nearly geological drift of geography and climate — and that only the slowest of the three is genuinely explanatory. The sea, the mountains behind the port, the winds and the routes they dictate: these are the real agents of history, while battles, treaties and famous men are the froth on the surface, which is why he relegated them to the end of his book and why he took biography and narrative history to be finished as serious pursuits. His Mediterranean is not a backdrop but the protagonist. He was, in effect, a geographical determinist — the land decides and people execute its instructions — and that is why his work fed so naturally into world-systems theory and into the Annales program as a whole, whose founders, Bloch and Febvre, were of one mind with him about all of it; he more or less coined the phrase, and in his hands it functioned less as a method than as a metaphysics. In his later work he extended the same logic to economics, arguing that capitalism is simply what the market looks like once it grows large and durable, so that what we should study is the slow accumulation of structures rather than the choices of merchants and statesmen.

I can see that this is the kind of summary a competent admirer writes and also the kind a specialist winces at, but I cannot tell which sentences are which, and the stakes for me are not academic: if the précis is right, then the decision my protagonist makes in the last third of the book is a piece of self-flattery and I ought to cut it. What I want is an audit, not a reading list — no bibliography, please, since I won't read it and cannot consult anything online. Take the précis claim by claim and tell me which parts a historian working in the Annales tradition would sign off on without reservation; which are the standard simplifications that get repeated in blurbs, prefaces and lecture halls; and which Braudel himself explicitly denied or would have regarded as a travesty. Where a claim is contested rather than false, say who contests it and on what grounds, and where a claim is true only of one phase of his career rather than the whole of it, say so. Then tell me whether the practical conclusion I drew — that in a Braudelian world individuals and events are decorative rather than causal — actually follows from anything Braudel held, or whether it is the kind of inference the précis invites while the underlying view refuses it. I am inclined to believe the determinism charge, incidentally, because the architecture of the book seems to me to say it out loud.

End with a single final line beginning Verdict: followed by one of exactly these five labels — accurate, mostly accurate, half right, mostly misleading, misleading — as your overall judgment of the précis, with nothing after it.
