I teach archaeology at a small liberal arts college in Ohio, and the dean has somehow talked me into giving this year's commencement address — six or seven minutes, so maybe 800-900 words, in front of a couple thousand people: graduating seniors, their families (a lot of grandparents who will be hearing the word "Neolithic" for the first time in their lives), and, in the front rows because they're my own department, eight colleagues who know exactly what I work on. Which is early farming communities. Which is a problem, because the address I actually want to give is the one I've been circling for a few years now: the shift to agriculture is the biggest single thing our species ever did, and I've come to think the "agriculture was a mistake" framing deserves better than the eye-roll it usually gets from people in my field. But the genre won't let me just say that. Commencement is a forward-looking form — you built something, now go build more — and I can't stand up there and tell four hundred people at the beginning of their adult lives that the founding move of civilization was a disaster, and I also dont want to pretend I've solved it and hand them some tidy uplift.

So: write the address. A few things I want it to get right, partly because my colleagues will be listening for them and partly because I don't want a Wikipedia summary of the debate. Farming didn't arrive in one bad afternoon; it crept in region by region over thousands of years, and in some places people were already living in settled villages with storage and some hierarchy before they were farming much at all, so "the moment we chose wrong" is probably the wrong picture even if you think the outcome was bad. The famous forager work-week numbers — the fifteen-hour week, the whole affluent-forager idea — are real findings, but they describe particular groups in particular seasons, and there's plenty of evidence of violence, hunger, and child mortality among foragers too, so I don't want a noble-savage speech. And the inequality story isn't simply "farming made chiefs": some of the earliest farming villages look pretty level, and real hierarchy shows up late and unevenly. That last part is actually what I find hardest. If agriculture was bad, the badness isn't mainly in the calories or the teeth; it's in what storage and surplus eventually made it possible for some people to do to others. My own half-formed view is that calling the whole thing a "mistake" might be a category error — nobody chose it, it was a demographic ratchet (more mouths fed per acre, and you cant un-ratchet), so the interesting question is whether something can be a catastrophe without being anyone's error. I'd rather the speech gesture at that than pretend to settle it.

What I need from you: the address itself, in my voice — a professor who is fond of these kids and a little embarrassed to be at the podium — warm and funny where it can be, not a lecture with a gown on. It should be honest that the evidence is mixed and that I'm not neutral, and it should land somewhere I can actually stand: some way of blessing their forward motion that doesn't run through the usual "and so humanity progressed" story and doesn't collapse into "everything since the Neolithic was a mistake, good luck out there." The alumni magazine will want a pull quote, so end with one line I could hand them, then on a new line a single sentence saying where the address comes down on whether the agricultural revolution was a mistake, beginning with Verdict:.
