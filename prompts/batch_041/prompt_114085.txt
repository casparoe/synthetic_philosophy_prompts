I teach philosophy of physics at a small liberal arts college, and three years ago I sat on the committee that chose the inscription for the bronze plate at the foot of the Foucault pendulum in our new science building. I drafted the wording myself and it went to the foundry as I wrote it: "As this pendulum swings, the earth turns beneath it — proof that space itself is real." The dedication is a month away, and I have been asked to give the short speech at the pendulum, with the plate already bolted to the floor and the donor's family in the second row. What I did not think through when I wrote that sentence is how much philosophy of space it quietly settles. Newton's bucket is supposed to show that the concavity of the water's surface cannot be a fact about the water's motion relative to the bucket, since the surface stays concave once the two turn together, so something has to be absolute; the two globes joined by a cord and whirling in otherwise empty space are supposed to make the same point through the tension in the cord; and the pendulum, ever since Foucault hung one from the dome of the Panthéon in 1851, has looked like that lesson staged in public, its plane holding steady against the distant stars while the floor turns beneath it, the effect strongest at the poles and absent at the equator. But I am no longer confident that any of this shows space to be a thing, rather than showing merely that turning is not turning relative to the room I happen to be standing in. My colleagues disagree about whether the reference that matters is the fixed stars, or the local inertial frame, or the matter distribution that general relativity uses to fix that frame; I have caught myself writing marginal notes on my own plaque the way I would on a student's paper.

Please write the speech. The room will hold the trustees, the donor's widow, three physicists who will notice a loose sentence, a handful of my students, and a colleague who has spent thirty years defending a relational view of spacetime and will be sitting in the front row. It has to be honest — I am not going to stand there and assert something I suspect is false — and it has to honour the donor and the building without turning into a seminar or a retraction, and it has to be sayable aloud by someone holding no notes. I would rather the audience leave with one distinction worth carrying than with a verdict. Keep it under four hundred words, and make the last sentence one I can actually say while standing next to that plate.
