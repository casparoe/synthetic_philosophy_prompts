Ok so I've got a 30 minute slot at a small workshop on epistemic foundations of game theory in three weeks — joint econ/philosophy crowd, maybe twenty people, and I've got the Q&A slot right before lunch so everyone will be caffeinated and impatient. What I need from you is the prep sheet for the question period, not a defence of the paper. I lurk on LessWrong and read the old Overcoming Bias comment threads, so I'm primed to hear "this is a dissolve, not a solution" and I want to be ready for it rather than flattered.

Here's the thesis, in one breath. The standard story is that common knowledge of rationality is what earns you the solution concept: CK of rationality plus CK of the structure is supposed to get you backward induction in perfect-information games, or failing that rationalizability, and the epistemic characterisation of Nash is CK of rationality plus CK of everyone's conjectures. I want to argue the literature quietly runs two seperate things together. There's rationality-as-maximisation, a substantive constraint on choice, and rationality-as-consistency, a condition saying the model contains no room for a player who does the thing the model itself would flag as a mistake. My claim is that the first can never be common-knowledged in any interesting game: the moment you try, either the epistemic model goes inconsistent whenever there's more than one rationalizable outcome, or you've silently upgraded the players' information to something no finite agent could have, i.e. you've turned "rational" into "omniscient about which move is rational". So what's actually doing the work in these theorems is CK of the game form plus a much weaker no-known-improvement condition — rationality-as-consistency. My rude name for the manoeuvre is solution-laundering: you bake the solution concept into the players' information partitions and then "derive" it back out. The tell, I claim, is that in these models you can read the solution off the type space before the rationality clause is ever invoked — the rationality clause never eliminates a type. If I'm right, a chunk of the field is proving theorems that are true but doing no explanatory work.

The room, as best I can work out: (i) two or three people who actually build these knowledge and belief models and prove the characterisation results, so they'll want to know exactly which theorem I'm calling non-explanatory and what work "non-explanatory" is doing operationally; (ii) a philosopher of action who thinks rationality is irreducibly normative and will say I've just rediscovered that an ought doesn't fit inside a partition; (iii) a behavioural guy who'll say fine, but my deflationary version is even less falsifiable, whereas the strong CK story at least made predictions about centipede games that experiments then falsified, so which of us is being the realist here; and (iv) a committed Bayesian who'll press on whether no-known-improvement is a common-prior assumption in disguise, and whether I have any equilibrium concept left at all once the strong reading goes. There's also a senior person in the room who built one of these models, and everyone defers to them, so their objection will land harder than it deserves to. And I have alot riding on this not being the talk where I'm politely told I've reinvented something from thirty years ago.
