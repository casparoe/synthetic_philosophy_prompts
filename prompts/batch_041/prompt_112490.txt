The episode I want analyzed is the informal truce system on the Western Front of the First World War — not the well-known fraternization of Christmas 1914, but the quieter, longer-running arrangement that historians call live and let live, in which opposing infantry units in static sectors settled into routines of non-lethal fire. Treat the following as given rather than as things to look up. The arrangement was tacit and unwritten; neither side sent an envoy, and the signals were the firing patterns themselves, with units firing at fixed times, aiming high, or holding fire while the other side fetched rations, repaired trenches, or recovered bodies. The restraint was deniable, since any single act of forbearance could be explained away as ordinary bad shooting. The arrangement collapsed when a high command ordered raids, harassing fire, or an offensive, and sometimes reassembled once the order was spent. It held for months across stretches of front where the same two units faced each other.

Analyze this through epistemic game theory, with common knowledge as the central concept. Take the following apparatus as given, since the analysis should not depend on your having memorized the literature. A fact is mutual knowledge if everyone knows it, and common knowledge only if everyone knows that everyone knows it, and so on without end; the two are not the same, and the difference is not a technicality. The coordinated attack problem, also called the two generals problem, is the result that no finite exchange of messages over an unreliable channel can make anything common knowledge that was not already so: each acknowledgement needs an acknowledgement, and the regress never closes. And there are results showing that when common knowledge is only almost common knowledge — when each player assigns a small but nonzero probability to the other's not having received the signal — coordination can unravel entirely rather than merely degrade, so that arbitrarily long finite chains of I-know-that-you-know behave quite differently from the infinite chain.

Now do three things.

First, the mapping. What did the two sides actually need to know, and about what: each other's payoffs, each other's rationality, each other's intentions on this occasion, the fact that the other had received the signal, the fact that the other had received the acknowledgement? Did the arrangement require common knowledge, or would mutual knowledge, or something weaker, have sustained it — and what follows for the role of the coordinated attack result here? What plays the role of the unreliable channel, and what plays the role of the acknowledgement, given that the signals were the behavior itself rather than messages? Which of the standard equilibrium notions does the case fit — a repeated-game equilibrium sustained by the shadow of the future, a correlated equilibrium without a correlating device, a focal point — and where does it resist all of them?

Second, the reverse direction. The theory says that coordination rests on common knowledge, and that almost-common-knowledge coordination collapses; the case shows sustained, mutually beneficial cooperation under conditions that look like almost common knowledge at best. Does the case confirm the theory, disconfirm it, or show that it is being applied to the wrong game — for instance, that the cost of attacking alone was low enough, or the horizon long enough, that the unraveling result does not bite? Say which features of the episode do the work.

Third, the breakdowns and the limits. What changes, epistemically, when an order to raid comes down: is it a change in payoffs, in what each side knows, or in what each side knows the other knows? And where is the theory silent? It models the soldiers as instrumentally rational maximizers of given payoffs, but part of what held the arrangement together looks normative — a shared reluctance to kill men who were doing you no harm — and the theory has little to say about how such a norm becomes common knowledge, or what an equilibrium is worth once it does. Mark the silence honestly rather than filling it in.

Be concrete about who knows what at each step, keep the two directions of analysis distinct, and where the episode is indeterminate, say so.
