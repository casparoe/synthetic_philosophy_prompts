ok here's what i'm stuck on. i'm putting together a session for a working group on moral epistemology, and the topic is the preference/bias thing — you know, when is a moral judgement a bias and when is it just a preference, or a commitment, or a taste. the more i read the more i think nobody actually has a theory here, they just have a vibe about which judgements they want to call biased.

so before i go any further: what would a theory of this distinction have to do? give me the desiderata. i want:

- the cases it has to get right, each one with the case attached, not just the abstract requirement
- the distinctions it can't collapse
- the things it has to be consistent with (empirical stuff counts)
- which of these are non-negotiable and which are just what one camp happens to want
- and if some pair of them can't be had together, say so loudly, thats the part i actually care about

some things i want the list to speak to, because these are the ones that keep breaking my drafts:

the case where the judgement comes out of a grubby process and turns out to be right anyway — does the grubbiness still make it a bias; the case where someone's preference has been shaped by their circumstances until they can't tell it apart from a conviction (the fox and the grapes, but with something that actually matters morally); the case where you can see your own conviction is probably a bias and can't just stop having it; the fact that calling something a bias is itself a move in an argument and usually ends it; the fact that any account of the form bias = judgement produced by a distorted process needs some way of telling distorted processes from ordinary ones that doesn't just check whether they landed on the right answer; and the awkward one where bias seems to mean off the truth, but we want the word to do work against people whose moral views we think are false, without assuming our own are true. also, i'm using bias in the loaded sense, a distortion, not just something you're partial to — though part of the question is whether those two ever come apart.

and: is it a distinction in kind, or a continuum with a line drawn somewhere — and if its a continuum, who draws the line and on what grounds. also tell me which single requirement does the most work, i.e. rules out the most otherwise attractive views.

purpose, so the bar isn't crazy: i want a theory we could actually use in the room, to adjudicate two people who each think the other's judgement is a bias and not a preference. so a list thats extensionally perfect but unusable from the inside doesn't help me much — flag it if you think those two pull against each other.

skip the bibliography. if you want, one short paragraph at the end on how a purely procedural account, a purely genealogical/debunking one, and a purely coherentist one fare against the list — but the list is the thing.
