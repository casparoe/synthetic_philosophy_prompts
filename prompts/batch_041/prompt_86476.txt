I am taking a third-year seminar in population ethics, and the unit on whether a larger population is better has left me more interested in why the debate will not close than in which side is right. The seminar runs the familiar circuit: a totalist holds that, other things equal, a world with more people whose lives are worth living is better, and a pronatalist classmate pushes this toward the claim that adding good lives is always an improvement; against them stand a critical-level view and a person-affecting view that refuse to accept that a vast population of barely-worth-living lives is better than a smaller flourishing one. The arguments on each side are old, both sides understand the other's moves, and nobody in the room has shifted in a term. I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict on it.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the two camps are not really disputing how much to value additional lives but disagreeing about what kind of object betterness is being asked to be. I have started calling the two demands the ranking requirement and the comparison requirement. The ranking requirement says an adequate axiology must deliver a single, context-independent ordering of outcomes — a total order — and that any failure of transitivity or completeness is a reductio. The comparison requirement asks for less: a family of pairwise judgments, each compelling where it is made, that need not compose into one global ranking, so that a claim of the form this world is better than that one can be true as a comparison while no overall ordering exists to make it true. If that is the real fault line, then the Repugnant Conclusion is a reductio for only one side, and the long stall is a disagreement about the standards an axiology must meet rather than about the value of people.

Please test this against what each side actually says, and tell me whether the fault line is really where I have put it or somewhere else — for instance a hidden disagreement about personal identity and the same-people assumption, about whether reasons must be person-affecting, about whether the relevant cases differ only in population size or also in which people exist, or about whether our intuitions here are jointly inconsistent, so that the question is ill-posed rather than merely hard. Then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a formal result, a distinction, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected, and why. I am not looking for a survey of positions or a reading list; I want the structure of the disagreement and an honest verdict on whether it can be moved.
