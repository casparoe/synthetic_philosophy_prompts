I run a small coaching service -- two of us, really -- out of a nonprofit credit union, where people come in and we set up "commitment" arrangements for money and health stuff: locked savings accounts, auto-transfers with a penalty for pulling out early, website blockers where a friend holds the password, and then the softer stuff (weekly check-ins, texts, a fifty-dollar bet with a sibling). We do maybe twenty intakes a month, sessions are 45 minutes, and the people I see are not patients, I have no clinical training, and I'm the one who signs off on which device we actually build -- so when it goes wrong it's on me. I keep hitting the same wall. Someone comes in wanting to lock away money for a house, and eight weeks later calls me upset because the car died, or their kid needs something, or they just changed their mind, and I honestly don't know whether I'm supposed to be the person who says this is the whole point of the device, no, or the person who says fine, we release you. Flip side: someone asks me for a hard lock on something and I can't tell whether their long-term goal was ever really their goal or just something they said in a good mood on a Tuesday.

So I want a procedure I can actually run, in the room, in 45 minutes, grounded in what philosophy and the behavioral stuff actually say about weakness of will and about how we discount the future. Raw material I've picked up and want you to use: the classic puzzle is that akrasia is supposed to be acting, freely and intentionally, against your own better judgment, which sounds nearly impossible -- if you really judged that A was better all things considered, how did you intentionally do B? And the resolution I find most useful is the one (Holton's, I think) that says weakness of will is really about revising a resolution without adequate reason, not about acting against a judgment, which would mean someone who calmly changes their mind isn't weak-willed at all, while someone who caves to a craving while still thinking they shouldn't is. On the future side there's the standard story: people discount the near future much more steeply than the far future, so a reward at day 1 vs day 2 gets weighed very differently from day 30 vs day 31, which produces preference reversals -- you sincerely prefer the gym at 11pm and sincerely prefer the sofa at 6am -- and commitment devices are the usual fix. I gather the literature also separates being sophisticated (you know you'll reverse) from naive (you don't) from partially naive, and that the design advice differs a lot depending on which one the person is. And there's apparently a real debate about whether near-bias is even irrational -- maybe discounting is fine and only the inconsistency is the problem -- plus something about a bias toward the future that makes time-neutrality look less mandatory, which I dont fully understand but which I suspect matters for whether I should be overriding anyone at all.

What I want: an ordered decision procedure -- the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good-enough answer to each, where it terminates in a no, we don't build this device, and where it has to stop and hand the call to the person themselves (or to a clinician) because it's a values question rather than a factual one. Include the small set of tests I can run in the room, and be explicit about which steps are just judgment wearing a procedure costume. Then run it on three cases: (a) the house-savings person who wants out at week eight because the car died; (b) someone who wants me to lock their phone in a timed safe for six months to finish a thesis; (c) someone still inside their commitment who asked me in advance to talk them out of quitting, where I suspect the honest answer is that I should let them quit and I won't like it. Also tell me the one question in the procedure that's actually doing the work -- where I'd get the worst outcome if I skipped it or misapplied it -- and what the procedure just can't do. I assume it can't tell me whether the long-term self or the short-term self is the real one, but say what that means for me in practice.
