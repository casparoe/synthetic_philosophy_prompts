I got into a stupidly long argument with a friend last week about underdetermination and now I want my students to actually feel the thing instead of nodding at the word. Her line was: you can always cook up a rival theory that fits all the evidence, so science never really lands on one truth. Mine was: thats the cheap version, anyone can fit a curve through finitely many points and it costs nothing to do it. The version that bites is when the rivals tie on everything we care about — fit, simplicity, scope, novel prediction, coherence with the rest of what we believe — and still disagree about the world. She didnt buy it. So design me a 90-minute activity for 16 undergrads (200-level philosophy of science seminar, one room, whiteboard, projector, no money) where they build the rivals themselves and then have to admit they can't break the tie using the standards they set.

Keep it physical, not a lecture: a sealed device (cardboard box with something inside, or a black box whose outputs get projected; either way the rule is nobody opens it), a data sheet of input-output pairs, a held-out prediction round, and a grant panel that has to fund exactly one theory at the end. Roles for two rival theory teams, a scoring panel, and the funding board. Print the rubric on cards so it's fixed and public — fit, simplicity (count the free parameters and the moving parts), scope, novel prediction, coherence with background belief — and have them score each other out of a fixed budget of points, with a minute-by-minute-ish timeline for me to follow.

Heres the part I care most about, so build it in deliberately: I want the tie guaranteed by construction, not hoped for. The two mechanisms the teams end up with should provably produce identical outputs on every input the game can ever generate, held-out round included, while differing about what's inside — different ontology, different guts, same behavior. Tell me how to build it and how to check it, because I don't want to find out on the day that one team's theory accidentally wins the prediction round.

Then add a round where a refutation lands and a team is allowed to save itself with an auxiliary assumption, followed by a rule that auxiliaries have to be simple and independently testable too. I want the debrief to use that round to seperate two things students always merge: the auxiliary/holism point (Duhem-Quine) and underdetermination proper. In the debrief, map the moments of the game onto the idea, make them notice what they actually reached for when they finally broke the tie, and handle the two replies that will come: that if the rivals agree on everything observable the choice is merely verbal, and that we should just wait for more data (the settleable-later kind versus the never-settleable kind). No lecture up front and don't let the words "underdetermination" or "holism" out of my mouth until the debrief.

And be honest with me about two things: what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it (I assume someone will say the simpler theory is obviously true and refuse to play), and which feature of the idea the activity definately can't reproduce, so I know what I have to say out loud myself.
