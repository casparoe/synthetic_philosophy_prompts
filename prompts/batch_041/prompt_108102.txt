I am a novelist, and I have been asked to open a symposium on how fiction means things with a short statement of first principles. Before I commit it to print I would like an audit of the position, because I suspect I assembled it from incompatible sources and would rather learn that now than from the audience.

Here is the position.

1. Nothing written is meaningful in itself. Marks on a page become a message only relative to a decoder and relative to a world the message can be mapped onto; meaning lives in that mapping, not in the ink.

2. Every message arrives in three layers, and I have built my whole account of fiction on them. There is the frame message: the signal that this is a message at all and is to be taken as one. There is the outer message: the signal telling you which code or convention to apply. And there is the inner message: the content you are left with once the first two have done their work. My claim is that in a novel the frame and outer messages carry the load and the inner message is comparatively thin — that the sentences and the plot are largely a vehicle for the framing.

3. The author's intentions have no bearing on what a text means. I wrote the book; I do not own the reading of it.

4. All the same, the frame and outer messages of a novel are things I design. I choose to write a murder mystery; I choose to signal that the narrator is unreliable. When a reader takes the novel as memoir, or takes the irony straight, that reader has misread it, and there is a fact of the matter about that.

5. A text can mean something that no one has yet recovered. There are readings waiting to be found; this is what I take the claim that a book is inexhaustible to amount to.

6. There is no unframed text. Because the frame message must itself be decoded — you have to know that something is a message before you can ask what it says — the regress never bottoms out, and what stops it in practice is not the text but the reader's situation: what they have been trained to treat as a message, and by whom.

I am not asking whether these claims are true. I am asking whether one person at one time can hold all six. Tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which merely look that way, and rank the genuine ones by the cost of repairing them. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest fix would be — cheapest, not most elegant; I would rather patch than rebuild. Then tell me which single revision would clear up the most, and what I would be surrendering by making it.

Two requests. Do not tell me the package is fine because the tensions are productive or dialectical; I want a verdict on each pair that matters, including the pairs that survive scrutiny, and a plain statement of what makes them survive. And do not let me off with the observation that some of these are really one commitment in two costumes — if that is so, say which costume to discard. You may skip pairs that are obviously compatible; what I want are the ones I have not noticed.
