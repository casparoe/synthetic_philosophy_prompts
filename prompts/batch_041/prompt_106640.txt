Okay so last night my friend Dana and I got into a two-hour arguement about Ayn Rand and now I have to write a wedding toast, so I'm outsourcing this to you. Setup: my brother Theo gets married Saturday to Priya. Theo is a real Objectivist — not a phase, he's read all of it, he genuinely believes self-interest is the foundation of ethics and that altruism is the thing rotting the culture. Priya is a hospice social worker. Her family is a big warm Catholic-ish clan from Cleveland, her grandma basically runs the parish food pantry and has loud opinions about people who won't help others, and a solid third of our side of the room are Theo's Objectivist friends who will hear any false note instantly. So the toast has to warm up Priya's family AND not turn Theo into something he isn't.

Here's where Dana and I got stuck, and it's why I need help. Every standard toast move seems poisoned for this room. He'd give her the world. He'd do anything for her. They'd sacrifice for each other. Theo would say that's exactly the altruism he rejects — that a man who'd sacrifice himself for his wife isn't romantic, he's a doormat, and Priya would be insulted by it too. Dana's take is that I'm overthinking it: he says Rand's real target was never kindness, it was the idea that self-sacrifice is the standard of morality, that your life belongs to others, and that on his reading generosity and devotion toward people you actually value are fine, even admirable, because they're expressions of your own values and don't cost you anything you'd rather keep. The charity Rand hated was charity as an obligation, as the thing that proves you're good. So Dana says a Randian can adore his wife completely and still be a Randian, and the toast should be about how Priya is the best thing Theo has ever chosen to value, not about how he'd lose himself for her. I told him that's way too tidy — Rand clearly had contempt for the whole serve-others instinct, not just the coerced version, and you can't scrub that out and call the remainder Randian. We never resolved it. Anyway: write me the toast, like three minutes, spoken aloud, no lecturing, and please don't quote her (Theo thinks turning her into a slogan is tacky). It has to be a real toast that makes both halves of that room feel something, not a philosophy seminar with champagne. If you have to pick a side in what Dana and I were fighting about, pick one and let it show — just don't paper it over with vague warm fuzzies that would ring false to the Objectivists or cold to Priya's grandma.
