I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, though I've read enough on my own to have formed a settled view about the gap between descriptive and normative claims, and I'd rather you stress-test that view than explain it back to me. The view is this: no set of premises that are purely descriptive — premises that say only how things are, were, or will be — logically entails any conclusion about how things ought to be. Any argument that appears to move from is to ought must be smuggling in a normative premise, so the normative conclusion is never derived from the descriptive facts alone but only from those facts plus an evaluative assumption. I tend to picture it as a typing rule: descriptive claims and normative claims belong to different types, there is no well-typed function from the first to the second, and introducing ought-vocabulary is like introducing a new primitive that the descriptive theory cannot manufacture. What I want from you is a map of what would count against this, ranked by how decisive each consideration would actually be, separating the ones that would be fatal from the ones that would be merely awkward.

I've collected a few candidate objections over the years and I don't trust my own verdict on any of them, so please assess each rather than just listing them. First, supervenience: if the normative necessarily supervenes on the natural — no normative difference without some natural difference — does that give a necessary connection strong enough to amount to entailment, or does it leave the gap exactly where it was? I genuinely can't tell which way that cuts. Second, so-called thick concepts: words like cruel or cowardly seem to carry description and evaluation in a single term, and if saying an act was cruel entails that it was in some respect bad, is that an is-to-ought inference or merely an entailment internal to a concept, and does the answer matter for the thesis? Third, institutional facts: that someone made a promise looks like a descriptive fact if anything does, and it seems to entail that they ought to keep it, given only the rules of the practice — is that a counterexample, or is the premise that one ought to follow the practice an unstated normative premise doing all the work? Fourth, I once read that logicians call a theory a conservative extension of another when it adds new vocabulary without proving any new claims expressible in the old vocabulary, and I've wondered whether the whole is-ought thesis is just the demand that normative vocabulary be conservative over descriptive vocabulary — if so, is there actually a proof behind that, or is it a slogan dressed as a result, and where does the analogy break down? Fifth, the pragmatic reading: if the inference is invalid as logic but licensed because the missing premise is conversationally implied rather than stated, does that defeat the thesis or only refine it into something narrower? Sixth, what I think of as the first-ought problem: if the thesis is true, then no ought can ever be derived from non-normative facts, so every normative system must begin from an underived normative premise. Is that a commitment the view has to accept, and is it an acceptable one, or does it push the view toward intuitionism or non-cognitivism that its proponents might not want to own?

Please also tell me which of these the view's own defenders would concede as genuinely counting against them and which they would dismiss out of hand, rather than giving me only the critics' side of each dispute. I'd like to know whether the position as I've stated it is falsifiable at all, or whether it has been qualified into something no observation or argument could touch, and if so whether that insulation is a defect or just the price of a thesis of this kind. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict on the view, and where your own reasoning in answering me is most likely to fail.
