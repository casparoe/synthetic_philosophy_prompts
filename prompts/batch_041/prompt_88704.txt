I have been trying to work out what I actually think about killing one person to save several, going by my gut on particular cases, and I want to know what principle my verdicts add up to, if any.

Diverting a runaway rail car onto a spur where it will hit one worker instead of the five on the main line: fine. Detonating a charge that derails the car short of the five, where the blast will also kill a bystander standing near the track: fine. Shoving a worker into the car's path so that his body stops it: not fine. Diverting the car onto a short stub spur, where it will hit one worker and his body will stop it before it can roll back onto the main line and kill the five: fine. Seizing a passerby and throwing him onto the tracks to derail the car: not fine. Standing by and letting the five die because the only way to stop the car costs one life: not fine.

The pair I cannot square is the stub spur and the shove. In both, one person's death is what saves the five, and in both I have done something to bring that death about; yet one feels fine and the other monstrous. I have tried telling myself the difference is that on the stub spur I am redirecting a threat that already existed, whereas in the shove I am creating a new one — but the charge also creates a new threat, the blast, and I do not object to that. I have also tried "I may never use a person as a means," but that would condemn the stub spur. And "I may never intend a death" does not obviously separate them either, since on the stub spur I am counting on the worker's death too.

Is there a principle that gets all six verdicts, or close? If not, which verdict should I give up? And what does the surviving principle say about a case I have not put to myself: I can save the five by diverting the car onto a spur, but the switch is jammed and the only way to free it is to cut a cable whose recoil will kill a worker standing by — is that the shove, or the stub spur, or something else?
