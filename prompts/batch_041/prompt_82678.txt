For a philosophy club paper I want to treat jaywalking as a test case for a theory of convention, and I would like the analysis to run in both directions. The episode: the word jaywalker entered American usage in the 1900s and was promoted through the 1910s and 1920s by motoring interests who wanted the street redefined as a place for cars; the first widely copied ordinance, Los Angeles's in 1925, was drafted by the auto lobby; and in recent years several American states have repealed or narrowed their jaywalking laws. The account I am working with goes roughly like this: a convention is a regularity of behavior that solves a coordination problem, is arbitrary in the sense that some other regularity would have served as well, and is sustained because each person conforms in the expectation that others will. Does jaywalking law fit that account, or does its origin as the project of one interested party disqualify it, and if it is not a convention, what is it instead? If it does fit, what does that show about whether conventions obligate us, given that this one is on the books yet widely violated? And what does the account say about a pedestrian visiting from a city where crossing mid-block is unremarkable, who arrives somewhere it is illegal and locally frowned upon — what does she owe, and does that survive the thought that the norm is the residue of a successful campaign rather than a shared solution to a shared problem? I would rather be told where the account is silent than see it stretched.
