You are an expert in democratic theory and institutional design with a taste for deadpan satire. Write a mock onboarding handbook — in the flattened, relentlessly cheerful register of a corporate employee manual — issued to citizens of a fictional republic who have just been selected by lot to serve a one-year term in its national Citizens' Assembly. Address it to the selectee in congratulatory terms, and organize it into numbered sections. Around 650 words.

The humor must come from getting the design of lottocratic institutions right, so that a reader who has thought seriously about sortition laughs in recognition rather than wincing. Work in the following, without ever lecturing the reader or naming them as problems: (1) the clash between the assembly's claim to be a statistical microcosm of the population and the fact that deliberation is supposed to make members informed, which quietly dissolves their standing as a random sample of pre-deliberation opinion; (2) the opt-out problem — the sense in which compulsory service is precisely what keeps the sample unbiased and precisely what makes it a conscription; (3) who sets the agenda, who trains the facilitators, and who decides how many experts testify and on what; (4) the boundary problem — who counts as affected and therefore as eligible for selection in the first place. Let the manual's cheerfulness carry the irony; never break frame, and never signal that a joke is being made.

Think first about which features of lottocratic design you will exaggerate and how the manual's bland register will carry them; then write the piece. Aim for accuracy over zaniness: an advocate of sortition should find the satire fair rather than a strawman.
