So my housemate has gotten really into Buddhist-adjacent philosophy lately and he's landed on a view he calls tranquilism, which as far as I can tell is this: the only thing good in itself is the absence of craving, and the only thing bad in itself is craving and suffering. Pleasure isn't good in its own right, it's good only when it's the relief of a craving -- i.e. it's good because it removes some of the bad thing. So a being with no cravings sitting there neutral is doing as well as a being can possibly do; a blissed-out being isn't doing any better, it just gets to the same zero by a different route.

I pushed back and we ended up with this argument, which I wrote down in numbered steps because he kept moving the pieces around. I'm a software engineer, I don't know the proper jargon for any of this.

1. The only intrinsic good is the absence of craving; the only intrinsic bad is the presence of craving and suffering. Pleasure is good only instrumentally, by relieving craving.
2. A state of affairs is better than another just when it contains less craving -- equivalently, a greater absence of craving.
3. A being with no craving and no suffering is already at the ceiling: nothing can be better for it. Its badness is 0, and there's no going below 0.
4. A rock has no craving and no suffering. So does a corpse. So does an empty galaxy.
5. So a universe with exactly one serene being in it and a universe with literally nothing in it have the same total craving (zero) and the same total absence of craving (everything).
6. So tranquilism can't say the empty universe is worse than the serene one, or the serene one better than the empty one.
7. Now take the serene being and painlessly annihilate it, instantly, with no craving at the moment of death. The absence of craving is preserved: zero before, zero after.
8. So on tranquilism that annihilation is value-neutral. The only reason not to do it would be to prevent future craving, which if anything is a reason *to* do it.
9. So the best world is an empty one, and moving toward it is good, and the population size of serene beings is irrelevant -- a trillion of them are worth exactly as much as one, which is worth exactly as much as none.
10. But that seems to prove way too much. It says there's no reason to keep anyone alive who isn't currently suffering, and it makes a universe of flourishing, contented people indistinguishable in value from a lifeless void.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? My housemate wants to keep 1 and 3 and says 6-9 are just consequences he accepts. I'd like to keep 1 and 3 too if I can, but I'll give up whatever is cheapest -- I just want to know what it costs me.
