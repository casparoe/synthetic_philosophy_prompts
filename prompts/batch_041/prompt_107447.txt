I'm teaching the Respuesta de la poetisa a la muy ilustre Sor Filotea de la Cruz (1691) in a seminar on early modern women and philosophy, and every year the first few pages trip me up more than the bits everyone already knows. Can you help me get a grip on whats actually happening rhetorically in the opening? Here's the stretch I mean:

"El primero (y para mí el más riguroso) es saber responder a vuestra doctísima, discretísima, santísima y amorosísima carta. Y si veo que preguntado el Ángel de las Escuelas, Santo Tomás, de su silencio con Alberto Magno, su maestro, respondió que callaba porque nada sabía decir digno de Alberto, con cuánta mayor razón callaría, no como el Santo, de humildad, sino que en la realidad es no saber algo digno de vos."

and a bit later:

"No es afectada modestia, Señora, sino ingenua verdad de toda mi alma..."

and then the odd bit where she says silence has to be given some brief label, otherwise silence will say nothing, since saying nothing is its proper office:

"...es menester decir siquiera que no se pueden decir, para que se entienda que el callar no es no haber qué decir, sino no caber en las voces lo mucho que hay que decir."

Two things I want from you. First, a close reading: what is she doing with the Aquinas/Albert comparison, and what work is that little paradox about silence supposed to do in a letter that is, after all, a very long breaking of silence? Second, the interpretive question I genuinely can't settle. Half the room reads this opening as strategy -- she's a woman under real ecclesiastical pressure, so she wraps herself in abjection and then spends the rest of the letter demonstrating that she isn't in fact abject, with the humility functioning as a shield. The other half reads it as sincere: she really does hold that a creature's knowledge is nothing before God, and the defense of her studies is consistent with that rather than in tension with it. I keep flip-flopping. Does the passage itself push one way, or is it underdetermined? And whichever way it goes, does it change how we should read her later claims about women being taught and about women's education more broadly, or is that a separate argument that survives either reading?

Last thing: is it anachronistic for me to be asking about "strategy" here at all, given the conventions of self-abasement in that genre? I'd like to know if my framing is smuggling in modern assumptions that the text wouldnt recognize.
