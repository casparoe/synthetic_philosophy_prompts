I teach the final-year seminar in moral and political philosophy, and this year I have had to rebuild its closing unit for a reason I would not have chosen. A cousin of mine was killed last winter when a shell struck the street where he lived, and in the months since, several people who meant to comfort me have told me that the world is becoming less violent — that his death, however terrible, sits on a long downward slope. I do not think they are simply wrong. But I found that I could not say what would have to be true for their claim to be true, and I have come to think that my inability to say it is the philosophical problem rather than a gap in my reading. I am plainly an interested party here, which is one reason I want the exam to be answerable from the armchair.

Please write a single essay question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme.

Constraints on the question. It must not be answerable by summarizing the standard debate, and it should not reward students for having read more of the literature than their classmates do. So do not ask them to recount the history of the thesis or to name its defenders and critics; the exam is closed-book and they will have no citations to offer anyway. Instead, hand them the analytical tools inside the question stem and require them to use them. Three in particular:

1. The distinction between a rate and a count, and the observation that the two answer different questions — how likely is a given person to die violently? versus how much violent death is there in total? — and that neither is a proxy for the other once the population is changing. A falling rate with a rising count does not mean the same harm is being spread differently; it means the distribution of risk has changed.

2. The heavy-tail point. Where a quantity such as the death toll of an armed conflict has a distribution heavy-tailed enough that the mean does not converge, the sample mean is unstable and the notion of an expected annual toll is not well defined; a per-capita trend estimate can then be driven by whether one extreme event happens to fall inside the window being measured.

3. The recognition-expansion confound. Every measure of violence is a measure of recognized violence. If what counts as violence widens over time — as it has for domestic violence, for violence against children, for sexual violence within marriage, for what is often called structural violence — then measured violence can rise while the harm it is meant to track falls; and the historical baseline was compiled by institutions that counted only what suited them.

I also want the question to force them onto the conceptual issue, not just the statistical ones: whether the question of whether violence is declining has a fact of the matter at all. The essay should make them separate the reading on which the question is perfectly determinate once a definition and a measure are fixed, the reading on which it turns on which definition is correct, and the reading on which violence is a partly evaluative concept, so that the question smuggles in a moral judgment. I want them to see that the third reading does not automatically win, and that the second is not the same as the third.

Concretely, I would like the stem to open with a short exchange between two students — one who holds that the falling homicide rate settles the matter, the other who holds that the fall is largely an artifact of who does the killing and who does the counting — and then to pose the task in terms of what each would have to concede, which parts of their disagreement are verbal, and whether the dispute has a determinate resolution.

Format: a single essay, 90 minutes, 40 marks, one of three questions on the paper, so the question itself must be answerable in about 75 minutes of writing. The model answer should be a genuine answer — it must take a position on the fact-of-the-matter question and defend it — not a survey. Build the marking scheme on five must-hit points worth 8 marks each, with a one-line note for each on how candidates typically get it wrong, and a closing note on what separates a first-class answer from a solid second.
