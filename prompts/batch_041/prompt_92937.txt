I teach a decision theory / philosophy of probability seminar — mix of grad students and a few strong undergrads, 14 people, one 90-minute session — and I want them to feel why someone would flatly refuse to hand over a single number for a credence, and then feel the thing that makes the imprecise-probability picture uncomfortable rather than just noble. Here's the shape I have in mind, redesign it if you see something better.

Fake chips, no real money. Everyone starts with a balance. For each bet they have to post two numbers: the lowest price at which they'll buy a $1 ticket, and the highest price at which they'll sell one. Then a dealer — me, or a rotating student — gets to choose which side of the trade to take at the student's own quoted price. So if you quote a single point you get skinned; the only way to survive a round is to quote a spread. First half: bets on urns, some with compositions everyone knows and some where the mix is fixed but nobody knows it (including one where the split was set by a student who's now left the room). I want them to discover for themselves that their spread is wide on the mystery urn and collapses to nothing on the known one — that the width of the spread is doing real work and isn't just timid hedging.

Second half is the part I actually care about. I want them to walk into dilation without me saying the word. After they've settled into their spreads, reveal something that looks completely irrelevant to the mystery urn — a genuinely fair coin, or a draw from an urn whose odds everyone knows cold — and make them re-quote. Arrange the mechanics so that the pull they feel, and the argument they can actually give, is toward widening their spread on the mystery urn even though nothing they just learned has anything to do with it. Then the reveal: this isn't a trick of my setup, it's what happens when your spread really is the lower and upper bound of a set of priors and you've been quietly letting those priors disagree about how the two urns are wired together. I'd like the debrief to push them in both directions at once — that a sharp credence would also have been exploitable in round one, and that the imprecise one is now getting vaguer for free.

Give me the whole thing: materials, exact urn compositions and payoffs, a minute-by-minute timeline, the numbers I should have on the board so the arithmetic doesn't bog us down, the dealer's rules, and a debrief that maps each moment back onto the underlying distinction. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — my guesses are that someone tries to game the dealer, someone refuses to quote anything at all, and the spread arithmetic eats ten minutes I dont have — and how to handle each. And be straight with me about which part of this I can't stage: my hunch is the dilation step only lands if I've already smuggled in the right dependence structure between the two urns, and if that's right I want you to say so and say what the game therefore can't show.

One more thing. For anything in the debrief that's actually contested — whether ambiguity aversion is a mistake or a virtue, whether imprecision is a real alternative to sharp credences or just a way of hiding higher-order uncertainty, whether dilation is a reductio of the imprecise framework or just a feature of it — seperate what people who work on this agree on from what's still genuinely up for grabs, and dont hand my students a live disagreement dressed up as consensus.
