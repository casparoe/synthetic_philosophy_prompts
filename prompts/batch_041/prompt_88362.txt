I've got a half-finished essay I want your comments on, because I've hit a wall with it and I've stopped trusting my own ear.

Context: I'm a novelist. Last spring a reader emailed to tell me my book was clearly about X (her mother, basically), I wrote back that no, actually I'd meant Y, and she replied — politely! — that it didn't really matter what I'd meant. Which stung more than it should have, and I've been chewing on it alot since. So the essay is about whether she's right. It's for a lit mag, or maybe I'll use it as a craft lecture, so it has to work for people who aren't philosophers.

Here's the shape of it.

Part one, the easy part. She's right that my say-so is bad evidence. Novelists lie in interviews, forget what they were doing, rationalise after the fact; and anyway meaning the right thing isn't the same as pulling it off. I'll say "the intentional fallacy" here, wave at the New Critics and at Barthes, and move on. Everybody in the audience has heard it.

Part two, where it gets hard. I want to argue you can't get a determinate text without positing some intention. The words alone don't do it — English underdetermines what any given sentence is up to — so the reader has to decide what kind of person would have written this, in this order, to this end. Refuse to do that and you don't get freedom, you get mush, or the reader's own autobiography. So the meaning is what a reader who knows the conventions and the period would be justified in thinking I meant, where "justified" means: the reading that makes the most of the text. I think that view has a name — hypothetical intentionalism, where the author you appeal to is a postulated or idealised author rather than the actual me. If I've got that wrong, tell me, because the whole middle of the essay leans on it.

Part three, the twist. If that's right, my own testimony is evidence but not decisive. Which is a strange thing to believe about your own book, and I'm not sure I can actually live in that position, but fine, it's an essay, not a lifestyle.

Part four, the wall. When I tried to write part three honestly I realised I don't have one intention, I have at least four, and they don't agree:

- what I planned before I started;
- what I was actually doing sentence by sentence, which I'd swear I know but suspect is mostly reconstructed from the text;
- what I think it means now, which is different and probably better;
- what an idealised reader would attribute to me in order to make the book come out best.

Do those four compete, or are they just four different things that only look like they're fighting? I genuinely can't tell.

Part five, the thing I think is my best card, and I want to know whether it's actually a card. There's a distinction I keep running into between intending what your words shall mean and intending what kind of thing you're making at all — not "I intend this sentence to say P" but "I intend this to be a novel and not a memoir," "a comedy and not a botched tragedy," "an unreliable narrator and not an incompetent one." Those second-kind intentions look constitutive rather than evidential: if I didn't intend the narrator to be unreliable, then he isn't unreliable, he's just wrong, and the book is a different book. You can't settle those questions from the words alone. So at least some authorial intention isn't optional. But I suspect the hypothetical intentionalist just shrugs and says the hypothesis includes the category — and if that's so, I can't see what's left of my argument. Which would mean parts two and five are pulling against each other and I should cut one. Which one?

What I want from you:

1. Am I getting hypothetical intentionalism right, or have I invented a view nobody holds? And does it really differ from "the text means whatever a competent reader gets out of it," or is that the same thing with better manners?
2. Is the four-intentions list a real problem, or am I making a problem out of a list?
3. Is that categorial/categorical point — the kind-of-thing intentions — the strongest argument for my side, and can the other side absorb it for free? If they can, say so; I'd rather know.
4. Does any of this land for a general reader, or is it inside baseball? Where would you cut?
5. An ending. I don't want to finish on some wishy-washy both-sides thing.

Don't send me a reading list, I won't read it. And if you use a term that isn't in the draft, define it — I'll be reading this on a train.
