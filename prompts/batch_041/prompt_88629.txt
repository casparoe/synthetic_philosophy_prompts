A friend and I got into a stupidly long arguement about whether you could have come from a different sperm and egg, and I came away realizing I don't actually know what the thesis is supposed to be. The version I always hear is: your origin is essential, so a person conceived from different gametes would not have been you, however similar the resulting person turned out. Fine. But when I try to say what counts as your origin, it feels like three different things get run together — the particular pair of gametes, the genome that got assembled out of them, and the zygote as a particular token thing rather than a type.

Can you explain the issue properly? I want to see the cases where those three readings come apart, and why they matter: an embryo that splits into identical twins (so one gamete pair, two distinct people); a chimera, where two early embryos fuse so a single body has two genomes and four gametes in it; and a clone made by putting someone's cell nucleus into an egg whose own nucleus has been removed (different origin, nearly the same genome). For each one, say which version of the origin claim it actually puts pressure on, and whether the usual replies do real work or just stipulate. The twin case is the one that nags at me: if each twin's origin is the gamete pair, then origin doesn't individuate anybody and the thesis gets a lot weaker; if it's the zygote, then it's strange that neither twin is the zygote. And if a chimera has two origins, what's left of the idea that origin is essential?

I know the necessary-a-posteriori basics, so skip those. I want the conceptual structure: what the thesis is really claiming, how the readings come apart, which of the cases is the strongest objection and why, and what the best repair costs you — does it end up needing a primitive thisness, does it make your essence partly historical rather than something about you, does it quietly turn into a claim about our concept of a person rather than about what's necessary?
