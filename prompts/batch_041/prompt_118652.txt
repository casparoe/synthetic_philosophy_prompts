I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy with a master's in sociology, applying to a one-year fellowship at a centre that funds work at the boundary of philosophy and the social sciences, and the application requires a two-page research proposal that I need you to write. My half-formed idea came out of an argument with a friend who works in marketing analytics: she kept talking about the preferences of the youngest adult cohort as though the label picked out a group with a shared psychology, and I kept wanting to say that the label is a commercial artefact — but I couldn't say it cleanly, because some cohort effects are plainly real (people who came of age during a particular recession, or inside a particular media environment, do differ in measurable ways), and because the same birth-year window does double duty as a demographic index and as a name for a culturally self-aware group that recognises itself under that name. What I want to argue is that the usual realism-versus-anti-realism framing about generations is the wrong shape. There are at least three different things doing work under the one word — the cohort as a birth-year interval used as an explanatory variable in demography and epidemiology; the cohort as a self-conscious cultural formation constituted by a shared formative experience; and the cohort as a segmentation category used in marketing and journalism that feeds back into the self-understanding of the people it labels — and the live questions are (a) whether the realism question gets a different answer for each of the three, and (b) whether the roles interact in a way that makes any of them unreal rather than merely constructed. Two things I want built into the proposal, because I think they are where the real work is. First, a technical constraint: birth year, age and period are linearly dependent, so once age and period are accounted for, a separate cohort effect cannot be estimated without imposing a constraint that is not in the data; my instinct is that the explanatory kind invoked when someone says "it's a generational effect" is therefore underdetermined by the evidence in a way that more data collection cannot repair, and I want to know whether that underdetermination is a fact about cohorts or a fact about our models. Second, feedback: labels like these are used to address people, and being so addressed changes what people do and how they describe themselves, so the category may be self-fulfilling or self-undermining — that is the part I most want to get right and the part I am least sure how to handle.

Write the proposal. My supervisor suspects this is a sociology paper in disguise and wants the philosophical payoff made explicit, so the proposal has to show what turns on the answer beyond the sociology. In particular, tell me whether the project as I have framed it is in danger of collapsing into the uninteresting claim that the word "generation" is ambiguous, and if so what change of framing would save it. I want the reasoning self-critical rather than encouraging: say which of my two build-ins you would drop if only one could survive, where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong, and what argument or consideration would change your mind about the framing.
