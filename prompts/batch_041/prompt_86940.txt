I'm in a decision theory reading group and we just spent a whole session on the Pasadena game, where a fair coin gets flipped until it lands heads, and if it takes n flips you win or lose 2^n/n depending on whether n is odd or even. The wild part is that when you work out the expected value, the terms come out to plus 1, minus 1/2, plus 1/3, minus 1/4, and so on, so the series never settles on anything -- you can rearrange the terms to get any real number you like, or positive or negative infinity. So unlike the St. Petersburg game where the expectation is at least infinite, here it's just undefined. Nover and Hájek came up with this in a 2004 paper, and I get why people say it's a deeper problem than St. Petersburg, but I'm having trouble seeing what actually follows from it. A couple people in the group said it just shows expected utility theory is incomplete and needs to be replaced, while others said it's a pathological edge case that doesn't matter for anything real.

What I really want is for you to walk me through the most important implications of this undefined-expected-value situation -- not just the headline that expected utility theory can't assign a number, but what that actually means downstream. Does it undermine the idea that rational agents should maximize expected utility in general, or only in contrived cases? Does it change how we should think about the foundations of probability and utility, or about the assumption that outcomes can be summed in a well-defined way? What about the move of just refusing to play the game, or treating undefined expectations as a reason to reject the setup as illegitimate -- does that dodge the problem or just beg the question? And is there any lesson here for ordinary decisions where I can't assign precise probabilities or payoffs, or is that a totally different issue? I'm especially curious what you'd single out as the single most important implication, and why.
