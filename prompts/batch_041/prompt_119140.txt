I am a novelist, and I have written myself into a scene I cannot fake my way through. Six characters are at a dinner table in the last year of a graduate department's funding, and one of them — a lapsed economist with a taste for holding forth — is asked to tell a joke. It has to land for the two people at that table who have spent years on infinite expected value, and it has to remain a joke rather than become a lecture. So I need two things from you: the joke itself, and an annotation I can keep beside the manuscript while I revise the scene.

Here is the material the joke must turn on, so that we are not working from the well-worn version. The familiar argument runs: a stranger stops you, demands your wallet, and claims to be able to deliver an outcome of astronomically large value, or of astronomically large suffering, with some tiny probability; the expected value of complying swamps the value of your wallet, so a strict expected-utility maximizer seems obliged to hand it over — and then obliged to hand it over to the next stranger, and the next. I want the joke built on a complication of that argument, not on a recitation of it. Consider, for instance: a second claimant arrives before the first has been paid, and both offers are infinite, or both are so vast that no one can say which is greater. If the two are equal in expected value, the theory is indifferent between them and cannot direct a choice; if they are merely unmeasurable, the comparison may not be defined at all. Either way the maximizer is left unable to move — which means a sufficiently clever pair of muggers can extract nothing, or can extract everything, depending on how the tie is broken. A joke that finds the comedy in that paralysis, or in the bidding war that follows, or in a victim who tries to set the muggers against each other, is what I am after. You may invent your own complication if you find a better one, but it must be a real structural feature of the problem rather than a decorative one.

Constraints for the joke: it must be tellable aloud in under ninety seconds; it must not require the listener to have read anything; it must not be a joke whose entire content is the name of a concept; it must not turn on wordplay that dies when spoken; and its first line must not announce the topic. It should be funny to someone who already knows the argument, not merely puzzling to someone who does not, and it should be speakable by a character who is not a specialist. If the joke only works on the page, tell me so and give me the spoken version instead.

After the joke, give me a single JSON object and nothing after it, with these fields: "joke_text" (the joke exactly as the character would say it, punctuated for speech, with no stage directions); "setup" (the opening beat alone); "punchline" (the final sentence or exchange, verbatim); "target" (the specific step in the Pascal's-mugging reasoning the joke turns on, named in one line); "mechanism" (one or two sentences on why it is funny — the inference the audience must make, and the instant at which it either lands or misfires); "prior_knowledge_required" (an ordered list, most to least necessary, of what a listener must already understand); "twist_used" (which complication of the standard argument the joke depends on); "runtime_seconds" (an integer estimate for a natural telling); "delivery_notes" (where to pause, which word carries the punchline, what to do with the hands — brief); "failure_modes" (an array of two or three audiences or settings where the joke dies, each with a clause explaining why); and "viability" (one of "works", "works with a caveat", or "does not work", plus one sentence of justification). If the joke cannot be made to work, say so in "viability" rather than substituting a weaker one.
