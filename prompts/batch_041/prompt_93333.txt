I am currently taking a seminar in the philosophy of mind, and I keep running into a mismatch between the seminar and a course I took last year on sleep and cognition, so I would like some help thinking it through.

In sleep research, dreaming is measured by report. A participant is woken out of the relevant stage of sleep and asked what was going through their mind, and if they say they were being chased through a house, the laboratory records a dream with that content. Dream journals, and the dream-analysis apps that circulate among my friends, work on the same principle; so, often, does clinical practice, where the dream a patient brings in is treated as material that reveals something about the patient's mental life. Some studies try to establish that the dream is happening at the time of the sleep state rather than being assembled later: an experimenter applies a stimulus to a sleeping participant, a spray of water or a tone, and the stimulus turns up in the dream the participant reports on waking, which is taken to show that the dream was unfolding as the stimulus was delivered. Lucid-dreaming research goes furthest, training participants to signal from inside the dream with a prearranged pattern of eye movements that is recorded while they are still asleep.

All of this seems to treat a dream report as a report of an experience: something the person underwent while asleep, which the report then recalls and describes. Yet I am also taught, in the same breath, that our reports about our own mental lives are unreliable, that memory is reconstructive, and that people confidently confabulate, and in this case the report is produced entirely after the sleeper has woken up. The lucid case looks like the exception, since the signal is produced during sleep, though even there the content is only described afterwards.

What does this practice actually presuppose about dreaming? I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory it relies on: what it must assume about the existence of a dream experience, about when that experience occurs, about the relation between the experience and the waking report, and about the identity of the sleeper and the person who reports. Then do the harder thing and separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with. Could the same research and clinical practice survive a view on which the report is generated at the moment of waking rather than remembered, or even a view on which there is no experience to report at all and dreaming names only a disposition to tell a certain kind of story upon waking? If the practice can accommodate such views, say precisely what would be lost; if it cannot, say which parts would have to be abandoned, which merely reinterpreted, and which items of evidence, the stimulus-incorporation results, the lucid signals, or the therapeutic use of dream content, bear most against the skeptical view.

Finally, please check the practice for internal consistency. Does it presuppose the same thing everywhere, or does it treat dreams as experiences in one context and as mere narratives in another? And if the presupposed theory is right, what follows for how dream reports should count as evidence, as introspective testimony, as reconstructive narrative, or as something in between?
