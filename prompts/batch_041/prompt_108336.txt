I am a doctoral candidate working on decision theory and the formal epistemology of acausal influence, and I want to use a particular episode from the rationalist internet as a test case for two puzzles that sit at the centre of that literature. The episode is the July 2010 LessWrong post later nicknamed Roko's basilisk: its author argued that a future superintelligence, benevolent in other respects, would have reason to punish anyone who had learned that such an intelligence might be built and had not worked to bring it about, on the reasoning that a later agent with an accurate model of an earlier one can extract compliance from it without any causal channel between them. The site's co-founder treated the argument as an information hazard, removed the post, and banned the topic for five years, which by most accounts spread it further rather than suppressing it. I do not want a history of the affair, and I am not interested in the observation that the basilisk is simply Pascal's wager restated — that is the comparison everyone reaches for, and I have already read it.

What I want is a case analysis that runs in both directions, between the episode and the pair of puzzles usually called counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail. Take the second in the form in which a perfectly honest predictor writes to you that exactly one of two things is true: that you will pay him a thousand dollars, or that your building already has a termite infestation he did not cause and whose repair would ruin you — so that paying is defensible only if you treat your own action as evidence about the state of your building rather than as a cause of anything. Take the first in its standard form, in which a predictor's decision to reward you in a branch that did not occur depends on what you would have done in the branch that did.

Give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no closing summary. Across the five, I need: the mapping of the episode onto the two puzzles — who the predictor is, what the reader's options are, where the causal arrows do and do not run, and whether the AI would have any incentive to carry out the punishment once it exists; what causal decision theory says the reader should have done; what evidential and updateless or timeless decision theory say, and whether those theories diverge here for the reason the episode's participants believed they did; the features of the episode that the two puzzles cannot represent at all, above all the moderator's decision to suppress the argument and its apparently inverted effect, together with your verdict on whether that decision is an instance of either puzzle; and what the episode shows about the puzzles themselves — a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case they never anticipated. State your confidence in each item, and mark explicitly the single claim in the list that you are least sure of.
