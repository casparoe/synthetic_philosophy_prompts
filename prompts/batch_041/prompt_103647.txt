I am a secondary-school student, and for a philosophy project I have been trying to work out my own verdicts about when it is reasonable to believe a conspiracy theory. I am not asking for a reading list, citations, or names of scholars; I want the principle, if there is one, that best fits the verdicts below, and I want to know which verdict I should abandon if no such principle exists.

Here are my verdicts. A town council is suspected of quietly rezoning a park for a developer; the evidence is one anonymous email, a vote that happened faster than usual, and a councillor who owns land nearby. I think a resident who believes there is a deal, while remaining unsure, is being reasonable. A global theory that jam manufacturers have a standing agreement to shrink jars while holding prices steady, with no whistleblowers and no documents, seems unreasonable to believe even though the same pattern of shrinking jars is real. A parent believes the school is covering up a teacher's dismissal; the parent has a redacted letter and a reliable friend on staff. I think that belief is reasonable, though the parent should hold it loosely. A theory that a foreign intelligence service has spent thirty years subtly altering weather forecasts to make a population complacent, requiring thousands of meteorologists to keep silent, seems unreasonable even if a few forecasts were indeed wrong. A theory that a pharmaceutical company buried an unfavourable trial result because the company stands to lose money and one former employee says so: I think that is reasonable to believe tentatively. A theory that the same company buried the result because a shadowy international body ordered it to, with no further evidence, seems unreasonable. And a theory that a celebrity's death was staged, where every apparent proof is either ambiguous or explained away by the theory itself, seems unreasonable even to entertain as more than a story.

The pair I cannot reconcile is the jam theory and the rezoning theory: both infer a coordinated plan from a pattern plus a motive, but I want to call one reasonable and the other not. Another pair is the parent's school theory and the weather-forecast theory: both rest on a single insider-like source and a suspicious document, yet I treat them differently. I have tried one principle, which I call coincidence economy: a conspiracy belief is reasonable only if the conspiracy explains more stubborn coincidences than the official story, and requires fewer independent secrets than the official story requires lucky coincidences. On that principle the jam theory should be reasonable, since independent manufacturers all shrinking jars at once looks like a coincidence, while the rezoning theory should be unreasonable, since one fast vote can be a coincidence. But my verdicts are the reverse, so the principle is either wrong or missing a clause.

Is there a principle, perhaps a repaired version of mine, that captures all of these verdicts or nearly all? Please state it precisely, show which case motivates each clause, say what it predicts for a case I have not listed, for example a theory that a local shop is secretly a front for money laundering, supported by odd opening hours and one nervous employee, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which I should therefore abandon. Begin with a one-sentence statement of the principle, then give the case-by-case fit, then the further case, then the verdict to abandon. Please write in British English, and in no more than 650 words.
