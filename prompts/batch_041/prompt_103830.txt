I serve as the lay member of the ethics panel of a small humanitarian NGO, and I am asking for help with a dispute I could not referee.

Last year our field team's chief negotiator, Alia, was in talks with an armed group holding five of our staff. The group offered to release all five in exchange for Ines, one of our volunteers, who was not among the hostages. Ines asked, publicly and repeatedly, to be handed over; she had worked in the region for eleven years, understood what captivity there would mean, and appeared to be deciding for herself. Alia refused to make the exchange. Negotiations continued, and the five came home eleven months later; one of them lost the sight of an eye. Ines left the organization soon afterwards, telling colleagues she had been overruled about her own life.

Two members of the panel, Nadia and Emeka, have argued about it ever since, and I cannot decide between them.

Nadia's position is that you may not make one person the instrument of another's release, and that Ines's volunteering does not change what the act is. Consent, she says, can release the rest of us from any complaint Ines might later make; it cannot convert the use of a person into something the rest of us may do. If willingness were enough, the rule would protect only the unwilling, and anyone with a sufficiently urgent cause could recruit a volunteer and call the result permission.

Emeka's reply is that Ines is precisely the person whose claim the rule exists to protect, and she waived it. To refuse on her behalf is to treat her as someone to be protected from her own judgment — the very thing the rule was meant to prevent. He adds that Nadia has quietly turned the rule into a rule for producing the fewest uses of people: in this case we could have prevented five people from being used as bargaining chips by using one, and if the rule cannot say why we must not, it is because the only difference left is the number.

Nadia answers that consent waives a claim, not a permission; that a wrong can be done even when no one is wronged, because the rule is addressed to the agent rather than to the victim's ledger; and that the numbers do not enter not because they are small but because they answer a question the rule does not ask. On her account Alia did not weigh five against one; she declined to do a kind of thing.

Emeka's rejoinder is that this makes the rule a matter of the agent's record rather than of anyone's claim. If the constraint is addressed to Alia and not to Ines, its point is that Alia's hands stay clean — and we are then being told that a scruple of that kind outweighs eleven months of five people's captivity and one ruined eye. He adds that Nadia has weighed in any case, in concluding that Alia should refuse; she has merely arranged the scales so that one consideration can never be placed on them, and a weight that can never be placed on a scale is not justifying anything.

Nadia's last word: there is a difference between a consideration that does not count and a consideration one may not act on. The captivity of the five counts enormously — it is a tragedy and Alia should be haunted by it — but it is not a reason of the right kind for doing that to Ines. She compares it to an interrogator told that a confession would make five convictions stick: that is a reason to want the confession, not a reason he may act on. She adds that if Emeka's point is pushed, every rule whatsoever becomes a weighing, and the difference between rules and optimization disappears by stipulation.

Emeka's final move: then answer the consent point without that manoeuvre. If Ines's waiver releases her complaint but not our permission, the act would be forbidden even if she were the only person on earth. But the rationale usually offered for the rule — that people must not be treated as mere instruments — is a rationale about how the person is treated, and Ines is not complaining. What remains is: do not be the one who does it. He grants that this can be a genuine reason, but insists that a reason of that kind must be weighed against five captives; if it can be weighed, the original difficulty returns, and if it cannot, a self-regarding scruple is being treated as absolute. He sees no way out.

Please adjudicate this exchange rather than summarizing the general issue. Go move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and why, naming the specific insight or confusion in each. Then answer four questions. (1) Does Ines's consent alter what Alia was permitted to do, or only what Ines has grounds to complain about? (2) Can the rule against using a person be justified by what it protects, given that here it protected no one who wished to be protected — and if it cannot, does that show the rule is defective, or that we have misunderstood what it was protecting? (3) Is Emeka's "you weighed anyway" argument sound, or does it prove too much? (4) What is the strongest available formulation of the rule on which Alia's refusal comes out right — or, if no such formulation survives, say so plainly and say what follows for the case. Finally, say who has the better of the argument overall, and identify what both parties are missing.
