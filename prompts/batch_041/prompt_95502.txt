Last spring I was naturalised in a country whose head of state is a hereditary monarch, and the ceremony ended with an oath of allegiance sworn to the monarch by name — not to the constitution, not to the people, not to the state. I expected the moment to feel like a formality, and it did. But I have not been able to stop picking at it since, and what I have arrived at is a pattern I cannot find a name for.

The pattern is this. There are powers that everyone agrees are real: no bill becomes law without the monarch's assent; the head of government takes office by the monarch's appointment; on paper the monarch may refuse assent, dismiss a government, or dissolve a parliament. Everyone also agrees that these powers must never actually be used. Their value to the system lies precisely in their not being used: the monarch is trusted with them because the monarch is not competing for anything — no party to reward, no election to win, no constituency to please. And when one of them has been used, as I understand happened once when the crown's representative in a Commonwealth country dismissed an elected prime minister, the consequence was not that the power was vindicated but that it became toxic; the argument afterwards was never about whether the power existed, only about whether anyone should ever have touched it. So what I am circling is a resource that is real, is never spent, and would be destroyed by being spent — and which is useful to the system exactly because it is never spent.

I have tried to find out whether this has a name, and I keep landing on the same three answers: reserve powers; constitutional conventions; and the distinction between the ceremonial and the effective parts of a constitution. Each of these seems to me to name the furniture rather than the structure, and none quite covers what interests me — the logic of a power that is self-cancelling in use, held by someone whose authority to decide comes from being excluded from the contest. That logic seems to recur well outside constitutions, in umpires, auditors, trustees, returning officers, and the people an institution appoints to settle something precisely because they have no stake in the outcome.

What I want is therefore not a label. Tell me what the nearest established concepts are, how each differs from what I have described, which fits best, and where the edges fall. Concretely: is a presidential veto, which is meant to be used, the same kind of thing or a different one? What about a judge's contempt power, a central bank's emergency lending facility, or a referee's power to abandon a match? And is there a name — established, or failing that proposed — for the general structure in which the authority to decide is conferred by exclusion from what is decided?

Since what I am asking for is a conceptual carving rather than a survey, please be explicit about how confident you are in each claim you make, and single out the point you are least sure of.
