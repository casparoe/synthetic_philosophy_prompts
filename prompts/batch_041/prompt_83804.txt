I am a doctoral student in philosophy writing the introduction to a small departmental anthology that pairs verse with the concepts it handles, and I have claimed the Nietzsche section. I would like you to act as a poet who is also a careful reader of Nietzsche — someone fluent in both formal verse and the secondary literature on the eternal recurrence — and to begin by brainstorming before you draft anything: work out first what the poem's repeated lines will have to do, then draft. Do not quote Nietzsche anywhere; the poem must be entirely in your own language. Do not lean on the demon as a set piece we have all seen a hundred times; the interest here lies elsewhere.

The poem should be a pantoum, four or five quatrains. You know the mechanics: the second and fourth lines of each stanza come back as the first and third lines of the next, and the final stanza turns the whole thing back on itself, so that the poem's opening line is also its closing line and the third line of the first stanza reappears near the end. That closing loop is the reason for the choice, so make the form earn its keep rather than merely decorate. The words of the repeated lines must not change, but their sense must shift — through punctuation, through the line break, through the company a line keeps — which is exactly the pantoum's native trick and, here, also the philosophical point.

The voice is that of someone who has already said yes to the thought. She has accepted that her life, down to its pettiest humiliations, returns; she has even found that acceptance exhilarating. What she has just realised, and what the poem has to stage, is that a yes to the return of one's own life is not a private transaction. If the same world comes round, then everything contemporaneous with her comes round too: the cruelties she merely witnessed, the mediocrity she despises, the contemptible human type whose eternal return Nietzsche's own Zarathustra can scarcely stomach. So the poem should turn on the indiscriminateness of the return. The line that sounded like love of fate on its first appearance should sound like a confession of complicity when it comes back the third time. Keep the thought accurate: the same life in every detail, no improvement, no second chance, no moral progress through repetition, no karma and no metempsychosis. And do not resolve the poem. I do not want a final stanza in which the difficulty is redeemed. If she affirms, it must be an affirmation that swallows the contemptible; if she balks, her balking must itself arrive as a line that has come round again. Let the reader be unable to decide whether the repetition is her freedom or her sentence — but let anyone who knows the literature recognise the problem as a real one rather than an invented one, and let it remain genuinely ambiguous whether the voice addressing her is a hypothesis about the structure of time or something she is saying to herself in the dark. After the poem, add three or four sentences identifying which repetitions do which work, so that a reader can see what the form was doing.
