Ok here's a weird one. There's this idea I keep bumping into, I think people call it moral trade: two people with different value systems can both come out ahead the way countries do from trade. I care a lot about farm animal suffering and not much about abortion politics, you're the exact reverse, so I spend my political capital on your issue and you spend yours on mine, and we each end up further along our own ranking than if we'd both just pushed our own list. Nobody changes their mind, nobody's being a hypocrite, and there's supposedly a whole frontier of these deals.

What would Kant have done with that? My guess is he'd hate it, but the reason I find interesting isn't the obvious one ('you cant buy and sell duties'). It's the motive thing. If I do the animal work because of the deal rather than from duty, have I done a moral act at all? The trade might poison the very acts being traded. But he also has those imperfect duties, where you're required to adopt some end and get a lot of room about how and how much you pursue it — and that room looks exactly like the place a trade would live. So does the trading happen inside the latitude, or does the latitude turn out not to be tradeable either?

Give me the strongest Kantian line you can, then tell me where it breaks. And I dont just mean 'he'd say no' — I mean whether gains-from-trade is even the right model when the thing changing hands is an obligation rather than a good. I suspect the analogy is doing more work than it can support. If you think he'd have said something completely different from my guess, say that instead, but either way I want the part where the machinery jams.
