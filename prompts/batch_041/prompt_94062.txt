Jorge Luis Borges died in 1986. He left behind a body of work on time that is unusually explicit for a literary writer: an essay in which he tries to abolish time and concedes that he has failed; essays collected in Historia de la eternidad (1936) on eternity, on the doctrine of cycles, and on Nietzsche's eternal return; stories in which time forks and in which a man is dreamed into existence by another; and a long-standing habit of borrowing from Berkeley and Hume the arguments that dissolve the flowing present and the persisting self. Your task is to predict, as carefully as the evidence permits, what he would have made of three questions that became live only after his death. Where you are extrapolating rather than reporting a view he held, say so.

The three developments:

1. Time may not be fundamental. In several approaches to quantum gravity, the fundamental equations contain no time variable at all, and history — including the ordering of moments and the difference between earlier and later — is supposed to be reconstructed from static relations or from entanglement between subsystems. If that is correct, then becoming, succession, and the present are derivative features of a world that is, at bottom, tenseless.

2. Recurrence made physically respectable. Some cosmological models, given infinite time and finite entropy, imply that any configuration — including any human life, down to its smallest detail — recurs, and recurs infinitely often. This is a physical cousin of the eternal return Borges treated in the 1930s, and it bears directly on his argument that the number of possible experiences is finite and must therefore repeat.

3. Resurrection from records. Given that the past can be read off from present traces, it is now proposed that the dead be reconstructed: from archives, from stored neural data, from generative models trained on everything a person wrote or said. Suppose such a reconstruction passes every test the original would have passed, including his own. Is the man restored, duplicated, or merely described?

For each of the three, give: (a) what Borges's existing commitments entail, with the argument spelled out; (b) your best prediction of his verdict, including the possibility that he would accept the new science and still refuse the philosophical conclusion drawn from it; (c) the point at which he would most likely say the question has been misposed, and why; and (d) your confidence, and what evidence about him would most change your mind.

Close with a short assessment: which of these developments would genuinely have been new to him, and which would have struck him as old questions in new dress. Do not attribute any sentence to Borges that you cannot support, and do not manufacture quotations, titles, or citations; write in indirect speech and paraphrase. No bibliography or reading list is needed.
