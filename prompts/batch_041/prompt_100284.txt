I keep encountering a case that Chesterton's fence does not seem built for, and I want to know whether the phenomenon has a name. The fence maxim tells you not to remove an institution until you can say what it was for. But some institutions appear to be ones whose value is destroyed by the very act of asking what they are for. Consider a norm of promise-keeping that people observe because breaking a promise is simply not done: its worth lies in the assurance standing on its own, and that worth drains away once everyone observes promises because of the expected benefits of universal observance. Or a village custom that does its work only so long as nobody asks what the custom is for; the day someone convenes the villagers and asks them to justify it, it has stopped doing that work, and you cannot then remove it to discover what it was doing, because the asking has already changed the thing. For practices of this kind the conservative injunction is not merely difficult to satisfy but self-defeating: the knowledge you are told to obtain before acting is destroyed by obtaining it. Is there an established concept for this class of practice, or for the predicament of the reformer who confronts it? I do not want a bare label. I want to know how the phenomenon stands to Chesterton's fence, to the idea that certain goods are lost when they are deliberately aimed at, and to the suggestion that a rule may hold only on condition that people do not follow it because it holds — and where the edges of the thing lie. If nothing in the literature fits, coin a term and say what falls inside it and what does not.
