I'm teaching the function unit in our upper-level philosophy of biology seminar and I've somehow ended up writing the final. The essay I keep setting is the usual "compare selected-effect and causal-role accounts," and every year it gets the same canned answer -- heart, blood, done, see you next semester. I want a question that can't be answered by reciting the labels, so I've invented a case to hang it on, and I'd like you to write the question, a model answer, and the marking scheme for me.

Here's the case, feel free to dress it up. A frog I'll call the copper reed frog: males have a gland on the forearm. In the ancestral population the gland secreted an antimicrobial compound that kept the wound from the mating grip getting infected, and it was selected for exactly that. Fast forward: in the derived population the pathogen is long gone, but the secretion happens to make females more receptive, and now the gland is under sexual selection -- more output, more matings. There's also a lab colony that's been kept germ-free and where females are paired at random (no choice at all), and in that colony the gland's output makes no difference to anything and just sits there, fixed. And finally, some males in the derived population have a gland whose duct is blocked, so nothing comes out, though the gland tissue itself is present and looks normal otherwise. I want students to have to say what each family of accounts says the function is in each of these populations -- including what happens once you split the etiological family into the "selected for at any point in its history" version and the "selected for in the recent past under the current selective regime" version -- and then to actually take a side on which account comes out best and at what cost. They should also have to say something sensible about the blocked-duct males, because that's usualy where the causal-role account starts to squirm. Don't name any particular authors: the students haven't done the reading yet and I don't want it to turn into a name-dropping exercise, just describe the positions.

So: please write the exam question itself (90-minute essay, upper-level undergrad seminar, they've had two weeks on this), then a model answer of about 600 words, then a marking scheme. Build the scheme around five must-hit points, give each a mark value so the whole thing totals 20, and for each point add a one-line note on the mistake that typically costs students that point. The question should make a student who's only memorized "the heart pumps blood" visibly flounder.
