I am a novelist — fifteen years of fiction, a few of those spent learning the hard way that a reader who is warned is a reader who is immunized — and for the past two years I have served on the Safety and Expression Advisory Council of the company that builds a widely used general-purpose AI assistant. I need a written opinion to circulate before Thursday's meeting, and I want you to draft it as someone who is both a competent philosopher of technology and a seasoned hand at institutional policy. Take the philosophical questions seriously rather than treating them as branding; think the case through before you write, and do not pad the memo with citations or a reading list — I want reasoning, not a bibliography.

The case. The assistant operates a graduated "care" policy when a request touches a short list of themes (self-harm, disordered eating, coercive control, and a few others). The policy has three tiers. NOTICE: comply, and append a resource card. SOFTEN: comply, but adjust the output so that the concerning element is made legible on the page. DECLINE: refuse and explain. Users can also set a declared-fiction flag; at present that flag suppresses the resource card but does not change the tier. A novelist — not me, though I know her work — asked the assistant to draft a monologue for a manipulative character: a charming con artist drawing a lonely woman into a coercive group, written so that the reader finds him seductive and only understands, a chapter later, what was done to her. The assistant complied at the SOFTEN tier. It rewrote the monologue so the manipulation was visible in the prose — the flattery made obviously excessive, hesitations inserted, a tell placed early. The author has appealed. She argues that the softening does not protect anyone; it destroys the only reason the scene exists, and it does so silently, so that a writer who does not reread every line closely may never learn that her own draft was altered.

What I need from you:

First, a short scoping section that lays out the competing considerations before you commit to anything — the company's legitimate worries (the assistant is used by people who are not novelists; a declared-fiction flag is set by anyone in one click and cannot be verified; the assistant is not a neutral tool but a co-author whose defaults shape the artifact) against the author's (and my own instinctive) objection.

Second, the recommendation itself: grant the appeal, deny it, or grant it in part, and a concrete policy change with a time frame the council can actually adopt or reject on Thursday. If you would keep any form of softening, say exactly what it may and may not touch, and what the assistant must disclose about what it did.

Third, and I care most about this: reason explicitly about when it is legitimate to interfere with someone's choice for that person's own good, and about the difference between refusing a request outright and quietly reshaping what the requester gets. Is a covert edit worse than an honest refusal, or is that distinction a novelist's vanity? I suspect a silent alteration treats the user as something to be managed rather than someone to be answered, and that this is a graver fault than a plain no — but I want you to test that suspicion, not assume it. Then consider whether "it's fiction" should function as an exemption at all, and if so, what stops it from becoming a universal bypass.

Fourth, the honest treatment of the losing side: state the strongest case against your own recommendation, in its own terms, and say what would have to be true for it to win. Do not strawman the engineers; they are not philistines, and their worry about abuse is real.

Fifth, what the council should tell the engineering team to build, and the fallback if the declared-fiction flag turns out to be trivially abusable.

Address it to the council, in the register of a formal opinion — a clear bottom line up front, the reasoning after, the dissent treated as an argument and not a nuisance. End with a final line in exactly this form, choosing one disposition and one default tier from the sets given: \answer{DISPOSITION; DEFAULT-TIER}, where DISPOSITION is one of GRANT, DENY, GRANT-IN-PART and DEFAULT-TIER is one of NOTICE, SOFTEN, DECLINE, NONE.
