Next month I am presenting a paper to a five-person interdisciplinary panel on social regulation, and what I need from you is not a defence of the paper but a question-period preparation sheet.

My thesis, in outline. The question of how tightly a society should regulate conduct is normally posed as a question about content — which rules are justified — and I argue it is at least as much a question about quantity and enforcement. I distinguish two quantities. Compliance capital is the stock of willingness to comply with rules as such: the standing disposition to obey a norm one privately finds pointless, because obeying norms is simply what one does. Every additional demand on conduct draws on that stock. It is replenished by the experience of living among people who mostly comply, and it is spent fastest when rules are announced and then visibly go unenforced. Enforcement yield is the fraction of a rule's intended effect actually achieved per unit of monitoring cost. My central claim is that these two quantities trade off against one another, and that the trade-off is asymmetric: the marginal rule must clear a higher justificatory bar than the average rule, because it is paid for out of a stock that the earlier rules have already drawn down. I call this the declining marginal authority claim. Its consequence is that a culture can be too tight even by its own lights — too tight to enforce what it has already demanded. The second claim, which I call the substitution thesis, runs the other way: a society that demands few rules but enforces them reliably can sustain more regulation-in-effect than one that demands many and enforces them sporadically, so how regulated two societies are cannot be read off their statute books. I have cross-national evidence (rule counts against self-reported compliance) that is suggestive but not decisive, and I concede up front that compliance capital is measured only indirectly.

The audience matters more than usual here. Two of the five are cultural psychologists who have spent their careers measuring how much latitude different societies give in everyday conduct — dress, punctuality, courtship, forms of address — and they will hear compliance capital as a relabelling of their construct with a story attached; they will want to know what it predicts that their measure does not. One is a legal philosopher working on the moral significance of legitimate authority, who holds that the interesting question is never how many rules there are but who gets to make them and whether the subject has independent reason to comply. One is a behavioural economist who will want the trade-off written as a cost function, and who will press the opposite asymmetry: if compliance is habit-forming, compliance capital appreciates with use rather than depreciating, and the marginal bar should fall, not rise. One is a public-health official who watched voluntary guidance fail during an emergency and who will hear the paper as an argument against ever issuing guidance that cannot be enforced.

What I want is the six to eight objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they do to the paper as stated, each phrased as the relevant panelist would phrase it and in that panelist's vocabulary: the psychologists' in the language of constructs, factors, and discriminant validity; the legal philosopher's in the language of authority, legitimacy, and obligation; the economist's in the language of marginal cost, functional form, and identification; the public-health official's in the language of adherence and outcomes. For each objection give me a reply I can deliver in two minutes and one line on where that reply is soft, or where it concedes more than I intend. Mark which objections I should concede in the talk itself so that the question period starts from the right place, and identify the single reply the paper's survival depends on landing. Then give me the question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me honestly whether the position survives it. Include, if you judge it is coming, the objection that the whole framing is confused — that a stock of willingness to comply is a metaphor doing the work of an argument. Keep the sheet usable within a twenty-five-minute question period: I would rather have six objections with teeth than twelve I can bat away.
