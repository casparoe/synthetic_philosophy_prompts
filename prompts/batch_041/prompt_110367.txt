Ive been kicking around a view about Zeno that I call kinematic deflationism, and I want you to tell me what would count against it. The view has one core commitment — call it the no-extra-traversal principle — and then some applications. The principle: once you've specified the position function of a body over an interval, you've specified everything kinematic there is to specify; there is no further fact of 'getting there' or 'traversing' that the function leaves out. If you take the at-at picture seriously, motion just is being at different places at different times, with velocity as a derivative rather than a little internal push. Then the dichotomy, Achilles, and the arrow aren't really paradoxes about motion or the infinite. They're artifacts of asking a kinematic question — where is the runner at each instant? — while silently demanding an interval-based answer — what completed distance has been traversed? The sequence of halfway points is a convergent sequence with no last term before the limit; that's a feature of the order type, not a hidden task. The arrow is even easier: instantaneous rest isn't a state a moving body is in at an instant; 'at rest' is defined over an interval, so the arrow premise equivocates. The stadium is the one that might need seperate treatment, because it's about relative motion, and that's just velocity addition, not a contradiction. So the whole family is dissolved rather than solved. The cost is that you have to stop treating 'traversing' as an action that takes place at an instant or as a sum of infinitely many prior actions. This isn't the 'infinitesimals fix it' line and isn't the 'the calculus fixes it' line — it's that the calculus was never the issue; the issue is a category mistake about what kinematic descriptions quantify over.

What I want from you is a map of its exposure. What would have to be true for it to be false? Which of the paradoxes, if any, survives as a genuine problem for the at-at view? What does it predict or rule out that a rival — say, a process-first account or an infinitesimal-first account — doesn't? What would a defender of the view have to concede? Are there formal results — about measure, supertasks, nonstandard analysis, constructive reals, or the topology of time — that make the deflationary move harder than I'm making it sound? Is there a possible world or a physical theory where the at-at picture fails and Zeno's challenge comes back? And is my view in danger of being unfalsifiable or of just relabeling the puzzle?
