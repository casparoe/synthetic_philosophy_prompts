Friedman's essay, which leads off his 1953 collection *Essays in Positive Economics*, is the classic statement of the view that a theory is appraised by the accuracy of its predictions and not by the descriptive accuracy of its assumptions. His central claim is that the more significant the theory, the more unrealistic the assumptions. "Unrealistic" here doesn't mean false; it means abstract — the hypothesis strips away a mass of detail that's predictively irrelevant and keeps only the features that generate the prediction. So the claim is a descriptive generalization about successful theories, not a recipe.

He illustrates with the expert billiard player, who makes his shots as if he had solved the equations of mechanics, though he's done no such thing. The hypothesis that the player shoots as if he knew the laws of mechanics predicts the shots very well, and it would be a mistake to reject it because the player's head plainly contains no equations. Same structure with the leaves: the hypothesis that leaves position themselves to maximise the sunlight they receive predicts the observed distribution of foliage, and it isn't refuted by the fact that leaves don't do any calculating. What the "as if" formulation is doing is relocating the content of the theory from an inner mechanism to a predicted pattern of behaviour.

Samuelson dubbed the position the "F-twist," the F standing for the falsity of assumptions, which is the twist Friedman gives to the usual demand that a theory's assumptions be true. Samuelson's objection is that once you exempt assumptions from any realism requirement you can smuggle any falsehood into a theory, and the theorist is then free to cherry-pick the phenomena his theory happens to get right. Friedman's reply is that testing assumptions directly would rule out exactly the abstractions that give a theory its predictive power, and that the real comparison is always between rival theories and their predictions, not between a theory and the world it describes.

On (d): Friedman's view is not that assumptions are irrelevant. He says assumptions do work — they specify the class of phenomena a theory is meant to cover, and they make the theory simple enough to use. The point is only that their descriptive falsity is not itself a ground for rejecting the theory.

I think Friedman comes out of this well, but I'd push back on one thing. His claim that unrealism is a mark of significance slides into the stronger claim that unrealism is a mark of virtue, so that the less realistic the assumptions the better the theory. So I'd say the essay's central insight survives, but the rhetoric overshoots.
