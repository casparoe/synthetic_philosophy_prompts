I teach a third-year seminar that sits between philosophy of science and econ -- 16 students, one 80-minute session, and I want to do a session on Goodhart's law that doesn't just trot out the usual Soviet nail-factory and teaching-to-the-test stories, because they've all heard those and they nod along without feeling anything. I want them to run into the thing themselves.

Here's the specific thing I want them to feel, and it's more than "people cheat." I want them to see that a measure can stop tracking what you care about for at least four seperate reasons, and that only one of them is dishonesty. (a) The measure tracked the goal in the past only because nobody was selecting on the measure; the moment people start choosing actions to push the measure up, that old correlation is precisely the thing you've destroyed. (b) The measure is fine in the normal range, but you're now pushing it to extremes where it stops meaning anything. (c) Intervening on the measure changes the goal itself, so even a perfectly honest and totally ungameable measure would still move the target. (d) People game it, the measure-setter notices and changes the measure, the gamers adapt to the new one, and you're in an arms race with no fixed point.

Design me the activity. Rules, roles, materials, a scoring scheme that doesnt need real money, and a timeline for the 80 minutes. Two things I care about: the collapse has to show up in what happens, not in what I say afterwards, and it shouldn't be driven only by self-interested cheating -- I want at least one round where gaming is forbidden or impossible and the measure still degrades, because the selection effect is the part they always miss. There should be a ground truth that only I can see, so that at the end I can reveal the real quality scores next to the measure and let them watch the top of the proxy ranking come apart from the top of the true ranking. And I want a round with survival pressure, so that whoever is doing well by the measure gets to keep playing and the rest get thinned out, so the population drifts even if no individual is being sneaky.

Then a debrief that maps the moments in the game onto (a)-(d) above, one at a time, and says which round was which mechanism. Don't let the name Goodhart enter the room until the debrief. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to fix it on the fly, and be honest about which of the four the activity can't really reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself. Keep it runnable with pen and paper, no laptops.
