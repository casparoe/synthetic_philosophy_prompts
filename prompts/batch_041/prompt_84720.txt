For the past several months I have been keeping a hive of honeybees on my balcony — a gift that turned out to be a far larger commitment than I anticipated — and, perhaps because I have also been sitting in on a graduate seminar in metaphysics, I have found myself standing at the entrance and asking a question the seminar keeps circling: is the colony a single thing, or is it just a great many bees that happen to live together? The seminar calls this the special composition question, and the standard positions we have rehearsed all leave me cold. One holds that any plurality of things composes a further thing, so the answer is trivially yes and everything from a boulder to a random assortment of objects on my desk is an object; another holds that nothing ever composes anything, so strictly speaking there are no bees and no hive, only fundamental particles arranged in a certain way; a third permits composition only where there is a life, so the colony counts as one thing while my desk does not. I can appreciate the motivation behind each, but none of them seems to capture what I am actually looking at, which is a system that persists only by continuously drawing in and burning through energy, and that would collapse into a heap of dead insects and wax within days if the flow stopped.

What I would like is an essay that takes up this controversy and reaches a verdict. I am especially interested in a criterion a friend of mine — a physicist, not a philosopher — proposed when I described the problem to her. She said that some things compose a further thing exactly when they constitute an ongoing, self-maintaining process held far from equilibrium: a pattern that must keep importing energy in order to keep being what it is, and that dissolves the moment the flow ceases. She thinks this is why we are willing to call a bacterium, a hive, or even a candle flame a single thing, while a pile of sand or a boulder, however firmly stuck together, is merely stuff. The proposal appeals to me, but it seems to cut against our ordinary habits in both directions: it would make the flame on my stove a thing, and it might leave a wristwatch or a printed book — which strike me as paradigm objects — on the wrong side of the line. I cannot tell whether that is a fatal defect or an illuminating result. So please weigh the standard answers against her dynamic criterion, and against any other option you think earns its place, then tell me whether composition should be understood as a matter of self-maintenance and defend a definite conclusion. I would much rather you commit to something than catalogue the options, and I would be grateful if you would say plainly what you take the hive in front of me to actually be.
