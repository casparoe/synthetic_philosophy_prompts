I'm a third-year PhD student putting together a handout for a seminar I'm TAing on decision theory, and I've gotten completely sick of the standard tiny-chance-of-something-enormous case — every discussion of fanaticism turns into an argument about whether 1-in-10^100 is even a real probability and nobody learns anything. So I want a case that does the same job with all the usual escape hatches nailed shut, and I need the case to satisfy every clause of this spec: (i) all utilities finite, with the agent's utility function stated explicitly and made bounded (there's a max and a min), continuous, and strictly increasing in some ordinary good — no thresholds, no lexical jumps, no jumps at all; (ii) every probability in the case is at least 1 in 20, so nobody gets to say "too small to matter"; (iii) it's a one-shot decision — exactly one shot, no series of independent offers, no resampling, no "it'll wash out in the long run"; (iv) no gods, no demons, no infinities, no multiverse, no immortality, and no astronomically large populations of beneficiaries, because I want the only people affected to be the people in the story and I want the reader to be able to imagine being the one who eats the cost; and (v) the stakes have to be ones a real person could face — a job, a house, a body, a relationship — not a trolley and not a space colony.

Two things I actually want out of this. First, a scenario meeting (i)-(v) in which the EV-maximizing choice is one that makes basically everyone's skin crawl, followed by the full walk-through: what the EV maximizer says and why, what a risk-averse (rank-dependent or risk-weighted) agent says and why, and what someone deploying my friend's proposed "ruin clause" says — that's her rule that no option which can leave you with literally nothing is ever permissible, no matter the expected value — and I want to know whether that clause is doing any work the utility function couldn't do on its own. Same for the standard move of "just bounding the utility function": my suspicion is that this is either confused or a sneaky change to the agent's ordinal preferences rather than a real fix, since utility is only pinned down up to positive affine transformation and EV verdicts ride along with that, and I'd like you to either confirm that or show me where I'm wrong.

Second, my hunch is that once (i)-(v) are all in place, the case I'm asking for either can't be built at all, or can be built but isn't really about fanaticism anymore — it's plain risk aversion wearing a costume, and the only way to get genuine fanaticism back is to let one of (i)-(v) go. So tell me which clause is load-bearing: build the case if it can be built, and if it can't, say which clause is doing the actual work and why dropping it brings the monsters back. Also say what happens if I take whatever case you build and quietly raise the agent's endowment so the bad outcome stops being "losing everything" — does it collapse into a boring good bet that everyone agrees on, and if so, is the whole thing really about the ratio of the stake to what the agent already has rather than about probability? Finish with a note on how a fanatic would attack your case (I assume: "that's just loss aversion, and the utility function is the agent's own, so refusing is irrational") and whether that attack lands. Last line please: \answer{...} with just a short label for the load-bearing feature — something like {probability, unbounded-utility, aggregation-over-people, stake-ratio-to-endowment, one-shot-ness, none-specs-unsatisfiable} — or your own short phrase if none of mine fit.
