I'm a novelist — third book, don't feel obliged to be encouraging — and I'm stuck on the one scene in it I actually care about. Two of my characters are fighting about a decision one of them already made, some version of leaving a safe life for an unsafe one, and the fight keeps circling the same spot: he says that if it was really the right choice then she shouldn't still be grieving the road not taken, and she says the grieving isn't evidence of anything, it's just what the choice cost. I want to close the chapter with a poem, and I want the poem to carry the philosophy properly instead of gesturing at it, because I've read too many novels where a character recites a famous problem and it lands like a footnote. What she's groping toward, I think, is incommensurability: two options each better than the other in some respect, no common currency to convert them into, so the choice was never a calculation, and the fact that you can't rank them isn't missing information — it's the actual shape of the situation. And there's a finer point I want in there too, the one Ruth Chang makes: that when two things are like this they aren't exactly equal either, because equal things are interchangeable at some rate and these aren't, so whatever relation holds between them is its own strange thing. Then the hard part, the part I keep losing when I try to write it myself: even choosing well leaves a remainder, and that leftover isn't a sign you chose wrong, and the poem shouldn't pretend otherwise.

Form-wise, here's what I want, and I care about this more than I care about it sounding pretty. Twenty-four to thirty-two lines, no fixed meter, but one structural rule: each stanza should reach for a comparison — this weighed against that, the way we always instinctively try — and each comparison should get cut off just before it lands, a scale that tips and then the poem refuses to read you the number. There should be a refrain that comes back three or four times and drifts a little each time, so that by the end it has quietly turned into the line that says there is no rate of exchange, rather than the line that keeps looking for one. Don't let it resolve into both matter in their own way or follow your heart — I'd rather it end in the place the argument actually ended, with the two of them still not convertible and neither one of them wrong about that. Just the poem, no notes on it after, I'll figure out where it goes in the chapter.
