A colleague pressed me with Fitch's paradox of knowability last week — the argument that if every truth is knowable, then every truth is in fact known — and I was embarrassed to find that, although I could rehearse the derivation, I could not actually explain to her where the pressure comes from or what is really at stake in the various replies. The topic has since become something of an obsession, and what I want is a careful conceptual explanation rather than a survey.

Please give me the anatomy of the argument. Walk through the derivation and, for each move, say what it presupposes: the factivity of knowledge, the way knowledge distributes over conjunction, the substitution of the particular conjunctive proposition that p and yet it is not known that p, and the modal machinery used to carry the conclusion through. I want to understand why the conclusion — that all truths are known — follows, and, more importantly, which single premise a defender of the knowability thesis ought to be most suspicious of, given that the derivation looks almost too simple to be wrong.

Then I would like you to take up two questions I have not been able to settle on my own. First, is this really a paradox about knowledge, or does it arise for any factive propositional attitude? If I replace knowable with it being possible that someone truly believes that, or understands that, or verifies that, does an analogous result go through — and if some of these survive while others do not, what does that difference reveal about what is doing the real work in the original argument? Second, the pivotal proposition is a conjunction of the form p and yet no one knows that p, which has the flavour of a Moorean absurdity. I want you to say whether the knowability paradox is, at bottom, Moore's paradox dressed in modal clothing, or whether there is a genuine additional ingredient. If you think the parallel is merely superficial, show me exactly where it breaks down.

I am not looking for a bibliography, and I do not need the names of the people who have defended each line; I would rather have the reasoning itself, argued in your own voice. Where you think a widely repeated reply to the paradox is weaker than it is usually made out to be, say so and defend the claim rather than hedging. End by telling me which response you would actually endorse, and what it costs to endorse it.
