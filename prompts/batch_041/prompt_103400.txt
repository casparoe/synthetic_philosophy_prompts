Here's what I keep running into and can't name. I work at a small local heritage trust, so I'm elbow-deep in this stuff. There's a festival / costume / dialect recitation / whatever, in a village or a region, that everyone presents as ancient — "our people have done this for centuries." Then you dig, and it turns out the whole thing was cooked up around the 1920s or 30s by a schoolteacher, or a revival society, or a nationalist club trying to give the place a pedigree. Fine — that part is just invented tradition, I get it, Hobsbawm territory, no mystery there.

But the twist I keep bumping into is this. The thing wasn't invented out of nothing — it was invented *as a revival*. The founders said they were bringing back something that had been lost, some older custom that had supposedly died out a generation or two before. And when you actually look, the thing they were "reviving" either never existed at all or existed in a completely different form — so the rupture they were healing, the loss they were mourning, is itself part of the fabrication. The revival *is* the invention. And now two or three generations later nobody remembers the founders; the villagers sincerely believe it's continuous, and the revival story has quietly become the tradition itself. There's no cynicism left in it at all — that's the part that fascinates me.

And whenever I point any of this out to the locals or the tourist board, I get the same move back: "sure, but every tradition was invented at some point — Christmas trees, handshakes, whatever — so why does it matter that ours is recent?" It definately flattens something real, but I can't quite say what it flattens. It feels like they're using "invented" in one sense when it suits them and another sense when I use it, but maybe that's not the whole story.
