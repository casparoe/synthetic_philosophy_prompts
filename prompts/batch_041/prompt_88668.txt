Somethings been bugging me about end-of-life care and the law around it. The standard line in bioethics is that withholding a treatment you never started and withdrawing one you've already started come to the same thing -- same outcome, same intention, so no moral difference. But in practice it's clearly not the same to the people doing it: nurses and families find withdrawing much harder than withholding, some clinicians will flatly refuse to withdraw but will happily withhold, and plenty of hospitals have policies that make the one easier to do than the other. Meanwhile the law in most places lets a doctor stop a ventilator but not give a lethal injection, even when the patient dies either way and everyone involved just wants the suffering to stop. So what is the practice actually presupposing about the difference between doing and allowing? Is it really committed to a moral difference between killing and letting die, or can a control-based or intention-based story account for everything the practice does?

And here's the part I can't get past: a ventilator isn't a single act, it's a standing intervention that somebody has to keep re-authorizing -- every day, someone chooses to leave it running. Does that turn each additional day of ventilation into a fresh act of doing, so that withdrawing is really the omission and continuing is the doing? If so, does the whole practice rest on a picture of causation and agency we'd have to give up, and what would end-of-life policy look like if we did?

I'd like the implicit theory reconstructed, the parts that are strictly presupposed separated from the parts that are just conventionally attached, and an honest note on where the practice is underdetermined -- I suspect several quite different views of doing and allowing would license the very same rules.
