I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on Spinoza's political philosophy, and I've been asked to give the short address at my university's naturalization ceremony this spring: about forty new citizens and their families, a presiding judge, and, in the second row, the professor who taught the seminar, who will notice at once if I soften anything. Please write the address, roughly four minutes, built around the moment the oath is taken.

Two Spinozan commitments should do structural work in it rather than decorate it. The first is that a commonwealth's right is coextensive with its power — what the state can do and what it may do are, for him, a single question — so naturalization cannot be described as the transfer of an authority that was waiting inside the new citizens to be handed over. The second is his separation of obedience from judgment: law reaches what a person does and cannot reach what a person thinks, which is why he treats the governing of belief as inept rather than merely unjust. I want the address to say something honest in that register to people who are having an unambiguously happy day, without hiding the consequence that the freedom being celebrated is one the ceremony has no power either to grant or to withhold. It should also not paper over the circularity in Spinoza's own position: he defends free thought while leaving it to the sovereign's power to fix the point at which speech turns seditious.

The audience has no philosophy in it. Make it land as a speech and not a lecture, name him at most once, and put nothing in it that he would have called false — no will united with a general will, no suggestion that the state has generously conferred something no state can confer.
