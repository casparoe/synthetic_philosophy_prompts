Ok, help me out with something. I've been circling a line for months and I want to know what it actually commits you to before I put it in a piece. The line is "The spirit is willing, but the flesh is weak." (I have it verbatim from a Bible translation, I just can't remember which one — doesn't matter for my purposes.)

Here's where I keep running into it. First, at a behavioral econ workshop last spring, during Q&A after a talk about commitment contracts — the kind where you post a bond, block the apps, hand your credit card to a friend, tie yourself to the mast, all that. Someone asked the obvious question, which is why people who deliberately buy these devices still blow through them. The presenter shrugged and said, basically, the spirit is willing but the flesh is weak, and moved on. Everyone nodded. It was doing real work in that room — it was the whole explanation, not a joke.

Second, on a podcast a few weeks later, a philosopher used the exact same sentence to argue the opposite conclusion — that there's no such thing as weakness of will, strictly speaking, because if you genuinely judged that A was better than B and you still did B, then your judgment must have been some lesser, conditional thing all along; a real verdict would have moved you. Same eight words, and in his mouth they meant something close to "there is no such thing as a weak will."

Third, and this is the one that needles me, a friend used it about himself after he blew off a promise he'd made to his kid, in a tone that made it sound like a confession but functioned like a pardon.

So what I want from you is a menu, not a lecture. Take that one sentence and spell out the distinct precise claims it could be making — what kind of "spirit," what kind of "flesh," whether it's positing two parts of a person or two forces or one person plus one appetite, whether "weak" means outvoted, or worn down, or unfree, or just misaligned with a preference that was never really yours. Sort them from the flatly trivial readings (the ones that can't be wrong about anything) up to the radical ones. Tell me which are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which are actually incoherent once you try to state them precisely.

Then tell me which reading was in play in each of my three encounters — the shrugging economist, the philosopher who denied weakness of will, and my friend the pardon-seeker — and whether the reading that supports each of them is one you've already given a pass to.

A few specific things I'd like you to take seriously, because they're the reason I'm asking instead of just looking it up: (1) if the empirical story is that people discount the future at a rate that falls as the reward gets closer — so your ranking of two options actually flips as the earlier one approaches — does that describe a weak will, or does it describe a change of will, in which case the sentence is a misdescription and commitment devices are less like self-control and more like a treaty between successive selves? (2) Does the sentence require that you knew better the whole time, or only that you'd resolved, or only that you'd said you would? Those come apart, and I think they matter for the pardon use. (3) Is the sentence an explanation, a diagnosis, an excuse, or all three at once, and can it be any of them without also implying something about whether you could have done otherwise?

Lay it out as a list of readings with a verdict on each. Don't send me a reading list — I won't read it, and I don't need citations, I need to be able to say which claim I'm making when I use the line myself.
