I am assembling a small grant program for an interdisciplinary ethics institute: enough money for roughly five researchers for two years, to be spent on the philosophy of intergenerational discounting -- the cluster of questions around pure time preference, the social discount rate, and how heavily the far future should weigh in decisions made now. I need to decide which open questions to fund, and I would like your help in the form of a defended research agenda rather than a survey of the field.

Two things should shape the answer.

First, make the grounds for the ordering explicit. For each candidate question I want to know whether it is upstream of others -- how many other questions change their answer depending on it; how tractable it is for people whose tools are conceptual and formal rather than empirical; how neglected it is; and whether settling it would actually change what a government, a philanthropist, or a philosopher does. Where those criteria pull apart, tell me which you would let dominate and why.

Second, take seriously a framing that one of our trustees keeps pressing, because I think it usefully reorganizes the field and I have not seen it developed with care. Call it the symmetry thesis: a pure time preference -- valuing a benefit or harm less merely because it occurs later in time, holding fixed who receives it and how it is caused -- is structurally the same kind of thing as a pure spatial preference, valuing a harm less merely because the person it befalls is farther away. The claim is not that either preference is obviously irrational. It is that whatever defense one offers for the one must be available, with the same structure, for the other, and that most defenses offered for time preference quietly fail that test. I want the agenda built so the thesis is a live constraint: which open questions become central once the time case and the space case have to be defended together, which recede, and whether any of the standard defenses of a positive pure time preference survive the pairing. If you find places where the analogy breaks -- asymmetries between the two cases that are not mere hand-waving -- I want those flagged, since they may be the most valuable part of the exercise.

You may treat the terrain as including, at minimum, the distinction between a pure time-preference term and the growth or uncertainty components of a consumption discount rate; the bearing of population ethics, and of the fact that future people's identities may depend on what we do now, on claims about their welfare; and the aggregation problems that arise when the horizon is indefinite or infinite. Do not let that list constrain you, and tell me if any item on it is a red herring.

Concretely: give me a ranked list of four to six questions, a paragraph of defense for each, an account of the dependencies among them (which are upstream of which), and a closing note on what would move a question up or down the list over the next two or three years -- the argument or counterexample that, if it appeared, should make me redirect the money. I care more about the reasoning than about the list, and I would rather have a short list defended hard than a long one defended thinly. You need not cite any literature; argue from the ideas themselves.
