I have been going back and forth with a friend about the moral equality of combatants, and we have reached the stage where each of us can recite the other's arguments, which usually means the real disagreement lives somewhere else. My credence that the doctrine is true is around 0.35; hers is around 0.8. I want your number, but what I want more is the derivation.

To be concrete about the proposition: a combatant who fights for an unjust cause has, simply by virtue of being a combatant in a war, the same permission to kill enemy combatants as a combatant fighting for a just cause. Please give me a credence that this is true, then decompose it into the sub-questions you think it actually turns on. I would suggest at least the following, but split or add as you see fit: (a) whether liability to lethal attack tracks individual responsibility for an unjust threat or the social role a person occupies; (b) whether the unjust combatant is typically excused even where he is not justified, and whether excuse is enough to restore the symmetry that matters; (c) whether the doctrine is best defended as a convention or division of labor whose point is to limit war's horrors rather than as a claim about individual desert; (d) whether a practice-level justification of that kind can survive the fact that it appears to license killing by people who are not even excused; and (e) whether the doctrine is doing one job or three, since permission to kill, immunity from punishment, and equal evaluability of conduct may come apart, and the answer may differ for each. Assign each a rough probability with a line of justification, say how the terms combine into your final number, and identify the term your number is most sensitive to.

Then two specific things. First, take a case I keep circling back to: a conscript in an unjust war who fights only when his own position is overrun, never advances, and would desert if he could, versus a volunteer professional in the same war who signs up for offensive operations for pay. Does the best version of the doctrine treat these two alike, and should it? If your defense distinguishes them, say plainly whether that is still a defense of the moral equality of combatants or a quiet retreat from it. Second, given my 0.35 and my friend's 0.8, tell me which of the sub-questions we most likely disagree about, and which single argument or piece of evidence would move your own number the most.
