I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on decision theory, and I'm putting together a case study for a chapter that tries to pin down how much work the distinctive machinery of updateless decision theory (UDT) actually does, as opposed to how much of it merely redresses conclusions we could already reach with ordinary repeated-game reasoning. The episode I want to use is the live-and-let-live system that emerged in the trenches of the First World War, and I'd like you to analyze it through UDT and then tell me honestly what the theory carries and what it doesn't.

The facts, which you should treat as given (I'm not testing your knowledge of the war, and I don't need citations): once the Western Front froze, units facing each other across quiet sectors settled into tacit restraint. Snipers passed up obvious targets; artillery fired at fixed hours into unoccupied ground as a ritual signal of intent rather than an attack; both sides refrained from shelling during the other's mealtimes, and sometimes warned the other side before a real barrage. Retaliation was calibrated to the offense, and the system was strongest where the same units faced each other for long stretches and weakest in sectors with new or rotated units. The Christmas truce of 1914 is the famous overt instance. Commanders at division level and above knew about the pattern and worked against it with raids, patrols, unit rotation, and the monitoring of casualty statistics. The standard game-theoretic reading treats the whole thing as an iterated prisoner's dilemma sustained by something like tit-for-tat, though the payoff assumptions behind that reading have been disputed.

The version of UDT I'm working with goes roughly like this. Causal decision theory evaluates acts by their causal consequences and evidential decision theory by the evidence they provide about the world; UDT instead selects a whole policy -- a mapping from situations to acts -- maximizing expected utility, and treats the fact that this policy is the algorithm being run in every relevant instance of the situation as part of the world it is evaluating. Its distinctive consequence is that agents whose decisions are computed by the same policy should treat one another's acts as logically correlated rather than merely causally connected, which is the old superrationality idea, and that self-binding is rational when the binding is itself part of the policy. Two pieces of machinery come with that: a criterion for which situations count as instances of the same decision problem, and a well-defined logical counterfactual, i.e. a determinate answer to what the world would be like if the policy returned a different act.

What I want from you:

First, the mapping. Who are the players, what are the acts, what are the payoffs and the informational structure, and what exactly is the UDT agent supposed to be choosing here -- an act, a policy, a precommitment? Be specific about whether the trench case even gives you a well-defined space of policies.

Second, the question of redundancy. The two sides here were in direct causal contact: they could observe each other and respond. Does the updateless treatment add anything to what repeated play plus expectations already delivers? Say whether this episode gives any independent support for the logical-correlation machinery, or whether the machinery is idle and the case is being carried by ordinary reciprocity.

Third, where the case stresses the theory. Does the trench case make vivid the need for a criterion of problem identity and for logical counterfactuals, and does the verdict change depending on where you draw the boundary between instances of the problem?

Fourth, the command's policy of raids, rotation, and casualty monitoring as a device for destroying the equilibrium. Is that a commitment device in the UDT sense, and does it make the high command rather than the private soldier the interesting UDT agent? Relatedly, the restraint was held by units, not individuals: does UDT have anything to say about a policy maintained by a group with potential free riders, or is that outside its reach?

Finally, the two-way verdict. Which features of the episode does UDT explain, which does it misdescribe, and does the case tell for or against the theory? If its verdict here coincides with what reciprocity-based reasoning already gives, say so plainly rather than dressing the same conclusion in updateless vocabulary, and tell me where the theory is simply silent -- about how the norm arose and decayed, about what the soldiers' shared situation did, about the ethics of any of it.

I would much rather have a candid account of what the theory cannot carry than a forced fit. If the honest answer is that this episode is not a discriminating test of UDT at all, because causal reciprocity does all the work and any decision theory handles it, then say that, and tell me what kind of episode would actually discriminate.
