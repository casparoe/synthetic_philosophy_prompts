I am a compiler engineer who works on language standards, and most of my professional life is spent on conformance, well-formedness, undefined and implementation-defined behaviour, extensions, and the committee process by which a standard is revised. I would like you to restate the Hart–Fuller debate between legal positivism and natural law in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Treat the standard document together with the committee's practice of revising it as the analogue of Hart's rule of recognition; treat a program that conforms to the standard as the analogue of a valid legal norm; and treat Fuller's inner morality of law as the analogue of whatever constraints make a standard not merely authoritative but legitimate. You may find it useful to treat undefined behaviour as the analogue of open texture and implementation-defined behaviour as delegated discretion, but do not let the analogy do more work than it can. For each position, set out what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what cannot be expressed in the compiler vocabulary at all. Then give me the residue report. Does the internal point of view — the participant's sense that the rules are binding rather than merely predictive — survive as anything more than a programmer's attitude towards a standard? Is the normativity of legal obligation captured by a conformance requirement, or is it lost? What becomes of the grudge informant, the open texture of language, and the distinction between law as it is and law as it ought to be? Finally, tell me whether the residue points to a defect in the compiler vocabulary or to something in the legal debate that is less clear than it looks. Please answer in British English.
