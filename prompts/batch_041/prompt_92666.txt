I am a novelist midway through a book in which two characters — a retired judge and a young actuary — fall into a long argument over a thought experiment I suspect most readers will have met: a single person is to be tortured for fifty years, or some astronomically large number of people are each to receive one dust speck in the eye for an instant, and we must choose one. My actuary takes the position I want to defend and my judge the position I want to see answered, but I have read enough philosophy to know that I cannot simply have the actuary say "the torture, obviously" and be done with it; the whole interest of the scene lies in whether that verdict can be given a foundation that does not collapse the moment it is pressed. I would like you to write the argument for me — not the scene, the argument — as a self-contained piece my actuary could deliver in two or three pages and that would survive a hostile reader.

What I want argued is a specific thesis, and I am deliberately stipulating the machinery rather than borrowing it, so that you cannot simply recite the standard moves. Suppose we distinguish, among very small harms, those that are small in magnitude but still register as a determinate debit in a person's welfare from those that fall beneath what I will call the hedonic floor — so slight that the person's life is, in the relevant sense, neither better nor worse for their having occurred, the way a single extra grain of sand on a beach is neither felt nor unfelt. My thesis is this: harms beneath the floor do not aggregate at all, while harms above it aggregate in the ordinary additive way; and therefore the correct answer to the choice is the torture, not because specks are trivial (which is the lazy answer) but because the countless specks are not the sort of thing that can be summed in the first place. I want you to defend that floor, in particular against the charge that it is a merely arbitrary cutoff drawn where it suits us — the objection that, since any harm can be slightly increased, no principled line can be drawn, and we have merely smuggled in a distaste for large numbers. I would also like the argument to say what becomes of harms that are each above the floor but individually modest — say a million people each suffering a broken arm — so that the view does not quietly become a licence for ignoring large numbers of real harms; and to explain how it differs from the simpler claim, which my judge will surely offer, that we should refuse to aggregate across persons at all. If the floor view cannot be made to work, say so plainly and tell me what a defensible variant would look like; I would rather have a sound argument than the one I sketched.
