I'm a novelist — my last book was about a family shipping firm, so I've spent years in ledgers and port records — and I'm now trying to write a scandal novel, except that every time I get near the actual history the moral catagories start slipping. I've been reading alot about Lockheed in the mid-1970s: a company that had just been kept alive by an American government loan guarantee, paying something like $22 million in 'commissions' around the world; a Japanese prime minister who ended up in court over it; a Dutch prince stripped of his military functions; an Italian president who resigned; and then the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act of 1977, which made it a crime for American firms to pay foreign officials, and which — I'm told — carves out an exception for 'facilitating' payments to get routine bureaucratic business done, which is more or less the exact line I'm trying to draw in the novel. What keeps snagging me is that nobody in the story thought they were doing anything wrong at the time. The payments were commissions, the hospitality was custom, and the men on the receiving end mostly seem to have thought they were taking what the position was owed.

So what I want is the episode run through the concepts people actually use for this, not the standard greedy-company/corrupt-official retelling. The principal-agent picture of corruption, where the official is an agent who betrays a principal — and I want you to say who the principals are supposed to be here, because it isn't obvious: the shareholders, the Dutch state, the Japanese electorate, the American taxpayer who stood behind Lockheed's loans? — versus the collective-action picture, where everyone's behaviour is a rational response to everyone else's and there's no single villain to hang a story on, versus the anthropological reading in which gift-exchange and reciprocity amount to a competing moral economy rather than a deviation from an impartial one, versus the clientelism literature, which treats the exchange of favours for political support as a form of distributive politics rather than a crime at all. Which of those fits and which distorts? Where does the episode refuse to cooperate with whatever theory it's being read through, and what does it do in turn to the claim that 'corruption' names a universal category rather than a Western export — does it matter, for instance, that the law only ever criminalized the payer, which might tell us more about who could be hauled into an American courtroom than about who was guilty?

Don't hand me a menu on any of this; take a side and tell me what you'd believe, since I have to make my characters believe something and I'd rather they believe something defensible. Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no introduction and no closing summary — I'll be copying lines straight into my notebook.
