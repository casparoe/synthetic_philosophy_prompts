The occasion is a political theory workshop next month, where I am presenting a paper on fortune and virtù in Machiavelli, and the question period will run about thirty minutes. I would like a preparation sheet for that question period rather than further comments on the paper itself.

My thesis is that the usual contrast — fortune as the residual contingency of the world, virtù as the human capacity to resist it — misdescribes both terms. In The Prince and the Discourses on Livy, I argue, fortuna functions chiefly as a name for the portion of a prince's situation that is constituted by other people's unconstrained wills, and virtù names the art of converting dependence into asymmetry: making others need you, owe you, or fear you more than you need them. What I lean on is the company the terms keep. The things that destroy princes in the examples are not weather but defection — allies who change sides, captains with interests of their own, the withdrawal of popular favour, the arrival of a foreign army — and the flood that fortune resembles is fed by agents rather than by rain. The practical injunctions follow the same line: on mercenaries and auxiliaries, on fortresses, on being feared rather than loved, on keeping the people indebted to you and the great men unable to do without you. If the reading holds, fortune's share of our actions is not a claim about luck but about the share of one's fate that runs through other wills, and virtù is less a trait of character than a relational position — which would explain why it can be lost without any change in the man, and why the same man can be virtuoso in one city and not in another. I am aware the reading is under pressure from more than one direction, and I would rather see that pressure on paper now than meet it for the first time in the room.

The audience is the difficulty. Roughly half are historians of political thought in the Cambridge mould, who work on the vernacular humanist vocabulary; they will treat my talk of dependence as a modern concept smuggled into a text whose relevant terms are capacity, prowess, and worth, and they will ask what is left of fortuna once I have stripped away its providential and personified senses — they will point out that in these texts fortune is a goddess, a woman, a river, and a storm, and never a crowd of wills. Two Straussians will be present and will say that I have domesticated a deliberately impious teaching: by turning virtù into a bargaining position I have made Machiavelli respectable and have left out the founding, the religion, and the terror. Two neo-Roman republicans will say that my concept of dependence runs against theirs — they read Machiavelli as a theorist of freedom as non-domination, whereas I have made virtù a technique of dominating others, so that either I am celebrating servitude or my dependence is power with a philosophical accent. One participant works on the popular and plebeian strand of the Discourses and will press me on whether a relational account of virtù can be extended to the virtù of peoples and of Rome collectively, or whether my reading is prince-shaped by construction. There will also be two or three graduate students who will put the deflationary question in its bluntest form.

Please write the prep sheet: the eight to ten objections I will actually get from this room, not the ones I would get from an audience of analytic metaphysicians, ordered by the product of how likely they are and how much damage they would do, with a note on which two will arrive first. Phrase each objection the way the relevant bloc would phrase it, in their vocabulary rather than mine, and give me for each a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, together with a note on where that reply is soft or where I am conceding more than I can afford. Tell me what to concede in my opening remarks so that the three most damaging lines of attack are pre-empted rather than merely survived, and which single reply has to land for the paper to be taken seriously, and what follows if it does not. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.

Do not include a bibliography or suggested reading; I neither need citations nor want them invented, and you should assume you cannot look anything up. Refer to Machiavelli's works by title and to passages by their content rather than by chapter or page number, and where you would otherwise quote him, paraphrase.
