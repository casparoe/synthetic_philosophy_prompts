I'm in a political theory seminar and we've spent three weeks on the problem of dirty hands, and I honestly can't tell anymore wether anyone in the room disagrees with anyone else or whether we're all just talking past each other. Here's the shape of it. Camp one: the politician who refuses to do the ugly thing — order the strike, let the informant hang, sign off on the deal that throws the union under the bus — isn't morally clean, he's a coward who's offloaded the dirty work onto someone with less power to refuse it, and the whole point of holding office is that you absorb that guilt so the rest of us don't have to. Camp two: that's confused, because if the act is justified all things considered then it isn't wrong, and the "dirty hands" talk only reminds us that the act was prima facie wrong or regrettable, which nobody ever denied — calling it wrong and required at the same time is a contradiction dressed up as profundity. Camp three: no, it really is both; the act is wrong, you should do it anyway, and the guilt afterward isn't a malfunction but the correct response to having done something that leaves a remainder, something still owed to the person you wronged even though you were right to wrong them. And then there's my friend Priya, who says all three of us are indulging a fantasy of individual agency: in any modern state the decision is made by forty people across six agencies, nobody's hands are ever literally dirty, and the whole drama is a literary hangover from the age of princes.

My own hypothesis is that we're not four positions on one question but three or four different questions wearing a single name — a metaphysical one (are there genuine moral dilemmas, or does every conflict resolve into a verdict with some regret stapled to it?), a semantic one (what does "wrong" even mean if it can be true of an act you ought to do?), a psychological one (what should the agent feel, and does feeling it make her better or worse at the job?), and a political one (is the phenomenon about the office, the individual, or the institution?). A competing hypothesis I can't rule out is that the real fault line is between people who think a moral theory's job is to hand down a verdict and people who think its job is to map the reasons that survive the verdict. I know there's a Walzer essay and some Weber and a Williams thing in the background, but I don't want a reading list — I want the structure of the disagreement.

So: diagnose it. Which of those questions is actually doing the work, is any of the apparent disagreement merely verbal (a fight over "wrong" that would evaporate if we all agreed to say "prima facie wrong but justified" or "all-things-considered wrong but excused"), and are the camps really answering different questions under one heading? Engage what each camp actually claims rather than a textbook version of it. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a concession, a change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected and say why. And if you think the whole thing is ill-posed, say so. No citations, no bibliography, just the argument, in American English.
