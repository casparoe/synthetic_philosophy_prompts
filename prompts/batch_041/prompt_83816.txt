I am writing a paper on how moral practices die, and a friend in my seminar has handed me an argument that I can no longer see my way around. I set it out below as he gave it to me.

(1) The case against slavery — that it treats persons as property, and so as things — was made, and made forcefully, centuries before slavery ended; the cases against dueling and against foot-binding were likewise old by the time those practices were given up.

(2) An argument that has been available for centuries while the practice it condemns survives is not what ends the practice.

(3) Each of these practices ended when it became shameful rather than when it was refuted: the duel came to look like the resort of a man afraid of being thought a coward rather than the proof of a man of honor, and bound feet came to look like a national humiliation rather than a mark of refinement.

(4) So what ended these practices was a change in what was honorable, not the force of an argument.

(5) If a change in what is honorable explains why a practice ended, then the cogency of the argument against the practice explains nothing about why it ended.

(6) If the cogency of our moral arguments explains nothing about what we do, then moral truth is inert.

(7) Moral realism is worth having only if moral truth is not inert; a realism whose truths make no difference anywhere is a thesis we could drop without noticing.

(8) Therefore we have no reason to be moral realists, and the fact that slavery ended tells us nothing about whether slavery was wrong.

He adds a principle that he thinks licenses both the step from (4) to (5) and the step from (5) to (6): a consideration counts as a reason for a change only if it could have caused the change, and it could have caused the change only if its arrival coincided with the change. He calls this the causal-reason principle and says it is the only honest way to say what a reason is.

I cannot accept (8), and I would give up almost any other step on the list to keep it from standing. Tell me which step does the damage, why it looked innocent, and what it costs to reject it. I am fairly confident the trouble is not in (1) — the historical claim is rough but defensible — but I cannot tell whether the weight falls on the causal claim in (2), on the exclusion being run in (4) and (5), on the notion of inertness in (6), or on the metaethics in (7), and I do not trust my own sense of where the weak joint is.

Then do the harder thing. Take the standard ways out — that the arguments available earlier were not the same arguments, that honor is itself responsive to argument, that the truth of a claim and its motivational power are different matters, and that realism need not be a theory of what moves people — and tell me what each one costs and whether any of them survives the strongest reading of (1) through (4). If the causal-reason principle is the culprit, say precisely what is wrong with it; if it is not, say what work it is doing that the rest of the argument cannot do without. I do not want a survey of who said what, and I do not want a reading list. I want a diagnosis, and I want to know what I am paying.

Write it as continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, and no numbered list of your own. Open with your verdict rather than a preamble.
