I run a fortnightly reading group that has slowly collapsed into a single argument: whether history locks outcomes in or whether the same outcomes would have arrived anyway. The case we keep returning to is the typewriter keyboard — a standard that, on the usual telling, beat out a rival at least as good as itself and then stayed put long after anyone could claim it was optimal. Someone immediately objects that the abandoned layout was never shown to be better, that the celebrated comparisons were methodologically worthless, and that the market's refusal to switch is itself evidence that no switch was worth making. From there nothing moves. The lock-in side holds that once a standard gets a head start under increasing returns — learning by doing, network externalities, complementary investments — the market cannot be trusted to select the better option, so the absence of switching tells us nothing about whether switching would have paid. The convergence side holds that until you can show the discarded option really was better, the thesis evaporates into the truism that history matters, which nobody has ever denied. A year of this and I am fairly convinced the two sides are not disagreeing about the same proposition, or not in the same sense of it.

My hypothesis, which I want tested rather than humoured, is that three separable claims are being run together: contingency (a different initial accident would have produced a different outcome), persistence (once selected, an outcome survives even when it is inferior), and inefficiency (the survivor is worse than what the counterfactual would have delivered). Contingency is close to trivial and the convergence side would happily grant it; inefficiency is an empirical claim that looks answerable in principle and almost never is in practice, because the losing option was never built at scale; persistence is the genuinely interesting one, and I suspect it does all the real work while the other two provide rhetorical cover. There is a further wrinkle I cannot straighten out on my own. The convergence side often seems to be asking a type-level question — would some layout with the same functional properties have won out eventually? — while the lock-in side is asking a token-level one — was it this standard, and could it have been another? If that is right, part of the dispute is verbal. But what worries me more is a symmetry: each side accuses the other of unfalsifiability. The path-dependence claim is said to be unfalsifiable because any outcome whatsoever can be narrated afterwards as locked in; the efficient-outcome claim is said to be unfalsifiable because any surviving standard can be declared efficient afterwards. And one member keeps insisting that the whole vocabulary was borrowed from institutional history, where it means that a critical juncture forecloses alternatives rather than that the survivor is inferior, and that importing it into a story about keyboards is what produced the mess in the first place.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict. What kind of disagreement is this, actually — substantive and empirical, conceptual and verbal, a clash over what would count as evidence for a counterfactual that cannot be observed, or two different questions wearing one name? Where precisely does the impasse live, which parts of it are genuine and which are artefacts of how the question has been posed, and is the trouble that the question is ill-posed or merely that it is hard? Engage with what each side actually says rather than surveying the field, tell me plainly which of my hypotheses is wrong, and then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a discriminating case, a changed question — or argue that none should be expected and explain why.
