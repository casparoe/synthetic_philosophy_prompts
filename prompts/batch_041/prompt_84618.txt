For two years I have been writing a novel whose two principal characters — a working mathematician of the old unapologetic platonist sort, and a philosopher of mathematics who is a nominalist of the fictionalist kind — have been paid by a foundation to produce what the foundation calls an adversarial collaboration. I have reached the chapter in which they hand over the document, and I find I cannot fake it. I need the document itself, not a summary of it and not a transcript of their arguing. It is to open with a numbered list of claims they both sign — about mathematical practice, about what they jointly accept as proof, about what their disagreement is not — each drafted so that neither is signing something he privately reads as a concession. There follows a table of their residual disagreements: in the first column the point at issue, stated in neutral words; in the next two the same point as each would put it for himself, each formulation written so that its own author would accept it as fair; then the crux or cruxes beneath it — the sub-question such that a settled answer would move at least one of them; and finally, for each crux, the case, argument, or constructed example that both agree would bear on it, with each side's verdict registered in advance and in the conditional (if the case goes this way, I concede such-and-such; if that way, I concede the other). Neither may cite a book or a paper; the foundation forbade it and they have agreed, so the document must stand on argument and example alone. Where they cannot agree even on what would count as a test, they are to say so plainly rather than invent one to fill the column.

I have given them three instruments of my own devising, because I want the collaboration to bite on something neither of them has read in a textbook, and I want you to use them. The first is the ledger test: take any sentence of a physical theory and rewrite it so that no numeral and no apparent quantification over numbers survives, replacing the numerical apparatus with plural or relational vocabulary of their choosing. They must state jointly what a faithful such rewriting is required to preserve — truth-value, explanatory power, modal profile, predictive equivalence, or something narrower — and then say whether the ledger test, run on actual physics, is one the nominalist can pass and the platonist must concede, one no rewriting can pass, or one both can pass and which therefore decides nothing. The second is the orphan theorem: a purely combinatorial statement whose only known proof runs through a detour in a stronger theory, so that the detour does work no paraphrase can do. I want their rival accounts of what, if anything, is known in such a case, and whether the detour is an embarrassment for the nominalist or a windfall for the platonist. The third is the successor-physics counterfactual: stipulate that physicists one day write their theories in a formulation empirically equivalent to the present one that contains no numerical ontology at all. I want each of them to say, before the fact, what that would and would not show, and whether the platonist has any counterfactual outcome available to him that would refute the nominalist's reading of it.

They have also agreed, at my insistence, to a section I care about more than they do: the verbal one. I suspect — and I would like them to test the suspicion rather than flatter it — that much of their quarrel evaporates once one separates a lightweight reading of existence claims about numbers, on which such a claim is a bookkeeping remark about how we talk, from a heavyweight reading on which it is a claim about what the world contains, and that the fictionalist can grant the lightweight reading entire while the platonist needs the heavyweight one and cannot have it for free. I want them to say how much of the dispute remains standing once that distinction is drawn, which of their formulations were only ever about words, and whether anything of substance — the applicability of mathematics, the unity of mathematical practice, the epistemology of the a priori — survives the deflation. If they conclude that the residual disagreement is real but that neither can presently offer the other a test, that is a finding, and I would rather have it honestly recorded than papered over.
