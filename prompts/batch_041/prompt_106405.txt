I am trying to decide whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible, and my verdicts on particular cases are not obviously consistent. By a genuine dilemma I mean a situation in which, through no prior fault of the agent, every available option is morally wrong, so that the agent does wrong whichever way they choose.

My verdicts:

A parent can save only one of two drowning children. Not a genuine dilemma: the parent is required to choose, and is not blameworthy toward the child who drowns.

A doctor can save five patients only by killing one healthy visitor. Not a genuine dilemma: killing is wrong, but failing to save the five is a tragedy rather than a wrong of the same kind.

A soldier promised to return to his dying mother and also promised to protect a village; he can do only one. Genuine dilemma: whichever he does, he breaks a binding promise and owes reparation to the one he failed.

A judge can convict an innocent person to prevent a riot that will kill many. Not a genuine dilemma: convicting is wrong, and the riot is not the judge's wrong.

A spy under torture can either betray his comrades or let his child be killed. Genuine dilemma: both options are wrong, though one may be less wrong.

Someone knowingly makes two incompatible promises. Not a genuine dilemma: the wrongness traces to the earlier fault, not to the unavoidable choice.

A friend proposed the residue test: a case is a genuine dilemma only when, whichever option the agent takes, they owe something—apology, compensation, or at least a regret that no later action can fully discharge. I am inclined to accept this for the soldier and the spy, but I am unsure whether it distinguishes them from the parent, who will certainly feel regret, and from the judge, who may owe an apology to the person he wrongly convicts.

What principle, or small set of principles, captures these verdicts? State it precisely, identify which case motivates each clause, say what it predicts for a case I have not listed—whether it is permissible to break a trivial promise to meet a friend for coffee in order to save a stranger—and tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate. In doing so, please say whether the residue must be a moral remainder that survives the choice or can be merely psychological, and whether it entails blame or only liability to compensate.
