A colleague in an alignment lab explains inverse reinforcement learning with a video-game analogy: watching an agent play and inferring the score it is maximizing is like watching a person's behavior and inferring their values. The moves are the policy, the score is the reward function, and the human's values are what the method is supposed to recover. She uses this to argue that if an AI observes enough human choices, it can read off our preferences and thereby align itself with them. Audit the analogy. Give the correspondence table—what plays the role of the player, the game, the moves, the score, the rules, and the observer—and then say where the mapping breaks. In particular, address the fact that inverse reinforcement learning is underdetermined, that behavior can diverge from preference, that the standard optimality assumptions are questionable for humans, and that the environment is not a fixed game with a scalar score. Which features of the video-game source does the analogy quietly import into the target, and does the conclusion she draws survive once those disanalogies are on the table? If the analogy cannot bear that weight, say so plainly; if my framing of the technical issues is confused, correct it rather than accommodating it. If there is a better analogy for what inverse reinforcement learning actually delivers, offer it.
