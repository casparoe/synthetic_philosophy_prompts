My closest friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about Dr. Strangelove and got nowhere, so I would like a reading of the film that is more careful than the table-thumping we managed. My friend's position is that the film is a satire of deterrence theory, with the doomsday machine as its central object; mine is that the film is at bottom a study of escalation, and that the cleanest way to see this is to read it as a dramatization of the dollar auction. Since that game is not common currency, here is the setup as I understand it: an auctioneer sells a dollar bill, the highest bidder pays what he bid and receives the dollar, and the second-highest bidder also pays but gets nothing. Starting at a nickel, both bidders can see a profit until roughly the halfway point; past it, the sums already committed mean that only winning can recover anything, so each raises the bid again, and the auction routinely runs well past a dollar with both bidders worse off than if neither had entered. The remedy the literature proposes is a precommitment device: a binding ceiling, an escrow, or an auctioneer who simply refuses another bid.

Read the film through the auction, and the auction back through the film. Who are the bidders, and what exactly is the dollar? Does the second-highest bidder's forfeiture map onto anything real, such as the way both superpowers carry the cost of the arms race whether or not either prevails? What is the doomsday machine, given that it is the precommitment device the theory prescribes — the bid that cannot be raised, made automatic and irreversible — and what does the film show about that remedy? Note that the film has the machine's designer concede it only deters if everyone knows about it, and that the announcement was scheduled for the following week; is the machine then a failed commitment device, or a commitment device that was never switched on? Then press on the assumptions. The auction presumes two bidders with a fixed prize, common knowledge of rationality, and a closed game. General Ripper is a player whose preferences are not the game's and who acts outside the two-player structure; the CRM-114 discriminator is a commitment device that blocks the recall and costs two days to defeat by brute force; General Turgidson, once the attack is under way, proposes to let it proceed and send reinforcements. Does the film violate the assumptions one at a time, and does any single violation do all the explanatory work?

I also want the psychological account held at arm's length. Escalation-of-commitment research explains persistence through sunk costs and self-justification; does the payoff structure of the auction suffice for the film, or does the film need the psychology as well, and does it show the two coming apart — players who are not personally invested and are locked in anyway?

Finally, the honest part. Tell me where the auction misdescribes the film, and where the film adds something the game-theoretic treatment cannot supply — in particular, whether the trap in the film is constituted less by the payoffs than by the players' conception of what backing down would mean for their credibility, which the game takes as given. And tell me whether my friend's deterrence reading is a rival to mine or merely a version of it. No dialogue quotations and no reading list; I know the film, and what I want is the argument.
