Bit of a weird ask, hope it makes sense. I spent 35 years in a psychophysics lab — mostly hearing, loudness scaling, the usual fights about whether a sone is really a ratio scale or whether we'd just baked the answer into the instructions we gave subjects — and since I retired I've been sitting in on a philosophy of economics reading group that a former colleague runs. It's been great fun, because half the arguments in welfare economics look to me like arguments we settled (or conspicuously failed to settle) in sensory measurement in the 50s and 60s, and nobody in the room seems to know that literature exists.

Anyway, in two weeks it's my turn to lead, and I want to defend a fairly blunt thesis: that the claim interpersonal utility comparisons are meaningless — the thing that comes down from Robbins and the ordinalist revolution — isn't a result about utility at all, it's a leftover of a philosophy of measurement that psychophysics gave up on decades ago. On the scale-type picture I grew up with (nominal / ordinal / interval / ratio, and which transformations each one survives), vNM utility is an interval scale, and that's exactly the situation you're in with temperature read off two different thermometers: once you fix a common unit, differences are meaningful and ratios aren't. We compared loudness and brightness across subjects for decades and nobody thought the whole enterprise was nonsense; we fought about the right function (log versus power) and about the scale type, which is a real fight, but not about whether there was anything there to compare. So my line is going to be: the interesting question isn't whether interpersonal comparisons are meaningful, it's which comparisons are licensed by which scale assumptions and who gets to fix the unit — and the honest answer to the second half is a convention, which is fine, we do it with the metre too. Sen has been sitting in the back of my head through all of this and I suspect he's the one who'll do me in.

So what I want from you is not a defence of my view — its a Q&A prep sheet. The room is about a dozen people: mostly economists, including a couple of social choice people who will know Sen's comparability machinery far better than I do, and one or two who think the whole 'measuring utility' business is a category error because utility just is preference representation; plus two philosophers of economics, one of whom I'm fairly sure is a realist about welfare and the other a deflationist who thinks the question is confused. Give me the objections I'll actually get from that room, in their vocabulary, ordered by how much damage they'd do to me rather than by how likely they are, with a two-minute reply to each and a note on where the reply is soft — I'd rather find out the weak spots now than in the room. Include the one I'm hoping nobody asks. And please keep the settled/contested line bright: tell me which parts of this are things people who actually work on it agree about, and which are live disputes where I'd be wrong to talk as though the room should defer to me — I'm a visitor here, and I don't want to be the guy who confidently restates a position that got quietly abandoned in 1975.
