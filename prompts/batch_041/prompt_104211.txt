In a philosophy of science reading group last week, a discussion of whether our best-confirmed theory deserves our belief was cut short when someone said: 'the evidence underdetermines the theory — for any body of evidence there is always a rival theory that fits it just as well, so the choice between them is not really a rational matter at all.' Nobody pushed back, and I have been uneasy ever since, because I cannot tell whether that remark is a truism dressed up as a scandal, or a genuine argument that ought to sink scientific realism on the spot.

What I want from you is not a general exposition of underdetermination, but a disambiguation of that one sentence. Lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, and order them from the trivial to the radical. At a minimum, I would like the readings along these axes to be separated:

- What is the claim about — logic, confirmation, the rationality of theory choice, or merely the psychology of scientists?
- What is the scope — any finite body of evidence or all possible evidence; any pair of rival theories or only some pairs?
- How strong is the rival — a curve fitted through the data points, a redescription of the same theory in new vocabulary, or a genuine competitor with its own ontology and its own predictions elsewhere?

For each reading, give me a verdict: is it a platitude that nobody would deny, a substantive claim that is defensible, or an overreach that is simply false? Say briefly why. Then tell me which reading the person in my reading group most likely intended, and — this is the part I care about most — which of the readings would actually license the conclusion they drew, namely that the choice between the theories is not a rational matter. Some of the weaker readings seem to me to leave that conclusion entirely unsupported, and I would like to know exactly where the gap opens.

Finally, address the question of how a tie is supposed to be broken: can considerations such as simplicity, explanatory unification, or predictive fertility legitimately favour one theory over an empirically equivalent rival, or does invoking them merely smuggle in extra evidence under another name? Say what turns on the answer for the realism debate, and whether the slogan is meant as a report on the present state of some science or as a permanent condition of all enquiry.

I want a menu to take back to the group, with verdicts attached, not a survey of the literature.
