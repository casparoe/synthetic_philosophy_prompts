I direct provenance research at a mid-sized encyclopedic museum. My desk vets roughly forty acquisitions and loans a year and answers a growing number of restitution claims from families and from governments, and each one turns on a single question: is it established that this object came to its present owner the way that owner says it did? The grounds we actually have are always some mixture of documents and statements, the object itself, what follows from the object's features, and resemblance to objects whose history is documented. I have been reading my way into the Nyāya school of Indian epistemology, and I think its machinery has exactly the right shape for this problem. I want it turned into a procedure my junior researchers can run.

Please write that procedure. Build it on the Nyāya account of warranted cognition: the four knowledge-sources the school recognises — perception, inference, analogy or comparison, and the testimony of a reliable speaker — treated as genuinely different grounds with different failure modes rather than as one scale of confidence; the conditions the tradition places on testimony, which turn on whether the speaker knows the matter and whether the speaker is moved to report it as it stands; the catalogue of faulty reasons, including the reason that holds equally of the cases where the claim fails, the reason that proves the opposite, the reason that merely restates the claim, the reason that is itself unproved, and the reason overturned by a stronger source of knowledge; and the school's insistence that a cognition is not presumed true, its warrant lying in the absence of defects in the conditions that produced it. I also want the five-step form the Nyāya logicians required of a sound inference — thesis, reason, the general rule together with an example, the application of the rule to this case, and the conclusion — used as the template in which any provenance assertion must be set out before we act on it, since the step our arguments habitually omit is the example.

Keep the procedure to a page. The verdicts must be few and honest: it must be able to end in doubt rather than forcing a finding, and to state what would settle the doubt, because doubt is a result and not a weak conclusion. Run it on three cases: an object with a clean-looking chain of documents that all descend from a single dealer's stock book; an object whose only evidence is a family's recollection from the 1940s; and an object whose material and construction point one way while its export paperwork points another. One of these must come out against the museum.

The researcher running this has no Sanskrit and no philosophy, half an afternoon, and nothing to consult; the acquisitions committee signs off on anything we take in. Mark the points where the procedure cannot decide and a person must, especially where the question stops being what we know and becomes what we owe. Tell me where this translation will misfire, and what Nyāya cannot do for us.
