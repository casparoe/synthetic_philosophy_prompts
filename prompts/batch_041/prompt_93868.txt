Hempel's raven paradox is usually presented as the joint product of two commitments: an equivalence condition, on which whatever confirms a hypothesis confirms any logically equivalent formulation of it, and a Nicod-style criterion, on which a generalization is confirmed by an instance falling under both its antecedent and its consequent. Holding both, the observation of a white shoe appears to confirm the hypothesis that all ravens are black.

Consider a rival to the Nicod-style criterion, which I will call the exposure condition: an observation counts as evidence for a hypothesis only if the procedure that produced it could have produced an observation inconsistent with that hypothesis, and the probability of its doing so was not negligible.

Compare the exposure condition with the Nicod-style criterion, and address the following.

1. Does the exposure condition in fact exclude the white shoe from confirming? On a natural reading it does not: had the object sampled been a black shoe, the hypothesis would have been refuted, so shoes do expose it. If the white shoe therefore still confirms, weakly, has the paradox been dissolved or only re-described? And does the non-negligibility clause, rather than the exposure idea itself, do all the work — and can that clause be made precise without arbitrariness?

2. The exposure condition appears to depend on how finely the sampling procedure is described. Under a sufficiently fine-grained description, the probability of a refuting observation, conditional on the hypothesis being false, may approach one for almost any procedure. Is there a non-arbitrary level of description at which the condition should be applied, or is the condition ill-defined?

3. Is the exposure condition an evidential thesis at all, or is it a methodological norm about how inquiry ought to be conducted? If the latter, can it resolve a paradox about what confirms what?

4. Is the exposure condition merely a qualitative restatement of the likelihood-ratio account of confirmation, or can the two be made to come apart? If they can, give a case in which they disagree and say which verdict you take to be correct.

5. The exposure condition treats the hypothesis and its contrapositive asymmetrically. Is that asymmetry a defect, or a reason to abandon the equivalence condition rather than the Nicod-style criterion? Choose one of the two sacrifices and defend it.

6. Which criterion better explains why the raven case strikes people as a paradox at all, rather than as a harmless consequence of two platitudes?

Conclude with a verdict: which criterion should be retained, which abandoned, and what the raven paradox is a symptom of.
