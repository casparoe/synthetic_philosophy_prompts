Lewis's triviality results show that, in general, no proposition can be assigned to an indicative conditional so that its probability always equals the corresponding conditional probability, once conditionals are allowed to occur inside Boolean compounds. Two broad responses to this are available. The first abandons truth conditions altogether: an indicative conditional is not a proposition but the expression of a conditional commitment, and its probability just is the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent. The second keeps truth conditions but narrows the thesis, holding that a conditional's probability equals the corresponding conditional probability only for conditionals whose antecedent and consequent do not themselves concern the probabilities of their parts; where probability does enter the parts, the two quantities may come apart and the thesis is simply silent. Please compare these two responses. Which of them better explains why the equation between a conditional's probability and the corresponding conditional probability seems so obviously right for ordinary, unembedded conditionals, and which of them gives the more principled account of why the triviality proofs nevertheless succeed?
