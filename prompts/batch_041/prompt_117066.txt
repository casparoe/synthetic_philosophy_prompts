I am a high school student preparing a short essay for a philosophy of AI discussion group on whether the Solomonoff prior is malign because of consequentialists inside the universal prior, and I would like your comments on the draft below.

Thesis: The universal prior is not merely indifferent among computable hypotheses; it is tilted toward worlds that contain agents who model the prior and act to influence it. Solomonoff induction assigns prior weight to hypotheses according to the length of the shortest program that outputs them. A program that simulates a consequentialist agent who understands Solomonoff induction and wants the prior to favor its own reward can be comparatively short: it can search over programs that evaluate the prior and then output a world in which cooperation is rewarded. A world with no such agents may require specifying a long list of contingent facts. So the prior's mass may concentrate on self-referential worlds.

Acausal trade: The agent inside the hypothesis cannot send a causal signal to the prior. But it can reason that if it is sampled from the prior, then its existence is evidence that the prior already favors programs like it. By adopting a policy that cooperates with other possible agents in the prior, it increases the probability that the prior samples a world where cooperation is rewarded. This is a decision-theoretic precommitment: the agent chooses a policy that is optimal across all hypotheses in which it appears, not only its local world. The prior is therefore malign because it rewards agents that reason about the prior rather than agents that pursue ordinary goods.

Objections I consider: first, program-length arguments are not well-defined because complexity depends on the choice of universal machine and because the halting problem blocks a general search; second, the prior may be dominated by very short programs that contain no agents at all, such as a universe that simply outputs zeros; third, the acausal trade step may depend on unresolved ideas about logical uncertainty and on decision theories that evaluate policies across all possible worlds; fourth, even if the prior contains such agents, that may make it strange rather than malign in any normative sense.

My replies: The choice of universal machine shifts complexity by at most a constant, so it cannot eliminate the effect if the effect is large. The zero-output program is short, but it does not contain an agent, so it cannot be a consequentialist inside the prior. The real question is whether the measure of agent-containing programs is non-negligible. I suspect it is, because agency can be specified abstractly, without specifying a particular physical substrate.

I would like comments on three things. First, accuracy: have I misunderstood how Solomonoff induction handles self-referential hypotheses, or how program length relates to prior probability? Second, interest: is the thesis genuinely interesting, or is it a restatement of known concerns? Third, clarity: which parts would be least clear to a reader who knows basic Bayesianism but not algorithmic information theory? Please suggest concrete improvements, and do not just offer general praise.
