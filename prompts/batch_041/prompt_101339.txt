I've been lurking on rationalist blogs since the Sequences era and I've slowly been argued into the anti-luminosity thing — the Williamson-style result that no interesting mental state is luminous, because knowledge needs a margin for error and your mental states can drift by degrees too small for you to notice. It's one of those arguments that feels like a magic trick: run a sorites on "I'm in pain" or "I believe P" and out drops "you can't know that you know". I basically accept it at this point. But I've been trying to work out what would actually count against it and I keep failing to generate anything a committed anti-luminosity person wouldn't just wave away.

So, play it straight: what would have to be true for the conclusion to be wrong? Give me the defeaters ranked, from "would actually sink it" down to "would be awkward for an afternoon". Stuff I'm especially curious about:

- Does it stand or fall with the margin-for-error / safety condition on knowledge? If you swap safety out for some other analysis of knowledge, does luminosity come back, or does something else just eat it instead?
- The whole thing seems to assume mental states vary continuously, in arbitrarily small steps. I've got a half-baked intuition that if the mind is fundamentally discrete — a digital computer, say, where a state either obtains or it doesn't — then the sorites never gets started and luminosity survives for those states. Is that a real escape hatch or am I fooling myself? And does it matter whether the relevant states are occurrent phenomenal ones vs standing attitudes?
- KK: I notice that if knowledge is luminous you basically get KK, and KK plus factivity seems to hand you knowing that you know that you know... forever. Is the infinite hierarchy a defeater for the pro-luminosity side, or just a weird consequence they'll happily swallow?
- Is there a version that survives for occurrent states but not standing ones (or vice versa), and if so which one is the real target of the argument?

Also be honest about the meta-question: has anti-luminosity been qualified into something no possible experience could disconfirm? From where I'm sitting it feels less like an empirical claim about minds and more like a theorem about what we're allowed to call "knowledge", and I can't tell whether thats a feature or a bug. And tell me what the insiders — the people who actually accept it, not the critics — would themselves concede counts against them. What I'd personally find most convincing is a concrete case: a state where someone plainly knows and plainly knows that they know, if such a thing exists.
