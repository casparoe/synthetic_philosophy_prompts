I have been wrestling with an argument about insects and would like you to write an essay that reaches a verdict on it rather than leaving the question open.

The argument goes like this. Take any single insect: whether it can suffer at all is deeply uncertain, so suppose the odds are only about one in a thousand. Now note how many insects there are — by the usual estimates, something on the order of a billion billion alive at any given moment. If the moral weight of a choice is the probability that a creature is sentient multiplied by the amount it suffers, then a tiny probability attached to an astronomical number can outweigh the interests of the humans and animals we already take seriously. On this reasoning, swatting a fly, spraying a field, or driving home at dusk could rank among the gravest acts available to us — not because any one insect matters, but because the count is so vast.

I find this both persuasive and suspicious, and I want to know which response is warranted. My own instinct is that the argument does something illegitimate somewhere in the step from "the number is huge" to "the expected weight is huge," but I cannot say exactly what. I am especially unsure how it behaves once we separate two different uncertainties: not knowing whether insects are sentient at all, and not knowing how much a sentient insect would matter. Please sort this out and commit to a position. Do not dodge by asserting that insects are certainly not sentient — grant the possibility and reason from there.

Structure the essay as follows:
- "The argument at its strongest" — roughly 150 words, stating the scale argument so that it is genuinely compelling.
- "Where it might fail" — roughly 350 words, laying out the most serious objections.
- "Verdict" — roughly 300 words, stating and defending your conclusion.
- "What would change the verdict" — roughly 100 words, naming the evidence or argument that would move you.
