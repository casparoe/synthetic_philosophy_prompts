For several years now I have been arguing about politics online, and I keep running into a pattern that I have never seen named to my satisfaction. I would like to know whether it is a recognized phenomenon, and, if it is not, whether it can be given a clean name and a definition.

The pattern is this. People accuse one another of arguing in bad faith, and the accusation does not seem to track anything the accused person actually does so much as whether the accused person's conclusion is one the accuser can tolerate. Suppose someone raises a methodological objection — the sample is small, the effect is fragile, the poll is oddly weighted. If the objection hurts the other side, it counts as honest scrutiny; if it hurts one's own side, it is a dishonest tactic, a way of manufacturing doubt. Suppose the same person concedes a point; the concession is read as a trick, a way of seeming fair while quietly loading the deck, and if they concede nothing they are called dogmatic. Suppose they lead with their conclusion; they are said to be working backward from a verdict, and if they save it for the end they are said to have buried it. What strikes me is not merely that people are inconsistent, but that each of these readings is defensible on its own and that the reading seems to be selected after the verdict is already fixed, so that no conduct is safe — objecting, agreeing, and every way of arranging one's remarks all get filed as evidence of bad faith, and the accusation becomes impossible to answer, because trying to answer it is treated as more of the same.

I have heard labels that seem to circle the same thing — concern trolling, sealioning, moving the goalposts — but each of them names a tactic someone can be caught using, whereas what I am after is the way the standard for good faith itself gets chosen to fit a desired conclusion, so that the standard can never be caught failing. I have also heard it said that people give themselves the benefit of the doubt and attribute their opponents' views to malice rather than principle, but that seems to be about how we explain conduct, not about the criteria we use in the first place to decide whether an argument is offered in good faith. I am not asking for a reading list, and I would be satisfied with no label at all if there is nothing established here. What I want is the analysis: whether this is one phenomenon or several that I am conflating; what the nearest recognized ideas are and precisely where each of them stops short; which, if any, fits best; and, if nothing does, a proposed term with a definition, together with boundary cases — cases that resemble this one but are not it, and cases that sit on the edge.
