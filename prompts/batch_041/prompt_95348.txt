I retired from an experimental physics lab a few years back and have been teaching myself philosophy since — badly, but enthusiastically. A few of us in a reading group got onto Thomson's lamp last week (the one where the switch flips at the half-minute mark, then the quarter-minute mark, and so on, and you're supposed to say whether the lamp is on or off at the one-minute mark), and I came away irritated, becuase every answer anyone gave was really an answer about which idealizations we'd smuggled in, and nobody would own up to it. So I don't want the standard tour of who said what. What I want is a tier list from you: the available ways of responding to the puzzle, ranked by how much you'd actually trust each one to settle it rather than just relabel it. Here's the menu I've assembled, partly from reading and partly from arguing over beers — feel free to tell me that some of these are the same thing wearing different hats:

(a) The sequence of states never converges, so "on at t=1" has no truth value and the question is simply ill-posed.
(b) The lamp's state is fixed by its mechanism: the switch's position does converge to a definite limit, so you read the lamp off the mechanism's response at that final position — the state of the limit, not the limit of the states.
(c) There is no last switching, so nothing acts on the lamp at t=1; it just keeps whatever state it had before the flipping started.
(d) Nothing in the setup pins down the lamp's state, and two lamps with identical histories must have identical states, so the description underdetermines the answer — which is different from the answer being unknowable.
(e) Any real switch would wear out, arc, or quantum-tunnel long before the flipping got fast, so the idealization is unphysical and the puzzle dissolves.
(f) The state at t=1 is a fact about the world like any other; t=1 is just another point in the block, and the "supertask" is a redescription of the setup rather than a process that has to finish.

Give me tiers (S/A/B/C/D, or a 0–1 score, whichever suits you) with a sentence or two on why each item lands where it does, then a short closing paragraph on the one or two spots where you think the whole dispute actually turns — especially if any pair from the list turns out to be answers to different questions rather than rivals. Keep the whole thing under 500 words; my attention span for this stuff is about one cup of coffee.
