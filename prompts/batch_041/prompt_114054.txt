Two mates of mine in the reading group had a proper row in the pub last week about Williams and the integrity objection, and now they want me to write it up and settle it, mostly because I was the one sober enough to remember the whole thing. I'm giving you the exchange as straight as I can and I'm not going to tell you which of them I think is right, because I genuinely don't know. Both of them can argue and neither would budge.

1. Nadia went first. She said the thought-too-many point isn't a complaint about how a deliberation feels, it's a complaint about what makes the act right. If the only thing that makes it right for a man to save his own wife rather than a stranger, or rather than two strangers, is that doing so produces more impersonal good, then his wife is figuring in the story as one more locus of value, swappable for anyone who'd serve the sums as well. A relationship can't survive being justified from outside like that. And the same thing generalises into the integrity objection: a theory that tells you every project you have is provisional, to be dropped the moment the sums shift, is a theory that asks you to have no life of your own — to be, as she put it, a receptacle for the general good.

2. Dev said that's an argument about the sound of a deliberation, not about what's true. The theory says save her because more good comes of it — fine, but nobody has to recite that at the riverbank. We don't recite the justification for most of what we do, and that doesn't mean we haven't got one. He said she was running together the order of justification and the order of motivation.

3. Nadia said recitation was never the point; the point is that the justification, once you actually state it, has the wrong shape. And the "you don't have to think it" move cuts both ways: if the man saves her because she's his wife, and the consequentialist story is only a caption added afterwards, then the theory isn't doing any work in the case at all. She said the integrity point isn't that you'd have to run a calculus in your head, it's that a theory which holds your deepest commitments hostage to the sums isn't a theory for a creature that has a life to lead.

4. Dev came back with two things. (a) If that's an objection it proves far too much: ordinary morality is demanding too — it makes you keep promises you'd rather break, pay debts you'd rather dodge, care for parents you'd rather avoid. If demandingness is the complaint, then it's a complaint against morality as such and not against consequentialism, and Williams owes us a story about why the line falls exactly where he wants it. (b) It also proves too little: a consequentialist can happily grant that the husband's motivating thought is just that she's his wife. The theory is a criterion of rightness, not a decision procedure. You're not required to run sums at the riverbank; you're allowed to use dispositions, rules of thumb, love, whatever gets the best results.

5. Nadia said the criterion/decision-procedure distinction rescues the theory by making it idle. If he acts out of love and the sums happen to agree, then it's pure luck that the loving act and the right act coincide, and the theory is taking credit for a coincidence. And if you insist instead that the theory must be able to guide, then the husband is committed to being ready to drop his wife whenever the numbers say so — which is the integrity problem restated. As for (a), she said it's just false that ordinary morality is demanding in the same way: it asks specific things of you, it doesn't put the whole shape of your life up for auction.

6. Dev said fine, let's test the ground-projects bit. (a) Why should a theory protect whatever a person happens to care about most? A man whose ground project is something monstrous gets exactly the same shield from the integrity objection, so integrity can't be a trump — at most it's one value among others, to be weighed against the rest. (b) And he thought she was conflating impartiality in justification with impartiality in motivation: the theory doesn't ask you to feel the same about strangers as about your wife, only to count them equally when you're working out what to do. That's a much weaker demand than the objection makes it sound.

7. Nadia said that if integrity is just one more value in the pot then the theory has already won, because "one value among others, to be weighed" is the theory's own picture. And she offered a test case: suppose the wife is herself a consequentialist and sincerely hopes that if it ever came to it he'd save whoever the sums favoured. Does that dissolve the objection?

8. Dev said no, it relocates it — now the husband is honouring her preferences rather than loving her, and if he's honouring them because she prefers it, that's a thought too many all over again. And then the pub closed.
