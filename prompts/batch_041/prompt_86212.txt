My closest friend is director of operations at a mid-sized hospital group and is currently the lead defendant in a negligence suit. The facts that matter are these. Over roughly fourteen months, two of the group's hospitals filed internal incident reports about the same infusion-pump model: one from a ward where staff had repeatedly mis-set the devices, and one from a biomedical engineer who found the interface labelling ambiguous in a way that invited precisely that error. Read alone, each report was routine and went nowhere. No one at either site ever saw the other's report, and no individual in the organization ever entertained the proposition that the pump was unsafe. After a patient was harmed, the claimant's expert placed the two reports side by side and showed that together they entail that conclusion. The claimant's case is that the group knew the pumps were unsafe well before the injury. My friend says this is a fiction: knowledge requires someone who knows, and there was no such person.

He is confident and I am not. I do not need a prediction about how the court will rule. What I want is a clear-headed account of what the organization did and did not know, and of whether the claimant's use of "knew" is defensible rather than merely rhetorical. Two details seem to me to matter, and I cannot say how. First, whether the inference was obvious, of the sort a clerk could have drawn in an afternoon, or whether it required an expert's eye. Second, whether it makes any difference that the group maintained a shared reporting database in which both reports sat unread together, so that the two pieces of information were, in some sense, already in one place. Help me think this through, so that I can give him an honest answer rather than repeating that it is a legal fiction.
