You are an expert in the philosophy of law, with a specialism in the theory of statutory interpretation and a secondary interest in how a decision-maker's operative rule can come apart from the rule she professes. Treat the following as an exercise in rule attribution and prediction, not as an invitation to summarize the textbook opposition between textualism and purposivism. I am a philosophy professor who teaches a seminar on interpretive theory, and I use a fictional judge, Imogen Vance of the Court of Appeals of the (fictional) State of Calder, as a running case study. Everything below is stipulated; none of it is drawn from real litigation. Please do not reach for real cases, real statutes, or named scholars, and do not present anything as a citation — I want your reasoning, not your bibliography.

Here is the relevant track record. I give each case a nickname, the facts, Vance's disposition, and the reasons she gave in the opinion.

1. The park trails case. A statute provided that no person shall operate a vehicle on a state park trail. Two appeals were consolidated: one from a cyclist, one from the rider of a motorized stand-up scooter. Vance held that the bicycle fell outside the statute and the scooter inside it. Her stated reasons: the provision enumerated "automobile, motorcycle, truck, or other vehicle," and its heading read "Motorized Traffic on State Park Trails"; the word was therefore being used in a restricted sense, which captured the scooter but not the bicycle.

2. The coma case. A statute required a tort claim against the state to be presented within thirty days after the injury. The claimant was in a medically induced coma for six weeks and filed on the tenth day after regaining capacity. Vance held the claim timely. Her stated reasons: the notice requirement exists so that the state can investigate while evidence is fresh; a rule obliging an unconscious person to file would defeat that object and cannot have been intended; she therefore read in a tolling rule for incapacity.

3. The school-zone case. A statute prohibited possession of a firearm on school property. The defendant had an unloaded rifle, cased and locked, in the trunk of a car parked in a school lot. Vance held the conduct covered. Her stated reasons: possession is unqualified in the enacted text; a committee report had proposed a readily-accessible limitation that did not survive into the statute; a court may not add a limitation the legislature declined to enact. In the course of this opinion she also remarked, in passing, that legislative history is not law and cannot be used to narrow a clear text.

4. The hospital case. A statute exempted charitable organizations from an annual registration fee. The applicant was a nonprofit hospital open to all patients regardless of insurance, roughly a tenth of whose care was uncompensated, which also ran a free clinic. Vance held it exempt. Her stated reasons: charitable carries a settled legal meaning, namely relief of the poor and provision of public benefit, rather than the mere making of gifts; the provision's history confirmed that sense.

5. The cross-reference case. A benefit program was made available to employees of covered employers, with eligibility defined by reference to section 4(b)(2). Section 4(b)(2) in fact defines covered employer, while 4(b)(3) defines something else entirely; read literally, no employee could ever qualify. Vance held the reference to be a clerical error and read it as 4(b)(3). Her stated reasons: a reading that makes the program inoperative is one the enacting legislature cannot have intended, and she characterized what she was doing as correcting a scrivener's error rather than construing the text.

Now the case awaiting decision, on which I want your prediction.

The farm processing case. A chapter of the licensing code provides: "A person who grows agricultural products in this state and processes those products on the premises where they are grown is exempt from the licensing requirements of this chapter." The definitions section does not define process. A neighboring provision, section 3, speaks of the duty to "pack, grade, or otherwise process raw commodities." The chapter heading is "On-Farm Packing and Grading." The chapter's preamble contains legislative findings declaring that small agricultural enterprises should be encouraged to add value on the farm and that regulatory burdens should not fall on the smallest producers. The committee report on the exemption provision, however, says the exemption was meant to spare farmers the cost of licensing for packing and grading raw commodities in their unprocessed state, and states that operations which transform a commodity — canning, baking, fermenting — should remain licensed because they present food-safety risks the licensing scheme is designed to address. The appellants grow strawberries and make and sell jam from them in a commercial kitchen on their farm; they are a small operation and also sell some berries raw. The licensing board denied them the exemption.

Before you commit to a prediction, brainstorm on paper. Then give me, in this order: (a) at least two distinct interpretive rules that are jointly consistent with all five of Vance's dispositions, stating each rule precisely enough that it could be applied mechanically, and noting for each whether it matches her professed methodology or only her practice; (b) what each candidate rule predicts about the farm processing case, with the reasoning that gets it there; (c) your best guess at her disposition, with your reasons for preferring that rule to its rivals, and an explicit statement of how confident you are and what would raise or lower that confidence; (d) which of the five precedents is the closest analog and whether the analogical route itself is determinate here; and (e) the further case that would best discriminate among the candidate rules, specified with enough detail that I could actually put it to her — in particular, I am interested in whether her purpose-based reasoning has a genuine textual ceiling and in whether the purpose that does the work is the chapter's general aim or the specific provision's own aim. Where the record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a resolution; and where two of the candidate rules differ only verbally, say which part of the dispute is merely about words.
