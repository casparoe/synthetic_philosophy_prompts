"In class we read the handout arguing that Nyāya's parataḥ-prāmāṇya — the thesis that a cognition's validity is established from something else — makes it a proto-fallibilist position, in contrast with the Mīmāṃsā svataḥ-prāmāṇya view. And here's what the student wrote:

"Naiyāyaikas accept four pramāṇas. Pratyakṣa, perception, arises from the contact of a sense faculty with an object — the pramāṇa is that contact, though Nyāya uses pramāṇa and pramā interchangeably, so it comes to the same thing as calling the resulting cognition the pramāṇa. Anumāna, inference, goes from a mark (liṅga) to what it marks: seeing smoke on the hill, we infer fire, since wherever there is smoke there is fire (vyāpti). Upamāna is knowledge of something through its resemblance to something already known — the stock case being the man told that a gavaya is like a cow, who then recognises one in the forest and thereby knows what gavaya means. Śabda is verbal testimony: accepting the assertion of an āpta, a trustworthy speaker, e.g. a doctor telling you which medicine to take. Naiyāyaikas also accept arthāpatti, postulation, for cases like inferring that Devadatta must be outdoors because he is alive but not at home; they borrowed it from Mīmāṃsā and kept it as a subordinate pramāṇa.

On error: in the shell-silver case, the silver remembered from the shop is apprehended in the shell. Nyāya calls this anirvacanīyakhyāti, the indescribable error, because the silver that appears cannot be classified as either existent or non-existent.

On validity: Nyāya holds that a cognition's validity is intrinsic to it — svataḥ-prāmāṇya — provided its causal conditions are unimpeded, which is why ordinary perception does not need a further cognition to certify it; only invalidity has to be discovered from something else. And since Nyāya is a realist school, it has to hold that each pramāṇa delivers certainty within its own domain, so the proto-fallibilist reading in the handout cannot be right: a realist cannot allow that a cognition which is valid now might be overturned later.

The five members (pratijñā, hetu, udāharaṇa, upanaya, nigamana) make inference a public, checkable procedure — you state the thesis, the reason, the example that carries the pervasion, apply it to the case in hand, and draw the conclusion. Since the pervasion rests on repeated observation, the conclusion is only as strong as our induction, which is a further reason a Naiyāyika would not be a fallibilist."
