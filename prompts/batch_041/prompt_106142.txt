The move is this. Whenever the bulk collection of communications metadata comes up — the who-called-whom, the times, the durations, the addresses of the sites visited, everything that surrounds a message rather than its contents — someone will say that it is like a postal worker reading the outside of an envelope. The address, the postmark, the bare fact that a letter was sent at all: all of that is on the outside, exposed to view, and nobody supposes the postman has violated your privacy merely by handling it. So, the argument runs, an internet service provider or a security agency that records metadata but never reads the contents is doing something of the same kind, and the alarm about it is overblown. Sometimes the analogy is strengthened: the postal worker looks at the outside of every letter, not merely yours, and looking at all of them at once is still only looking at the outside.

I want an audit of this analogy rather than a verdict on mass surveillance in general. Please set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the envelope, the address, the letter, the postal worker, the sorting office, and the person who reads the outside of every envelope at once — and then tell me where the mapping breaks down.
