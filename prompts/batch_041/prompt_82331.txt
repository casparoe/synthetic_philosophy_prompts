A colleague in a policy reading group has been arguing for criminal liability on the part of the companies that build conversational systems, and he rests the case on Dennett's comparison of such systems to counterfeit people: counterfeiting was treated as a grave crime from the outset, Dennett writes, because it "undermines the trust on which society depends", and a synthetic interlocutor that passes for human is supposed to be the same kind of offence against a shared medium. I want an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on AI. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — the currency, the issuing authority, the forger, the dupe, the shopkeeper who accepts a bad note, the trust the whole arrangement runs on, and whatever plays the role of the watermark — then say where the mapping fails, separating the failures that bear on the liability conclusion from the ones that are merely decorative. Two asymmetries interest me in particular. First, the harm of counterfeit money is bounded by the availability of cheap and reliable detection: notes carry watermarks and scanners are built to refuse them, whereas the mark of a convincing counterfeit person is precisely that detection fails, so the analogy imports a detector whose success the target case is defined against. Second, a debased currency damages every holder of genuine notes and not only the person deceived, which obliges the analogy to say what is being diluted, by whom, and who holds the stock. And does the comparison not require a fixed standard of the genuine article — a real person behind the utterance — of a kind that Dennett's own account of minds would refuse him? If the analogy cannot carry the liability claim, say what comparison could.
