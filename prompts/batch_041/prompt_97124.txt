I was at a board-game night last week and two friends got into a weird argument about a one-shot prisoner's dilemma variant they'd read about. Each player secretly picks split or take. Both split: 3 each. Both take: 1 each. One takes and the other splits: the taker gets 5, the splitter gets 0. No repeat rounds, no reputation, they'll never meet again — they both agreed on all that upfront. It went roughly like this.

Sam: If we're both reasoning about the same problem with the same information, we'll reach the same conclusion. So either we both split or we both take. Both-split pays 3 each, both-take pays 1 each, so the pair of us should split, and since I'm one of the pair, I split.

Rita: That's just wishing. My decision can't make yours happen — we're causally seperate people. If a take by me made a take by you, fine, but it doesn't. And you're not me. Suppose you knew I got a small bonus for taking, so our payoffs weren't the same. Your symmetry argument would have nothing to say, but the game would still have a right answer. An argument for what to do that evaporates the moment the other player isn't your clone can't be an argument about rationality at all. Worse, if there's any chance you're the type who takes, then me taking gets me 5 instead of 0 when you split, and costs me only 2 when you take.

Sam: The identity isn't a trick, it's the point. I'm not saying my choice causes yours. I'm saying my choice is evidence about yours, the way finding out I'm a certain sort of person tells me something about you when we're the same sort. Given I split, you split; given I take, you take. So the options aren't 'split and maybe get exploited' versus 'take and maybe exploit', they're 'both split, 3 each' versus 'both take, 1 each'. On those conditional probabilities, splitting wins.

Rita: But you only get those conditional probabilities by assuming we reason identically, and that's a fact about us, not about what's rational. And if evidential reasoning like that is allowed it proves far too much — it says my choice is evidence about things that happened before I was born. Also your whole 'the pair of us should split' framing quietly switches to picking one decision procedure for both of us at once, which is a different question from what a single player should do in a one-shot game.

Can you go through this and tell me who's right at each step — not who wins overall, but each move on its own, including anything they've both missed. And please say what would change your mind on whichever move you think is the crux, and where your own reasoning is most likely to go wrong. British English, please.
