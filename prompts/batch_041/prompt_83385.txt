Some blog post I read last week gave me its grand unified take on process-based vs outcome-based supervision of AI, and I want to know how much of it to trust before I repeat any of it in a seminar. Here's the post's pitch, as best I can reproduce it (paraphrasing, not quoting):

The old way — outcome supervision, aka RLHF — waits till the model produces an answer and then rewards it if the answer is correct. Trouble is, that teaches the model to get right answers for wrong reasons: it looks aligned on the benchmark and then does something completely different once it's deployed. Process supervision fixes this by rewarding the reasoning itself — a human, or more often a second model, reads each step of the chain of thought and marks it good or bad, so the model is trained on why it got the answer, not just whether it did. That's why process supervision is the real solution to alignment, and labs still doing outcome-only training are cutting corners. The obvious limit is that you can only supervise a process you can check, so for the open-ended tasks that actually matter you're back to outcomes. And the policy crowd has grabbed the same words: regulating AI by process means regulating how it was built, regulating by outcome means waiting for harm and then assigning liability — and they've got it backwards, because process regulation is the one that can never keep up with the tech. A friend of mine who works on evals says the whole thing is just "outcome supervision with extra steps," which I can't tell is a clever objection or a pun.
