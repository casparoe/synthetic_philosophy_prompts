I'm a first-year PhD student in economics, and this term I'm sitting in on a methodology seminar in the philosophy department. Last week the instructor circulated a short reading — an excerpt from a trade book on how economists think, with an introductory gloss of his own — and I have been chewing on it ever since, partly because I keep hearing compressed versions of its argument in my own department whenever someone questions the assumptions of a model. The gloss runs roughly as follows. "Friedman settled all of this in his 1953 essay: the realism of a theory's assumptions is simply irrelevant to how good the theory is, and the only test that matters is whether the theory generates accurate predictions. Hence his notorious dictum that the more significant the theory, the more unrealistic the assumptions — unrealism is a sign of theoretical power rather than a defect. His own illustration is the expert billiard player, who 'made his shots as if he knew the complicated mathematical formulas that would give the optimum directions of travel': we need not suppose that players actually solve the equations, only that they play as if they had. Firms are the same. No manager literally equates marginal revenue with marginal cost, but the hypothesis of profit maximization still yields the right comparative statics, and that is all a model needs to do. For Friedman a theory is a tool rather than a body of claims about the world, so its assumptions are not even the sort of thing that could be true or false. Critics have grumbled about unrealistic assumptions for seventy years, but the grumbling rests on a confusion: it asks assumptions to be descriptive, which is to demand a photograph where what we want is a map. So when a colleague objects that a model assumes perfect information, costless adjustment, or a single representative household, the correct reply is that this is exactly what a good model does — the more idealized the assumptions, the more general the theory, and the less need we feel to test the assumptions themselves."

My problem is that I cannot tell how much of this to trust. I have read enough to suspect that the final sentence is doing something the essay does not license, and I have heard the phrase "the F-twist" used as an insult without ever learning what exactly is supposed to be twisted; but I have also seen senior people wave away exactly this kind of objection, so I am inclined to think the passage is broadly right even if its rhetoric is overheated. I also have a professor who says, whenever the topic arises, that the whole argument trades on running together several different senses of the word "assumption" — an assumption that some factor is negligible, an assumption that fixes the domain within which the theory is supposed to apply, and an assumption that is merely a provisional starting point for theorizing — though I have never understood what turns on the distinction.

Please take the passage claim by claim and tell me, for each, whether a careful reader of Friedman would accept it as stated, whether it is a standard simplification that does no real damage, whether it is a contested reading of a genuinely ambiguous text, or whether it is simply wrong — and where a defender of Friedman would push back hardest and where even a sympathetic reader would have to concede. Then tell me whether the practical moral at the end, that objections to idealized assumptions can be waved away without further argument, survives once the rest of the passage has been corrected, and whether some version of the underlying philosophical claim remains defensible even in a form that a critic of Friedman could accept. Keep the exegesis and the philosophy separate wherever they come apart, say plainly when you are reconstructing a position rather than reporting one, and do not tell me to go read the essay — I would rather understand the issues than be pointed at a bibliography.
