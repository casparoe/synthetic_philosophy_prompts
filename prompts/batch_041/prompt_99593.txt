I am a third-year PhD student in the philosophy of biology, and this year I serve as the graduate student member of my university's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. I need to draft the written opinion I will circulate before our next meeting, and I would like your help producing it.

The case before us is a protocol from a lab in my own department. Over three years it would rear, expose to candidate compounds, and then kill on the order of forty to sixty million larvae of a stored-product pest moth. The scientific case for the work is strong and the principal investigator is well regarded; nobody disputes that the experiments are competently designed or that the alternatives proposed so far are less informative.

The live issue is jurisdictional. Our charter defines the animals within our remit by a list that names all vertebrates and, following a review two years ago, the decapod crustaceans; it says nothing about insects, and the attending veterinarian reads that silence as placing the protocol outside our authority entirely. A colleague from entomology has argued the opposite in an unusually forceful memo. Her claim is not that we should be confident these moths are sentient. It is that the decision should turn on expected rather than certain welfare: if there is, say, a one-in-a-thousand chance that each larva has morally relevant experience, then even a modestly bad experience for each of them, multiplied across tens of millions of individuals, would produce an expected welfare burden larger than the institution's entire vertebrate program combined. On that reasoning, she says, a committee that scrutinizes a few thousand mice and waves through a billion insects has its priorities backwards, and the silence in the charter reflects an oversight rather than a considered judgment.

The chair, who is sympathetic but uneasy, has floated a specific decision rule and wants our opinion on it. Under what he calls a probability-weighted headcount, the committee would compute an expected burden as the number of individuals times a credence that the taxon is sentient times a severity factor, and would assert jurisdiction whenever that product exceeds some threshold relative to our existing animal use. He would set the sentience credence low for insects, apply the rule, and extend our authority on that basis. I am not sure the rule is doing the work he thinks it is, and I am not sure the committee is the right body to be making the underlying judgment at all.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate. It should reach a concrete recommendation -- assert full jurisdiction, decline jurisdiction, or assert a limited jurisdiction with stated conditions -- with a time frame for whatever action you recommend, and it should be addressed to the committee rather than to me. It has to engage the entomology colleague's expected-welfare argument on its merits rather than deflecting to the charter's text, since the text is precisely what is in dispute. I would also like it to say plainly whether the chair's probability-weighted headcount is a defensible basis for committee action or whether it quietly imports commitments the committee has not agreed to, and if the latter, what a cleaner procedure would look like.

Finally, please tell me where your own reasoning is most likely to fail and what would change your recommendation -- which fact about the species, which feature of the decision procedure, or which argument on either side would move you to a different verdict. I would rather hand the committee an opinion that names its own weak point than one that reads as settled.
