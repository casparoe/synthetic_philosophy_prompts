I recently moved from a software engineering role into a bioinformatics group, and I keep running into an analogy that my new colleagues treat as self-evident: the genome is a program, and the cell is the machine that runs it. At a journal club last week it was used to settle whether viruses count as alive. The reasoning was that a virus is plainly a piece of code, that the code is the part that matters, and that a program does not stop being a program merely because it has to borrow someone else's computer; on that view the virus is alive in the only sense worth caring about, and a computer virus is not a bad model of a minimal organism. The same picture was then stretched to two further conclusions: that because the code is the essence, life is in principle substrate-independent, and that once we can write a sufficiently long genome we will simply have made life, the way compiling a program produces a running one. The part I found odd was how quickly the room moved past the borderline cases that seem to stress the picture hardest — prions, which replicate and cause disease while carrying no program at all, and plasmids, which are arguably code that has left home and lives in someone else's machine.

I would like a careful audit of how far this analogy actually holds. Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the source code, the compiler or interpreter, the hardware, the operating system, the power supply, the programmer — and then walk through where the mapping breaks, paying attention to the fact that in biology the program builds the machine that executes it, that the same molecule is both instruction and physical part, and that there is no clean specification-versus-implementation split. Separate the disanalogies that actually bear on the virus conclusion from the ones that are merely picturesque. Then tell me what the analogy quietly imports from the computing side that the biological case lacks: intentional design, modularity, a stable instruction set, substrate independence, the assumption that identity lives in the code rather than in a metabolic process. Finally, say whether the three conclusions it was used to support — that viruses are alive, that life is substrate-independent, and that synthesizing a genome amounts to making life — survive once those breaks are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusions all along. If there is a better analogy in the vicinity, such as a genome as a recipe rather than a blueprint, or an organism as a self-maintaining autocatalytic network, tell me what it fixes, what it costs, and which of the three conclusions it would support instead.
