Okay, so Dennett's two-bitser thing — the vending machine rigged to swallow real quarters and reject the slugs — has always bugged me, and I want you to build the strongest argument for a position I'm not even sure I hold yet: that the two-bitser actually cuts against Dennett rather than for him. The official line is that whatever inner states the machine has only 'represent' quarters relative to us and our interests, so its intentionality is derived, and there's no original intentionality anywhere down the line. Searle says that's incoherent — derived intentionality has to be dervied from somewhere, namely from a mind whose intentionality isn't itself borrowed.

Here's the wrinkle I want you to bake in. Suppose the machine's makers are long gone and nobody remembers what it was for. It's dug out of a ruin and every expert who studies it decides, wrongly, that it's a slug-sorter; they describe its states as representing slugs, wire their tests around that, publish on it. By Dennett's own account its intentionality is derived from us, so it now represents slugs. But nothing about the machine changed. I want the argument that this shows the derived/original distinction is doing no work at all — that 'derived intentionality' isn't a kind of intentionality but just a fact about what we're inclined to say, and that Dennett's move collapses either into Searle's view (there is original intentionality after all, namely ours) or into flat eliminativism about content. Then give me the best reply Dennett has — I expect something like: the intuition that 'nothing changed' is precisely the intuition he's putting under pressure, since a change in what everyone says about the machine IS a change in its derived intentionality, and there was never any other kind — and tell me whether that reply actually works or just relabels the problem.
