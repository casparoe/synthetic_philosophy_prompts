Ok so I need help prepping for a workshop and I'm mildly panicking. I'm a fourth-year PhD student in sociology, though my committee keeps reminding me I'm really doing philosophy of social science with extra steps. In two weeks I present a working paper at our department's methods workshop, and the Q&A is going to be rough because the room is a bad mix for my argument.

My thesis: generations — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, whatever — are not natural kinds. There's no homeostatic property cluster holding a cohort together, no shared causal mechanism, the birth windows are gerrymandered to fit whatever the analyst wants, and the whole thing looks like the textbook case of reifying an administrative convenience. So far so standard. But I don't want to just say they're social constructions and call it a day, because they obviously do something in the world — people self-identify, marketers target, HR departments run trainings about "managing the Gen Zs." So my move is to say they're what I'm calling ratifying cohorts: a birth-window plus a label plus a feedback loop, where people who take up the label start adjusting their attitudes and behavior toward the stereotype of their cohort, which makes the cohort look more real, which reinforces the label. On this view generations are real in degrees — as real as their loop is tight — and you can in principal measure the loop by tracking label uptake and stereotype-conformity over time. Some generations are real kinds; some are just arbitrary slices. The test is empirical.

The audience, roughly: (1) four or five quantitative cohort/demography people who use birth year as a variable constantly and get irritated when a philosopher tells them their main predictor is fake; (2) two philosophers of science — one is deep in the Hacking / social-construction / looping-kinds stuff and will think I'm either reinventing it or getting it wrong, the other is a hardline natural-kinds realist who thinks something is a real kind only when it carves nature at the joints; (3) one economist who only cares about identification and will ask how I'd ever seperate the labeling effect from a plain period effect; (4) the workshop organizer, a life-course sociologist who's genuinely sympathetic to generations but thinks the interesting unit is the life stage, not the cohort.
