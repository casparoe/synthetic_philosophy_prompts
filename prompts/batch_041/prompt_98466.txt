I'm writing on the timing of experience — the cluster of results around the color phi phenomenon, metacontrast masking, and the Libet-style findings on when subjects become aware of a stimulus or of their own intention. I keep meeting claims that the mind either revises experience after the fact, so that the experience itself is edited before it is had, or else leaves the experience untouched and edits only memory and report. Before I take a side, I want the specification: what must a theory of the timing of experience actually deliver in order to count as adequate?

Give me the desiderata list. For each requirement, state the case or consideration that generates it — the phenomenon it has to accommodate, the distinction it has to respect, the result it cannot contradict — and say why that consideration is a requirement rather than an optional nicety. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable for any theory worth the name and which are really commitments of a particular camp (say, those who insist that experience is confined to what is available to report, versus those who want to separate the time an experience represents from the time it occurs).

Then flag any subset of the requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, and explain what forces the choice; I care about this more than the rest, because I suspect the Orwellian/Stalinesque framing is one of the places where an unsatisfiable pair is doing the work. Finally, say which single requirement does the most to rule out the popular accounts on offer, and, briefly, how the main contenders fare against the list.

Three things to fold in, because they are where I expect the list to get interesting:

1. A stipulated pair of cases the theory should also have to handle. First, the ordinary color phi result: a dot moves from one position to another, changes color midway, and subjects report the color change as occurring before the second dot was presented. Second, a constructed mirror case, which I will call the delayed-content case: subjects report the color change as occurring at the time of the second dot, yet the color they experience is that of the first dot. Treat the second as a genuine target for the theory even though it is invented, and say what it would take for a theory to handle both without special pleading.

2. The list should say something about what the theory may posit. In particular, whether it is allowed to appeal to backward causation in the brain, and whether it must assign a determinate time to each experience or may treat temporal content as itself constructed.

3. The list should fix what the theory is a theory of: the time an experience represents an event as occurring, the time the experience occurs, or the time the subject reports it. If these come apart, the requirements should reflect that.

Separate as you go what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus.
