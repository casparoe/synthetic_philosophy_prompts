I'm running a ninety-minute evening session for a group of adult learners — mostly people who took one stats course in college and now work in product or ops, plus two philosophy grad students who wandered in because the title sounded fun — as part of a short "how machines learn" series at a community centre. My slot is concept learning from a handful of examples, and I want to do it through Bongard problems, the ones where you get six little boxes on the left and six on the right and have to work out what the left ones have in common that the right ones don't. I don't want you to explain Bongard problems to me, and I don't want you to solve mine — I want you to plan the session: what has to be in place before the very first puzzle even makes sense to them, what depends on what (so the order is forced rather than just plausible), which single puzzle should carry the whole thing and what it needs to have and to lack in order to do that, which misconceptions they'll arrive with or form along the way, exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off, and what I should deliberately leave out and tell them I'm leaving out. And one question I can put to them at the end that would show me the idea landed, as opposed to them just having had a nice time.

Some constraints from my end, because I'm drawing the puzzles myself rather than using the classic set — partly so nobody recognises them, partly so I can steer things. Three I've sketched: (a) every panel has exactly two shapes, a circle and a square, and position, colour and fill all vary meaninglessly; on the left the circle is always the bigger of the two, on the right it's always the smaller. (b) Left panels contain only shapes with no inward notch, right panels each contain at least one shape with a notch — but I drew them sloppily and it so happens that every left panel has an odd number of shapes and every right panel an even number, which I only noticed afterwards. (c) The rule isn't visible in any single panel: on the left, each panel is a rotation of the one above it, on the right each is a mirror of the one above it. I'm also toying with a fourth where I put all twelve panels on one side and none on the other, just to see what happens. Use whichever of these earns its place, tell me which one to lead with and which to cut, and be honest if one of them is doing damage.

Things I don't want in the session: the formal learning-theory apparatus (capacity, generalisation bounds, that whole zoo) — one sentence of hand-waving at most, since half the room will glaze over; a debate about whether the original puzzles have unique correct answers, which I can see swallowing the whole ninety minutes; and any framing that makes it about AI benchmarks, because that pulls us off the actual question. What I do want them to come away with is the thing that surprised me when I first got into these, which is how much of "getting the concept" is really about knowing what the rule is allowed to be about, and how little a pile of examples settles on its own. I'll be honest, I'm not totally sure (c) is teachable in the time we've got, so if you think it should be a ten-minute coda rather than the centrepiece, say so and tell me what to put in its place.
