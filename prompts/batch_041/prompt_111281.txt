Last spring I learned that I carry a mutation putting me at high risk for a late-onset neurodegenerative disease. Predictive testing for conditions of this kind is cheap and accurate, and a substantial fraction of people at risk decline it — a pattern the information-avoidance literature has documented and tried to explain. I read my way into the decision theory expecting to find the reassuring standard result: in Savage's framework, an expected-utility maximizer offered costless information before choosing can always ignore it, so the option to learn can only weakly raise expected utility. Good's theorem, and the earlier unpublished note of Ramsey's that it turns out to have anticipated, gave me a clean statement of what I had intuitively assumed about my own case. What I did not expect was how fragile the result looks once you leave expected-utility maximization or relax the assumption that information is genuinely free, and how little agreement there is about which of the escapes from the theorem is the philosophically interesting one.

That is the background. What I want is a prioritized research agenda, not a survey. Suppose I have two or three years and a philosopher's toolkit — formal decision theory, some probability, no empirical lab — and I want to work on the value of information. Which open questions in this area most deserve that time, and in what order? I am asking for a defended ranking. For each candidate, tell me what the question is, whether it is upstream of other work, how tractable it is for someone with my background, how neglected it is, and what a decisive result would even look like. Then tell me which of the standard candidates I should deprioritize, and why.

At minimum I would like you to weigh the following, though I would rather you tell me if I have mis-framed the space than simply rank what I hand you:

The status of the negative-value counterexamples. Information can have negative expected value under non-expected-utility rules such as maximin and minimax regret, and under ambiguity-averse or imprecise-probability models; the value of information is also sensitive to whether the information is truly costless, whether the agent can commit in advance to ignoring it, and whether the agent's preferences or identity change on learning. Is there a principled characterization of exactly when information can hurt a rational agent, and does that characterization illuminate rationality, or only the particular decision rule we happened to assume?

The relationship between the theorem and actual information avoidance. The empirical literature — the Golman, Hagmann and Loewenstein review is the obvious entry point — documents people declining free and useful information, and the low uptake of predictive testing for conditions such as Huntington's disease is the paradigm case. Is this best modelled as a violation of expected utility, as utility that depends directly on beliefs, as a failure of the assumptions about what counts as the same decision problem before and after learning, or as something else? Which of these would genuinely change how much normative force the theorem has?

The theorem's dependence on the sure-thing principle and on the partition of states. Good's result is a theorem inside Savage's framework. How much survives when we weaken the sure-thing principle, when the state space is vague or open-ended, or when the agent's hypotheses are not antecedently well defined — which is the normal situation in science? Is there a version of the result for non-Bayesian or logically uncertain agents, and if not, is that a limitation of the result or a genuine failure of the principle?

The individual versus the social value of information. In strategic settings, information can have negative value for its receiver because it changes what others can infer, and it can be harmful even when acquiring it is individually rational. How much of the philosophical interest lies in this contrast, and how much of the decision-theoretic literature has actually engaged with it?

The bearing of the theorem on the epistemology of inquiry: whether one ought to seek evidence, how to weigh the risk that inquiry is self-undermining, and whether the theorem supports or undercuts the norm that one should gather more evidence before acting.

I want the ordering argued rather than asserted. State your criteria — importance, tractability, upstreamness, neglectedness — say how you trade them off, and say what would move a question up or down the list. Where you are uncertain, say so explicitly. Do not give me a reading list: I want the argument for the ordering, and the reasons each question is or is not worth the years.
