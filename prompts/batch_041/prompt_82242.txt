Hey — I write the Science & Ideas column for a mid-size metro daily, and I keep tripping over the same puzzle in local stories, so I'm handing it to you.

Setup. I spent most of last month at a county historical commission — call it the Harlow County Heritage Board, it's real, I'm just not naming it — that's been arguing for two years about what goes on a set of roadside plaques about an 1870s massacre. The documentary record is thin and contested: two newspaper lines, a missionary's letter that contradicts them, a handful of property deeds. The descendants of the victims have an oral account carried down six generations, and it flatly disagrees with the letter about who did what and roughly how many died. The Board wrote a rule into its bylaws: when the community's memory and the written record conflict about an event that belongs to that community, the plaque follows the memory. They were deliberate about it — one member told me the archive has been wrong about these people before, and the purpose of the plaque is repair, not forensics.

Then, a week later, the same Board hears a land claim from a different family, and now they want deeds, tax rolls, anything written, and they wave off oral testimony as "anecdote." Same board, same room, two totally different rules for what counts as knowing what happened.

Here's what I want. Put on your philosopher-of-history hat — the kind of person who'd write this up for a literary review, not a textbook — and think this through carefully before you commit to anything. Start by brainstorming out loud, briefly, about what's actually going on in that room, then give me the goods:

Reconstruct the implicit theory of historical knowledge the Board is running on. Does deferring to memory presuppose that the past is constructed by communities rather than discovered, or could it be a straightforward view about who has standing to speak, or about the ethics of repair, or just about which sources happen to be reliable here? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out? Then tell me whether the two halves of the practice — memory wins for the massacre, documents win for the land — are actually inconsistent, or whether there's a principle that covers both and I'm just not seeing it. Be sharp about the difference between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely happens to fit well with, and tell me where it's philosophically underdetermined, so that three or four different theories would all license the same plaques and the same land rulings. Finally: if you swapped in a plain correspondence view of history — the past is one way, evidence is evidence, and the community's memory is just another fallible source — what concretely has to change at the Harlow County Heritage Board, and what, if anything, does the Board lose that's worth keeping?

Dont give me a reading list or citations — I can't use them and neither can you here. I want your reasoning, a verdict, and no hedging at the end.

After the essay, tack on a short structured summary for my notes, as a JSON object with exactly these fields:

- presupposed_view: one sentence naming the view the practice commits it to.
- rivals_ruled_out: array of strings, each a view the practice makes unavailable.
- strictly_presupposed_vs_merely_fits: object with two array fields, strict and fits, splitting your claims accordingly.
- coherence_verdict: one of "coherent", "incoherent", or "coherent only if", plus a sentence saying which half of the practice is doing the real work.
- underdetermined_points: array of strings, the places several theories would license the same practice.
- correspondence_swap_consequences: array of strings, what changes if the correspondence view is adopted.
- bottom_line: one sentence.

Keep the prose piece under 900 words, and make it sound like me, not a journal article.
