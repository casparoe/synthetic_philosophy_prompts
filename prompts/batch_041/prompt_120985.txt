I teach one 90-minute session of an upper-level seminar in decision theory and social choice (18 students, movable chairs, whiteboard, no computers), for students who have seen the Nash bargaining solution defined but have never had to choose between it and a rival. I want a runnable activity that makes them feel the pull of the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, and the price of that pull.

Treat the following as the whole of the theory the activity may assert, and add no further claims about which axioms each rule satisfies and no citations of any kind; everything the debrief says must be checkable against the materials you supply. Two parties bargain over a convex set of achievable payoff pairs with a disagreement point d. The product rule is the point maximizing the product of the two gains over d, and it is insensitive to irrelevant options: if the point it picks is still achievable after some options are deleted, the deal is unchanged. The ray rule is the point where the segment from d to the ideal point (each side's best achievable payoff, taken separately) crosses the frontier; equivalently, the two sides end up with the same fraction of their gains over d. It is not insensitive in that way: deleting or adding an option that is not part of the deal can move it.

Design the activity so that students run into this rather than hear it. Deliver all of the following.

1. A minute-by-minute run sheet for the 90 minutes, with the materials, the board layout, and what the facilitator says at each stage — including what the facilitator must not say until the final twenty minutes. No lecture up front; the names of the two rules appear only at the end.

2. Case cards, with the numbers supplied. Each case is a menu of at most four contracts that the parties may mix across a long run of identical transactions (so any point in the convex hull is available), plus a disagreement outcome, plus payoffs in round numbers a facilitator can handle at the board in under a minute. Each card carries a plotted diagram of the feasible set in payoff space with the disagreement point marked. Across the cards you must include: (a) a case with a kinked frontier and lopsided ceilings — one party's best possible outcome at least three times the other's — where an equal split, the product rule and the ray rule land at three points far enough apart to be told apart at a glance; (b) at least one case whose disagreement point is not the origin, so that the fractions are fractions of the gains over d; (c) a pair of cases identical except that one adds a single contract that raises only one party's ceiling and that neither rule selects as the deal, chosen so that the addition moves the ray rule's deal while leaving the product rule's deal where it was. Verify every claim arithmetically, and give the facilitator a table of the three candidate points (equal split, product rule, ray rule) for each case.

3. The staged experience. The pairs negotiate the lopsided case first and defend their split; then negotiate the second member of the case pair and compare; then, before any rule is named, each pair writes the standing rule it would want a mediator to apply to an unseen third case, knowing that its own side will be drawn at random afterwards. The facilitator then draws sides, applies the written rules, and only then puts the two named rules on the board.

4. A debrief that maps each moment onto the idea: equal shares versus equal proportional sacrifice; what the ray rule needs the ideal point for; what the phantom contract exposes about each rule; what the pairs' own splits and written rules reveal. Be explicit about which intuition each of the two rules violates.

5. At least four predictable failures with handling — including what to do when a pair converges instantly on an equal split, when someone rejects the mixing story, when someone insists there is a right answer, and when the tally of written rules is too vague to apply.

6. A frank closing note on what the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply, including that the ray rule is defined for all convex feasible sets and not only for menus, that case cards cannot show why insensitivity to deleted options is normatively attractive, and that the proportional reading of sacrifice presupposes something about comparable scales that both rules officially disown.
