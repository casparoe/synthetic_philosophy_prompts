Some friends have been poking at my take on personal identity and I want to know where the soft spots are before I try to defend it again. Here's the package, roughly how I'd write it down:

1. Physicalist, no further fact, no soul, no extra ingredient. A self is a center of narrative gravity — a posit we make in order to predict and blame people, real the way a center of mass is real, not an item in the world's inventory.
2. Reductionist about survival: what matters is psychological connectedness and continuity, not identity itself. Identity isn't what matters.
3. But desert feels identity-dependent to me in a way that isn't just instrumental. Punishing someone who's psychologically continuous with the offender but numerically distinct from him isn't merely inefficient, it's unjust, and I can't tell whether that's a confusion I should train out of myself or a datum.
4. I think a life that hangs together as a story is better than one that doesn't, and not just aesthetically. Related: I've got this notion of narrative debt. If you rewrite your self-story hard enough — deradicalizing, a conversion, getting sober, leaving a marriage — the self whose story got rewritten has some kind of claim on you, and the new story can be a bad revision of the old one rather than just a different one.
5. Verdict on a case: there's a drug that lets you remember a bad stretch of your life as something that happened to a stranger. No distress, no gaps, you function fine. I think taking it is a real loss, even though the resulting person is happier and just as coherent as before.

I suspect somthing in here is doing more work than I've noticed, but I honestly can't tell which piece. Go through the pairs: which ones are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and how bad each real one is. For each real tension say what would have to give and what that costs me. Rank them by severity instead of telling me they're all a bit tense, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most.
