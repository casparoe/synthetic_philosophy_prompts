so my philosophy of science reading group has been going in circles for the better part of a year on the same thing and I don't want a verdict, I want a diagnosis of why we can't move. the question is whether falsifiability can actually do the work of drawing the science / pseudoscience line. the positions in the room, roughly:

one camp: of course it can, that's the whole point of Popper's criterion. a claim is scientific if it rules something out, if there's some observation that would kill it. astrology, the various energy-medicine claims, and the cruder versions of psychoanalysis are pseudoscience because their defenders can absorb any result — if every possible observation is compatible with your theory then your theory isn't telling us anything.

the other camp: that's a slogan, not a criterion. anything can be made falsifiable with enough cleverness — you add an auxiliary hypothesis, you blame the instrument, you say the conditions weren't right. Popper knew this (the Duhem-Quine point) and his fix was to forbid "ad hoc" rescues, but nobody can define ad hoc without the criterion collapsing into a matter of taste. and there's the self-application problem: the falsifiability criterion isn't itself a falsifiable empirical claim, so by its own lights it isn't science either.

then there's a third person who keeps saying the question is malformed — that "science" is a famly-resemblance word, that "psuedoscience" is doing social work (it's what you call a rival when you want it kept out of the curriculum) rather than picking out a natural kind, and that Popper only ever meant the criterion as a normative proposal about what we ought to count as good method, not a description of anything. nobody in the room knows what to do with this move, including the person making it.

my own suspicion, which I'd like you to either back or knock down: the two main camps aren't disagreeing about the logic of confirmation at all. one is asking "what is science?" and the other is asking "what should we trust?", and falsifiability answers the second badly and the first not at all. so what kind of disagreement is this, actually? is there a hidden empirical question doing the work — does the criterion track anything real about how inquiries that actually succeed tend to proceed? is it a difference about standards — does a criterion have to be a necessary-and-sufficient definition, or can it be a graded ideal where theories are more or less testable and the binary question is just the wrong shape? or is it bedrock, where some of us think pseudoscience is a real category and some of us think it's a slur in a lab coat? and given whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would actually count as progress here: a distinction, a change of question, a case study that breaks the symmetry, or nothing at all because this one should just be dropped.
