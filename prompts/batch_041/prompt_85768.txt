Our health authority decides what to pay for by ranking treatments by cost per quality-adjusted life year and funding everything that comes in under a threshold — though we also give a bump to treatments for very severe conditions and a separate bump for rare diseases, and those two overlays don't obviously compose into a single ranking. The whole thing depends on being able to put a number on a health state and then sort. Someone in our journal club keeps insisting this assumes 'better than' is transitive, and points to a family of puzzles where you go in small steps down a chain, say from short intense pain to longer and milder pain, where each step looks better than the one before, and yet the far end of the chain doesn't look better than where you started at all. So, he says, betterness isn't transitive and our league table is built on sand. I can't tell if he's right. What commitments does our funding rule actually have about betterness: does it strictly need transitivity, or would a ranking of just the options in front of us, or a cardinal measure, or some local pairwise procedure do the same work — and are any of those really weaker assumptions? Which views about incommensurability or non-transitive betterness does the arithmetic rule out, and which does it merely sit badly with? And if we took the puzzles seriously, what's the smallest change to how we fund treatments that would accomodate them — could we keep sorting at all, or does ranking itself collapse?
