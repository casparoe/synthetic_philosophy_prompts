I chair the accommodations committee at a large public university, and we are being asked, dozens of times a term, to approve or refuse the use of external aids — phones, personal notebooks, task managers, AI assistants, human support workers — during proctored, closed-book exams. Our current practice is ad hoc, and we lose appeals because we cannot explain why two similar requests came out differently. Please turn the extended mind thesis into a decision procedure we can actually run, one request at a time, in about ten minutes, and write up so that a committee member with no philosophy training could reach the same result from the same file.

Start from the conditions that literature standardly imposes — that the resource be reliably available and routinely used, that what it returns be taken at face value rather than checked, and that it be retrievable without effort — and add a fourth test our own draft proposes: that the student be able to notice and correct the aid's failures unaided. The procedure should say which questions to ask in what order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates in a refusal, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question at issue is a value judgment about what an exam is for. It should not be so permissive that anything a student happens to consult counts as part of their mind.

One of my colleagues argues that if we concede that an aid is part of the student's cognition, we must also hold the student answerable for whatever the aid produces, errors included. The procedure needs to say whether that consideration tells in favour of granting fewer accommodations or more.

Then run it on three requests: a student with ADHD whose entire working system is a phone-based task manager they maintain with a support coach; a student who wants to bring a general-purpose AI assistant they cannot inspect and whose outputs they do not understand; and a blind student requesting a screen reader, where I suspect our whole framework is the wrong one. Close by telling me what the procedure cannot decide and where it will misfire.
