I have just spent three hours arguing with a friend about Quine and ethics, and I want a written treatment I can send him. The dispute was this. My friend says Quine's naturalism is a package deal: once you accept that epistemology is continuous with empirical science and that there is no first philosophy empowered to legislate for inquiry from the outside, you have also accepted that moral inquiry is continuous with empirical science, so moral claims are to be assessed the way any other claims in the web of belief are assessed — by how well they serve the purposes we use them for. On his reading there is no principled reason to treat the norms of reasoning differently from the norms of conduct, and Quine's dismissiveness about moral truth is therefore a failure of nerve rather than a consequence of his naturalism. I take the opposite line: I think something in Quine's position distinguishes the two, and I want to know whether I can defend that without smuggling in a piece of traditional philosophy that Quine would have rejected. The point I keep returning to, and which I would like you to stress-test, is the asymmetry between two ways of naturalizing a norm. One way treats the norm as a describable habit or practice, explained by psychology and sociology, with no question of correctness left over. The other treats the norm as an instrument assessed by how well it serves an end, where the assessment is itself a piece of empirical reasoning — engineering, in Quine's sense, rather than first philosophy. If the second way is legitimate for norms of inference, it looks legitimate for norms of action too, and my friend wins. If it is not legitimate even for norms of inference, then Quine's naturalized epistemology carries less normative content than his admirers suppose, which is a different problem. I suspect the resolution lies in whether the end being served is fixed independently of the norm being assessed, but I have not been able to make that thought do the work I need it to do.
