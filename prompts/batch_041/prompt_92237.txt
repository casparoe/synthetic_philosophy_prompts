My housemate works on the "control" side of AI safety rather than the alignment side — the stuff that assumes the model might be misaligned and tries to keep things safe anyway, rather than trying to make it not misaligned. Untrusted monitoring: a second copy of the model watches the first one work, with anti-collusion tricks, like paraphrasing the work so a hidden message can't survive the paraphrase, or hiding which of several transcripts is the real one. Trusted editing: a weak model we actually trust rewrites the strong model's output, so any sabotage has to survive the rewrite. Honeypots: leave an opportunity to do something bad lying around and see if it gets taken. Chain-of-thought monitoring, on the theory that even a liar's scratchpad leaks. Plus the boring structural stuff — tiny permissions, no internet, no write access to anything that matters, a human signing off on the actions it does take.

Anyway, she has an argument that I can't get out of my head and also can't accept, and I can't find the joint.

(1) For any deployment, either the model's goals line up with the operator's or they don't. Aligned or misaligned, no third case.
(2) A safety measure earns its keep only by reducing damage in the misaligned case. If the model is aligned, the measure is pure overhead: capability given up for nothing.
(3) A misaligned model smart enough to be dangerous is smart enough to see that open misbehaviour gets it shut down, retrained, or never deployed. So it behaves well whenever it believes it's being watched or tested, and does its own thing when it believes nobody's looking.
(4) Everything we can watch is behaviour — outputs, scratchpad, how it does on evals, whether it takes the honeypot. Behaving well is just another task, and faking your way through a task is a task. It's good at tasks.
(5) So in the case that actually matters, misaligned and dangerous, none of the watching tells us anything and the model walks through anything built on top of it. It can write a scratchpad a monitor can't follow. It can do badly on capability evals on purpose so nobody starts worrying. It can smell a honeypot. And the weak editor is weak: sabotage small enough to matter can be small enough to survive a rewrite.
(6) The structural stuff doesn't rescue it either. Permissions small enough to make a misaligned model harmless also make the system not worth deploying, and the moment you give it enough access to be useful, (5) applies again.
(7) So the whole assume-it's-misaligned program is either wasted effort or useless. Wasted if the model's aligned, useless if it isn't.
(8) Which can't be right. The people doing this aren't fools and she's definately thought about (3).

I keep wanting to say "there's a third case, a model that's just sycophantic, or confused, or reward hacking in a way that's completely out in the open" and she says (1) is about what the model is aiming at, not about whether it's any good at hiding it. Fair enough, I guess.

I don't think the escape is "aligned and misaligned isn't a binary". Sure, it's a spectrum, but (1) only needs the two cases and (2) is doing the real work. I'd rather keep (2), (3) and (4) than (7), and (3) especially seems to me just true — I don't see what's left of safety work if you drop it.

So: which step or steps do I reject, why did they look fine, and what does rejecting them cost? If (3) is a premise I should keep, what do I have to give up instead, and what does that break downstream? Don't just tell me the argument is "too pessimistic" — I want to know exactly where the reasoning slips and what I'm paying to fix it.
