I'm a philosophy professor and Ive been kicking around a view I call the identification-gap thesis. Roughly: for any system you like, the accessible information — everything any possible observer could in principle get at — always leaves the ontology underdetermined, and what settles which ontology is correct are facts that are in principle inaccessible. Not just the usual underdetermination-by-evidence stuff; I mean the stronger claim that the inaccessible facts are doing the metaphysical work, so even an ideal reasoner with all the accessible facts couldn't identify the ontology. To make it concrete: suppose the accessible information is a bunch of conditional probabilities over some algebra of events, and the ontology is a hidden structure that generates them; I claim you can always find multiple hidden structures that generate the same accessible probabilities, and what makes one the right one is a fact that no observation could ever reveal. Ive been defending it in a seminar and I want to know what would count against it. Not just the standard objections, but which arguments, formal results, intuitions, or possible cases should actually move me, and how decisive they'd be. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. And be honest: does the view risk becoming unfalsifiable, and if it does, is that a problem for a view of this kind? I'd also like to know which defeaters a committed defender of the identification-gap thesis would themselves concede count against them, as opposed to ones they'd just dismiss. What would a rival view predict that mine doesn't? What would have to be true for my view to be false? Are there cases where accessible information alone does determine ontology, and if so, does that refute the strong thesis or only a weaker version? I'm also worried it might collapse into either verificationism or instrumentalism, and I want you to tell me if that's a real risk or a cheap shot. Please write your answer in American English, as continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Just write it like an email or a letter.
