I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of fifteen years, and the argument I have never managed to settle to my own satisfaction is the one about probability in the many-worlds interpretation. It resurfaces every few months, usually in a comment thread that starts with someone insisting that if the wavefunction never collapses then nothing ever happens with any probability at all, continues with a reply that probability was never anything but a measure of rational credence and the branching structure supplies one, and ends with a third party observing that the whole dispute is about whether the measure is being discovered or stipulated. The quantum-suicide thought experiment is where most people I argue with first meet the problem and, I have come to think, where they learn to avoid it. I now teach the upper-level philosophy of physics seminar in my department, and I am writing the final. The unit covered the measurement problem, decoherence, and the various attempts to make sense of the Born rule; my students can fluently report that there is a decision-theoretic derivation and a self-locating-uncertainty derivation, and I am reasonably sure that not one of them could say what either derivation is actually doing. I want a single essay question that cannot be answered by reproducing that report.

The crux I want them on is this. Consider a figure I have taken to calling the measure-agnostic Everettian, who accepts the branching structure of the wavefunction and the completeness of the unitary dynamics, but denies that the amplitudes attached to branches have any normative authority over what she should believe or how she should bet; she counts branches instead, and treats every branch as equally likely. The obvious objection is that branch counting is basis-dependent: rewrite the same post-measurement state in a different basis and the count changes, and in the continuous case there is no count to be had at all. The interesting question is what follows from that objection. Does it refute the measure-agnostic outright, or does it only show that she owes us a preferred basis, and if the latter, what has she really conceded? Does the decision-theoretic style of argument — preferences over acts, a representation theorem, and an axiom rendering the agent indifferent between branches she cannot distinguish — hand the amplitudes back to her, or does it smuggle in the very measure it purports to derive, and if it does, is that a fatal circularity or simply the honest shape of any attempt to get a normative quantity out of a physical one? And what precisely does the self-locating-uncertainty style of argument add: does it tell her what to believe, or only, and separately, how to act?

Please write the exam question itself — one essay, ninety minutes, twenty marks — together with a model answer of roughly eight hundred words and a marking scheme built on five must-hit moves, giving the marks each is worth and, for each, a one-line note on the characteristic way candidates miss it. Phrase the question so that a student who has memorized that decoherence explains why we never observe interference, and that the Born rule is available to those who want it, has nothing to write. The model answer must be entirely self-contained: my students sit the exam without internet access, so nothing may turn on citing who argued what or when, and the marking scheme should award no marks for names. Assume familiarity with the standard formalism and with what a squared amplitude is supposed to mean, but no mathematical sophistication beyond that — they are philosophers of physics, so let the difficulty live in the concepts rather than in the algebra.
