I clerk for a justice on our state's highest court, and I am trying to anticipate how she will vote in a capital case we will hear next term. What I want from you is not an abstract prediction but a reconstruction of the rule she is actually running, together with an honest account of how far her record underdetermines it.

Her capital votes over the past decade, as I would summarize them for a colleague:

In State v. Halloran she voted to affirm the death sentence. Halloran had abducted two teenagers, held them for a day, and killed them himself. He acted alone, intended the killings, and offered no substantial mitigation. The evidence was overwhelming: his own detailed confession, corroborated by DNA and by physical evidence at the scene.

In State v. Vance she again voted to affirm. Vance had tortured and killed a man who owed him money. He was the sole killer, acted with intent, and had no substantial mitigation. The evidence was essentially unassailable: two eyewitnesses with no connection to each other and Vance's fingerprints on the weapon.

In State v. Pruitt she voted to vacate the death sentence and remand for life without parole. Pruitt had waited in the car while his cousin robbed a gas station and shot the attendant. There was no evidence that Pruitt knew his cousin was armed or expected a killing, and the State's own theory was that the shooting took him by surprise. What tied Pruitt to the plan was the testimony of a jailhouse informant who received a reduced sentence in exchange — evidence I would call fragile.

In State v. Okafor she voted to vacate for a related reason. Okafor had served as a lookout; his role in the killing was peripheral, and the State conceded that he neither killed nor intended a killing. His conviction rested largely on a statement he gave after eleven hours of interrogation without counsel, a statement the trial court suppressed and the appellate court later held should never have reached the jury.

So her four capital votes divide cleanly in two: she affirmed in Halloran and Vance and vacated in Pruitt and Okafor. What I cannot tell from this record is which feature is doing the work, because two features — and arguably a third — have coincided in every case. In Halloran and Vance the defendants stood at the apex of culpability: they personally killed, they intended death, and there was no substantial mitigation, while the evidence of guilt was rock-solid. In Pruitt and Okafor the defendants fell well below that apex, and the evidence was fragile. Desert and reliability have never come apart in her record. Neither has the general-deterrence value of the crime, which was high in the two stranger-on-stranger predatory killings and low in the two cases of marginal participation.

The case we hear next term is State v. Sorenson, and it is the case that pulls these features apart. Sorenson was convicted of abducting, torturing, and murdering a young woman — on its face about as grave a killing as Halloran or Vance, and, if he did it, one that would place him at the apex of culpability. But the evidence against him is of the kind her earlier opinions treat with suspicion: the State's case rested principally on a single eyewitness identification made after a suggestive lineup and on a jailhouse informant's account; the only physical evidence, a hair, was later shown to be consistent with a large share of the population; and since trial the eyewitness has partially recanted. The defense has moved for a new sentencing hearing on residual-doubt grounds.

Please do the following. First, set out the distinct rules that fit her four earlier votes equally well. I suspect there is more than one — that her votes are consistent with a rule keyed to desert alone, with one keyed to reliability alone, with various ways of combining them, and perhaps with one keyed to deterrence — and I want the whole family rather than a single best fit. Second, say what each rule predicts about Sorenson and group the rules by the verdict they imply. Third, give me your best guess at how she will actually vote, and tell me what in her record, if anything, justifies preferring one rule over the others; if the record genuinely does not decide it, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a reason. Fourth, name the further case — the factual configuration not yet before her — that would separate the rules that agree about Sorenson, and explain what each would do with it. Finally, tell me what would move you personally: which additional detail about Sorenson, or which pattern in her prior opinions, would make you confident, and which would leave you guessing.

Write your answer in American English.
