My reading group has spent this whole term crawling through Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge — the counterfactual one from Philosophical Explanations, where knowing that p requires your belief to track the truth: you wouldn't believe it if it were false, and you would believe it if it were true. The session I keep chewing on was the one about closure, where Nozick just bites the bullet and says knowledge doesn't carry across known entailment. You can know you have hands, know that having hands rules out being a handless brain in a vat, and still not know you're not a handless brain in a vat, because the closest world where that last thing is false is a world where you'd be having exactly these experiences. What gets me is how un-spooky it is — the nearest world where the thing fails is usually just an ordinary Tuesday where the family hides the test results, or every barn in the county is a facade.

For the last meeting everyone's supposed to bring something short and non-academic, and I want to bring a poem instead of yet another handout. Can you write me one, and make the form do the argument rather than just decorate it? Here's what I was imagining. A chain poem, where each line begins with the last word (or a homophone of the last word) of the line before, so the whole thing reads as one long inferential chain, premise to consequence to consequence. It should start from something boring and certain-feeling — my hands, the mug on the desk, my friend is doing fine — and it should look like it's going to close the loop, with the final line reaching back toward the opening word. It must not close. Either it stops one link short, or it gets to the word it promised and can't do anything with it. 18 to 24 lines, no rhyme scheme needed. And I'd like one moment where two lines sit next to each other that are almost identical but differ in mood, one in the indicative and one in the subjunctive, so you can see that sensitivity and adherence are two different directions of the counterfactual and not the same condition said twice. Keep the counterfactual worlds mundane and domestic — no sci-fi.

Accuracy matters as much as beauty here, and honestly maybe more — someone in the group who actually knows this literature shouldn't wince. So at the end, can you add a few sentences telling me where the poem takes liberties or leans on a reading people argue about, and be upfront about what's settled versus what's genuinely contested? I don't want you to present the closure-denial as obviously correct or as obviously a reductio, and I don't want you to pretend everyone agrees tracking is still a live theory — I know there are serious objections out there, so just flag where the poem is quietly taking a side. Also, if I've got sensitivity and adherence backwards, please say so, because I've definately mixed them up alot this term.
