I host a modest podcast on technology and philosophy, and next week's guest is an independent researcher who defends a specific and contestable thesis: that the AI alignment problem is, at bottom, a multipolar trap — a race to the bottom among competing actors, none of whom can unilaterally slow down or invest in safety without being outcompeted, so that the much-discussed technical problem of getting a system to pursue intended goals is downstream of a coordination failure rather than a specification failure. His characteristic move under pressure is to say that alignment is a coordination problem in control-theory clothing, and that proposals to build one safe, well-controlled superintelligence merely relocate the trap to the stage at which someone must be trusted to build it.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that do more than let him restate the thesis. I want at least one question on whether a multipolar trap is a genuine mechanism or merely a Nash equilibrium with unusually bad payoffs, and what explanatory work the label does beyond the game-theoretic description. I want at least one on the charge that personifying the phenomenon — naming it after a Canaanite deity — smuggles agency into what is supposed to be a structural explanation. I want at least one on cases where competition appears to push toward safety rather than away from it, a race to the top rather than a race to the bottom, and what that does to the thesis. I want a question on the reply that a single decisive actor, or a pre-commitment device binding all actors at once, escapes the trap, and whether he thinks that reply succeeds. I want a question on what, if anything, would count as evidence against the coordination framing, given that it seems equally able to explain racing and restraint. And I want one on the normative implication: if the problem is coordination rather than specification, the remedy looks like governance, which raises the question of who legitimately sets the target and on what authority. For each question, add a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give.

Begin your reply with the questions themselves, in order, with no preamble, no restating of this request, and nothing before the first question; each question may be followed by its follow-up and a single line noting what you are pressing for.
