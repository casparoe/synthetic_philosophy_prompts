I am assembling a specification before I commit to any position on whether death is bad for the one who dies. I want you to write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of the badness of death must deliver. Set it out as a list of requirements, each stated as a demand on the theory and each accompanied by the case, intuition, or consideration that motivates it. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; I care less about scoring the familiar contenders.

I expect the list to include at least the following, though you should add whatever else you judge belongs and drop nothing that is genuinely required:

- The requirement to make sense of death being bad for the person who dies, given that the dead do not exist and experience nothing, and that death is not a state anyone is ever in. State how strong this constraint is and what it rules out.
- The timing problem: the account must either locate the badness of death at some time or explain why it need not be located anywhere.
- The asymmetry between our prenatal and our postnatal nonexistence: the account must either explain why it is fitting to lament the one and not the other, or explain away the intuition that there is anything to explain.
- The subject problem: if harm requires a subject who is harmed, say what the subject of the harm of death is, or give grounds for rejecting the requirement.
- Whether badness must be analyzed comparatively, as a relation between the actual life and some counterfactual life, and if so, what the baseline is and why.
- The requirement that the account accommodate the fact that death can be a good thing, or neutral, and not only a harm.
- The requirement that the badness of death not depend on the person's being aware of it.
- The requirement that the account say whether events after death (the thwarting or fulfillment of desires, the fate of one's projects, one's reputation) can be bad or good for the dead, and whether it can say this without positing harm to a nonexistent subject.
- A requirement of neutrality on the metaphysics of personal identity: the account should not presuppose either that you persist as the same person over time or that you are a series of person-stages, since the badness of death is meant to be assessable under either view.

Test the list against cases that a candidate theory must get right, and build the case for each requirement from them. Include at least these, and add others you think expose a requirement:

1. Two people, one killed painlessly in her sleep, the other in an irreversible vegetative state whose body then stops: does death add a harm in both, in one, or in neither?
2. A person whose remaining life would have been dominated by unrelievable pain or severe dementia: the theory must permit death to be a benefit here.
3. A person who dies just short of a long-anticipated and reliably expected good, and one who dies immediately after attaining it: the theory must be sensitive to counterfactual futures, or explain why not.
4. A person who at the time welcomes death and would have chosen it: say what the theory must deliver about whether death is bad for her.
5. A person whose life would have been worth living but only barely: what the theory must say, and why the answer matters.

After the list, mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are parochial, in the sense that only a particular camp must accept them. Then, and this is the part I most want, identify any pair or subset of the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, and argue for the incompatibility. If space allows, close with a brief pass over how the main families of view fare against the list, but do not let that displace the criteria. I do not want a reading list or citations; give me the specification itself.
