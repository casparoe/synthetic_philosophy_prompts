I'm a third-year PhD student in cognitive science, comp-neuro-ish background, thesis on mechanistic explanation in systems neuroscience. Besides the thesis I referee maybe six or eight papers a year for a mid-tier journal and I sit in this informal "explanation standards" reading group my lab runs, where we tear up each other's chapters and drafts before they go anywhere.

The thing I keep being useless at, in reviews and in the group, is the crane/skyhook thing and greedy reductionism — Dennett's distinction, where a crane is a mechanism that does real work out of parts that were already there and already accounted for, a skyhook is something hauled in from nowhere that quietly presupposes the very thing you were supposed to explain, and greedy reductionism is when you leap straight to the bottom level and announce the phenomenon is "nothing but" some lower-level process, skipping all the intermediate levels that were doing the explaining. I keep either waving the labels around without being able to say what makes a given proposal one or the other, or the reverse: I let something through because it had the right shape of a mechanism and it turned out to be a relabeling.

What I want from you is a decision procedure I can actually run, in about 40 minutes, on a paper or a draft chapter. My constraints: I'm not the decider — I write a review paragraph or a group comment, and the editor or the senior author has to sign off on anything that turns into a real objection. I have no lab access to the data, only the text and whatever's in the figures. And crucially, I do not want a procedure that rejects everything promissory: a lot of good work names a crane it hasn't built yet, and the honest verdict there is "this is a promissory note, here is the program it commits you to," not "reject." So the procedure has to separate the promissory-crane case from the skyhook case, since that's the distinction I actually keep fumbling.

So, concretely: (1) the questions to ask in order, (2) for each, what counts as a sufficient answer and what an evasion looks like, (3) where the procedure stops and says no, (4) where it has to hand the call to a person because it's a judgment about how much promissory credit this field is willing to extend rather than a fact about the text, and (5) a short test or two I can apply to a single sentence — the "X is just Y" sentence and the "the system represents / computes / infers Z" sentence, since those are the two I see most and they fail differently.

Then run it on three cases, and please make one of them hurt:
- A paper explaining infants' early object expectations by positing an innate core object-representation system that is simply stipulated as present from birth.
- A paper explaining adult moral condemnation as "just" reward learning over social feedback, with no account of the intermediate levels (concepts, norms, the difference between disapproval and prediction error).
- And my own chapter, where I explain the transmission of a tool-making technique across generations by invoking "cumulative cultural evolution." Run the procedure on it honestly; I suspect it comes out badly and I'd rather find out now.

Take the role of a philosopher of science who's worked seriously on mechanistic explanation and on this crane/skyhook business — someone who'd be annoyed by the labels being used as mere rhetoric. Before you write the procedure, spend a paragraph or two thinking out loud about which parts of the distinction are load-bearing and have to survive the operationalization and which are decorative and can be dropped; I'd rather see the thinking than a polished checklist that hides where the joints are. Don't give me a reading list or citations — I've read the stuff and I can't check references anyway, just work from the ideas. Also be explicit at the end about where the procedure will misfire: which real explanations it will wrongly flag as skyhooks, and which skyhooks it will let through because they're dressed up as cranes.
