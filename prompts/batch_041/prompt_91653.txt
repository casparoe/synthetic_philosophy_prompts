Two classmates in my seminar are stuck on a variant of Parfit's hitchhiker. In the variant, the driver is a perfect predictor who scans the hitchhiker for the reason he would act on: he will pick him up if and only if the scan shows that the hitchhiker would pay afterward because he promised, not because he fears the driver. The scan is deterministic and is shown to the hitchhiker before the driver decides; once in the car, the hitchhiker can still choose. The first says the hitchhiker should pay, because the scan is already fixed and paying for that reason is part of the pattern that made it come out as it did; refusing would be to treat a settled prediction as open. The second says that is confused: the scan is causally upstream, and paying now cannot change it, so the later self has no reason to pay; at most, the earlier self had a reason to have the disposition. The first replies that if the hitchhiker could reason his way out of paying, the scan would have shown that, so the second's advice is self-defeating. The second replies that self-defeatingness is a fact about the policy, not the act; once the ride is secured, the act is irrational, and rational irrationality only labels the paradox. The first then says that if the act is irrational, the policy is too, because the policy just is the act plus a prediction. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and say what, if anything, both are missing.
