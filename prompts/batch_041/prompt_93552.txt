As a secondary-school student writing an extended essay in philosophy of mind, I am trying to understand a conceptual issue that I suspect is often glossed over. I can state the standard distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness in rough terms: access consciousness concerns information being available to a subject for reasoning, report, and the guidance of action, whereas phenomenal consciousness concerns the subjective character of experience itself. I also understand that global workspace theory explains consciousness in terms of information being broadcast to a wide network of specialised processors, thereby making it available to many cognitive systems at once. My difficulty is that these two descriptions seem to fit one another almost too neatly: if consciousness just is global broadcast, then the theory looks like an account of access, and the phenomenal aspect either drops out or is assumed. I would like a careful explanation of this issue, written for someone who knows the basic vocabulary but not the technical literature.

To make the explanation concrete, please work through a distinction I have devised for the essay. I want to distinguish four possible readings of what a global broadcast might accomplish: (1) broadcast as mere availability to downstream systems; (2) broadcast as availability to a single central reader or narrator; (3) broadcast as availability to the system's own self-monitoring; and (4) broadcast as the kind of availability that makes a state reportable in the first person. For each reading, say whether global workspace theory requires it, whether it is sufficient for access consciousness, and whether it could ever be sufficient for phenomenal consciousness. I am especially interested in cases where the readings come apart.

Then consider the following case. A system has a global workspace, and a particular state is broadcast widely across it. However, the system has no capacity for self-monitoring, retains no memory of the broadcast, and produces no behavioural report; the broadcast happens once and is never integrated into later processing. Would a global workspace theorist be committed to calling that state conscious? If so, does that commitment reveal a problem in the theory, or does it show that the theory is not really attempting to answer the question of phenomenal consciousness? If not, what additional condition is doing the work, and is that condition part of global workspace theory or an extra assumption?

Please also consider a system that contains two global workspaces which never communicate with one another. Would global workspace theory predict one stream of consciousness or two? Explain what the theory would have to say, and whether that answer follows from the theory itself or from an added assumption about what counts as a single subject.

Finally, please state the strongest version of the objection that global workspace theory explains access consciousness and then quietly helps itself to phenomenal consciousness, and the strongest reply available on the theory's behalf. I am not looking for a list of researchers, dates, or readings; I want the conceptual structure of the problem, with the pressure points clearly marked and the places where the theory is silent identified honestly.
