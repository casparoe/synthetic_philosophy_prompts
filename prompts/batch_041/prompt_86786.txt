I'm in a graduate seminar on historical sociology, and we keep returning to the question of what counts as a societal collapse. I have been keeping a private scorecard, and my verdicts do not obviously fit one another. The end of the Bronze Age palatial systems in the eastern Mediterranean: collapse. The abandonment of the Classic Maya lowland cities: collapse. The transformations on Rapa Nui after ecological change and European contact: not collapse, or at least not the kind of collapse the word usually names. The disappearance of the Norse Greenland colony: collapse. The abandonment of Cahokia: collapse. The fall of the western Roman Empire: collapse, though I grow less sure each time I defend it. The dissolution of the Soviet Union: not collapse. The deindustrialization and population loss of Detroit: not collapse. The end of the Han dynasty: collapse. The end of the British Empire: not collapse, even though a vast political order came apart. Two pairs are especially hard for me to reconcile. I call the Maya a collapse even though Maya people, languages, and communities persist, yet I do not call the Soviet Union a collapse even though a state, an ideology, and an economic system vanished with unusual speed. And I call Norse Greenland a collapse even though its scale was tiny and Inuit Greenland continued, while I resist calling Rapa Nui a collapse even though its population and ecology were radically transformed. I suspect my verdicts are tracking a loss of some level of complexity, but then I cannot see why Detroit and the Soviet Union escape, and I cannot say whether the complexity is in the system, the population, or the elite institutions.

I have tried to formulate a principle of my own: a society collapses when it loses a level of organization that it cannot rebuild within a generation and that is not simply replaced by an organization of the same kind. That seems to make the Soviet Union a collapse, since the party-state was not rebuilt, and to make Rapa Nui something less than a collapse, since people reorganized rather than disappeared. Please test this principle against all the verdicts above, repair it if you can, and give me a small set of precise candidate principles if no single one survives. For each clause, identify the case that forces it. Then tell me what the best principle predicts for cases I have not listed: the Indus Valley cities, the Aztec and Inca states after conquest, the end of the Ming, the decline of the Ottoman Empire, the collapse of the Atlantic cod fishery, and the bankruptcy of a company town.

Finally, tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether a simpler principle that overturns one of my verdicts is preferable to a more complicated principle that saves them all. I would also like to know whether collapse should be defined relative to a unit — a state, a civilization, a population, a mode of production — and whether that choice, rather than any fact about the cases, is doing most of the work in my scorecard. Please do not simply say that collapse is a value-laden term and leave it there; I want the principle or the menu of principles worked out.
