I teach a small graduate seminar in moral and legal philosophy -- fourteen students, mostly second-years, plus two colleagues who sit in -- and next month we're doing a session on the moral status of everyday conventions: tipping, queueing, which side of the sidewalk you keep to, jaywalking, that whole family of things. What I want them to actually feel rather than recite is whether these things obligate you at all, and if they do, whether the obligation travels with a visitor who doesn't know the local convention. My colleague and I have been argueing about this for weeks and we've each got a pet distinction we're quietly hoping the students will run into on their own. Mine is that these conventions come in kinds: some are pure traffic rules (which side to pass doesn't matter in itself, only that we all pick one), some are desert-tracking (a queue encodes a claim about who waited and who's next), and some are status- or gift-tracking (a tip is a small transfer that says something about the relation, and the amount is calibrated to a local script nobody wrote down). His is that a visitor's ignorance excuses far more than we like to admit, which makes the locals the interesting party -- a local who knows the visitor doesn't know is now positioned either to inform or to exploit, and both of those carry weight the bare convention doesn't.

What I'd like from you is a 75-minute in-class activity that stages all this. No lecture, no debate prompt -- I want them doing something, a role-play or a staged market or a simulation with rules and small stakes, where the rules themselves do the teaching and my distinction and his never get announced from the front. My rough sketch: split the room into locals and visitors, hand the locals a card listing three invented conventions for a made-up town (I've been calling it Rill) and give the visitors only a vague brief, then run three quick rounds -- a coffee-and-tip-jar round, a single-printer queue round, and a street-crossing round -- with a twist that hands at least a few players a private reason to break the local rule. What I can't work out is how to make the rounds actually bite, or how to stop the visitors from just asking the locals what the rules are in round one, which would dissolve the whole thing. I had one idea: in Rill it's a mild breach of manners to ask about or explain a convention out loud, so the visitors' obvious move is itself sanctioned and the locals have a small standing cost to pay if they spell things out. I'm not sure whether that's too cute or exactly the right kind of nasty.

So give me the full design -- the roles, the cards, the three rounds with their stakes or payoffs, the timing, and what the facilitator does and doesn't say -- and then a debrief that maps specific moments back onto the questions I care about: whether a convention binds because it's fair, because it's expected, or simply because it's the local rule, and what exactly the visitor's excuse is an excuse for. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover, and be frank about which piece of the idea the activity just can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself.
