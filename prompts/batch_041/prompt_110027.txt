I've ended up in a weird spot. I just started a job fact-checking for a small local paper, and my uncle (who is, bless him, convinced that anything he hasn't seen with his own eyes is a conspiracy) has decided I'm the perfect person to argue with. His favourite move is: you believe your doctor, you believe the weather forecast, you believe the guy who inspected the bridge you drive over every morning -- did you check any of that yourself? No. So why is my belief any different? And when I try to answer him properly I hear myself saying "well, you need independent evidence before you trust someone," and then I realise I don't have any either, I just... trust.

Anyway, apparently philosophers have been having this exact fight since Hume and Reid and havn't settled it. As I understand it, there's a reductionist camp -- roughly, that a testimonial belief is only justified if the hearer has some non-testimonial basis for it, either global (testimony in general is reliable) or local (this speaker, on this topic, is worth trusting here) -- and an anti-reductionist camp that says testimony is a basic source of knowledge like perception or memory, that demanding reasons before you trust anyone is both psychologically false and epistemically wrongheaded. Both sides have been at it for decades and neither has given ground.

What I want from you isn't a verdict on who's right. I want a diagnosis of why it's stuck, and of what kind of disagreement it actually is. Is "justified" being used in two different senses -- one about what the individual hearer can produce right now, one about what a community can vouch for? Is one side doing psychology and the other doing permission, so they never actually touch? Is there a hidden empirical question underneath (how credulous humans actually are, whether default trust is adaptive) that neither side wants to own? Are they answering different questions, one about when a testimonial belief is knowledge and one about when it's rational to accept something? Or is it bedrock -- one side treats the individual knower as the unit of analysis and the other treats the social network as the unit, with no arguing across that?

Here's my hypothesis, and I'd like you to tell me if it's wrong. I think the real crux is the case my uncle keeps pointing at: the belief is held by a long chain of people, none of whom has checked the thing themselves -- a bridge, a diagnosis, a historical fact -- so there is no single person anywhere who has the non-testimonial basis the reductionist asks for. If that case is the crux, then the two sides are really disagreeing about whether a chain like that can ground knowledge at all, and everything else is downstream of that. But maybe that's not the crux and I'm just pattern-matching to my uncle.

Given whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would actually count as progress -- a distinction that dissolves the fight, a kind of case one side can't handle, something formal about how trust propagates along a chain -- or say plainly that no progress should be expected and why. And please be careful to separate what's actually settled from what's genuinely contested among the people who work on this. I don't want one camp's position served to me as the consensus, and I don't want a tidy "philosophers broadly agree that..." if that isn't true. I can't look any of this up right now, so if you're unsure, or if it's a live fight, just say so.
