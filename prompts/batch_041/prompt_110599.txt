Bit of a weird one, but it's been bugging me for a while and I don't really have anyone to ask. I work in accounts payable for a mid-sized exporter and I've had to sit through the annual anti-bribery training three years running now, and something about it keeps nagging at me. The rule they drill in is basically this: anything of value given to a public official to get them to do something they're already supposed to do, or to do it faster, is a bribe, full stop — even if it's the local custom, even if it's small, even if it's a chicken. Meanwhile the same company flies clients out to a golf resort, the people at the top of our parent company donate to political campaigns, and we hire retired regulators as "advisors," and none of that shows up on the training slides at all. And where I grew up, you give the fella at the land office a gift when he sorts your papers out, and everyone would look at you sideways if you called that corruption — it's just how you show you're not a stranger to him.

So what I want to know is what all of this quietly assumes. When the anti-bribery world draws the line exactly where it does — the envelope to the clerk is corruption, the golf trip and the campaign cheque and the revolving door aren't — what does it have to believe about what corruption actually is? That it's always a public official plus a private payment? That it needs a specific intent, a this-for-that? That it's measurable in money? Does it secretly need a clean line between the state and the market, and between a gift and a payment, that a lot of places genuinely don't have? And which of these is it really committed to, as opposed to stuff that just happens to fit with it — like, could you keep the same enforcement setup while admitting corruption is really about informal systems of mutual obligation rather than individual bad apples? Basically I want to know where the whole thing is underdetermined, and where it actually falls apart.
