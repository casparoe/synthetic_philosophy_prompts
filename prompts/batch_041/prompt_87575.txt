I never went to college — I fix industrial control systems, PLCs and safety interlocks, that kind of thing — but I've been reading alot about self-modifying AI agents for a few years now (blog posts, and papers on arxiv I only half understand), and the guy who runs the local AI meetup talked me into giving a 20 minute talk there next month.

Here's what I want to argue. Everyone says a smart agent can't fully trust it's own reasoning, because of Löb's theorem — if it could prove that everything it proves is true, then it collapses and can prove anything, so either it's inconsistent or the trust has to be an axiom it takes on faith. I'm probably mangling the statement, I know. But my point is that people treat this as a fact about logic when it's really a fact about self-reference in general, and my day job is full of it. Our safety system has to be certified, the certification is done by a lab, the lab is accredited by a body, that body is recognised by someone else, and eventually it just bottoms out in somebody that nobody checks. Nobody thinks the plant is unsafe because the chain of certification ends there. It ends in something dumb enough that it doesn't need to audit itself.

So my claim is: split the agent. One part is a stupid proof checker — it checks proofs, it never reasons about itself, it can't rewrite itself. The other part is clever, and it's allowed to trust the checker. Then the clever part can trust its own conclusions, not because it proved itself sound but because everything it concludes gets checked by something it can't touch and that never has to trust anything. I think that gets you a self-trusting agent, and I think the obstacle as usually stated only bites if you insist the whole agent be one single system that has to certify its own certifier.

The room, roughly: two guys who train models for a living and think anything proof-based is a fantasy; a logic PhD student who comes for the free pizza and will probably tear me apart on the theorem itself; a woman who does forecasting and always says just put a probability on your own reliability and update; and the organiser, who thinks this is all basically Gödel and will ask something slightly off. Not a LessWrong crowd at all — half of them had never heard of the obstacle until I mentioned it.
