I'm setting the final for an upper-level seminar called Judgment, Evidence and Humility — mixed philosophy and cog-sci students, mostly third and fourth years. The last unit is the inside view vs. the outside view: planning-fallacy stuff, reference-class forecasting, and the way the disagreement literature tells you to take the outside view on your own opinions instead of trusting your gut. I need one essay question, 20 marks, about 45 minutes of writing.

The problem is that every practice answer I've marked is the same two paragraphs: define the planning fallacy, say the outside view fixes it, mention that you should ask how long similar projects took, done. I want something that punishes that. The crux I want them stuck on is whether the outside view is actually an epistemic virtue or just a way of not being the one who's wrong. Specifically I want them to have to deal with this: two analysts, both sincere, both deliberately take the outside view, both pick a reference class in good faith, and they land a factor of three apart — one says the trial finishes in a year because trials of this size take about that long, the other says eighteen months because trials run by this particular consortium take about that. Are they both being modest? Can modesty and accuracy come apart here? And what does that do to the claim that taking the outside view is the modest thing to do?

Second thing they have to confront: whether the outside view is even one thing. The base-rate version (here's the distribution for the class, take the mean) and the deference version (people who know about this think X, so lean X) get run together constantly and I don't think they're the same — one is about frequencies, the other is about whose judgment you're trusting, and only one of them is really about your own reliability. Then the self-application twist: I should take the outside view on my own forecasts is itself a claim made from the inside view, and I want to see whether they notice the circle and what they do with it.

Also make them say where the outside view should *not* be used — I want at least one case where the modest move is to trust your own read of the situation rather than the base rate, and I want them to say what makes the difference, without it collapsing into be modest except when you're right.

There's a case in the reading pack about a transport agency that now slaps a flat 40% on every cost estimate on the grounds that projects like ours always run over. Tell them they can use that, or one of the two analyst cases above, as the concrete thing to argue about. Don't lean on the class materials too hard though, most of the exam has to be answerable by someone who's only done the reading.

Don't explain the inside/outside view to me — give me the exam question itself, then a model answer of around 700 words at the level I should expect from a strong student, then a marking scheme. Build the scheme around five must-hit points totalling 20 marks, and for each point give me a one-liner on how students typically blow it. And flag which one or two of the points actually seperate an A from a solid B, because I have to justify the grade boundaries to the external examiner and last year she pushed back on my marks.
