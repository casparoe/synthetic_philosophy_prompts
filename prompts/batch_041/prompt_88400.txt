I am grading the midterm examination for an upper-level undergraduate course on the ethics of war, and I would like a second opinion on one script before I return it to the student. The question and the student's answer are reproduced below.

Question (20 points). The law of armed conflict imposes the same rules on all combatants, whatever the justice of the cause for which they fight: soldiers on both sides may kill one another, and neither side's soldiers may be prosecuted merely for having fought. Some philosophers hold that this legal symmetry rests on a deeper moral symmetry between combatants; others deny it. (a) State the doctrine of the moral equality of combatants as precisely as you can, distinguishing what it asserts about what combatants are permitted to do from what it asserts about what may be done to them. (b) Set out the principal objection to the doctrine. (c) If that objection is sound, does it follow that soldiers who have fought for an unjust cause are blameworthy for what they have done? (6 + 6 + 8 = 20)

Student's answer.

(a) The moral equality of combatants is the principle that the law of war applies in the same way to everyone who fights. A soldier who obeys the rules of war commits no crime by killing enemy soldiers, whoever he is fighting for, and his enemies are just as entitled to kill him. The doctrine has two sides, namely what a combatant is permitted to do and what may be done to him, but these are two sides of the same coin: if both sides are permitted to kill one another, then neither side's soldiers can complain of being killed, so the permissions and the liabilities stand or fall together.

(b) Walzer defends the doctrine by separating the justice of the war from the justice of the fighting. Whether a war ought to be fought is one question (jus ad bellum); how it is fought is another (jus in bello). A soldier's cause may be unjust without its following that everything he does is unjust. The war convention is the same for both sides, and it would be unworkable if it had to be applied differently according to which side was in the right. Soldiers are also not in a position to judge whether their state's cause is just, and cannot be expected to refuse to fight on that ground; the responsibility for an unjust war belongs to the leaders who chose it rather than to those who were sent. The law of armed conflict embodies exactly this: it is neutral between the parties, it protects prisoners of war and civilians on both sides, and it does not ask who began it. The moral equality of combatants is thus the legal equality of combatants, and the argument for it is that a law of war which took sides could not do its job.

McMahan's revisionism denies all of this. For McMahan, combatants are not exempt from the ad bellum question; it applies to them and not only to their leaders. It follows that soldiers who fight for an unjust cause act wrongly when they kill soldiers on the just side; and since an unjustified killing of a human being is murder, their killings are murders. The revisionist view therefore makes the moral position of the two sides asymmetric, one side's killing being justified and the other's not, and this is what it is to reject the moral equality of combatants.

(c) The revisionist challenge is powerful, but I do not think it can be accepted in its full form. If unjust combatants are murderers, then they are blameworthy for what they have done and could be punished for it. That is a very hard conclusion to accept: ordinary soldiers are usually ignorant of the facts and are under pressure from their state and from their comrades, and it seems wrong to make them answer for a decision in which they had no part. The traditional doctrine avoids this by holding soldiers responsible only for how they fight and not for the war itself, and that seems to me the better view.

End of answer.
