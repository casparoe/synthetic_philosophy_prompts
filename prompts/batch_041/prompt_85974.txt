For the past two years I have sat on a fellowship selection committee, and I have come to think that the way we compare applicants is not merely difficult but oddly shaped. Our procedure is pairwise: we take two files, discuss them, and decide which is stronger. In any single comparison the committee is usually confident — this candidate's research record is clearly stronger, that one's teaching is clearly stronger, and on balance we can say which file we prefer. The trouble shows up only afterward, when we try to put the decisions together. We have repeatedly produced sets of verdicts that cannot be made to sit still: candidate A is preferred to B, B to C, and yet when A and C are placed side by side, C is preferred to A. Each of the three comparisons, taken by itself, seems as solid as any judgment we make; together they are incoherent. I do not believe we are simply changing our minds, and I do not think this is the familiar spectacle of a group contradicting itself through voting, because the same thing happens when I do the ranking alone, late at night, with no one to argue with. Nor do I think it is just the usual difficulty of weighing several criteria at once, since the candidates differ by degrees along the same few dimensions rather than by kind, and it is the small differences that seem to be doing the damage. A colleague insists we are being sloppy and that a written rubric would dissolve the problem; we wrote one, and it did not.

The second thing I have no word for is a relation that turns up in the same files. Every so often two candidates strike me as genuinely balanced: I do not think either is better, and I do not think they are equally good either. When I try to settle the matter by imagining a small, unambiguous improvement to one of them — a better teaching award, say, or one additional publication in a good journal — my verdict does not budge: the improved candidate still does not strike me as better than the other. That last fact is what I find most puzzling, since I would have expected any genuine improvement to be decisive. Either I am misjudging these cases, or "better" is not the only relation I am relying on and I simply lack the word for the other one.

What I would like from you is not a label but an analysis. Is there an established name for either pattern, and if there is no settled name for one of them, please coin one and defend its boundaries rather than handing me the nearest familiar term without saying where it stops fitting. In particular: what exactly is the relation between two things when neither is better, neither is worse, and they are not equal, and how does it differ from my simply being unable to tell which is better, and from the two items not belonging on the same scale at all? Are the looping verdicts in my first case and the balanced pairs in my second one phenomenon or two, and does the one explain the other? What should I call the argumentative move in which I try to break a tie by making a small improvement and find that the improvement fails to break it — and is that move sound, or does it quietly assume what it is trying to prove? Please write your answer in American English.
