I spent thirty-five years as a taxonomist before retiring, and I have spent the last three years sitting in on the philosophy department's senior seminar. The arguments I find hardest to hear are the ones conducted as though the categories were given, so I want to build one session where the students have to do the classifying themselves. I have ninety minutes with about sixteen upper-level students, half from philosophy and half from biology, and what I want them to run into — before anyone names it — is the problem that the things we call pleasures appear to share no diagnostic character, together with the discovery that every cheap way of restoring one costs something. (I gather the problem has a name, the heterogeneity problem; the students should not hear it until the debrief.)

Design the session, replacing my sketch if you can do better. My sketch: give each student four or five cards describing a pleasure episode, have them circulate trying to find, from the cards alone, the one other person whose card is the same kind of pleasure as their own; plant exactly one genuine pair, so that the room converges on a hypothesis, relief perhaps, that the remainder of the deck destroys. Then offer a small symbolic prize, no money, to any group that can state a single character covering every card, knowing that the only ways to win involve characters like this is one I would choose again. Tell me the spread of cases I need to write onto the deck, the rules, the materials, the win condition, and a timeline; the debrief should map each moment onto at least three positions a philosopher might take about what pleasure is, including one the students will find unsatisfying, and show what each costs. Say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about what the activity cannot show — in particular, that it establishes only the failure of a common felt character, not the absence of any common property at all; I will have to supply that step myself, so tell me how to supply it without giving away the game.
