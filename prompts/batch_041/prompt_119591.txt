My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the procreation asymmetry — the idea that it can be wrong to bring into existence a person whose life will go badly, while no one is obliged to bring into existence a person whose life will go well — and we kept discovering that we were asking different things of any account that would explain it. I would like the specification written out properly, because I suspect the real disagreement is about which demands are legitimate and which are merely habits of one camp. The purpose I have in mind is an account solid enough to be used in arguments about reproductive decisions and population policy, so adequacy should be judged against that.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of the asymmetry must deliver. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it; say whether it is non-negotiable or only a preference of one camp, and which camp (for instance, those who locate all value in the welfare of people who exist or will exist regardless, as against those who rank outcomes by the welfare of whoever happens to exist in them); and say which other requirements it sits awkwardly with. Where some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, say so explicitly and show the conflict — that is the part I care about most, and I would rather have the conflict than a tidy list that hides it.

To keep this from being a recital of the familiar positions, I want the list stress-tested against some cases my friend and I invented, and I want each treated as a first-class requirement if it deserves to be:

- The two vantages. From the inside, a life can be worth living even when it was not worth starting; from the outside, before anyone exists, there is no one whose interests are at stake. An account must say which vantage the asymmetry is grounded in, and whether it returns the same verdict from both. Let us call these the insider and the outsider vantage, and make it a requirement that an account declare which one it is using and why the other is the wrong one to use.
- The transposition test. If the asymmetry rests on the badness of suffering or the goodness of enjoyment, the same structure should show up wherever those show up, not only at the moment of creation. So an account must either show that the asymmetry carries over to extending, rescuing, or restoring lives, or explain what is special about bringing someone into existence. A restriction that applies only to the moment of creation should be flagged as ad hoc unless it is defended.
- The zero point. Suppose a couple could create a person whose life would sit exactly at the level that makes a life worth living and no higher. Is that option ranked above, below, or level with creating no one? Whatever the account says, it must say it determinately, and must not leave the answer to the couple's preferences.
- The substituted sibling. A choice or policy that fixes which person comes to exist rather than how well off any particular person is. The account must say what, if anything, is wrong there, and must not quietly assume that the same person exists in both branches.
- The reversal. A person who already exists and suffers, and who would not choose nonexistence for themselves, set against the same quantity of suffering in someone who does not yet exist. The account must either treat these alike or give a principled reason for treating them differently.

Then, briefly, mark which single requirement does the most to eliminate candidate accounts, and say which candidates it eliminates.

I do not want a bibliography or a reading list, and please do not rest the answer on who said what; describe positions by their content. If you name a position, keep it to ones any reader would recognise, and do not attribute particular claims to particular authors.

Give the whole answer in American English. Present the desiderata as a table with these columns: Requirement (short name); What it demands; Motivating case or consideration; Status (non-negotiable, or camp-specific and which camp); Tension with (which other requirements, if any). Follow it with a short section on any subset of requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, and a short paragraph on the most eliminative requirement. The table should be the bulk of the answer.
