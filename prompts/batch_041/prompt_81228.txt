Write a customer-complaint response letter, roughly 500–600 words, in the flat, slightly over-reassuring register of a corporate service department. The company does not decide anything for its clients: it specifies a procedure, taking a client's own values and asking what that client's fully informed, fully rational, unpressured and temporally coherent counterpart would endorse, then acting — and billing — accordingly. The letter answers a client who objects that she never approved the charge. The humour has to come from the position itself, so the signatory should not be made to look stupid: every concession he makes must be one the position genuinely owes, and every reassurance must be the very thing that incriminates the procedure. In the blandest available language, let him concede that the idealization parameters were set by the company and could reasonably have been set otherwise; that a rival firm with different settings would have billed her differently for the same life; that on some accounts the counterpart is a different person from the client; that the counterpart's endorsement is consequently no evidence that the client endorses anything; and that even the counterpart's verdicts may not be determinate, since a counterpart given more imagination or a different time horizon may approve of different things — while insisting throughout that only a procedure of this kind could have authority over her at all. Include a short inset schedule of the company's Idealization Parameters (five or six entries: information, imagination, time preference, affective continuity, and so on), and close with an offer to escalate the matter to the Idealization Review Board, which should read as the dead end it is. Do not let the letter settle whether the idealized verdict binds the actual client.
