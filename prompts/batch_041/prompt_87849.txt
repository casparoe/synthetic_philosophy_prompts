The question was: "Explain Williams's distinction between internal and external reasons. Does his argument succeed in showing that all reasons for action are internal?" Give me a letter grade, but what I really need is the point-by-point comments I can write in the margins — where it's right, where it slips, and how much the slip should cost. Concrete over general, I've got thirty more of these tonight.

Here's the answer:

Williams's distinction is between internal and external reasons. An internal reason depends on something already in the agent's subjective motivational set — he abbreviates this as S — so whether you have a reason to do something depends on what you care about, want, or are committed to. An external reason is supposed to apply to you no matter what's in your S: the claim that the cruel man has a reason to be kind, full stop, even if nothing in his psychology responds to that.

Williams's main argument is that reason statements have to be able to explain action. If we say someone has a reason to do something and nothing in his S gives him any traction, then the claim can't explain why he acts or why he'd even be moved to act, so the external reason statement is doing no explanatory work. He concludes that all reasons are internal. This is basically Hume's point that reason alone is inert and motivation has to come from desire. On Williams's picture S is just the set of desires you happen to have right now, so whether you have a reason to do anything is contingent on your psychology.

He does allow that deliberation can add to S, but the deliberative route only rearranges what's already in there, so you can't get a reason out of it that you didn't already have. That's why he thinks external reason claims are always false. It also means that if Williams is right, moral criticism is basically just a way of manipulating people — you can't tell someone their values are wrong, you can only work with what they already want.

I think the argument is pretty strong, though Parfit pushes back. Parfit says there are object-given reasons and uses the example that anyone has a reason to avoid their own agony, which isn't supposed to depend on what you happen to want. I'm not sure that really gets Williams, since the fact that you want to avoid agony is still something in your S.
