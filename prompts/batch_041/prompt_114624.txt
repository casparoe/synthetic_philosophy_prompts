Two friends of mine got into an arguement about whether you can even talk about an AI "avoiding side effects" without first having a full picture of what we actually value, and I couldn't tell who was making sense. Sam said it's hopeless: calling something a side effect already means it wasn't the point of the action, and knowing what "the point" was is knowing what's valuable, so a so-called low-impact agent secretly has the whole value function smuggled in from the start. Robin said no, you can do it structurally -- just measure how much an action shrinks the set of futures still reachable from where you are, or how much it irreversibly locks things in, without ever saying which future is good; the agent's like someone playing a board game who's careful not to knock the pieces on the floor. Sam shot back that "reachable" and "irreversible" are doing exactly the same sneaky work -- deciding which features of the world even count as relevant, and treating keeping-options-open as itself the thing worth protecting, is a value choice, just a hidden one. Robin said every measure has knobs and that's different from baking in a full theory of the good; a speed limit isn't a moral theory either. Sam said fine, but then picture a robot told to get you coffee that won't shove a chair out of the way because moving it "changes the world" -- wherever it draws the line between what it's allowed to disturb and what it isn't is a value line, and you can't get that line just by being conservative. Robin said irreversibility draws it for you: knocking over the vase is permanent, sliding the chair isn't. Sam said that's not neutral either, since the best thing you could do is often a permanent change (curing something, say), so "avoid the irreversible" just quietly ranks some goals above others. Can you go through this and say who's right about what at each step, and what both of them are seperate-ly missing? Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro or wrap-up.
