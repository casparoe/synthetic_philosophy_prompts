I cover economics and the history of ideas, and there is one dispute I keep circling without ever getting a straight answer to the obvious question: why does it never end?

The dispute is over Malthus. On one side are people who treat the Malthusian prediction -- that population growth presses against a finite resource base and drives living standards down toward subsistence -- as a landmark case of a theory that was simply wrong: the catastrophe was predicted, it did not arrive, and two centuries of rising population alongside rising living standards is the refutation. On the other side are people who say the prediction was not refuted but deferred or displaced -- that it holds wherever technology, trade, and institutions are held fixed, that it describes the pre-industrial regime accurately and still governs the parts of the world that have not escaped it, and that our own escape is itself a precarious exception rather than a disproof. Both camps know the same history. The famous wager between Julian Simon and Paul Ehrlich -- the 1980 bet over whether the inflation-adjusted prices of five metals would be lower by 1990, which Simon won -- gets cited by each side as evidence for its own reading, which is itself a clue that something other than the facts is doing the work.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict on Malthus. Specifically: is this a substantive disagreement or a verbal one? Is it two groups answering different questions under one name -- one asking whether a mechanism exists (diminishing returns to a fixed factor), the other whether a particular historical trajectory materialized? Is it a disagreement about the standard of evidence -- point prediction versus directional trend, physical scarcity versus price, global versus local, this century versus the long run? Or is it a genuine empirical dispute in which the two sides are simply reading a large, noisy, non-replicable sample differently, with no experiment able to settle it?

I have a working hypothesis I would like you to test rather than assume. It is that the impasse is built into the structure of the theory itself: a Malthusian prediction is a conditional -- if the resource base is fixed and the response is unchanged, living standards fall -- and every escape from it works by defeating one of the conditions, which means the theory can be rescued from any refutation by pointing to the condition that changed, and can be reaffirmed against any period of improvement by pointing to the conditions that made the improvement possible. If that is right, then the phrase "right but early" is not a middle position between vindication and refutation but a category error, and the real question becomes what could count as a test of a conditional whose antecedent keeps getting falsified. I also want you to weigh whether the prediction has been self-undermining in a way that complicates the whole ledger -- whether Malthus's warning, by being believed, altered the very behavior that would have confirmed it.

Then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually move this dispute: a distinction, a formal result, a reframing of the question, a change in what we treat as the object being tested -- or nothing at all, because this is one of those disagreements that cannot be resolved and should not be expected to be. Be explicit about your own confidence, and tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail: what you would expect to see, or what argument you could imagine, that would push you toward a different diagnosis than the one you give.
