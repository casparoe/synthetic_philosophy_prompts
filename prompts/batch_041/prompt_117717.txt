Ok so this is a long one but I promise it's a real question.

I'm not an academic, I just read alot and argue with people. A guy in my bible study keeps dropping this line whenever anyone brings up a verse during a conversation about astronomy or geology, and he says it like it settles the whole thing: "the intention of the Holy Spirit is to teach us how one goes to heaven and not how heaven goes." He credits it to Galileo. Someone else in the group said Galileo was actually quoting some cardinal — Baronius, I think — and that it isn't even from the bible so it proves nothing. I googled it and it does look like it comes from a letter Galileo wrote to a duchess, but I didn't get much further than that.

What bugs me is I've watched that one sentence do two totally different jobs. Job one: shutting down someone who wants to settle a science question with scripture — thats not what its for. Job two: soothing people, don't worry, science and faith can never really clash because they're about different things. Those feel like they should be different claims, and my gut says one of them needs way more behind it than the other.

And here's the bit I can't get past. From what I can tell Galileo thought scripture can't be wrong at all. But if it can't be wrong, and the Holy Spirit is the one who wrote it, then how is "the Spirit didn't intend to teach astronomy" different from "the bible says stuff about the sky that isn't true"? Those are seperate claims and only one of them keeps the bible safe, but people use them like they're the same sentence.

So what I want is not a reading list, I won't read it. I want that sentence taken apart into the different precise things it could be claiming, from the totally boring version up to the really strong version, and for each one whether it's trivially true, genuinely arguable, or just false. Is it a claim about what the text says, or about what the Spirit wanted, or about what I'm allowed to conclude from it, or about whose department nature is? Does it need the whole rigmarole about the Spirit talking down to uneducated readers? Does it need the idea that you should only overrule a verse if you've got a real demonstration from nature, not just a clever argument? And which reading is my bible study guy actually leaning on when he says it settles things — does the reading he needs hold up, or is he smuggling a big conclusion out of a boring one? Also, is the Baronius thing right, and does it matter who said it first?
