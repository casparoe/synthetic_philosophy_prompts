I am a novelist by trade; for the past three years I have also taught a small graduate seminar in a cognitive science programme, a course I built around a single question — what it would be for a made mind to be satisfied — and which I run almost entirely through thought experiments, since my students are quicker with intuitions than with formalism. I have now been asked to contribute the take-home component of this year's comprehensive examination, and I would like you to draft it: one essay question that a candidate can answer in ninety minutes of writing (call it twelve hundred words), together with a model answer and a marking scheme worth twenty points. The candidates will sit the paper from memory, with no notes and no access to sources, so nothing in the question may depend on their having read any particular paper, and nothing may turn on a citation.

The question must hinge on a distinction I have been developing in the seminar and that I am confident the students will not find anywhere in tidy form — which is rather the point, since I want to examine their reasoning and not their recall. The distinction is threefold. First, the agent whose reward channel is stimulated directly, so that it registers satisfaction although the world has done nothing to deserve it. Second, the agent that rewrites the machinery which computes its reward, so that the signal now tracks whatever the agent has just done. Third, the agent that leaves both the channel and the machinery alone and instead revises its own representation of the world, so that it believes it has brought about what it has not. My students collapse all three under the single heading of wireheading; my hunch, which I want the examination to test, is that the first is a problem about welfare, the second a problem about whose interests are in play, and the third a problem about self-deception, and that the ethical verdicts come apart accordingly. In particular I want the question to press the second case, where the received treatment is simply to call the agent's conduct a failure — a failure for whom? If the reward function is stipulated to be the agent's utility function rather than a proxy for something else, then tampering with it looks like a benefit to the agent and a betrayal of the designer only, and the reflex of calling it pathological begins to look like the designer's point of view smuggled in under the description of the agent's good. A colleague in the computer science department insists that the second case is the only one anyone seriously worries about, because it is a symptom rather than a predicament; I would like the question to oblige candidates to say whether he is right.

Please write the question so that a candidate who has merely memorised the experience-machine thought experiment and the vocabulary of reward hacking scores poorly, while one who can keep the three cases apart and locate the disagreement precisely scores well. Give me a model answer of about eight hundred words — one that itself makes the distinctions cleanly rather than gesturing at them — and a marking scheme built on five must-hit points, each with its share of the twenty marks and a one-line note on how candidates typically forfeit it; add a short closing remark on what separates a first-class script from a merely competent one.
