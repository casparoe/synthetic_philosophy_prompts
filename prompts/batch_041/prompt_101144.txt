I've been chewing on this for weeks and I'd rather you stress-test my view than nod along with it.

Here's the position. What makes an influence manipulative rather than legitimate persuasion is that it bypasses or subverts the other person's rational agency, whereas persuasion engages it. Take B as the person being influenced. If the influence works on B's capacities for assessing reasons — giving her considerations, arguments, evidence, even a well-aimed emotional appeal that she can take up and weigh — that's persuasion. If it sneaks past those capacities (subliminal cues, playing on biases below the threshold of awareness, engineering the situation so she never notices there was a choice, wearing her down until she complies), that's manipulation. The moral difference tracks that: manipulation wrongs B by treating her as a mechanism to be triggered rather than an agent to be addressed.

So: what would count against this? I don't want the stock objections, I want the cases and arguments that should actually move someone who holds it, ranked from "this pretty much kills it" down to "annoying but survivable". Some things I've been turning over that I'd like you to address head-on:

- Gaslighting and other reason-engaging manipulations. The manipulator here works *through* B's rational capacities — offering arguments, demanding justification, making her doubt her own memory — but the whole point is to corrode her agency rather than respect it. Does that break the view, or can I save it by saying the capacities are engaged only in order to be disabled?
- The reverse case: B accepts a genuinely good argument, but only because of an irrelevant social cue — the speaker was confident, or high-status, or the room was on their side. Rational capacities "engaged", yet it smells like manipulation. Which way does that cut?
- What I've been calling the slow-narrowing case: no single act bypasses anything, every step is a consideration B could weigh, but over months someone selects and times those considerations so that her deliberative landscape is quietly pruned, and she ends up somewhere she'd have rejected if she'd seen the whole sequence at once. Engaged or bypassed? And if the answer is "both", what's left of the distinction?
- Cases where bypassing seems fine or even respectful — a nudge she later endorses, an arrangement she pre-committed to, the defaults we set for people who've asked us to. If bypassing isn't sufficient for wrongness, is the view doing the work I want it to?
- The rival that says the mark of manipulation isn't the mechanism at all but the attitude it expresses: using someone, treating them as a means, being willing to distort their will. If that explains everything my bypassing story explains *plus* the cases above, should I just concede and switch? Or is there something it can't get?

I'd also like you to tell me honestly whether the view has been quietly qualified into unfalsifiability — every counterexample absorbed by bolting on "unless she'd endorse it" or "unless it's for her own good" — and if so, whether that's actually a problem for a view of this kind or just what any decent moral view ends up looking like. Finish by saying which single case, if it turned out to be real, would most make you abandon the position, and which of my objections a defender should just concede rather than fight.
