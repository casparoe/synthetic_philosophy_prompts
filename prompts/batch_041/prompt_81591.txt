I'm building a small anthology of stories that dramatise philosophy-of-mind ideas instead of explaining them, and I need one built around the intentional stance — Dennett's line that calling something a believer is a question of whether treating it as one lets you predict it, not a question about what's going on inside it. Here's the setup I want; change the trimmings if you like but keep the teeth. A small team inside a public body has built a claims-triage system, and a inquiry has to decide whether it "believed" a claim was fraudulent. The wrinkle: every time the team leans on belief-talk their predictions get worse than the dull design-stance ones (what was it built to do, what's it optimised for) — except in one recurring kind of case where only belief-talk works, and nobody can find where the rule governing it came from. It behaves as if following a policy no one wrote down and, as far as anyone can tell, no one ever thought. I want the story to end without settling whether the thing believes anything.

Couple of rules so it doesn't turn into an essay: no scenes from inside the machine's point of view, no character explaining Dennett to another character, and don't let anyone win the argument cleanly. A few hundred words is plenty, and please write it in British English (realise, behaviour, that sort of thing).
