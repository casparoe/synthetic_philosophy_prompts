Since retiring from a career in microbiology I have been reading philosophy of biology, and I have a request of a rather specific kind. I do not want a survey of the literature on the definition of life; I want a single, fully worked example of a conceptual issue, constructed to order.

Let me say what I mean by a conceptual issue, because I have grown impatient with the way this topic is usually presented. The interesting thing about the question of what life is, it seems to me, is not that we lack a definition but that we possess several, each well motivated, each doing real work in a different context. I have four in mind, which I will state as criteria:

1. Metabolic: a bounded system that maintains itself far from thermodynamic equilibrium by continuously taking in energy and matter and exporting entropy.
2. Evolutionary: an entity that is a member of a lineage capable of open-ended evolution, that is, one in which variation, heritability, and differential reproduction are all in play.
3. Autopoietic: a network of processes that continuously regenerates the very components of which it is made, including the boundary that separates it from its surroundings.
4. A fourth criterion of my own devising, offered for the sake of the exercise: a system that (a) actively maintains a boundary, (b) carries a specification, internal to itself, of how that boundary is to be maintained, and (c) persists indefinitely without ever reproducing.

What I would like from you is one example, the single best case you can construct, of an entity, process, or system about which these four criteria do not agree. I would prefer a case that separates them four ways if such a case can be found; if it cannot, give me the closest thing to it and tell me what blocks the full separation. Please do not offer me viruses, prions, mules, mitochondria, or fire. I have seen all of them, and I want something that forces me to reason rather than to remember.

Once the case is on the table, tell me what kind of issue it is. Specifically: is the disagreement among the four criteria merely verbal, so that once we see what each criterion is for, the conflict evaporates? Or is it a genuine indeterminacy in the concept, of the sort a borderline case in the Sorites sense exhibits? Or is it something else again, namely a case in which the criteria are not rivals at all because they answer different questions, so that asking which one gets it right is a category mistake? Tell me which diagnosis fits the case you have given me, and what would have to be true for a different one to be the correct diagnosis.

Finally, tell me whether the choice among the criteria can legitimately be made to depend on the purpose for which the answer is needed. My own experience is with biosafety classification and planetary-protection protocols, where someone has to decide whether a thing counts as life before the philosophical question has been settled. If the criteria come apart, does that make the protocol arbitrary, or is there a principled way to select the criterion by the use to which it will be put? I am genuinely unsure whether that last thought rescues the concept or merely changes the subject, and I would rather be told which.
