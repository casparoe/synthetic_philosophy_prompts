I am preparing the handout for the second meeting of a graduate seminar on ambiguity and imprecise probability, and I would like the handout to be a decision tree rather than a list of positions. The purpose is diagnostic: each participant should locate herself by answering questions about cases rather than by picking the label she already likes, and by the end of the session we should be able to see which disagreements are substantive and which are terminological.

The tree begins from a commitment I expect everyone in the room to share: a rational agent's doxastic state is not always adequately represented by a single sharp credence function. From that starting point, please build a tree with six branching levels and between twelve and sixteen leaves. Every node must be a question about a concrete case, phrased so that a respondent can answer it without knowing which position the answer leads to; no node may ask whether one endorses a named theory. Every leaf must carry the name of a position where a standard name exists and be marked unnamed where it does not, together with one sentence stating the commitment that would embarrass its holder most.

I want the tree organized around three questions, kept apart because I think the literature runs them together: first, what the imprecision is a fact about — the agent's state, her evidence, the world, or the semantics of credence ascriptions; second, what imprecision requires of action — sharpen before choosing, choose by a rule that consumes the whole set, or hold that the theory underdetermines the choice; third, what an imprecise credence ascription reports — a determinate interval-valued state, an indeterminate one, a set of acceptable odds, or a refusal to commit. I suspect the third question constrains the first two more than anyone admits, and I would like the tree to expose that dependence if it is there.

These cases must appear as nodes:

- Two investigators with exactly the same evidence assign 0.4 and 0.6 to the same hypothesis, and neither has overlooked anything. Is one of them mistaken?
- A coin is either fair or double-headed. You will bet on it once, and the matter will never be resolved, because no further evidence can arrive. Is a credence of 0.5 an error, a permissible summary, or the wrong kind of state to be in?
- You are imprecise about an event, with credence between 0.3 and 0.7, and you are offered a bet, which you may decline, that pays two if the event occurs and costs one if it does not. Is accepting permitted, required, or forbidden? Then a variant in which the worst end of the interval is a catastrophe.
- One agent's interval is [0.49, 0.51] and another's, about the same sort of event, is [0.1, 0.9]. Does your theory treat these as the same state of mind at different resolutions or as different states of mind, and does anything in it turn on the width of an interval, or is imprecision simply the difference between sharp and unsharp?
- One of your intervals would narrow if you bought more data; a second reflects two experts who contradict each other; a third arises because the predicate in the hypothesis is vague. Does your theory give the same advice in all three cases, and if not, which of the three does it treat as central?
- You are imprecise about an event and then learn the outcome of an event you judge to have no bearing on it. May your credence in the first become wider than it was?
- An agent cannot say whether her credence in an event is the interval [0.4, 0.6] or the interval [0.35, 0.65]. Is that a coherent state, and does the regress of imprecision about imprecision terminate anywhere?
- A rule licenses each of two choices in sequence, and the pair leaves the agent foreseeably worse off than another pair the same rule would also have licensed. Is this a reductio of the rule, a reason to add a diachronic constraint, or no objection at all?

Among the leaves I need positions that: treat imprecision as a defect to be repaired; permit any sharpening whatever; identify the credal state with a convex set of acceptable betting rates; select actions by the rule that an option maximizes expected utility for some admissible credence function; select actions by maximality; select actions by worst-case or risk-weighted criteria over the whole interval; take imprecision to be a primitive of preference rather than of belief; take it to be an objective feature of evidence rather than of the agent; take it to be an indeterminate doxastic state about which there is no fact of the matter; and take credence talk to be too coarse to express imprecision at all. The choice-rule node must separate the two rules that agree on binary choices but diverge once three or more options are in play; if your tree cannot separate them, say so. I also want a leaf, which I suspect is unoccupied, for the view that imprecision is first and foremost a fact about what may be asserted or publicly committed to, with the representational and decision-theoretic questions downstream of it; name it if a view of that shape already has a name, and mark it unnamed otherwise.

Finally, note at least two combinations of answers that no one occupies, and say of each whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because it has simply not been explored; and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, distinguishing failures that are behavioural — the two positions choose alike in every case you can pose — from failures that are merely interpretive. Give me the tree as an indented outline with the surrounding explanation in prose. No citations, and do not attribute positions to individual authors.
