Had a long argument at a friends birthday dinner last week with someone who teaches metaphysics, and it's been rattling around my head ever since. Her line was that when we say something like "if I'd taken the job in Lisbon I'd be miserable by now," we're not actually talking about *me* — we're talking about some other person in another timeline who happens to be my nearest match, and there's no further fact of the matter about whether that person is literally me or just resembles me closely enough that we're allowed to talk as if. She kept saying "counterpart," I kept saying "no, it's me," and we got nowhere, which honestly is the best kind of argument to have. I want to turn it into a story and I'd like you to write it.

Here's the setup I've been chewing on. Somewhere in the near future there's a consumer device — a grey box, call it whatever you like — that lets you hold a conversation with a version of yourself from a timeline where one specific decision went the other way. The ad copy is all "meet the you who stayed." The twist I want the story built on is that the box doesn't actually give you *you*. What it gives you is the person over there who's the closest match to you, and that person can differ from you in all sorts of ways beyond the one decision — and worse, there can be more than one equally good match, and those matches needn't be good matches of *each other*. So you can be talking to two people who are each "the closest thing to you" on the other side, while they barely resemble one another. That detail is the whole reason I find this disorienting and worth a story, so please keep it load-bearing rather than decorative.

Write it as a short story, maybe 1500 words. I'd rather it live in one concrete situation than tour the idea — for instance someone deciding what they owe one of these counterparts, or whether to take its advice, or whether grief or guilt or gratitude even applies, and getting pulled up short by the fact that the two of them don't agree about who they are to each other. Nobody in the story should explain the philosophy or name any real philosophers or books; let the reader feel the snag rather than be lectured about it. Start with the story itself, no preface and no commentary afterwards.
