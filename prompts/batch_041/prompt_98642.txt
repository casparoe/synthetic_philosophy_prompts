Write the opening address that the chair of a citizens' assembly will deliver at its first meeting. The assembly, convened in the fictional town of Bellwether, must decide whether to reopen the Mill Creek Bridge. Its bylaws divide the question into three separate votes: Proposition A, that the bridge is structurally sound; Proposition B, that reopening it is worth its cost; and Proposition R, that the bridge should be reopened. The bylaws state that R is to be treated as following from A and B together and as failing if either A or B fails. On current preferences, a majority is expected to approve A, a majority is expected to approve B, and a majority is expected to reject R. The chair has decided to use a premise-first procedure: if A and B both pass, R will be declared adopted even though a direct majority on R would reject it. A faction has moved instead for conclusion-first voting, under which R would fail and A and B would be recorded as the assembly's separate findings. The chair is a retired schoolteacher, not a philosopher. She personally voted against reopening the bridge and expects the procedure to produce the opposite of her own judgment. The audience includes townspeople, a few engineers, local journalists, and assembly members who believe their direct vote on R is being overridden.

Write the chair's address, six to seven minutes long. It should sound like an occasion: address the assembly, acknowledge the town and the members' work, and carry the emotional weight of a public meeting. It must make the expected split vivid without using technical terms such as judgment aggregation or discursive dilemma and without naming or citing any author, book, law, or court case. It must explain why premise-first voting is not a trick to reach a predetermined result. It must give the strongest honest reply to the objection that the assembly is ignoring the majority's judgment on the very question it was convened to decide, and it must say why conclusion-first voting, though initially more democratic in appearance, is not the obvious remedy. It should acknowledge the chair's own opposing view and explain why she is defending the procedure anyway. It should end by asking the assembly to accept the procedure for this meeting, not by claiming that the underlying disagreement has been resolved. Do not use quotation marks.
