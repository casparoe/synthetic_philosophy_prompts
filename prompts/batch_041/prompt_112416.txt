Write a short story of under 1,000 words about the design of a picture language. Set it in a fictional post-revolutionary state whose statistical bureau has been charged with replacing written administrative prose with a universal pictorial script, built on two rules: every concept is a small, repeatable pictogram, and every quantity is shown by repeating identical pictograms in a row rather than by drawing one symbol larger or smaller, so that a citizen can read the state's numbers without a translator and without trusting anyone's word. (The obvious precedent, Otto Neurath's picture-statistics, may inform the script's rules, but the story and its world are yours.) Your protagonist is the designer now asked to extend the script into the one domain where it keeps failing: she must render a cluster of abstract and contested concepts — corruption, loyalty, the public interest, and, hardest of all, negation, the pictorial equivalent of saying that something is not the case or is simply absent. Give her one concrete rendering problem and one concrete failure, and let the story turn on conceptual difficulty rather than on politics or romance: whether a picture can assert rather than merely resemble; whether showing a thing's absence amounts to denying it; whether a script's demand to be free of convention is itself a convention; and whether a language meant to be read identically by everyone can survive concepts whose whole function is to be disputed. Do not let her solve the impossible; let the ending be honest. Invent the state, its history, and its people as you like, and give the story a title.
