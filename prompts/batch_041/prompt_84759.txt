I am a novelist, and the book I am at work on turns on an argument between two characters who work on the safety of machine-learning systems. One of them, the director of a safety team, keeps returning to the claim that her field should take its standards from engineering rather than from machine learning: engineers design to the worst case, machine learners to the average case. A bridge, she says, is not certified for the traffic it expects but for the traffic it can survive. From this she concludes that alignment should be pursued through worst-case guarantees rather than benchmark performance. The other character, who has spent a decade on empirical robustness, replies that the analogy flatters the safety team and that engineering's worst case is a far narrower and more domesticated thing than they suppose. I want their exchange to be one that practitioners would recognise as the actual disagreement, and I want to know which of them is being carried by the analogy and which by the facts.

Would you audit the analogy for me? Lay out the correspondences explicitly — what plays the role of the load, the design envelope, the material, the safety factor, the code committee, the certifying authority, the record of past failures — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in what the analogy quietly imports: a bounded and physical load space; a failure taxonomy maintained by an institution; an artifact that does not optimise against the process that certifies it; stakes that are grave but not civilisational. I have also read enough to suspect that engineering's worst case is itself a statistical quantity — a quantile fixed by a code rather than a maximum over every conceivable load — and if that is right, the analogy may cut against the character who is wielding it. I would like that suspicion either confirmed or corrected.

Tell me, finally, whether the conclusion she draws survives the disanalogies, or whether the analogy was doing the work all along. If a better comparison is available, give it to me; if the honest verdict is that the engineering model transfers only at the level of institutions — margins, containment, incident reporting, certification — and not at the level of mathematics, I would rather hear it plainly.

I do not need citations or a reading list; I will do that work myself. What I need is the audit and a verdict, written so that I can put it in front of a colleague who knows the field and be told where I have got it wrong.
