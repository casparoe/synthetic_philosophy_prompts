Ok so last night my friend Priya and I got into a two-hour argument about the discursive dilemma — she kept insisting it's basically an artifact of bare majority rule and that no sane committee would ever fall into it, and I kept saying she was missing the point, and we never resolved it. Now I have to write the midterm for my upper-level democratic theory course and I want the essay question to be built around exactly that fight. Here's the profile I want to use, so nobody can just recite the three-judge example everyone's seen.

Five members of a tenure committee vote yes/no on three propositions:
R: the candidate's research is strong enough for tenure
T: the candidate's teaching is strong enough for tenure
G: the candidate should be granted tenure

The department's written standard is that tenure is granted iff the candidate is strong on both research and teaching, i.e. G iff (R and T).

The votes: M1 R Y, T Y, G Y. M2 R Y, T Y, G Y. M3 R Y, T N, G N. M4 R N, T Y, G N. M5 R Y, T N, G N.

I want the question to make them (a) work out what issue-by-issue majority rule gives here and why the result is inconsistent even though every single member is perfectly consistent; (b) evaluate Priya's two proposed fixes — raise the threshold on all three propositions to two-thirds, or just put the bottom line G to one up-or-down vote and skip the premises — saying what each fix does on this exact profile and, more importantly, whether either one escapes the problem in general; and (c) say what all this shows about whether the dilemma is really about the 50% cutoff or about something structural in aggregating judgments over a connected agenda. Please give me the exam question itself, a model answer of about 550-650 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves an A answer has to contain that a B answer can get away without, plus a one-line note on how students typically screw up each part. Dont let them get away with naming the paradox, drawing a little table, and calling it a day.
