I've been kicking around a short essay on the Jantelagen / tall poppy / American Dream cluster, and I want to know where it's weak before I post it. Here's the draft — please be harsh.

Title: Who Actually Cuts Down the Tall Poppies

Everyone files the Law of Jante and the tall poppy syndrome under the same heading: small communities punishing anyone who rises above the crowd. And everyone files the American Dream under the opposite heading, the culture that celebrates the riser. I want to argue that this is backwards twice over. The American Dream isn't the opposite of Jante, it's the more advanced version of it. And the tall poppy image itself has been quietly flipped upside down.

Start with the image. The original tall poppies are Roman. Livy tells the story of Tarquinius Superbus, the last king of Rome, walking through his garden and knocking the heads off the tallest poppies with a stick. His son Sextus had sent a messenger from the town of Gabii asking what to do next now that he had the place under control. Tarquin never answered in words. Sextus got the message anyway: kill the leading men. And the older Greek tellings of the same scene — Herodotus has Thrasybulus of Miletus doing it, Aristotle's Politics has a version too — swap the poppies for ears of wheat, the tyrant cutting off the tallest ears as he walks the field. So cutting down the tall poppy started life as a tyrant's instruction to another tyrant about how to hold power by eliminating rivals. The jealous-neighbour reading came much later and is close to the opposite of the story. We took an image of top-down violence and turned it into a complaint about bottom-up envy.

Jante drifts the same way, just in a different direction. Sandemose didn't hand anyone a moral code. He invented a satirical one for a fictional small town in his 1933 novel A Fugitive Crosses His Tracks, ten little rules about not thinking you're anything special, capped by a nasty eleventh rule that functions as pure blackmail, since it implies the community knows something about you. He was describing the conformity he grew up inside. Ever since, the same text gets read as a description of Scandinavian egalitarianism, as an aspiration, and as a grievance by people who feel held back. Same words, opposite jobs.

Now the actual argument. Both systems exist to solve one problem: a standout makes everyone near him feel measured, and that feeling eats at a community. Jante solves it by pressing the standout down. Blunt, local, and it costs you your best people. The American Dream solves it by promising that the standout's spot is open to everybody. Which means the person who doesn't get there has nobody to blame but himself. That's a much better machine, because now the envy never has to be policed from outside. It gets routed through the promise of mobility — call it envy laundering — and the loser does the policing himself.

The usual ranking treats Jante as the petty one and the Dream as the generous one. I'd flip that. Jante at least admits there's a mechanism. The Dream denies there is one, and that denial is exactly what makes it cruel to the people it fails.

So the real question isn't celebrate versus cut down. Both cut. They just aim it differently. The question is whether a society can let someone rise without making everyone else's standing depend on whether they rise too.

More importantly: does the central move land, or is it the kind of reversal that sounds clever for one paragraph and then falls apart once you poke at it? And what's the strongest objection, the thing I'd need to answer if I sent this to someone who genuinely believes in the American Dream?
