I'm writing because I had a long argument last night with a close friend, and I can no longer tell whether he has the better of it or whether I am simply defending my own position. Both of us sit on the board of a small housing cooperative — eleven units sharing a boiler and a modest reserve fund — and what began as a dispute over filling a vacant board seat widened until we were arguing about how the co-op should make decisions at all.

His proposal is that we stop electing the board and fill it instead by drawing lots from among the member households, under two conditions: no household may be drawn twice in succession, and service is unpaid and, he concedes, time-consuming. He offers two arguments. The first is that elections reward the loudest and most ambitious neighbors, turn a co-op into a miniature campaign, and systematically hand decisions to whoever cares most intensely rather than to whoever has the best judgment. The second is that the draw treats every household's judgment as worth exactly the same, which he says is the entire point of a cooperative, and that across enough draws and enough rotation the resulting group will track the membership better than any slate we could vote in.

Two things he said unsettle me. When I observed that a single household owns three of the units and pays nearly a third of the budget, he shrugged and said that if I insisted, we could weight the draw by contribution — which strikes me as quietly abandoning his own principle. And when I raised accountability, noting that an elected member can be turned out while a household drawn by lot answers to no one, he replied that accountability is largely a fiction at this scale, and that a member who need not be re-elected will vote their conscience rather than curry favor.

My own worries are the obvious ones: that eleven households is too few for a draw to be representative of anything; that a household with a narrow stake could end up deciding an issue that barely touches it; and that if the work is unpaid, those who decline to serve will not be a random sample of anyone. I should also admit that I am the current chair and would lose my seat under his plan, which makes me distrust my own reasoning.

I am not asking you to confirm that my instinct is right. I want to know what the argument is really about beneath the practical details, which of us holds the stronger case once each side is stated as forcefully as it can be, and which of the objections on either side are decisive and which are beside the point. If he is right, tell me the design features I should insist on before agreeing. And if the small size of the co-op changes the answer, explain how.
