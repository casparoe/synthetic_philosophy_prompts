I am preparing a seminar on the ring of Gyges, and what I want from you is a credence rather than a précis of the debate — specifically a credence about a variant of the case that I have built to strip away the usual confounds. In the familiar story a single person acquires a ring that renders him invisible and untraceable and is thereby released from detection and punishment. My variant — call it the Mutual Ring — makes the protection universal and mutually known: everyone possesses such a ring, it is common knowledge that everyone does, and no transgression is ever detected, punished, or even suspected. To close off the internalized-sanction escape, stipulate further that the agents' psychology is such that guilt, shame, and the anticipation of self-reproach simply do not arise; whatever restraint they display must issue from something other than a felt sanction.

My question is whether morality survives that stripping. Please give me your credence, as a number, in the following precisely stated proposition:

(M) In the Mutual Ring world, an agent who breaks a promise or appropriates what belongs to another solely because doing so suits her is acting against a reason she has — a categorical reason, whose force depends neither on her desires nor on any sanction, actual or anticipated.

I want the number decomposed, not merely asserted. At a minimum, separate and assign a rough probability to each of the sub-questions the answer turns on: first, whether there are categorical reasons at all, i.e., whether any version of moral rationalism or moral realism is correct; second, whether the shift from the solitary ring to the Mutual Ring materially changes the self-interested case for restraint — for instance by making defection self-defeating through the collapse of assurance, or instead more attractive through the impossibility of coordinated punishment — and I want your judgment on which way that cuts, not a survey of opinions; third, whether the stipulation that no guilt ever arises is coherent or instead begs the question against the internal-sanction reply; and fourth, whether the operative sense of reason is desire-based, since on some accounts (M) is false almost by construction. Combine the terms into your overall figure, tell me which single term the final number is most sensitive to, and say what argument or formal result would move that term most.

One further thing. A colleague places her credence in (M) at about 0.8, and I place mine at about 0.3. Given your decomposition, which of us is more likely to be mistaken about which sub-question — that is, where does our disagreement most plausibly live?
