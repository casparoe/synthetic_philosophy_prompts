My mother tuned pianos for thirty-one years in a damp town on the coast, and she died of a disease that took her speech before it took anything else. Since then I have been arguing about her with my brother, who drives a truck and thinks I overthink everything, and the argument has settled into a shape I can't get out of on my own, so I would rather have it as a story than keep losing it at the kitchen table. In the version I keep turning over, the technology exists and works; I don't need you to explain the engineering, I need it to be a fact of the world like weather. It comes in two forms. The expensive one is a hospital program: it maps the dying person's brain in detail and keeps the thing running, so that when you speak to it, what answers you is a system whose insides move the way a brain moves. The cheap one is a service called Halda, and it is enormously popular because it costs a fraction as much. It never copies a brain at all. It was built from the person's whole recorded life — decades of letters, diaries, voicemails, and a last year of interviews — into a vast library of situations paired with what that person would say and do in each, and when you speak to it, it finds the nearest situation it has stored and gives you back what she would have said. My invented family can only afford to keep one of them running past the first year. That is the whole situation: the two are indistinguishable in every way the family can test — the same jokes, the same pause before answering a hard question, the same wrong name for the cat, the same flat insistence that she is herself — and the only difference anyone can point to is what is happening inside.

I understand that this is the old question of whether a person is the pattern rather than the material, and I am not equipped to settle it and don't want you to settle it either. What I want is a story in which the difference matters at the level of a kitchen table: a daughter who has taken on the testing because nobody else will, a brother who has already decided that the cheap one is obviously their mother and that his sister is being morbid, a mother's house with the piano still in it and the heating still on, money running out in a way that is boring and constant rather than dramatic.

Write it in the third person, close on the daughter, and keep it under 1,100 words. Nobody in the story should explain the problem to anybody else, and nobody should mention a philosopher, a book, or a named thought experiment; these are people who tune pianos and drive trucks, and they should sound like it. Don't resolve it for me — I don't want a scene where one of the two fails a test and the other passes, and I don't want the daughter to arrive at a verdict about which one is really her mother. What I want instead is the decision bearing down on her with the clock running, the brother's case for the cheap one being not stupid, and the testing itself: the questions she invents at two in the morning, the ones that get answered, the ones that get answered too well. End on something physical happening in a room rather than on a thought about what a person is.
