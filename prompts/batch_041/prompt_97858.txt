I recently helped organize a small cause-prioritization reading group in my department, and the experience has sent me back to Ayn Rand’s ethical and political writings, which I had not seriously engaged with since university. Our group keeps applying the familiar triage of importance, tractability, and neglectedness to rank problems, and I have found myself unable to decide what Rand would have made of the whole enterprise. Could you reconstruct, as carefully as you can, the position she would likely have taken if she had lived long enough to encounter the effective altruism movement and its cause-prioritization frameworks? I do not want a bare verdict that she would have condemned altruism. I want you to separate the strands: first, the ranking procedure itself, which treats moral claims impersonally and aggregates them; second, the motivational question of whether someone pursues a highly ranked cause out of self-sacrificial duty or as an expression of a rationally selfish life project; third, the neglectedness criterion, which tends to elevate causes that others overlook, including those involving nonhuman animals or far-future people; and fourth, the practice of earning to give, in which someone takes a lucrative job in order to fund such causes. Where would she see genuine continuity with her own praise of productivity, trade, and long-range rational planning, and where would she see self-abnegation or the exploitation of the victim’s own moral code re-entering?

I am especially interested in whether she would treat cause prioritization as a neutral tool that an egoist could use, or as an inherently altruistic framework that corrupts the user’s ethical outlook. Suppose, for example, she were shown a cost-effectiveness estimate suggesting that a dollar spent on an obscure animal-welfare intervention or on reducing far-future existential risk would do more good than a dollar spent on a familiar human charity. Would she regard the estimate as a piece of practical reasoning that a rational egoist might legitimately use to allocate resources, or would she regard the very act of comparing causes by their aggregate expected moral value as already conceding the premise she rejected? Please give the most determinate answer you can, explain which commitments in her ethics and metaphysics drive each part of the prediction, and flag the points where the prediction is genuinely underdetermined because the issue postdates her. I would also like to know which of the four strands above would be most likely to produce an outright rejection rather than a qualified accommodation.
