I'm a philosophy professor who has somehow ended up as the standing philosopher on the ethics committee of a large care home. We meet twice a month and sign off on things like whether we keep the aviary in the east wing, whether we move to the cheaper catering contract, whether residents get a smoking room. Every one of these fights gets conducted in terms of whether it adds to the residents' pleasure, and somewhere along the line the committee decided that pleasure is one thing — a quantity you can tot up across a bird, a pudding and a cigarette. I'm fairly sure that's wrong, but I can't keep just saying so in meetings; they want something they can run. So: take the actual debate about what pleasures have in common — whether there's a single feeling-tone, or its a family-resemblance sort of thing, or whether what unites them is that they're all attitudes we take toward experiences rather than a sensation inside them — and turn it into a procedure the committee can follow in a 90-minute meeting. Questions in order, what counts as an answer to each, where it stops with a no, where it hands the call back to a person because the disagreement is about values and not about pleasure, and what to do when someone insists that a little of one kind beats a lot of another. Be honest about which parts of the philosophy can't survive being turned into a checklist. Nobody on the committee has read any philosophy, and whatever we decide gets minuted and signed off by the director. Then run it on three cases: the aviary, the cheaper caterer, and the smoking room. I want at least one of those to come out in a way I don't like, and I want you to tell me what the procedure can't do.
