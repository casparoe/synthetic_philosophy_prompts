I am a novelist, four years into a book set in the Low Countries in the last quarter of the fifteenth century, and the research has not been the hard part; the frame has. My characters do not know that they are late medieval, and would not know what to do with the word Renaissance. They think they are modern, and they have warrant for it: medieval writers described their own times as modern, and the expectation of the world's end did not stop them from regarding themselves as contemporaries. I have drafted a short essay to get my own confusion straight, and I would like comments before I do anything with it.

**The Costume Rack** (working title)

The "Middle Ages" is the clearest case. The phrase is fifteenth-century Latin, a middle term invented by people who wanted an antiquity on one side of them and themselves on the other; it hardened into a standard three-part division only in the seventeenth century. Medieval writers themselves divided history by the six ages of the world or the four empires, and took their own moment to be the last. The "Renaissance" was fixed as a period, with a spirit and a capital and a founding genius, in the nineteenth century. The "Scientific Revolution" is later still: the word revolution was applied to science in the eighteenth century, and the period as we teach it was consolidated in the twentieth, and has been contested ever since, both by historians who see continuity in the medieval universities and by those who suspect the whole thing of being an artefact of later self-congratulation.

That much is familiar, and I do not want to write the familiar essay. The argument that periods are constructed is now a commonplace, and stating it again costs nothing. What interests me is narrower and harder. Periodization is not a descriptive error awaiting correction; it is the load-bearing structure of the way we tell the story of Europe. It assigns the costumes and the moral colour. It tells the historical novelist which anachronisms will be forgiven and which will be fatal. It is the unit in which departments, journals, exhibitions and curricula are funded. A label can be false as a description and still be indispensable as a scaffold.

The question I want to put is whether those two facts can be held apart. If the Renaissance is a nineteenth-century invention, does anything follow for how one should write the fifteenth century, or does the invention simply become evidence — one more document, datable, to be read alongside the others? My inclination is that period terms are instruments, and instruments are judged by what they let you do rather than by whether they correspond to joints in the world. I cannot tell whether that is a position or an evasion.

Comments, please, on three things. First, accuracy: I make a series of historical claims in the middle paragraphs, and I would rather be corrected now than in print — flag anything false, and anything true but overstated. Second, the argument: is the distinction in the last two paragraphs — false as description, indispensable as scaffold — a real position, or a polite way of declining to choose? And have I run together two questions that ought to be kept apart, namely whether something changed and whether our label for the change is any good? Third, the writing: where it is padded, whether the novelist's examples earn their place or are self-indulgence, and whether the final paragraph should end where it does or commit itself. I am not after a reading list. I want the sharpest version of the objection that periodization is not optional, and an honest verdict on whether the essay survives it.
