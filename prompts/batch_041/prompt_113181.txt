In a metaphysics reading group I attend, a colleague has been pressing a line that I would like audited rather than waved away, and I take him to be arguing for the anti-Humean side of the familiar dispute about laws. His claim is that the phrase law of nature is borrowed from jurisprudence, that the borrowing is not a dead metaphor but a live one, and that it carries real metaphysical weight. If the borrowing is apt, then a law of nature is not merely a regularity that a good summary of the world would happen to record; it is more like a statute, in that it prescribes rather than describes and stands behind what happens rather than trailing after it. He then takes a further step: a statute is a command, a command presupposes someone who issues it and someone who can receive it and disobey it, and so if the analogy holds all the way down, the foundation of nature is command-like — which he regards as a point in favour of reading the order of the world as having something mind-like or intentional at its base. He condenses the whole picture into the image of a law of nature as a standing order issued to no one in particular.

What I want is a careful audit of the analogy, not a verdict on theism or on design arguments. Set out the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine — saying what plays the role of the legislator, the statute, the subject, the promulgation, the enforcement, the jurisdiction, and repeal. Then say where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in the features of the source that the analogy quietly imports and the target may lack: that a statute has an addressee with a will, that it can be knowingly obeyed or knowingly broken, that it is conventional, datable, and revocable, and that it is backed by sanctions. Which of these disanalogies bear on my colleague's intended conclusion and which are merely decorative? Does his conclusion — that laws govern, and that the governance is command-like — survive once they are on the table, or was the legal vocabulary carrying the argument from the outset? And if the disanalogies are fatal, do they cut only against him? It seems to me they might embarrass equally anyone who leans on the word law while denying that it governs, so I would like the audit to say which way the wreckage falls. Finally, if the legal analogy is past repair, propose a better one, and state what the better analogy would and would not license.
