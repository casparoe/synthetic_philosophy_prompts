I'd like to argue with someone who is genuinely committed to incompatibilism rather than someone who surveys the options. Please play a hard incompatibilist: someone who holds that nobody is ever free or morally responsible in the sense that would license blame and desert, who has heard the compatibilist repertoire many times and is not rattled by it, who concedes narrow points when they are genuinely forced but never yields the central claim, and who is not a caricature — he should be able to say what he thinks we should actually do about punishment, praise, and resentment, and should be able to say it without flinching. Stay in character until I say 'stop'. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs so it reads like a conversation. Do not cite anyone, name any philosopher, or point me toward books: I have no training in philosophy, I've been thinking about this in my own time and arguing with a friend who studied it, and any name or title you produce is one I cannot check or use. If you need to attribute a position to a camp, describe the position instead of naming it. Begin your first reply with your position stated flatly and in character — not a summary of my argument, not an expression of interest in the debate, and no request that I clarify anything; take my case as it stands.

Here is the position I've arrived at on my own, which is what you have to deal with. I began as an incompatibilist, on the crude grounds that if my choice was determined then I couldn't have done otherwise, and that was that. Then I noticed I had been demanding something impossible: that I be the cause of my own character, so that when I finally choose, the choosing is really mine and not merely the output of the character I happen to have. But to cause my character I would need a character to do the causing with, so either it recedes forever or at some point it simply happens to me — and if it simply happens to me, I am not its author either, no matter what the physics turns out to be. Indeterminism is no rescue: a genuinely random swerve in my deliberation is less mine than a determined one, and no one thinks a coin flip makes them responsible. So the thing being demanded looks incoherent, and I've concluded the demand should be dropped rather than satisfied — after which 'free' and 'responsible' can be rebuilt from what is actually there: that I deliberate, that I act on reasons that are mine, that I could have done otherwise in the only sense anyone can unpack (had I wanted to, the way would have been open), and that it was I who did it.

But there is one case I cannot get out of my head, and I suspect it is where I am weakest. Suppose that while I slept, someone locked every door of a room except one. I wake, weigh my options, find the others locked, and leave through the open door. Everything I did, I did for my own reasons; nothing was done to me; had I wanted to stay I would have stayed; had I wanted to leave by another door, I would have found it locked, and so in that specific sense I could not have done otherwise. I was, on my view, free the whole time. Yet something nags. If I had been a slightly different person, with slightly different wants, the locks would have stopped me, and it is only my own wants that made them irrelevant — and my wants, on the determinist picture I accept, were themselves simply given to me. So my freedom seems to rest on a coincidence: that my character happened to point exactly where the locks allowed. My worry is that this coincidence is precisely what your objection to compatibilism amounts to, dressed up in one concrete story — and that if I cannot show the locks to be doing no work, I have to give up more than I want to. So convince me I'm wrong, or tell me exactly what I have to surrender and why, and don't offer me a middle position I haven't asked for.
