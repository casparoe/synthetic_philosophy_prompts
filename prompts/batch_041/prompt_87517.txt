Me and two friends watched a documentary the other night about a mayor somewhere in West Africa — I'm blanking on the country — who put his brother-in-law in charge of the water board, steered a road contract to a firm his cousin owns, and then every few months goes back to his home village, pays people's school fees and turns up at weddings and funerals with envelopes of cash. He's genuinely popular there. Anyways this set off a two-hour argument and they've been texting about it ever since.

Ravi said it's corruption, full stop. He's got a public office and he's using it to enrich himself and his relatives, and that's just what the word means. Doesn't matter how popular he is or how much he hands out — the brother-in-law's job and the cousin's contract are the thing, and the generosity is a fig leaf.

Tom said the definition is doing all the work there. He said in a lot of places there isn't a clean wall between the public purse and the obligations you have to your kin and your neighbours, and getting into office doesn't cancel those — if anything it's the point, you get in so you can do something for your people. He said the idea that an office is a trust you hold for some abstract public is a specific historical invention and not one everybody signed up for.

Ravi said fine, but look at the direction the money flows. Ministry job, fat contract, and in return the village gets some school fees and a party. He said the it's-their-culture line is always spoken on behalf of the people who win and never by the people who loose out — the woman who didn't get the water board job, the other bidder who did everything by the book.

Tom said that's an assumption, that plenty of patronage systems really do push money downward, and that the realistic alternative on offer was never a clean bureaucracy, it was a state that simply never showed up for these towns at all. He also said the country league tables everyone quotes are basically built out of what foreign investors say they *perceive*, so the global anti-corruption business is partly grading countries on vibes. And he made a point about the word itself: that corruption isn't neutral the way tall is, it's more like rude or cheating — you can't apply it without already having decided what someone is entitled to and what they owe, so using it across societies smuggles in the answer. He added that it's not mainly a story about bad apples anyway, it's a trap — if you're the one clerk who refuses the envelope your paperwork just sits in a drawer forever, so everyone pays and everyone knows everyone pays, and moralising at individuals does nothing.

Ravi said that last bit is a point about how to fix it, not about what to call it; a mugging is also a coordination problem if everyone's doing it. And he said the rude comparison cuts the other way — rude isn't culture-proof either, there are rude things everywhere and we somehow manage. He said if the test is whether real people get hurt he doesn't need anyone's permission to apply it, and that Tom is a relativist in the seminar room and a moralist everywhere else: if the brother-in-law got the job over Tom's own sister, Tom would be furious, and that fury is the whole argument.

Tom said he's not saying it's fine, he's saying it isn't *one thing*. He said there's a difference between a guy quietly skimming a budget into an offshore account and a system where office is understood as something your community holds *through* you, and if you can't hold that difference in your head then your anti-corruption programme just becomes the next round of outsiders telling people how to live. Ravi said the difference is real but it's a difference between two kinds of corruption, not between corruption and something else.

They both want me to settle it and I honestly can't. Can you go through it move by move and tell me who's right at each step — not who wins overall, but where each of them is standing on solid ground and where they overreach? And at the end, is there a position that survives both of their points, or do you actually have to pick a side?
