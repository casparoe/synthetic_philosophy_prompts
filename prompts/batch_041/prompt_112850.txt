For a while now I've been reading Schrödinger's essays on consciousness and the Upanishads, and I keep returning to the position he stakes out there: that the plurality of minds is an illusion of standpoint, that there is strictly only one consciousness, and that the scientific picture of many separate organisms each housing its own private inner life is a useful construction -- maya -- rather than the last word on what there is. As I reconstruct it, the argument runs on two planks. The first is that whenever we look for the subject of an experience we never find a distinct entity standing over and above the experience itself; the self is not one item among the contents of consciousness but the very medium in which contents appear, and nothing in the scientific account shows that medium to be divided by the bodies it seems to animate. The second is that the apparent plurality comes from the plurality of the objects and histories of experience rather than from a plurality of subjects: because your memories and mine differ, we infer two owners, but the inference is not forced, and the Upanishadic identification of the innermost self with the ground of all being is offered as the more honest description. I find the first plank genuinely gripping and the second almost too slippery to evaluate, and that asymmetry is part of why I'm asking.

What I want from you is a map of the position's exposure -- not a defense and not a refutation. What would have to be true for it to be false? Concretely: which observations, arguments, intuitions, or results would count against the claim that consciousness is numerically one -- the split-brain and dissociation literature, the manifest privacy and incommunicability of experience, the apparent dependence of mind on a plurality of distinct brains, the possibility of two phenomenally independent streams running at once within a single subject, the awkwardness of the view with any naturalistic story about how experience is generated. Sort these by how decisive each would be if it held up, and mark which are live options rather than merely logical possibilities. Then tell me what a committed holder of the view should concede as counting against them -- not the objections critics press, but the considerations that ought to give the view's own defenders pause -- and, for each, whether the view can absorb it without giving ground or whether it genuinely costs something.

I'm especially interested in whether the position risks anything at all. It has the shape of a view that can always retreat: if experiences are private, that is the illusion of standpoint; if brains are many, that is maya; if a subject splits, the one consciousness now has two windows. If that is how it works, say so plainly, and then tell me whether that kind of insulation is a defect for a metaphysical thesis of this sort or whether the thesis is the kind that could not be falsified in the first place without changing the subject. Finally, put the strongest single defeater first: if I could test the view against only one thing, what should it be?
