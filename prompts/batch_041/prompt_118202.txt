I belong to a small reading group that has spent the past term on decision theory and the philosophy of probability, and we have reached the point where one of our standing puzzles needs to be written out rather than argued over; I have been handed the job, and I would sooner have you write the fiction than attempt it myself. Here is the puzzle as we have been passing it around. A fair coin is tossed until it comes up heads for the first time. If that first head falls on the k-th toss, the player's net gain is (−1)^(k+1) multiplied by 2^k and divided by k pounds: so two pounds to the player if the head arrives at once, two pounds from the player if it arrives on the second toss, eight thirds to the player on the third, four pounds from the player on the fourth, thirty-two fifths on the fifth, and so on, the sign alternating and the amounts growing without limit. The gamble is perfectly well defined: the first head arrives after finitely many tosses with probability one, the chance of its arriving on the k-th toss is one over 2^k, and if you multiply each outcome by its probability and add the terms in the order the tosses occur, you get one minus a half plus a third minus a quarter and so on, which converges to the natural logarithm of two — about sixty-nine pence in the player's favour. But that series is only conditionally convergent: the sum of the magnitudes, one plus a half plus a third plus a quarter and so on, runs away to infinity, and consequently the very same outcomes can be grouped and reordered so that the total comes out at any figure one pleases, or at plus or minus infinity, without altering the coin or a single payoff. I can describe one and the same gamble as the most generous offer in the house or as a guaranteed road to ruin, and both descriptions are arithmetically honest. What none of us can do is say what the thing is worth. I do not want a verdict on that question; I want a story in which the absence of a verdict is something a reader feels before they can begin to argue about it.

For setting, I have been imagining a licensing office in a damp seaside town — some fictional borough with a harbour, a promenade and a municipal gaming board whose rulebook is older than anyone sitting on it. Every game the board licenses must display in its window a single figure, the mathematical expectation per play, and the rulebook forbids the licensing of any game whose expectation is either negative for the player or undefined; a game with no figure cannot be licensed at all. A new establishment on the seafront applies to offer the coin game above, and its solicitors have helpfully printed sixty-nine pence on the application, derivation attached. A consumer advocate on the other side of the table submits the identical gamble with the outcomes regrouped into blocks, arrives at a figure of minus several hundred pounds, and asks the board to refuse the licence. The junior actuary who must write the recommendation discovers that she can produce, for the same game, a positive figure large enough to make it the most attractive wager in the borough and a negative figure large enough to have it banned outright, and that she can defend either one on the arithmetic. The number she writes will be painted on a board in a window and read by people who will then hand over their wages.

Please write the story — a few thousand words at most, and in British English throughout (licence as a noun, realise, maths, colour, and so on) — as fiction rather than as a disguised essay: scenes, dialogue, someone with a job and a deadline, and the mathematics doing structural work in the plot instead of being explained by a lecturer. I want at least one scene in which a character actually performs the regrouping on the page and the running total visibly changes, with the numbers shown; I want a character who finds the whole fuss ridiculous and insists that the only sane answer is the limit of the partial sums in the order the coin tosses actually happen, and I want that character to be allowed to be persuasive rather than quietly defeated by a clever retort; and I would rather you did not resolve the puzzle for me or tidy the ending into a verdict. No citations, no footnotes, no survey of what anyone has written about this — treat the game as the characters' own invention and their own problem. And let the weight of it land on something ordinary at the end: a figure in a window, a licence granted or withheld, a queue at a door.
