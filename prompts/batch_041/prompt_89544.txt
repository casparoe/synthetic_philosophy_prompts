I am to deliver the chair's opening address at the first sitting of a constituent assembly for my city. The assembly was convened by referendum of the existing electorate — a body that excludes roughly a third of the city's adult residents, most of them people who have lived here for decades without citizenship. Its single remit is to settle who belongs to the city's demos: who may vote, on what terms, and by what procedure the franchise may be altered thereafter. In the room will be the electors who appointed us, the residents whose inclusion is the item under discussion, and observers from the national ministry, who will be reading the transcript for any claim that exceeds our mandate.

Write the address, roughly eight minutes spoken.

The difficulty is that the speech cannot presuppose the demos it is addressing. I cannot open by declaring what the people of this city have decided, because that is precisely the question; I cannot simply announce the franchise open to all residents, because the authority to announce it is what is contested; and I cannot retreat to procedure alone, since any procedure for counting votes already presupposes a roll of who counts. I want the address to hold that difficulty in view rather than dissolve it by fiat, and to say honestly what this assembly's authority consists in, if it does not consist in the consent of a body it has not yet constituted.

Two devices are on the table and I must speak to both. The first is the double-register rule we adopted at our preliminary session: any motion altering the composition of the electorate must carry a majority of the currently enrolled and, counted separately, a majority of all adult residents present; where the registers diverge the motion fails, and the record must state which register dissented. The second is a sunset clause: whatever we decide about membership lapses after twelve years unless re-enacted by the electorate our decision creates. I have not settled whether the sunset clause is a principled answer to the problem of a demos founding itself or an evasion of it, and I would rather the address take a position than straddle the question.

Someone will move that we simply open the roll and count anyone who signs it. I want the address to explain why that too draws a boundary — set by whatever it costs to appear and sign — without letting that objection become an apology for the boundary we already have.

Assume no one in the room has read any political theory and that a lecture would lose them. Do not name or cite theorists; let the argument be carried by the occasion itself. It should land for the residents whose standing is in question as much as for those who already hold it, and it should flatter neither. End with something the excluded can hear as an invitation rather than a concession, and the electors can hear as an argument rather than a surrender.
