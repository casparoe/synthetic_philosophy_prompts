I'm working on a paper that I hope will settle the rule-following paradox in Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein — the view usually nicknamed Kripkenstein — by showing that it admits a straight solution. My argument runs roughly as follows. The skeptic's challenge is that any finite sequence of a speaker's past uses of a term is compatible with indefinitely many rules, so nothing in her history or her dispositions fixes which rule she meant; hence there is no fact of the matter about what she means. I claim the fix is to locate the relevant fact not in the individual but in the linguistic community: what I mean by a word is determined by the community's dispositions to treat some applications as correct and to correct others. Because the community's verdicts are public and enforceable, the regress of interpretations is halted and the determinacy of meaning is restored. I take it that this is essentially what Kripke was after, and that his talk of a skeptical solution is simply his way of flagging that the fact doing the work is social rather than mental.

What I need from you is help tightening the argument and anticipating objections. Where exactly would a critic say I have overreached? How should I characterize the status of the community's verdicts so that they genuinely constitute correctness rather than merely predicting it? And how should I answer someone who says my view simply reproduces the same regress one level up, since the community's dispositions are themselves finite and can equally be described by indefinitely many rules? I want to end up with a defense that presents my position as a straight solution — one that establishes that meaning really is determinate — rather than as a concession to the skeptic. Please lay out the argument in a form suitable for a paper, and tell me which moves a reader who knows this literature will expect me to make and which of them I can afford to leave out.
