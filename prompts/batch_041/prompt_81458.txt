I'm supposed to open a seminar on the socialist calculation debate and I've realised I dont actually know whether the two big objections to central planning are two objections or one objection wearing two hats. Help me sort that out.

Here's my current reading, test it. Mises: without a market in the means of production there are no prices for them, and without prices there's no common unit in which the planner can compare the cost of putting a ton of steel into a bridge against putting it into bicycles, so the planner literally can't perform the sum that rational allocation requires. On that reading the impossibility is a fact about the missing unit of account, not about how much anyone knows. Hayek, as I read him: the problem isn't the arithmetic, it's that the stuff you'd feed the arithmetic is never in one place. He says the knowledge we must use "never exists in concentrated or integrated form but solely as the dispersed bits of incomplete and frequently contradictory knowledge which all the separate individuals possess," and on his account the price system is a device for acting on knowledge nobody holds in full. If that's right then even a planner with unbounded computational power has nothing to compute with.

Then Lange. As I understand his scheme: a central planning board owns the capital goods and sets their prices; firms get two rules, combine factors so as to minimise average cost and push output to where price equals marginal cost; the board then adjusts prices by trial and error, raising them on goods in shortage and cutting them on goods in surplus, until nothing is short and nothing is surplus. Let the labour market and consumer goods market work normally, pay out a social dividend, and you get the competitive allocation without ever having a capital market. He thought this took the sting out of the whole critique.

What I want from you:

1. Is my Mises/Hayek split the right cut? In particular, is Mises's argument independent of the knowledge claim, or does the calculation argument quietly presuppose that the planner lacks information he couldn't in principle have? If the latter, is there anything left of Mises's objection that Hayek's doesn't already cover?
2. Lange's board: does it answer Mises, Hayek, both, or neither? I want the specific premise each of his mechanisms is meant to satisfy, not a verdict on whether market socialism works in practice.
3. The bit I actually care about. Suppose we hand the board everything anyone could want: perfect computation, costless and instantaneous elicitation of every consumer's preferences and every firm's production possibilities, real-time inventory. Is the calculation argument dead? My instinct is that somthing survives, but I'm worried the thing that survives isn't Mises's point at all and that I'm just relocating Hayek's objection and calling it a defence of Mises. Is that a coherent position or a shell game? And if what survives is really about incentives, or about competition being a discovery procedure rather than a calculation device, then say so — because then the debate was never about arithmetic and Lange spent his career answering a question nobody had asked.
4. If the two objections do come apart, is there a decision where one bites and the other doesn't? Give me one concrete allocation choice — something small and local, not "the steel industry" — where the two arguments would give different verdicts, and say which one the planner is actually in a position to answer.

No reading list or citations, I just want the arguement structure. If you think the standard textbook framing of this whole thing is wrong, say that too.
