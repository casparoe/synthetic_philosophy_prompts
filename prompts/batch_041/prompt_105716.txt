I spent 35 years as a field geologist before I retired a couple years ago, and since then I've been reading philosophy of science pretty much on my own — a few books, an audited grad seminar, alot of arguing with myself in the margins. The topic I keep circling back to is inference to the best explanation, because it's either the thing I've been doing my whole career without knowing it or the thing that's smuggling in all my worst habits, depending on the day.

So here's what I'm after. If you had to tell a latecomer where the live action actually is in IBE right now — not the tidy textbook version where you pick the hypothesis that would, if true, best explain the evidence — which open questions would you say genuinely matter, and in what order would you rank them? The ones rattling around in my head: whether IBE is just Bayesian updating in a trench coat or is doing something extra that the probability calculus can't capture; what to do about the bad-lot worry, that the best of a lousy bunch is still lousy; how explanatory virtues like simplicity and unification could possibly track truth instead of just tracking what feels satisfying to us; and whether the whole approach survives the uncomfortable fact that scientists in the past kept failing to even conceive of the alternatives that turned out to be right — so being "best" among the ones you happened to think of looks like a pretty thin guarantee.

I care way more about the reasoning than the list. For each question, I want to know why it's upstream of the others, how much is really at stake for scientific realism (I came in sympathetic to realism and I'd like to know if I should stay that way), how tractable it is for a philosopher rather than a working scientist, and what would move it up or down your ranking over the next few years. And please flag any of these that are definately rabbit holes — things that look deep but don't end up mattering much downstream. The audience is basically me: someone with a scientist's instincts, a decent tolerance for technical detail, and maybe three or four years of serious part-time reading ahead of him.
