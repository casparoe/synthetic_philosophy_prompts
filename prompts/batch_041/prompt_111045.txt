I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, currently on a reporting fellowship, and I'm preparing an application to a foundation that funds long-form public scholarship — the kind of grant that supports a year of reading, reporting, and writing rather than a lab or a field site. The project I want to pitch has been forming out of my recent reporting on automated decision systems: a vehicle that didn't brake, a triage model that didn't flag a deteriorating patient, a screening tool that didn't surface an applicant. In every case, everyone agreed the system "caused" the harm by failing to act, and in every case the argument turned almost immediately on how finely we described the failure. Frame it as the system failing to apply the one rule that would have caught this particular case, and its causal role looks plain; frame it as the system not doing any of the infinitely many things it might have done, and the causal claim seems to dissolve. My half-formed thought is that we may be running two quite different things together under the single heading of causation by omission: a thin, counterfactual sense in which almost any absent condition counts as a cause, and a thick, responsibility-tracking sense that seems to depend on whether the omission breached a norm the agent was bound by. If that's right, then a good deal of the doing-and-allowing debate and the negative-causation debate may be partly talking past each other, and the interesting question is what happens to the thick sense once you try to separate it from the responsibility judgments it was supposed to explain.

Please turn this into a two-page proposal I can send to a funder who is intelligent but not a philosopher, and who will want to see that the question is real, tractable, and worth a year. State the central question so that it has a determinate answer — I take it to be something like whether the thick, responsibility-tracking sense of omission-causation can be given an account that does not covertly appeal to the very judgments of blame and obligation it is meant to underwrite; in particular, whether it can be reduced to a non-normative selection among contrast classes (statistical normality, salience, the way a question happens to be posed) or whether it bottoms out in norms and cannot be laundered into anything more neutral. Explain why the existing positions leave this open: the counterfactual-dependence accounts and the contrastive accounts each seem to handle one half of the phenomenon and then quietly help themselves to the other, and I want the proposal to make that split visible rather than to referee the usual dispute. Lay out the method as something I would actually do across the year — conceptual work on the individuation of omissions, plus a systematic battery of matched cases in which I hold the world fixed and vary only the description, the contrast, or the norm in play, drawn from tort, medicine, and machine-behavior reporting — and say why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives (a straight survey of intuitions, or a purely doctrinal legal analysis). Then tell me what a result would look like, what the competing conclusions are and which considerations would decide between them, and what would count as the project failing — including the possibility that the two senses collapse into one, which I'd rather be told in advance than discover in month nine. Finish with a realistic scope for twelve months and a note on what I should deliberately leave out. Don't worry about citations, a literature review, or a bibliography; I'll add those myself once the argument is in place.
