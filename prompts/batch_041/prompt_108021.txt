I am assembling a working list of patterns I have decided are real or not, and I want to know what principle, if any, they express. I work as a data scientist and came into this from field ecology, so my cases are drawn mostly from measurement and modelling rather than the usual philosophical stock. I know the shape of the standard view — that a pattern is real when it supports compression and prediction that rival descriptions cannot match, and that what counts as a pattern is relative to a stance we adopt toward the system, physical or design or intentional — but I do not want a summary of that discussion. I want you to treat my verdicts as the data and tell me what rule they add up to, where the rule breaks, and which verdict I should abandon.

My verdicts, stated as concretely as I can:

1. Debris-clearing dip. A river gauge reads low every Tuesday between 06:00 and 08:00. The dip holds across three years, replicates at two gauges downstream, and traces to a crew that clears debris on a fixed schedule. Real.

2. The branch-office field. A default model reaches 0.81 AUC by keying on a field that turns out to record which office entered the application. It has no causal link to default, and the signal vanishes on the next year's data. Not real.

3. The wallpaper tile. A repeating tile describes a wall's image in a few bytes and predicts nothing about anything beyond the wall. Real.

4. The trajectory file. I hold a file that stores the exact future of a chaotic system, state by state. It predicts perfectly, but it is as long as the history and admits no shorter description. Not a pattern at all — just the thing itself.

5. The credit grade. A rating agency's grade predicts default, everyone prices off it, and issuers restructure to reach it. If no one treated it as meaningful, it would stop predicting. Real.

6. The firm's plan. A company's acquisitions are well predicted by attributing to it a plan to dominate a regional market. No document states the plan and no individual holds it whole. Real, but only from the intentional stance; from the physical stance there is no plan to find.

7. The unoccupied stance. There is a regularity in a distant system that no possible observer could ever detect or exploit. I waver: sometimes I say it is not real, sometimes I say it is real and merely undiscovered.

8. Equal rivals. Two descriptions of the same dataset compress it equally and predict the held-out data equally well. One tracks a mechanism; the other is a coincidence of how the data were collected. I want to say the mechanistic one is more real, though I cannot say by what measure.

9. The pattern I keep returning to: a monitoring index for a bridge — a number engineers watch, that predicts inspections and repairs, and that is defined by a formula someone chose. Is the index real, or is the bridge real and the index merely a convenient fiction that the engineering community acts on?

Here is my tentative principle, for you to test rather than adopt: a pattern is real to the degree that it licenses compression and prediction that no rival description matches, and its reality does not depend on whether anyone actually uses it.

What I want:

- The principle, or a small set of rival principles, stated precisely enough to apply, with each clause tied to the case that forces it.
- An honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and which I should give up.
- Whether the principle should be stance-indexed (real from the physical stance, real from the intentional stance) or stance-neutral, and whether realness is graded or binary — with the cases that decide it.
- The principle's verdicts on two or three cases I have not listed, including one where the principle and my intuition would come apart.
- After the prose, a table with these columns: Case (short name) | My verdict | Principle's verdict | Match or clash | The clause doing the work. One row per case, including the unlisted cases you invent.
