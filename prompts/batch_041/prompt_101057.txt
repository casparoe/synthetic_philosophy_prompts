I teach a graduate seminar on distributive justice, and before my students begin defending or attacking any particular position, I want them to work from a clear specification of what an axiology of distributive justice — a theory that ranks whole distributions of benefits and burdens across a population — is actually being asked to deliver. Please act as a political philosopher specializing in axiology and write that specification for me. Think it through carefully before you commit to anything, and open with a brief brainstorming section in which you canvass the candidate desiderata and sort them into genuine requirements and mere prejudices of one camp; then give the settled list.

State each requirement as a criterion, and for each one give the case or consideration that motivates it: the phenomena the theory must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the constraints it must respect. Two distinctions I especially want handled. First, the difference between a distribution being worse because someone is worse off than they otherwise would have been and its being worse merely because it is unequal — the thought that a state of affairs should be judged good or bad for people, so that pure inequality with no losers cannot by itself count as an evil. Second, separate two readings of the objection to leveling down — the case in which we make the better off worse off and help no one: a causal reading, on which the objection targets a gratuitous act of harming, and a non-causal reading, on which the objection targets the ranking of the resulting state. Say explicitly which reading each requirement presupposes, since I suspect that conflating them is what makes the debate feel intractable. Please also test the list against a case I have been using in class, which I call the plateau case: we can raise a badly-off group from 20 to 30, but only by imposing a small loss on a much better-off group, say from 90 to 88, and there is no other way to help the worse off.

Then flag any subset of the desiderata you believe cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want to see, since a demonstration of joint unsatisfiability would itself be a result — and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise attractive theories. Finally, briefly, no more than a sentence or two each, say how utilitarianism, egalitarianism, prioritarianism, and sufficientarianism fare against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scorecard, because the criteria are what I want my students to argue about. Do not supply citations or a reading list; I want the conceptual work, not the bibliography.
