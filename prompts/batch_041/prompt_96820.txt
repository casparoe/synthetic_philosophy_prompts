I'm a fifth-year PhD candidate in philosophy, and next month I'm giving the works-in-progress seminar at the clinical ethics center where I've been a fellow this year — a talk that has become uncomfortably personal. For the past two years I've been the primary caregiver for my father, who has advanced Parkinson's, and the experience has pushed me toward a claim I now want to defend as a thesis rather than a sentiment: that the ethics of care is a genuine normative theory in its own right, not a supplement to principlism and not a sensibility that merely attends to what principles leave out. Its distinctive claim, as I want to put it, is that the fundamental unit of moral analysis is the relation rather than the individual — that a particular dependence can generate an obligation which no principle, contract, or impartial calculation would yield. My central move is a distinction I have not seen drawn in quite this way: between care as a *practice* (the labor of tending, which can be delegated, professionalized, scheduled, and paid for) and care as a *relation* (the standing of being answerable to someone whose good one is implicated in), and my argument is that the four-principles approach can absorb the first while remaining structurally blind to the second — which is why it so reliably produces the discharge plan that is procedurally immaculate and morally vacant.

The room will not be friendly, and I want to be ready for it. The center's regulars are mostly physicians and nurses who were trained on the four principles and use them every day; they will not be persuaded that a framework they can operationalize is the problem. There is a Kantian who will hear relational obligation as a license for partiality, and a health economist who will hear it as a refusal to triage. There is a feminist philosopher who thinks care ethics lost its nerve when it stopped asking who actually does the caring and under what conditions — she will want to know what my theory does about the migrant care worker on whom my own family's arrangement quietly depends. And the one I am genuinely afraid of is a disability studies scholar who has argued that the vocabulary of care frames disabled people as permanent recipients and thereby reproduces the paternalism it claims to oppose; she will not be moved by the fact that I mean well.

Prepare me for the question period. I want the eight or so objections I will actually get from these particular people, phrased in their vocabulary rather than in mine, ordered by how much damage each does to my argument — not by how likely it is, and not by how easy it is to answer. For each, give me a reply I can actually deliver in the two or three minutes a seminar question allows, and then be honest about where that reply is soft: what a persistent interlocutor would say next, and whether I should have seen it coming. Mark clearly which objections I should concede outright, which I should resist, and which I should simply decline to fight on the day. Then tell me the one I am hoping nobody asks — the objection that goes to the weakest joint in my own position, the one I have been quietly steering around for months. Don't pad the list with objections that are really the same objection in different clothes, and don't give me the ones I'd get from a general philosophy audience; I know those already. No citations or reading lists, please — this room knows the literature better than I do.
