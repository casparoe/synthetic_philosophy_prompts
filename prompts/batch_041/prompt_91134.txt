For three years I have sat on the housing committee of a mid-sized city, and the same argument recurs in every cycle without a single vote changing. The growth camp holds that the only durable remedy is to permit more homes — upzoning the arterials, legalizing small apartment buildings, abolishing parking minimums and discretionary review — and treats each new market-rate building as progress even when the units are expensive, on the theory that fresh supply absorbs demand that would otherwise bid up the price of everything older. The neighborhood camp holds that what actually happens is that a new building raises land values on its block, the long-standing tenants are pushed out when their leases lapse, and the affordable units promised in the negotiation arrive late, shallow, or never; they want the decision to rest with the people who already live there and with the tenants who stand to lose. Both sides cite the same affordability figures, both claim to speak for the poor, and neither has conceded an inch.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict. My own hypothesis is that this is not chiefly a clash of values but two disagreements tangled together: first, an unnoticed ambiguity in the central claim both sides make, a right that can mean either a right to arrive in the city or a right to have a say in how one's own neighborhood changes, so that the camps can agree on the words while meaning incompatible things; second, a disagreement about the proper unit and time-horizon of analysis, since the same policy can be a gain for the metropolitan area across a decade and a loss for a single block across a year. Test that hypothesis against the actual shape of the debate and tell me where it fails. Is the load-bearing disagreement verbal, in the sense that the two sides are answering different questions under one slogan? Is it a concealed empirical question — whether new supply lowers rents, by how much, and with what lag? Is it a genuine collision of two rights that both deserve the name, occurring to happen over the same parcel? Or is it bedrock intuition about whether a neighborhood is a commodity to be allocated or a commons to be governed — and if the last, is the quarrel even about allocation, or about who holds the authority to decide? Separate these possibilities as cleanly as you can, say which one is doing the real work, and then tell me what would count as progress here — a distinction, a piece of evidence, a formal result, a change in the question — or argue that none should be expected, and why.
