I am preparing a session for a seminar on non-constructive existence arguments — proofs that establish that something must exist without exhibiting it — and the instance I want to use is the strategy-stealing argument in games. Before I teach any particular instance of it, I want a specification rather than a survey: the list of requirements a general account of strategy-stealing has to satisfy, with the case or consideration behind each, and a view about which of those requirements are genuine and which are artefacts of how people happen to state the argument.

The background I will assume in the seminar, so that the list is anchored to something: a strategy-stealing argument proceeds by assuming that the second player has a winning strategy and showing that the first player could make an arbitrary move and thereafter imitate that strategy, playing something else whenever the imitated strategy calls for an already occupied square. The imitation is licensed by two premises — that the game is symmetric between the players, and that an extra move of one's own can never hurt — and the conclusion is that the first player wins, or at least does not lose. The argument is usually non-constructive: it shows that a winning strategy exists without producing one that can be played.

Here are the cases I want the account to classify, all defined exactly as I define them here and all of which will appear in the handout:

1. Strip: players alternately place a stone on an empty square of a 1-by-n row; whoever first occupies three consecutive squares wins; if the row fills with no three consecutive, the game is a draw. Symmetric, finite, perfect information, and a player's own extra stone never hurts.
2. A single stone on the table; players alternately remove stones; whoever removes the last stone loses. Symmetric, finite, determined, and the first player loses.
3. Overline: as Strip, except that a move creating four consecutive squares loses immediately, so an extra stone can hurt.
4. Builder and Spoiler: one player wins if she occupies a complete row of a 3-by-3 grid, the other wins if the grid fills without that happening; both players have exactly the same legal moves.
5. A version of Strip in which each player may pass once, and a version in which play can continue indefinitely with infinite play scored as a draw.

What I want from you is the requirements list itself: the phenomena an adequate account must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the results it must be consistent with, each with the case or consideration that motivates it. Among the distinctions I care about: whether an account is obliged to be constructive or is instead obliged to explain the non-constructivity; winning versus merely not losing; symmetry of the players' moves versus symmetry of their objectives; and the properties the account assumes of a game versus the properties it proves of one.

Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a particular way of stating the argument — for instance, whether mirroring and copycat arguments should count as strategy-stealing at all, or whether the extra-move premise should be a hypothesis of the account or a theorem about particular games. Flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most to exclude otherwise plausible accounts. A colleague proposes that the whole criterion is this: an account is adequate exactly if it licenses the conclusion that the first player does not lose for every finite perfect-information game that is symmetric and in which an extra stone never hurts. Tell me where that criterion is too strong, where too weak, and which of the requirements above it quietly drops.

I need the list usable as a decision procedure — given an unfamiliar game, I want to be able to say whether the argument applies to it and exactly what it licenses — so keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases, and skip the history of the subject.

Finally, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested: say which of your requirements are theorems with explicit hypotheses, which are folklore or terminological choices, and which are open questions, and do not present a contested or merely conventional reading as though it were the consensus.
