I am putting together materials for a seminar on the mathematics and philosophy of the infinite, and I keep running into the same stalled exchange. A colleague who works squarely in the Cantorian tradition answers finitist objections by telling the story of a hotel with infinitely many rooms, every one of them occupied: when a single new guest arrives the manager shifts each guest down one room and frees the first, and when infinitely many new guests arrive the manager moves the occupant of room n into room 2n and frees every odd room. He treats the story as settling the matter. The finitist in our group says it settles nothing, because the story presupposes a completed infinity of rooms and of instantaneous relocations, which is exactly what is at issue, and because the result it is meant to deliver, that a part can be equal to its whole, is the very proposition that pre-Cantorian intuition, following Euclid, treated as absurd. What I want is not another retelling but an audit of the hotel as an analogy. Lay out the correspondence explicitly, in a table if that helps: what plays the role of the rooms, the guests, the manager's reassignment, and the fullness of the hotel. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. Which features of the source -- a physical building with numbered rooms, occupants who are moved about, a manager who acts -- are imported into the target and do no work there, and which of the apparent disanalogies actually bear on the conclusion the story is being used to support rather than being incidental to it?

Two specific points I would like addressed. First, I suspect the slogan about the part equalling the whole slides between two different claims -- that a proper subset can be put in one-to-one correspondence with the whole, and that a proper subset simply is the whole -- and that only the first of these is what the story shows. If that is right, does the story support anything beyond a claim about bijections, and does the finitist's charge of circularity survive the clarification or dissolve? Second, the story seems confined to the countable case and to a picture of infinity as a totality already given, and I want to know how far that limits what it can do for the larger Cantorian project, including the uncountable results that carry most of the argumentative weight. Finally, say whether the conclusion my colleague draws -- that actual infinities are coherent and that the hotel turns the finitist's objection aside -- actually follows from the analogy, and if it does not, what a better instrument for the job would be.
