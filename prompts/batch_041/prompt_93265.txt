I got into this dumb argument with my flatmate after I read that Hofstadter idea about how a chunk of a dead friend keeps on running inside your head, like the person is partly still alive as a pattern in other people's brains, and it kind of broke my brain a bit. I want to see the two sides actually spelled out, so write me a dialogue between two characters who hold our positions — and make them both sharp, please, no strawman. One of them thinks a self just is a pattern, a self-referential loop of information, and patterns can be copied and run in more than one place at once, so it's literally true (not just a nice way of talking) that part of my late grandmother is still in me and in my sister, or that a couple married for fifty years have one self smeared across two skulls, the same I in two heads. The other thinks that's a category error: an I needs a single, continuously running, causally integrated system, and two brains running similar software are just two people who resemble each other, period — the distributed-self thing is a consoling metaphor that dies the second you ask who it is that's doing the experiencing.

Neither one should get to just assert their side; they have to take up the other's best case. Have the pattern person press the substrate person with a genuine puzzle — a brain slowly replaced neuron by neuron, or someone whose personality gets rebuilt by two friends who each carry half of it — and have the substrate person hit back with something where the pattern view seems to say obviously false things (identical twins, a perfect AI replica of you, the fact that you can't feel my toothache). Let them really go at whether 'same self' is a matter of degree or all-or-nothing, whether copying a self is surviving or just duplicating it, and whether any of it should change how we treat a dead person's memory or a copy of someone. Dont let either side win by definitional fiat and dont have them shake hands at the end just to be polite — if they stay stuck, let them stay stuck, but make the sticking point specific.
