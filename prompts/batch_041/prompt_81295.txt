I've been TAing a seminar on self-knowledge this term and I've managed to talk myself into a view I'm not sure is even coherent, so I want you to try to break it. The view is a kind of split. I think your occurrent experiences are luminous — if you've got a twinge in your knee, or you're suddenly angry, or you're picturing your kitchen, then you're in a position to know that you are; you can't be in one of those states and be in no position at all to know it. But I don't think your knowledge is luminous in that way. You can know something without being in a position to know that you know it, so the KK principle — if you know p, then you know that you know p — comes out false. States luminous, knowledge not. Tell me what would count against this, and I don't mean the stock objections everyone lists. Sort them by how much they'd actually move me, from things that would be fatal to things that are merely awkward, and tell me which of them someone who holds the split view would themselves have to concede rather than just deflect.

Two worries are doing most of the work for me, so please deal with them head on. The first is the margin-for-error argument — the Williamson-ish one where knowledge can't survive being just at the boundary of a gradual change. If that's what kills luminosity for knowledge, why doesn't it kill it for the experiences themselves? Aren't there borderline twinges, colours you can just barely make out, thoughts that are only half-formed? If the state-level claim gets to survive the margin argument but the knowledge-level claim doesn't, I want the principled difference, or an admission that there isn't one. The second is whether the whole thing collapses. If knowing that you're in pain is itself a state you're in, and states are luminous, haven't I just smuggled KK back in? And if knowing-that-you-know isn't a luminous state, why on earth would the first-order ones be? Beyond those two, I'd also like to know whether the answer turns on how we read being in a position to know — evidence, reliability, or just being the sort of thing that can't be mistaken — and whether that choice is what's secretly doing the work. End by telling me whether its well insulated (and if it is, whether that's a virtue or a cop-out) or whether it's genuinely exposed, and what I should give up first.
