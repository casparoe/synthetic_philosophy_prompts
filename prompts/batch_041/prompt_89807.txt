I am a novelist by trade — my last two books concerned, respectively, a painter who loses his colour vision and a woman convinced that her grief has altered the appearance of her own kitchen — and for the past year I have hosted a small podcast in which I interview philosophers of mind for an audience of curious non-specialists. Next month's guest is a philosopher of perception who has spent roughly a decade defending a deliberately narrow version of the cognitive-penetration thesis, and I want the interview to be about the substance of that view rather than a tour of the debate. Her position, which I would like you to treat seriously rather than as a strawman, is this: cognition does reach into perception, but it never rewrites what one sees or hears — it adjusts only the weight, precision, or salience that a perceptual content carries. Belief, desire, and mood turn the volume up or down on a signal; they do not change the station. Two cases she returns to constantly are the wine-price finding, in which tasters rate the identical wine as more pleasant when told it is expensive, and the radiologist who catches the nodule that the trainee misses, both of which she treats as instances of something cognitive operating at a genuinely perceptual level.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions for this conversation, each accompanied by a single follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so that I am not left improvising when she takes the predictable exit. I want the sequence to press where the literature actually presses: on how she individuates perceptual contents finely enough for her claim to have content of its own; on whether a change in precision can be distinguished from a change in attention without begging the question; on the suspicion that a thesis which forbids cognition from altering content has quietly conceded the field to those who deny penetration altogether; and on whether her reading of the wine and radiology cases is stable, given that one might describe the first as a shift in felt pleasure rather than in perception and the second as ordinary perceptual learning. Include at least one question about what evidence could count against her position and one about whether her view is empirically distinguishable from the flat denial of penetration, and close with whatever question you think she would least like to be asked.

Lead with the single question you would pose if you could pose only one — the one that, answered honestly, would render the rest of the interview redundant — and then list the remainder in the order you would actually ask them. No questions about how she came to philosophy, and nothing that invites her merely to restate the thesis. Keep the entire response under 1,000 words.
