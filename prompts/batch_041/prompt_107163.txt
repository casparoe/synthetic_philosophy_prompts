I teach a graduate seminar in social and political philosophy that spends a few weeks on collective choice, and this term I'm pairing Arrow's impossibility theorem with the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem — the result that no social choice function which is strategy-proof and whose range covers at least three possible outcomes can avoid being dictatorial, in the sense that there must be some single voter whose most-preferred alternative is selected no matter what everyone else reports. My students follow Arrow's formalism well enough but treat Gibbard–Satterthwaite as a curiosity rather than a live problem, and I've found that a poem read aloud at the start of a session lands better than another slide of conditions. So I'd like you to write me a villanelle on the theorem. Because a villanelle lives by its two returning refrains, I want the refrains to carry the theorem's two horns: one line should voice the ideal that no voter can ever do better by misreporting their true preferences — the promise of strategy-proofness — and the other should voice the conclusion that one voter's ranking rules for all. In the closing quatrain, where the two refrains finally stand side by side, the reader should feel why the poem cannot keep both at once; that collision is the theorem. Somewhere among the tercets I'd like the concrete scene to come into focus — three voters, three options, and one of them discovering that a false ranking gets what honesty would not — and one turn should acknowledge that with only two options the trap does not spring, so that the number three is doing real work in the poem. It should be legible to a reader who already knows the result, with nothing loose enough to make a specialist wince, and still function as a poem for a reader who does not.

Please write it in American English. And because this is teaching material, end with a brief prose note — a few sentences — that separates what the poem states as settled (the theorem itself, which is a proved mathematical result) from what it touches that is genuinely contested among people who work on this: how far the theorem should be read as an indictment of actual democratic procedures rather than of a particular formal idealization, whether proposed escapes such as restricting the domain of admissible preferences or permitting randomization really defuse it, and similar matters. I don't want a contested reading of the result presented to my students as though it were the consensus, and if the verse takes any liberty with the formalism for the sake of the meter, I'd like that flagged plainly.
