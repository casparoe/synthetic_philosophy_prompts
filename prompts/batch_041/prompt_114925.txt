I am a Dutch structural engineer who moved to Kyoto eighteen months ago, and I have stumbled into a disagreement that has followed me into two relationships at once. My partner, who is Japanese, finds my habit of giving an unsoftened verdict on a friend's manuscript or a colleague's proposal to be close to a kind of violence, while an old friend from Delft — an ethicist by training, and the bluntest person I know — insists that my partner's way of wrapping every criticism in two compliments and a non-committal maybe is a form of dishonesty, and that being handled so gently is its own insult. There is a Dutch saying to the effect that if you behave normally you are already odd enough, and I have always taken that as a compliment to my countrymen; I am no longer sure it is one. Somewhere in the middle of this I have started saying things like "it was a lovely evening" when it was not, and I cannot work out whether I have grown kinder or merely more cowardly. I would like to settle the matter properly rather than by picking a side, so what I want from you is the document that the two positions could actually both sign: the view that directness is a form of respect and that white lies are wrong, and the view that indirectness is itself a form of consideration and that white lies are often a kindness. Treat them as parties to a good-faith joint enquiry rather than as opponents in a debate.

What I need in it is this. First, the claims both sides can accept — about what a person is doing when they assert something, about what deception requires (in particular, whether it requires a false statement or whether a true statement deliberately inviting a false conclusion will do just as well), about what our two cultures' speech habits actually are as opposed to what foreigners say about them, and about the limits of any generalisation either of us is tempted to make. Second, the points where they genuinely still differ, each one phrased in wording that its own side would accept as fair to itself rather than as a straw man. Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — and, for each, the case, thought experiment or piece of evidence that both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down before we look. I am particularly interested in three things I keep failing to get straight. One is whether an indirect refusal, of the "that might be rather difficult" kind, is a lie at all: it is literally true, yet it is plainly designed to leave a false impression, and I want to know whether the distinction between what a sentence says and what it merely implies does any real moral work here, or whether it is a technicality that lets polite people off. Another is whether some of these exchanges are not deception but a mutual pretence that both parties knowingly keep up — as when a friend asks whether they look fine and everyone, the friend included, understands that only one answer is really on offer — and if so, what that does to the idea that the person lied to has been wronged; I would also like the document to take seriously the thought that a hearer can be entitled to the truth and still prefer not to have it, so that the real question becomes who bears the cost of a belief rather than whether the belief is true. The third is the possibility that the whole quarrel is partly verbal, with each side using "lying" for something different, and partly empirical in ways an armchair cannot settle: I have a hypothesis that Dutch bluntness is a consequence of a flatter society, in which a criticism costs less because it does not reorder a hierarchy, and that Japanese indirectness does a different job where obligations are more finely stratified and a refusal must be made without publicly assigning it; I would like that hypothesis stated in a form both sides would accept and then tested against whatever either of them can predict about how the two cultures handle the same awkward case. If any part of the dispute turns out to be about words rather than about conduct, say so plainly, and say which part.

Please write it as a single continuous piece of prose, in the form of a letter that I could read aloud to both of them — no headings, no numbered lists, no bullet points, no bold text and no tables, just paragraphs. I am writing from a hotel room with no library, so please do not give me reading lists, references or citations; the argument has to stand on its own in your own words. Write in British English.
