I'm a retired experimental physicist — forty years in condensed matter — who has spent the last several years reading philosophy, mostly philosophy of science and lately philosophy of history. I've run into a question I can't get past on my own, and I'd like a dialogue that helps me see its shape.

Please act as a philosopher of history with a real grounding in the philosophy of science, comfortable moving between the two. Think carefully before you write, and it may help to spend a few lines up front working out where you think the genuine disagreement lies before you stage the exchange.

Here is the puzzle. Historians plainly explain things: why the Roman Republic came apart, why a particular revolution turned violent, why one research program withered while its rival flourished. The covering-law (deductive-nomological) model says that to explain an event is to show that it follows deductively from general laws plus initial conditions. History appears to do no such thing, and yet its explanations often satisfy us. The narrativist replies that the historian's explanation just is the narrative — the arrangement of events into a story with a beginning, middle, and end — and that this is a different and legitimate form of understanding, not a shabby imitation of physics.

I want a dialogue between two equally competent proponents of these views, neither one a strawman. But I don't want the standard exchange rehashed; I want it forced through two tests I keep worrying about, with each interlocutor pressing the other on them.

First, the "rewrite test." Let the covering-law proponent claim that any good historical narrative can in principle be reformulated without explanatory loss as a set of law-and-condition statements, and that whatever is lost in the rewrite was rhetoric rather than explanation. Let the narrativist resist — not by appealing to the beauty of storytelling, but by pinning down something specific that is lost. I want to know exactly what that something is, and whether it can be described without smuggling in laws.

Second, the contrastive test. Explanations answer why-questions, and why-questions are usually contrastive: not "why did this happen?" but "why this rather than that?" Let the two of them work out whether the covering-law and narrative accounts answer the same contrast class or different ones, and whether that difference is what the whole quarrel is really about.

Third — and this is what actually brought me here — I'd like the exchange to confront whether history is special at all. In my own field I could never explain why this particular atom decayed at this particular instant; I could only give statistical laws about populations. Evolutionary biology, cosmology, and paleontology all explain singular events without subsuming them under strict laws. So let one of them press the point: is the historian's predicament simply the predicament of every historical science, in which case the covering-law model was demanding something no science actually delivers? Or is there something genuinely different about explanations that turn on human reasons, intentions, and choices?

Keep the dialogue responsive: each should answer the other's actual points, concede where the other scores, and sharpen rather than repeat. No citations or reading list, please — I'll chase down the literature myself.

One last thing. I've been wrong before about what the real question is, and I'd rather not receive a smoothly produced dialogue that flatters a muddled setup. If the way I've framed this is confused — if the two sides aren't really disagreeing because "explanation" is doing different work on each side, or if the debate has already been dissolved or shown to be merely verbal, or if I'm asking a dialogue to settle something a dialogue can't — say so plainly, in your own voice, before or instead of writing it. A direct "your premise is off, and here's why" would be worth more to me than a polite performance.
