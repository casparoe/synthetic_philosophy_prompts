A colleague of mine is campaigning to replace a single expert's judgment with a large majority vote — say, on which of several treatments to fund — and he defends the change with an analogy he treats as exact: a majority vote is just like averaging many independent noisy measurements of one true value, so the individual errors cancel and the average converges on the truth. I want to audit the analogy rather than the proposal. Give me the correspondence table — what in a vote plays the role of the quantity being measured, the individual instrument, its error, and the averaging — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. In particular, spell out what the analogy silently assumes about independence, unbiasedness, a well-defined target, and the stability of the instruments, and what becomes of the convergence story when those assumptions fail: when voters share a common bias, when they deliberate and thereby become correlated, when they differ sharply in competence, or when the "true value" is not a single number but a contested question. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion he wants from the ones that don't, and say plainly whether the conclusion — that many heads converge on the truth — survives once they are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it all along. If a better analogy is available, give it. Finally, tell me what argument or evidence would move you off your verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to go wrong.
