I've been going down a rabbit hole on famines lately and I keep hitting the same wall. Everyone says the Irish famine (the 1840s one, the potato blight) was either a natural disaster or a British crime, and honestly neither label seems to do much work. What I want is for you to actually take it apart using Amartya Sen's entitlement idea -- the one where people starve not because there's no food around but because their ability to get at it through work, prices and ownership collapses. Walk me through the Irish case as a case of entitlement failure: who held what, what they could swap it for, what happened to those exchange rates as the blight spread, and where the food that was still being grown and shipped out of the country fits into the picture. Then be honest about where the entitlement frame stops being useful. For example, does it have anything to say about the fact that the people running relief were working off a set of laissez-faire and Malthusian ideas that told them interfering would only make things worse? That feels to me like a failure of beliefs, not of entitlements, and I want to know if Sen's frame can even see that kind of thing.

Then flip it around. Here's a distinction I've been messing around with on my own: there are two different things we might mean by "man-made." One is that somebody intended the deaths, or at least knew they were coming and didn't stop them. The other is just that the deaths were foreseeable and preventable by people who had the power to prevent them. I suspect most of the crime-vs-disaster fight is really a fight about which of those two we're using without noticing. So: sort the pieces of the Irish famine into nature and politics as best you can, tell me which sense of man-made each piece actually satisfies, and then say whether the natural/political line survives that sorting at all, or whether it should just be replaced by something like intended-vs-unintended or avoidable-vs-unavoidable. And finish by telling me what all this shows about the entitlement approach itself as a theory -- does the Irish case confirm it, break it, or is it a case it simply never thought about?
