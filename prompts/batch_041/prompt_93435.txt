The question was:

"Epistemicism and supervaluationism give rival accounts of vagueness. (a) Explain what each says about the truth, and the knowability, of borderline predications. (b) Which handles the Sorites paradox better, and which handles higher-order vagueness better? Defend a verdict. (6 marks for (a), 9 for (b).)"

Their answer:

Epistemicism, the view usually associated with Williamson, holds that vague predicates like 'heap' or 'bald' actually have a perfectly sharp boundary. There is a fact of the matter about which man in the sorites line is the last bald one; we just cannot know where the line falls. So on this view vagueness is an epistemic phenomenon — a gap in our knowledge, not something in the world and not something in language. Supervaluationism takes the opposite line: there is no fact of the matter about where the boundary is. It evaluates sentences relative to precisifications, which are ways of making a predicate sharp. A sentence is supertrue when it comes out true on every admissible precisification, and superfalse when it comes out false on all of them. 'Bob is bald' is true on some precisifications and false on others, so it is neither supertrue nor superfalse: it's a truth-value gap.

That means the supervaluationist has to give up classical logic. The law of excluded middle is supposed to guarantee that 'Bob is bald or Bob is not bald' comes out true, but on this view neither disjunct is true, so the disjunction can't be true either. The epistemicist has no such problem: since every predicate has a sharp extension, every sentence is either true or false, and classical logic survives intact.

On the Sorites, the epistemicist says the argument is valid but not sound — one of the conditional premises is false, the one right at the boundary, and we simply can't tell which. That's why the paradox is so persuasive. The supervaluationist says something similar, except that the bad conditional isn't a single premise but a region: the conditionals that straddle the borderline aren't supertrue, so we can reject the argument without having to pick out a culprit. I think that's the nicer response, since it matches the way the argument feels — there's no one premise you'd want to point at.

The two views come apart over higher-order vagueness, i.e. the fact that 'admissible precisification' is itself vague. Supervaluationism handles this easily: the set of admissible precisifications is vague, so there's vagueness about which precisifications count, and so on up the hierarchy. The epistemicist, though, needs the boundary to be sharp, so they have to treat second-order vagueness as just more ignorance piled on top of the first lot, which looks like a dodge.

So I'd say supervaluationism wins. It explains why each Sorites premise seems true without positing unknowable sharp facts, and it absorbs higher-order vagueness without any extra machinery. The price is giving up excluded middle, but that seems worth paying.
