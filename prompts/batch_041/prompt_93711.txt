I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about the dogmatism paradox, and we adjourned without settling it. I would like you to make the case for the position I was defending.

Here is the ground we covered. If I know that p, I also know that if p is true then any evidence counting against p must be misleading. By closure of knowledge under known entailment, I therefore know that whatever counterevidence later appears is misleading — and I seem entitled to set it aside without further thought. My friend takes the argument at face value and concludes that the paradox is genuine: knowledge is inherently dogmatic, and the only clean escape is to surrender closure and admit that knowledge is not transmitted across known entailments.

I think that is the wrong diagnosis, and I want you to argue for mine. My claim is that the paradox trades on an ambiguity in the phrase misleading evidence. On one reading, it means the evidence points toward a falsehood — that it is evidence for a proposition that is not true. On another, it means the evidence may be disregarded without epistemic fault. Knowing p may well entitle me to the first verdict about counterevidence; it does not by itself entitle me to the second, and the paradox needs the second to reach its conclusion. The closure step, run carefully, delivers only the first sense. I would like the argument to say exactly where the slide from one sense to the other occurs, and to say what work the word know is doing in the first premise — whether the paradox can be restated without it, and what changes if it is.

Then, because my friend is not easily moved, put the strongest version of his position and answer it. Be honest about the price my position pays. In particular, address the worry that it drives knowledge and the rational response to evidence too far apart to be credible — that a view on which I can know p while still being rationally required to take counterevidence seriously makes knowledge epistemically idle.

Write the argument itself in about 700 words, addressed to a reader who knows the paradox and will not be impressed by a summary of it. After the prose, give me a single JSON object with the fields below and nothing else after it:

- thesis: one sentence stating the position defended.
- equivocation: the ambiguity the argument exploits, stated as precisely as you can.
- argument_steps: an ordered array of strings, one load-bearing step each, no more than six.
- strongest_objection: the best case against the thesis, in two or three sentences.
- reply: how the argument answers that objection.
- concession: what the position gives up or leaves unexplained.
- weakest_link: the step most likely to fail, with a one-line reason.
- residual_uncertainty: a number between 0 and 1 for how confident you are that the thesis is correct, plus a short clause saying what evidence or argument would move it.
