You're a philosopher who also writes literary fiction, and you know Davidson's stuff on radical interpretation well enough to keep it off the page. I want a short story (roughly 1200–1800 words) that dramatizes the problem of interpreting people whose words, on your best translation, come out mostly false.

Here's a setup — change names and details as you like, but keep the shape. A veteran field linguist has spent thirty years on an isolated island community, and her grammar and dictionary are the standard reference. A younger researcher, working through a newly digitized archive of recordings, finds a few hundred utterances that her manual renders as flat absurdities: claims about the recently dead being around, about a river running uphill, about the speaker having done things the record shows he never did. The younger researcher builds a rival manual that makes all those utterances come out true instead, and it fits every scrap of observable behavior the old manual fits. The wrinkle neither of them can get past: the community's word for true is the same word they use for ours, and the disputed recordings are all from contexts where elders drill children in saying things nobody is claiming. So how much charity to apply isn't just a choice about their language — it's a choice about what counts as assertion at all.

Don't lecture or explain the theory, no citations, no real texts quoted, nobody giving a seminar in a seminar room. Let it come out of scene, argument, and the recordings themselves. I'd rather the ending be a specific descision by a specific person than a tidy resolution of the philosophy. Before you start writing, take a minute to sketch two or three possible endings and pick whichever puts the most pressure on the characters. Begin your reply with the story itself, no preface, then close with two or three sentences on which way you left it and why.
