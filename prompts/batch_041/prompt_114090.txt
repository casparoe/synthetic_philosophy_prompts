I'm a science journalist — mostly neuroscience and philosophy of mind, with occasional forays into whatever's trendy — and I've spent the last few months sitting in on a grad seminar on imagination and modality for a long feature I'm writing, and it has ruined a certain move for me. The move is the one where someone says "I can imagine a world in which such-and-such," and treats that as showing that such a world is possible. I'm not asking you to relitigate that whole thing; I know roughly where the fault lines run and I know the zombies are somewhere in there. What I want to know about is a narrower thing I keep catching people doing, and I genuinely cant tell whether it's a real distinction or whether I'm just irritated by sloppy speakers.

Here's the shape of it. When I ask someone to actually walk me through the world they say they're imagining, it collapses in a very particular way. They have a scene — a lab, a person in a chair, a sentence being spoken, a needle on a dial — and the scene is genuinely imaginable, vivid, they can add detail all day. But the thing they claimed to be imagining turns out not to be in the scene at all; it's a label stuck onto part of the picture. "And inside, there's the experience." Push on that part and there's nothing behind it, no matter how hard you push. What gets me is that this seems to run the wrong way round: the less they fill in, the more possible the thing sounds, and every concrete detail I add makes the claim shakier, so the arguement is strongest exactly when it's doing the least work. Same thing happens to me with the physics case — I say I can imagine a world where gravity falls off differently, but what I've actually got in my head is a world where somebody has written a different equation on a blackboard, which is a fact about notation and not about what the universe could have been like. Or the water case: I can do it, but what I'm actually holding is a world where some clear wet stuff is called water and turns out not to be H2O, which is a different claim entirely and might even be true of this world.

So: is there a name for this? Not for the general question of whether imagining tells you anything about what's possible — I know that's a whole industry — but for this specific failure, where the imagining is real and the imagined content was never actually specified, so that what looked like conceiving of a possibility was really just a sentence plus a picture. If the nearest thing is one of those distinctions I half-remember about stronger and weaker grades of this — something about what you can merely fail to rule out versus what you can positively picture — then tell me which grade is which, where the line actually falls, and why that line is the one that matters. And if nothing fits, coin something, but tell me what it excludes: I need to be able to say what is *not* an instance, otherwise it's just a fancy way of saying I didn't find the argument convincing. Also, please be straight with me about your own confidence — tell me how sure you actually are that this is the concept you think it is, and flag the single part of your answer you're least sure of, because that's the bit I'll end up having to defend to my editor.
