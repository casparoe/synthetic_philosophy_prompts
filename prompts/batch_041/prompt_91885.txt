I'm a TA for an upper-level course on collective decision-making — it's cross-listed with the policy school, so it's a mix of philosophy majors and MPP students — and I've got a stack of midterms to get through before Thursday, so I'm trying to move fast without being unfair to anyone. The unit I'm grading is the one on the unilateralist's curse. The prof taught it with a worksheet where a bunch of agents can each act on their own without checking with anyone else, and the point she kept hammering is that the group can end up worse off than a single agent even when nobody is biased, irrational, or selfish, and that the whole effect depends on the agents' judgments being independent: she had them redo the numbers assuming the judgments were perfectly correlated, and the gap between the group and the lone individual disappears. The midterm question was this:

"A lab of twelve researchers share a server and a reputation. Any one of them can post a preprint on the lab's server without consulting the others, and anything posted reflects on the lab as a whole. Each researcher independently forms a judgment about whether a draft is sound and posts it only if she judges it sound; each researcher's judgment is correct 80% of the time, and the judgments are independent of one another; and suppose every researcher cares only about the lab's reputation and gets no personal credit for posting. (a) Show how the lab can expect to do worse, reputationally, than one researcher working alone. (b) Explain what the example is supposed to illustrate about the unilateralist's curse, and say what the curse does and does not show about the rationality of the individual researchers. (c) Assess the proposal that the lab adopt a principle of conformity: that a member should refrain from acting unilaterally unless she has reason to think that the group as a whole would endorse the action."

One answer has me stuck, and I honestly can't tell how much credit it deserves. Here's what the student wrote:

"(a) Each researcher is wrong about a bad draft one time in five. The chance that at least one of the twelve judges a bad draft sound and posts it is 1 − 0.8^12, which comes out to about 0.93. A researcher working on her own would post a bad draft only 20% of the time. So the lab as a whole is far likelier to end up with a bad preprint on its server than any single member would be alone, even though each member is reliable taken by herself. (b) This is the unilateralist's curse: because any one member can act alone, and because only one member has to get it wrong for the whole lab to suffer, adding members adds chances for a mistake to get through, so the group's error rate goes up rather than down. The case doesn't show that the researchers are irrational — each is doing her best with a private judgment that's right four times in five. What it really is, though, is a collective-action problem. The harm falls on the whole lab while the decision is made by one person, so the structure is the same as the tragedy of the commons, and the fix is to change the incentives — for instance, by making whoever posts bear the cost if the preprint turns out to be unsound. (c) A principle of conformity is a sensible response, since it tells members to hold back unless their own judgment lines up with what the group would decide, which would cut down on the number of bad posts. But I'd go further. If the chance of a bad outcome goes up with the number of people who can act, then letting people act unilaterally is just worse than deciding together, and the lab should stop members from posting on their own rather than merely discourage it. A group that lets its members act unilaterally is going to be less reliable than a single careful researcher."
