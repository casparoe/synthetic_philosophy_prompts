I'm in a graduate seminar on consequentialism, and for the last three weeks we've been stuck on cluelessness: the worry that the long-run, worldwide consequences of any ordinary act are so vast and so unknowable that a consequentialist who insists on counting them all cannot compare options at all, while a consequentialist who counts only what was foreseeable seems to have changed the subject. Every example anyone brings up is one of the usual ones — voting, charitable giving, buying a cheap shirt — and everyone already has a canned reply ready, so the discussion never moves. I want a case built to order instead, engineered rather than recalled, and I want it to earn its verdicts rather than stipulate them.

Please construct a thought experiment in which two consequentialists who agree on every local and foreseeable consequence of a single act nonetheless reach opposite verdicts, because one evaluates the act by its actual total consequences in the world and the other evaluates it only by the consequences any reasonable agent could have foreseen. The act must be something an ordinary person might do on an ordinary day: no science fiction, no time travel, no stipulated miracles, and no reliance on a single knife-edge coincidence, since I want the divergence to survive small changes in the background facts. Engineer it so that the foreseeable-consequences consequentialist reaches the verdict most of us find intuitive and the total-consequences consequentialist reaches the verdict we find repugnant. The divergence must arise from the causal structure of the situation — the way small acts ramify through markets, institutions, or long chains of other agents — rather than from the agent's ignorance of facts that were, in principle, available to them. And here is the constraint I care most about: the case must not rest on the mere size of the causal web. The move I find unpersuasive is exactly the one that says the consequences go on forever, so please make the two verdicts diverge without leaning on scale alone.

After the scenario, walk through both verdicts explicitly and say why each theory gives the one it does. Then identify the single feature of the case that does the work, and tell me what happens if that feature is removed. Finish by saying how a defender of the total-consequences verdict would try to defuse the case, and whether that reply forces them to give up anything they care about. If you find that the case cannot be built under these constraints, say so plainly and tell me which constraint is doing the blocking.
