I am a novelist, and for the past year I have been at work on a book whose central character is a fictionalized Hermann von Helmholtz, the nineteenth-century physicist and physiologist who died in 1894. I have reached the chapter in which he is confronted, by means the novel deliberately leaves unexplained, with the contemporary literature on predictive processing and the free energy principle. I want the chapter to turn on his considered verdict rather than on his astonishment, and for that I need your best reconstruction of that verdict, argued as a historian of ideas would argue it.

Take as your fixed points what Helmholtz actually maintained: that perception is a species of unconscious inference; that the senses furnish signs of external objects rather than copies of them; that our perceptual and spatial capacities are largely acquired rather than innate; and that nervous signals take measurable time to travel, so that what we perceive is always fractionally stale. Against that background, how would he receive the claim that a system which minimizes prediction error, or variational free energy, thereby models its world?

I would like him to press four difficulties in particular. First, whether the appeal to expected free energy disposes of the obvious objection that an agent genuinely minimizing surprise would do nothing but shut its eyes and go dark. Second, whether a principle elastic enough to accommodate any organism that persists is explaining anything, or only redescribing it. Third, whether his own account of sensory signs survives contact with the new framework: does a brain that generates and tests a model of its environment vindicate his long campaign against the copy theory of perception, or quietly reinstate it? And fourth, whether the theory's teleological coloring, in which a system strives to remain in its characteristic states, smuggles back the final causes that he and his generation of physiologists had labored to expel from the science of life.

Write the verdict as continuous prose in his voice, in the register of a nineteenth-century scientific essay, and let him concede the points at which the new framework genuinely extends his own. I have no need of citations, a reading list, or a survey of the field; I want the argument itself.
