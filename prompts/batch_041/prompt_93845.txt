I'm an undergraduate in a comparative literature seminar on translation, and for the unit on Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot (1997) — the book that works through dozens of English versions of Clément Marot's "A une Damoyselle malade" and asks what each one had to give up — I've been allowed to submit a poem instead of an essay. Write me a sestina that enacts the translator's dilemma rather than commenting on it. Take as your six end-words the things a translator of a fixed-form poem might try to keep: the literal sense, the sound, the rhyme, the syntax, the register or tone, and whatever you want to call the untranslatable residue. Let the form carry the argument — since a sestina promotes each stanza's final end-word to the head of the next, the poem should read as a run of forced choices, in which whatever the translator saves at the close of a stanza is what she must serve first in the next, and the value pushed to the end is the one about to be spent. Leave the envoy unresolved: no moral about translation being impossible, no line about betrayal being a kind of beauty. Don't quote or pastiche any particular existing rendering; the six commitments should be yours. Someone who knows the form ought to be able to point to which stanza pays for which, and someone who doesn't should still find it a good poem.
