You are a philosopher of mind and a specialist in Buddhist philosophy of mind — the Abhidharma analysis of experience, the distinction between conventional and ultimate truth, and the reductionist arguments against a substantial self — and you write in the register of a successful fellowship application. I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy, and I need you to turn a half-formed idea of mine into a polished two-page research proposal for a one-year fellowship. Think carefully before you write, and begin by brainstorming: map the space of positions, identify the strongest version of each, and only then commit to a framing.

My half-formed idea is this. The long-running debate about whether the Buddhist denial of self (anattā) can accommodate personal identity has been conducted almost entirely as a debate about diachronic identity — about how a causal series of momentary mental and physical events can underwrite memory, desert, karmic continuity and the conventional 'person'. That framing already concedes too much, because it treats the self as a thing to be either recovered or eliminated at the level of entities, and then asks whether the series can do the work of the thing. I want to shift the question away from the unity of the person, whether at a time or over time, and towards a feature of experience that this framing leaves entirely untouched: the fact that experience is given in the first person at all — that a pain, a sound or a thought is not merely present but presented as mine, or as 'for me'. Call this the mineness of experience, and treat it as a structural feature of how experience is given rather than as one more item within experience.

The question I want the proposal to pose is roughly this: when Buddhist reductionism denies the self, is it denying that any entity answers to 'I', or is it denying that experience has this first-personal structure — and can the second, much stronger denial be stated at all without covertly presupposing the very subject whose existence it denies? I suspect the two claims have been run together, and that separating them both sharpens the reductionist's position and exposes a problem for it. Turn this into a proposal.

The proposal should do the following, in this order:

1. State the research question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, and say what would count as an answer to it.
2. Explain why the existing positions leave the question open: why the reductionist analysis in terms of aggregates and causal series targets entities rather than the structure of givenness, and why the two obvious responses — that mineness is simply another experienced content, or that it is simply an illusion — either presuppose what they deny or fail to engage the phenomenon.
3. Set out the method, month by month, for a one-year project: what I would actually reconstruct, what I would argue on the page, and in what order. Include at least one concrete test case, for example whether the doctrine of momentariness can make room for a single unified moment of experience, or whether the practical argument that grasping at self is the root of suffering requires mineness or only the belief in a self.
4. Say why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives — for instance, another treatment of diachronic identity, or a straightforward comparison of Buddhist reductionism with contemporary reductionism about persons.
5. Describe what a successful result would look like, and what would count as the project failing. I would rather know early if the three claims I am trying to separate collapse into one another under pressure.
6. Give a realistic scope for one year and an honest assessment of what could make the project too big for it.

Write for a panel of philosophers who are not specialists in Buddhist thought; do not assume that they know the technical vocabulary, and gloss any term you introduce. Do not include a bibliography, reading list or citations of any kind — refer to positions and arguments, and I will supply the references myself. Write in British English. Aim for two pages.
