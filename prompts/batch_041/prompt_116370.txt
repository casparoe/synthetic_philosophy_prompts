There's a fight in my lab that's been going for about a year and I want an honest diagnosis of what kind of fight it is, because I can't tell anymore whether we're disagreeing or just talking past each other.

Background: we're a mix of people — a couple with econ training, a couple from psych/HCI, one from CS. It started over satisficing. The econ half says there's no such thing as a genuinely distinct "satisficing" rationality: if you make the search costs explicit, then stopping the search once the marginal cost of looking further exceeds the expected marginal gain *is* optimizing, just optimizing over a different variable set. So satisficing is either a special case of expected-utility maximization or it's a mistake. One of them keeps saying that if a person sets an aspiration level and then takes the first option that clears it, you can always redescribe that as maximizing subject to a constraint, so nothing new is happening — call it "as if" optimization if you want, but the theory has no extra content.

The psych half says that's a trick. Their claim is that the satisficer doesn't have a utility function or a probability distribution in the first place — she has an aspiration level that's a function of her history, it drifts up when things go well and down when they go badly, and the search stops when an option clears it. There's no optimization happening, there's a mechanism, and the mechanism is the thing worth explaining. They get especially annoyed at the "as if" move because they say it's unfalsifiable: any behavior can be redescribed as optimizing something, so the redescription buys you nothing. The econ half says fine, then tell me what your theory predicts that mine doesn't, and the psych half says it predicts the *process* — how long people search, how aspiration levels move, why they stop when they do — and the econ half says those are empirical facts about psychology, not a rival theory of rationality.

So now I genuinely can't tell what we're disagreeing about. My own hunch, which I'd like you to test rather than just agree with: I think there might be two or three different questions under one heading. One is descriptive ("do people in fact optimize, or do they follow aspiration-and-stop rules?"), one is about explanatory adequacy ("what does a model of decision have to include to count as explaining the choice?"), and one is verbal or near-verbal ("does 'rational' mean optimal by definition, or can a non-optimizing procedure be rational?"). And underneath all of them I suspect there's a disagreement about whether "bounded rationality" is even trying to be a theory of rationality rather than a theory of actual cognition that mostly ignores the normative question.

What I want: a diagnosis of the structure of this dispute. Is it a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word 'rational', or two groups answering different questions — one about what agents choose, one about how they get there — under a single label? Is the "you can always redescribe it as optimization" move a real refutation or a redescription that has to be argued for case by case? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work here (something like: are aspiration levels really endogenous in a way that breaks the optimization redescription, or not)? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a formal result, a distinction, a specific experiment, a change of question — or is this the kind of thing where no progress should be expected and we should just split the concept and move on? Please engage the actual positions as I've laid them out, not a survey of the literature. I don't need reading lists, I need to know what kind of disagreement this is.
