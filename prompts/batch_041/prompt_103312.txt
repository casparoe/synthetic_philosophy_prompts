I'm a third-year PhD student in comparative philosophy, working on classical Chinese ethics, and I've been tapped for the after-dinner slot at a small graduate workshop on Confucian self-cultivation next month. The audience is exactly the kind where everyone can rattle off the four sprouts and knows that Xunzi's chapter on human nature is framed as a takedown of Mencius, so a generic ancient-philosophers-were-quirky bit will die on its feet. What I want from you is a short joke — a two- or three-line routine I can actually deliver aloud in under a minute — whose humor turns on the actual mechanics of the Mencius–Xunzi disagreement rather than just name-dropping the two. Concretely, I want the punchline to depend on the real content: Mencius holding that we already possess the incipient beginnings of virtue — commiseration, shame, deference, and a sense of right and wrong — which need only to be nourished like seedlings, versus Xunzi holding that what we have by birth is detestable and that goodness is wholly the product of deliberate effort and ritual, the crooked wood that must be forced straight and the blunt metal that needs the whetstone.

If it helps, I've been circling a pun I'd love the joke to land on, and I'll spell it out so you don't have to guess: Xunzi's technical term for that deliberate, character-making effort is usually rendered as conscious activity or artifice, and the character for it is built out of the graphs for person and for to make or do — which is charming until you notice that in modern Chinese that very same character now means fake or counterfeit. So through a modern lens Xunzi is the philosopher who insists that virtue is counterfeit, while Mencius is the one who thinks it merely needs watering. I'd like something a specialist would genuinely laugh at because it gets the positions right — not a Confucius-says gag — and if you can offer a couple of options with slightly different angles, even better.
