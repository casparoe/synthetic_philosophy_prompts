I am a third-year undergraduate, currently taking a course on constitutional theory, and I have been asked to give the closing address at the annual handover dinner of the student society I have chaired for the past year. I would like you to write that address for me.

Here is what you need to know about the setting. The society is a small deliberative one: about sixty undergraduates, a dozen or so alumni, and, because the handover dinner is the one evening of the year to which families are invited, a good number of parents and partners who have never encountered anything the society argues about. Two years ago, when I first sat on the committee, I drafted and campaigned for a rule the society now calls the double lock. The founding articles may be amended only by a two-thirds majority at a general meeting, followed by a written case circulated to the whole membership, followed by a second two-thirds vote at the annual general meeting a full year later. Everything else, the budget, the term's motions, the membership rules, even how the committee itself is elected, changes on a simple majority on the night. I argued for the double lock on a worry I still think was sound: that a society of this kind, in a bad year, will be tempted to solve its problems by quietly dismantling the things that make it worth belonging to, and that the temptation will feel most urgent at precisely the moment it ought to be refused.

Now my difficulty. At the last general meeting of my year, an amendment passed its first vote, narrowly. It would shorten the waiting period from a year to a term and lower the confirming majority to a simple majority. In other words, it would use the double lock to loosen the double lock. The second vote falls to the committee I am handing over to, a year from now, when I will be gone. I have not settled how I want that vote to go, and I would like the address to help me settle it, which means it has to arrive at an actual position rather than a shrug. I have also noticed that I have been quietly rehearsing arguments for that vote in the shower, which is a slightly alarming thing to catch oneself doing: the entire point of the rule was to make a question like this be argued slowly and in the open rather than settled by whoever happens to be holding the room's attention.

What I want from the address is not a defence of the rule and not an attack on it. I want it to do the honest work of asking what a promise made by one year's members to the next can actually mean. Does it bind the people who voted against it? Is a rule that can be undone by the same procedure that made it a rule at all, or only a speed bump? Is a self-binding of this sort a promise to other people, or a device that one year's members use on themselves, and does the answer change what it is permissible to do with it? Is the value of the double lock in the bad amendments it prevents, or in the deliberation it forces, and if it is the latter, what should happen once the deliberation has genuinely been had? I have been thinking of the whole thing in my own notes as a Ulysses contract, though that phrase cannot appear in the speech, since it would lose two-thirds of the room.

The address should state its position plainly enough that the room knows what I am actually recommending, even if the position is that the rule has done its work and should now be let go. It should end by speaking directly to the incoming chair about how I think she should handle the year ahead. And it has to be honest about the fact that I am the person who wrote the rule and am now standing at a lectern with a glass in my hand, which is the most self-serving possible position from which to say anything about it.

A few constraints. The room is genuinely mixed: the alumni include two lawyers who will hear every reference to entrenchment, precommitment and self-binding and will judge the argument on its merits, while the parents will hear a twenty-one-year-old being solemn about a student club, so the thing has to be affecting without being pompous and has to make its idea plain without ever sounding like a lecture. Please do not quote any famous speech, text or line, since I cannot check them and would rather not stand up and misattribute something; if you want a phrase from somewhere, paraphrase it in your own words. Seven minutes, so roughly nine hundred words. Give it to me as continuous prose that I can read aloud from the page: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just the words.
