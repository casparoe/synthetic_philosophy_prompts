The topic is the transitivity of betterness, approached through the family of arguments that run along a spectrum: begin with a life (or a state of affairs, or a policy) of very high quality, then step down through a long series of alternatives, each differing from its neighbor by a difference so small that it seems absurd to say it makes things worse, until you arrive at something clearly much worse than where you started. Each adjacent step seems to leave betterness intact, yet the endpoints plainly differ in value. Something has to give, and I want the proposal to say what.

My half-formed idea — the part I need you to sharpen rather than restate — is that this debate has been conflating three things: (i) whether the value relation itself is transitive, (ii) whether the relation that governs rational choice is transitive, and (iii) whether the ordinary comparative we use in ranking options is transitive. I suspect that at least some of the standard counterexamples are cases where we ask a single relation to do work that only a purpose-relative or tolerance-governed notion can do — where a small difference simply does not count as a difference for the purpose at hand — and that transitivity survives once the purpose is made explicit, but only relative to a purpose. I cannot tell whether that is a genuine solution or just a relabeling of the problem.
