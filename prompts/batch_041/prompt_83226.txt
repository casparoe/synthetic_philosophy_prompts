I am convening a small, closed-door workshop on the epistemology of disagreement, and I want the two principal camps to leave it having produced one document together rather than two more position papers. Please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a conciliationist and a steadfast disputant.

For the purposes of this exercise, fix the two positions as follows. The conciliationist holds that when one learns that a party one regards as an epistemic peer -- roughly, someone as likely as oneself to have reasoned correctly on the matter and working from the same evidence -- has reached a contrary view, that discovery is itself evidence bearing on who has erred, and it rationally requires a substantial revision of one's credence toward the peer's. The steadfast disputant holds that no such revision is required: one may rationally retain one's original credence, because the disagreement supplies no new first-order evidence about the disputed proposition, and a rational agent is entitled to keep responding to the evidence as she sees it. Each party may refine its formulation in the document, but the result must be a text each could sign.

The collaboration must not be carried on the stock examples. Require the two parties to work through the following stipulated cases, and to treat the cases rather than the general slogans as the real locus of agreement and disagreement.

The Fixed Point. Two agents, A and B, hold credences of 0.7 and 0.3 in P. Each knows the other's credence; each knows that their track records on comparable questions are statistically indistinguishable; each knows that the other is reasoning from the same body of evidence; and each knows that the other knows all of this. Neither has any further evidence, and neither can inspect the other's reasoning. A three-agent variant has credences of 0.8, 0.5, and 0.2, all mutually recognized as peers.

The Self-Application. A and B disagree not about P but about which norm governs the Fixed Point. A holds the conciliationist norm, B the steadfast norm, and each regards the other as a peer on this disputed normative question. The document must state what each party's own norm prescribes for that party in this case, and must confront the charge that a norm which, applied to itself, directs one to lower one's confidence in that very norm is thereby defective.

The Opaque Peer. As in the Fixed Point, except that one party's credence is delivered by a process the other cannot inspect and does not understand, though its track record is as good as the first party's. The document must say whether the opacity changes anything, and if so, why.

The joint statement should contain, in order:

1. A numbered list of claims both parties accept, about these cases and about the epistemology of disagreement generally. Do not pad the list with truisms; each claim should be one that a reasonable member of the opposite camp would have denied before the collaboration.

2. A statement of the residual disagreement in neutral wording that does not presuppose either view, followed by each party's own formulation of its position, phrased so that its own side would accept the wording.

3. The cruxes -- the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one party -- presented as a table with a row for each, giving the shared case or argument both parties agree would bear on it and recording, in advance, each party's prediction about how that case comes out.

4. A section on verbal disputes: any part of the apparent disagreement that both parties agree dissolves once a term is disambiguated -- for instance, whether "evidence" is being used for first-order evidence only or for higher-order facts about the dispute as well. Identify which part, and explain why it dissolves.

Close with a short joint note stating what would settle the remaining disagreement, or a joint acknowledgment that nothing presently available would.
