I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or economics. I have been reading my way through Keynes's Treatise on Probability, published in 1921, and I have gotten stuck on the part where he separates the probability of an argument from its weight. As I understand him, probability registers the balance of the relevant considerations for and against a proposition, while weight registers the total amount of relevant evidence behind it, so that two propositions can be exactly equally probable while one rests on a much larger body of evidence than the other. He also seems to hold that a new piece of evidence can lower a proposition's probability while still increasing its weight, and that weight is not merely a curiosity but something a rational person may properly take into account in deciding what to do. That last claim is what I want to see tested, and I would like you to build the test case for me to my specifications.

Specifications for the case:

- It must turn on two propositions, or on two courses of action that hinge on them, that are exactly equal in probability. State the probability and make the equality verifiable by inspection rather than merely asserted; symmetry of the considerations on each side is the easiest way to do that.
- The stakes must be identical, including the decision-maker's attitude toward money, so the case cannot get its result from risk aversion, diminishing marginal utility, or any other difference in the payoffs.
- The two must differ only in the amount of relevant evidence behind them. One proposition should rest on a large body of individually weak but mutually reinforcing evidence; the other should rest on a small body of evidence that happens to leave the balance exactly where the first one leaves it. Describe both bodies of evidence concretely enough that a reader can see the second is strictly smaller without being weaker.
- The decision must be forced now: no deferring, no option of gathering further evidence, and no waiting for the situation to resolve itself.
- The setting must be ordinary, the kind of thing a person could actually face at work or at home, with no urns, coins, dice, decks of cards, or lottery tickets, and no exotic or science-fictional devices.

Then do the demonstration:

- State the two probabilities and the two bodies of evidence, and say what a decision-maker who looks only at probability and expected value concludes. I expect indifference, since probability and payoffs match; confirm or explain why not.
- Say what a decision-maker who also takes account of the weight of the evidence concludes, and why.
- If the only way to generate a rational preference for the higher-weight proposition is through some form of aversion to the possibility that one's probability estimate is badly off, that is, aversion to the epistemic situation itself rather than to the outcome, then say so plainly rather than smuggling it in as a payoff difference.
- Isolate the variable. Give the minimal modification to the case that removes the difference in weight while holding probability and stakes fixed, and confirm that the preference disappears, or explain why it does not.
- Finish with an honest note on three things: which feature of the case is doing the work; how someone who holds that probability is just degree of belief, with no separate notion of weight needed, would attack the case, which I expect would go by redescribing the low-weight proposition as a two-stage gamble, that is, a probability distribution over the first-stage probability, so that ordinary decision theory already covers it; and whether that attack succeeds or merely relabels the problem. My own suspicion is that the redescription earns the preference only by importing an assumption of its own, but I want your verdict, not mine.
- If the specifications cannot all be satisfied at once, tell me which one has to give rather than quietly violating it.
