My friend Dan and I have been having the same argument for about three years now, mostly in a group chat that started life as a LessWrong book club and has decayed into a place where we post at each other about whether the Stoics were right, and I want to turn the current state of play into a poem for the little zine we put out every winter. The sticking point is the dichotomy of control, the thing every modern Stoic starts with: some things are up to us and some aren't, and the ones that are up to us are our judgments, our desires, our impulses, our aversions, while our bodies, our reputations, our jobs, and the actual outcomes of anything we do are not. Dan thinks this is the most liberating sentence ever written. I keep saying it only works if the first half is true, and the first half is exactly the half I don't believe, because I can't decide to believe I'm six foot two, or decide to stop wanting the things I want — beliefs and desires arrive, they aren't issued. He comes back with the archer picture (you shoot as well as you can, the arrow's landing was never yours) and with the reserve clause, the Stoic habit of aiming at the preferred indifferents — health, a decent job, a friend who stays — while silently appending, as you aim, unless something prevents it. Then he says the modern self-help version just splits the thing three ways: what you fully control, what you partly control, what you don't. I say that's a different doctrine wearing the old one's coat, and that the wisdom to tell the three apart is a fourth item nobody has ever managed to put on the list. We never resolve it, and honestly thats the part I like.

So, the ask: write me a pantoum — the form where each stanza's second and fourth lines come back as the first and third lines of the next stanza, and the final stanza has to fold the opening stanza's lines back in, so the poem ends by swallowing its own beginning — about the dichotomy of control, and specifically about whether its first half survives the fact that belief isn't voluntary. I want the repetitions to do the work rather than just sit there: every time a line returns it should mean something different, because it's now landing on a case where the thing I supposedly control has slipped out of my hands, or where something I'd written off as not mine turns out to be sitting in my lap. By the last stanza the distinction should be closing the loop on itself in a way that makes the two of us sound like one person arguing with himself. Don't let it land in either of our favors and don't tack a moral on the end.

Accuracy bar: a classicist shouldn't wince. The archer, the reserve clause, the preferred indifferents, and the fact that the classic list of what's up to us starts with judgment and desire and never gets around to outcomes — that all needs to be in there and used correctly, not name-dropped. Four or five stanzas is plenty, and if a returning line needs a small tweak (tense, pronoun) on its way back, that's fine, pantoums always do that. I'd rather have it be true than pretty, but if it manages both I'll read it at the zine launch and let people assume I wrote it.
