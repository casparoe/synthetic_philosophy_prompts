I cover science and ideas, and I keep running into a distinction that popular writing blurs: acting rightly versus acting for the right reasons. I'd like a careful comparison of two ways of saying what gives an action its moral worth. On the first, an action is worthy only when the agent acts for the reasons that in fact make the action right — if she acts from habit, fear, or a wish to please, the act may still be right but earns her no credit. On the second, what matters is the quality of will the action expresses, a genuine concern for the morally salient features of the situation, which can earn credit even when the agent's beliefs about what morality requires are badly mistaken, and can forfeit credit even when the act is right. Compare the two on cases meant to pull them apart: the person who does the right thing out of unreflective habit; the person who does what she is sure is wrong because she cannot bear the suffering in front of her; the person who reaches the right act by a chain of reasoning that only accidentally tracks what makes it right; and the person who acts on the right reasons but only because she trusts someone who told her those were the reasons. Tell me which view handles these cases better, where each captures something the other cannot, and where the two agree on the verdict but diverge on the grounds — since that is precisely where praise and blame would come apart. Argue from the cases and the concepts; I have no library and do not need one.
