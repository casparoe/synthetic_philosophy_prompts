The paradox of deontology: constraints forbid violating a person's right even as a means of preventing more violations of the same right, yet constraints are supposed to be stringent because rights violations matter greatly. If violations matter greatly, why does their number carry no weight? If it carries no weight, in what sense do they matter greatly?

Give me your credence that this paradox has a genuine solution — one that preserves both the stringency and the non-aggregability of constraints — rather than only a dissolution that quietly abandons one of the two. Decompose the number into at least: (i) the probability that the tension is a real inconsistency in the deontological position rather than an artifact of stating constraints in the language of value; (ii) the probability that some independently motivated account of practical reasons — agent-relative reasons, second-personal claims, or a distributional account of responsibility — supplies the needed justification; (iii) the probability that such an account preserves stringency rather than reducing constraints to tie-breakers or to concerns about the agent's own integrity; and (iv) your credence in the specific resolution you would actually endorse. Combine the terms, say which one the total is most sensitive to, and say what argument or case would move that term most.

As part of (iv), assess this candidate on its merits: constraints are justified not by the impersonal badness of violations but by the way they apportion responsibility across agents, so that each agent answers only for her own deeds, and that apportionment is itself what makes the constraint bind. Does that succeed, or does it merely relocate the paradox — trading a puzzle about aggregation for a puzzle about why the agent's own ledger should weigh more than the victims'?
