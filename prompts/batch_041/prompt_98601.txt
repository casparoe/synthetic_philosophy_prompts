Write a short story that takes seriously the view that time's arrow is not built into the fundamental laws but is a statistical fact about our corner of the universe: the microphysics is very nearly time-symmetric, entropy increases only because we start from an extraordinarily improbable low-entropy condition, and "earlier" and "later" are defined relative to the direction in which disorder happens to be growing here. The reversibility objection — that every entropy-increasing process has a time-reverse the laws permit just as readily — should sit underneath the story without ever being stated in it.

Set the story in an unremarkable place: a repair shop, a night shift, a hospice, a kitchen. Give the protagonist a competence that depends on reading which way a process is running — not from a clock, but from the signs ordinary things carry: wear, rust, dust, dispersal, the way heat moves through a wall, the way a bruise fades. Then put one thing in front of her that runs the other way. It must not be magic, time travel, or a violation of any conservation law; it should be something the physics allows but that is fantastically improbable — a local fluctuation, a patch where the gradient tilts backward for a while — and small enough that no one else would believe her if she said so.

The story's real subject is the erosion of her grip on the difference between remembering and anticipating, and between causing something and having caused it, once she can no longer assume that her own memory-forming is aligned with the direction the world is running. Do not explain this with a lecture, a physicist cameo, or an explicit exposition of the physics; let it emerge from what she does, what she stops trusting, and what she chooses to write down. Keep it to roughly ten to fifteen paragraphs. Begin with the story itself — no preface, no framing remarks, and no commentary before or after.
