Last night a friend and I argued for several hours about whether the integrity objection to impartial morality, and the associated complaint about one thought too many, really shows something as strong as she thinks. She is a pediatric resident who had just come from a transplant committee meeting where children were ranked by expected life-years, and she put the following argument to me:

(1) A moral theory is defective if it requires an agent, in order to act rightly, to treat her deepest commitments as merely one input into an impartial calculation.
(2) Any theory that would have a parent weigh her own child's need against a stranger's need, even when the stranger's need is slightly greater, requires exactly that.
(3) So any such theory is defective.
(4) But a theory that tells the parent to save her child without weighing the stranger's need treats the stranger's equally real need as morally irrelevant.
(5) So any moral theory is either defective or arbitrary.
(6) Since morality cannot be arbitrary, morality is defective.
(7) Therefore a morally serious person must either abandon impartial morality or accept that her deepest commitments are, from morality's point of view, mere preferences.

She says this is the real force of the integrity objection: impartial morality cannot be lived from the inside. I think the argument proves too much, but I cannot locate the step that does it. Which premise or inference should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I would rather keep (4) than (1), what can I still say about the parent's love? And is there a way to distinguish the transplant committee's ranking from the parent's partiality without making the parent's love a merely private preference?
