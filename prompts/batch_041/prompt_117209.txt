I'm in a political philosophy reading group that's been meeting for about two years, and we keep circling back to the same fight: what is distributive justice supposed to be equalizing — resources, welfare, or capabilities? We've got a resourcist (equalize bundles of goods and opportunities, and then hold people responsible for how they convert those into lives), a welfarist (what matters is how well people's lives actually go, so equalize welfare on whatever measure you like), and a capability person (what matters is what people are really able to do and be, and since the conversion rates from goods into functionings vary so much between people, equal goods isn't equality of anything interesting). None of them are caricatures; they know each other's objections cold. And yet nobody has moved an inch.

What stalls us is that whenever we test a view against a case, the case just gets re-described in each person's preferred vocabulary. Case from last week: someone with a treatable condition who declines treatment, has adapted to a restricted life, and reports being perfectly content. The welfarist says she's fine, the capability person says she's missing something real, the resourcist says she's got the bundle and can do what she likes with it. We agree on every fact about her. So it isn't obviously an empirical disagreement — though one of us keeps insisting that the real dispute is about how much weight preferences can bear as a measure of anything at all.

My working hypothesis, and I'd like you to try to knock it down: the three positions aren't three answers to one question but answers to three different questions wearing a single label — what makes a life go well, what a just distribution has to equalize, and what an institution can legitimately measure and act on. And once you separate those, the bit of genuine disagreement left over is about where to locate responsibility: what a person can be held to, and what has to be treated as bad luck that the distribution absorbs. If that's right, the currency question is downstream of the responsibility question, and a lot of the literature is arguing about the wrong variable. The rival diagnosis I can't rule out is that it's verbal all the way down: any metric can be redescribed in the terms of the others — resources are just capability-inputs, capabilities are just resources indexed to persons, welfare is what capabilities are for — so the argument is really about which description we should let do the work, and that's a question about purposes, not about equality.

So: which diagnosis do you give, and why? I dont want a verdict on which currency is correct, and I dont want a survey of the literature — I want to know what kind of disagreement this actually is, engaging the positions as I've described them. Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work (measuring conversion rates, individuating capability sets, the structure of responsibility for ends)? Are the sides answering different questions under one name? Is part of the impasse a clash of starting intuitions about a handful of cases that neither side can argue for? And what would count as progress here — a distinction, a decision about what the metric is for, splitting the question in two — or should we expect this to run forever, and if so, why?
