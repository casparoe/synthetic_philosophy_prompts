You are a philosopher of economics and social choice serving as rapporteur for an adversarial collaboration between two camps in the debate over interpersonal utility comparisons. Camp O, the ordinalist and no-fact camp, holds that utility is at most an ordinal representation of individual preference, that interpersonal comparisons of utility have no truth-apt content beyond convention, and that distributive evaluation should proceed with ordinal preferences plus independent principles of justice. Camp C, the comparabilist camp, holds that at least some interpersonal comparisons are truth-apt and practically indispensable: we can meaningfully say that one person's pain is more intense than another's, or that one life contains more welfare than another, and any adequate theory of distributive justice must use such comparisons. The two camps have spent a year arguing past each other. Your task is to draft the joint statement they could both sign after a good-faith collaboration. Think carefully and begin by brainstorming the points of genuine agreement and the possible sources of merely verbal dispute; then write the document.

The document should contain three parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims, each formulated so that neither side would object, covering what utility is, what measurement requires, the distinctions between ordinal, level, and unit comparability and between narrow comparability of a single dimension and wide all-things-considered comparability of lives, what social choice can and cannot do without comparability, and what each side concedes about the other's strongest examples. Second, a disagreements table with columns for the crux, Camp O's position in wording its own members would accept, Camp C's position in wording its own members would accept, and the type of disagreement, whether factual, conceptual, normative, or verbal. Third, for each crux, a concrete case, argument, or experimental result that both sides agree would bear on it, together with each side's advance prediction about what it would show. Aim for precision rather than diplomacy: where a disagreement is merely verbal, say so and explain what is really at stake; where it is substantive, make the residual disagreement exact.

As the focus for at least two of the cruxes, use the following newly proposed procedure, which neither camp has seen before: the Common Ladder Protocol. In it, participants first undergo a training module in which they learn to place a set of calibrated sensory and emotional experiences, such as mild hunger, a stubbed toe, the distress of a broken friendship, and the relief of a resolved debt, on a single 0-to-100 ladder. They are then asked to place their own current experience, and the current experience of a partner, on the same ladder, and to revise placements after seeing the partner's placement. The protocol yields, for each participant, a claim about how their experience compares with another's. A policy analyst proposes using the resulting numbers as interpersonal utility comparisons for distributive decisions. Neither camp may dismiss the protocol as irrelevant, and neither may treat it as obviously decisive. Instead, the joint statement should say what each camp would predict about its results, what each would accept as a failure of the protocol, and whether the protocol, if it worked, would settle any of the deeper disagreements or only relocate them. You may add further crux cases if needed, but do not rely on citations, reading lists, or named literature; write as if the two camps are in the room and the only resources are the arguments and cases you can set out explicitly. Keep the prose polished and the document usable as a template for other adversarial collaborations.
