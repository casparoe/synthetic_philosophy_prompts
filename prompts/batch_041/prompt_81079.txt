I hold a view I will call inferential optimism about the material record: that archaeological remains can, under favorable conditions, provide genuine warrant for claims about past beliefs and institutions — not merely about behavior narrowly described — on the ground that material patterns are reliably produced by, and so evidentially connected to, the social and mental states that lie behind them. What would count against it? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be: the equifinality of material signatures, whereby quite different beliefs leave the same trace; the culture-boundedness of the ethnographic analogies that must supply the bridging principles, and the circularity of reading the past through the present; the suspicion that the strongest belief-inferences are parasitic on texts that already tell us what to expect; and the charge that beliefs are too loosely coupled to matter for the inference to amount to more than underdetermined guesswork. Which of these would a committed optimist concede as genuine costs, and which would they dismiss as merely awkward? Does the view predict anything its rivals do not — as against a strict behavioral archaeology that stops at subsistence and technology, or a thoroughgoing pessimism that confines archaeology to the material? And is there a version of the position that survives only by quietly restricting itself to text-aided cases, where the beliefs are independently known — and if so, is that restriction fatal or simply honest? I would find the parasitism objection most persuasive if the text-free cases really are systematically weaker; tell me whether that asymmetry is established or merely asserted.
