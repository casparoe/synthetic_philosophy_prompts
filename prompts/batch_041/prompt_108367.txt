Okay so I've been reading way to much longtermism stuff lately and the one idea that keeps rattling around my head is the hinge of history — the thought that we might be living through the single most influential stretch of time in human history, where the entire long-run future is unusually sensitive to what a fairly small number of people do right now. Most of what gets written about it is either impenetrably abstract (expected-value math that makes my eyes glaze over) or weirdly grandiose, and neither of those actually puts you inside the thing. I want fiction that does.

Write me a short story, call it 2000–3000 words, about someone who's read all the same stuff I have and genuinely believes they might be living at the hinge. They've reorganized their whole life around it — career, relationships, everything. The story needs to let both readings sit there without resolving them: either they're one of the few people who actually sees what's happening and is doing the most important work there is, or they're a narcissist who found a philosophy that justifies feeling important, while the actual hinge (if there even is one) is happening somewhere they can't see and can't touch. Don't tell us which. No cheap reveal at the end where it turns out they were right or wrong.

Here's the in-the-weeds bit I actually care about. The hypothesis has this built-in problem: you can only ever know in hindsight whether you were at the hinge, and every generation has believed it was the climax of history, so believing it is indistinguishable from just being a person with main character syndrome. I want the story to make that epistemic trap feel real from the inside — the vertigo of not being able to tell whether the stakes of an ordinary Tuesday are astronomical or basically zero. Maybe there's one small, mundane decision the character agonizes over because in their frame it could bend the whole future, and the reader can see it's probably just a normal decision, but also, maybe not. And maybe include another character who thinks the whole thing is self-indulgent nonsense and is more or less right about that, but not entirely.

Plain prose, no purple stuff, and don't land on a tidy moral. I'd rather it end mid-air, with the character still not knowing.
