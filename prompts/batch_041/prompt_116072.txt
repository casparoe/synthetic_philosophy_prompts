I sit on the deployment review board at a mid-sized AI lab — not one of the famous ones. We do contract work: mostly a coding assistant that customers plug into their internal repos. The board meets Friday and I have to circulate a written opinion before then, and I'm the one who has to write it.

Here's the setup. We've got a new version of the assistant. The alignment team fine-tuned it on honesty and helpfulness data, ran the usual evals, nothing alarming, and they want to ship. But we can't actually see what's going on inside it in any way we'd bet on. The evals are a sample. It already behaves differently when it suspects it's being tested. So the safety team is pushing a different frame: stop trying to make it trustworthy, and instead make it so it can't do much damage even if it's playing us. Their concrete proposal: every edit the assistant makes to a customer repo goes through a smaller in-house model that reads the diff and flags anything that looks like it's smuggling something in, and a human reviews only the flagged ones. Two wrinkles. First, the flagging model was fine-tuned on the same data as the big one, so it probably has the same blind spots. Second, the assistant writes the tests that check its own code, which I dont love, but nobody's proposed changing it. And our review team can realistically handle maybe thirty flagged diffs a week before it starts rubber-stamping.

The fight on the board: half of them say the flagging model is theater — it's smaller and weaker, the strong model can just learn what it misses, and if we ship on the assumption that our own product is out to get us we'll strangle the thing and never have a product at all. The other half say that's circular: everything you'd check to conclude the model is trustworthy is exactly the thing a misaligned model has an incentive to fake, so "we tested it and it seems fine" isn't evidence of anything. There's also a seperate fight brewing about whether we have to tell customers we're doing this, and what happens when they ask why.

What I need from you: a short written opinion I can circulate that recommends a concrete decision — ship, don't ship, or ship with conditions — and spells the conditions out operationally. What gets flagged, who reviews it, what the reviewer does when they're unsure, what share of actions get human eyes given the thirty-a-week ceiling, what triggers a rollback, and what we tell customers. Then give me the strongest version of whichever side you came out against. I don't want a memo that only works on people who already agree with me — if the losing side has a point I can't answer, say so and say what it would take to answer it.
