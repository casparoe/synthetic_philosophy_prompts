So two people in my reading group, Leah and Marcus, got into a thing about Adam Smith's impartial spectator and I genuinely couldnt referee it. The rough question was: can the impartial spectator ever disagree with the norms of the society you were actually raised in, or is it just those norms dressed up as a tribunal inside your head? It went a few rounds and both of them were making real points, which is why I gave up.

Leah: sympathy is a mechanism that runs on experience and imagination — you imagine what a spectator would feel, but your imagination is built out of stuff you've seen. So in a society where some cruelty is just normal, the spectator in your breast will approve of it. And Smith himself complains that our moral sentiments get corrupted by our admiration for the rich and the great, which only makes sense if the inner judge is downstream of society. So the impartial spectator is social all the way down. It's the internalized "we," not a god's-eye view.

Marcus: that can't be right, because Smith clearly distinguishes the judge in your breast from the actual spectators standing around you — we appeal from the crowd to that inner spectator, and we care about being praiseworthy, not just being praised. If the inner judge were just the community, that whole distinction collapses. And the point of the corruption passage is that there's a standard the corrupted sentiments are failing to meet, so there has to be a corrective spectator that isn't the community.

Leah: but where does a corrective standard come from, if not from some other actual community or from an idealization of us? Smith's spectator isn't omniscient and it doesn't run a moral calculus, it *feels*. And feelings are trained. Maybe the impartial spectator is just what you'd get if you stripped out your partiality toward yourself and your friends but kept your culture. That's a human standard, not a universal one.

Marcus: then Smith can't say a slaveholding society is wrong in any way that binds that society, and he obviously wants to say that. He also says the wise and virtuous man defers to the impartial spectator over the applause of the crowd, and that the man within is the only tribunal you can appeal to when everyone around you disagrees with you. So it has to be able to outvote the community.

Leah: maybe it can outvote the community without being culture-independent. Because sympathy isn't only with the spectator — it's also with the person being acted on. So the inner judge is a compound of perspectives: how I'd feel as an onlooker, how the person on the receiving end would feel. That can push past local norms even if it starts from them. Feed it enough of the victim's point of view and it starts to object.

Marcus: fine, but then everything hangs on how imaginative and how well-informed the person happens to be. If the spectator's verdict depends on contingent imaginative capacity, then the impartial spectator is a fragile local achievement, not a universal standard — and Smith is giving us something much weaker than he thinks. Also, does a person raised alone even *have* an impartial spectator? Smith seems to need society for us to see ourselves at all. If the feral kid has no inner judge, that's Leah's point, not his.

Then it kind of dissolved. Can you go through it move by move — who's right about what, and where each of them overreaches? And at the end, what do you think they're both missing about what Smith is actually doing with the impartial spectator? I care less about who "wins" than about which specific claims hold up.
