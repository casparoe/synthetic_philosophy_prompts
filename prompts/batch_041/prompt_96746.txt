Bit of an odd request. I'm a retired chemist — 35 years in a wet lab, mostly kinetics — and since I retired I've been reading philosophy, of science and of mind mostly. Heres the thing I keep chewing on.

In my old field, when a hypothesis died it died for a reason anyone could check. It made a prediction, the prediction came out wrong, and something else made a prediction that came out right. The aether didn't get voted off the island; it got replaced by something that did more work. In philosophy I don't see that. I see positions declared dead — "nobody serious believes that anymore" — but the death certificate is always an argument, never an experiment, and the same argument that kills a position for one generation gets shrugged off by the next, or the position comes back wearing a new label. So I can't tell if philosophy is making progress and I'm using the wrong ruler, or if it's just a very dignified conversation that never gets anywhere.

What I want is a dialogue between two people who've thought about this a lot, and who are both sharp — please dont make one of them dumb so the other can win. Call them P and Q.

P: philosophy does make progress, but I've got the wrong model of it. Progress isn't replacement-with-a-better-predictor; it's the permanent closing of options, a ratchet. Once you see the argument you can't go back, and even when the label changes the content doesn't really get revived. P should pick one concrete case and defend it as a genuine ratchet, and say what makes a closing permanent rather than just fashionable.

Q: the ratchet is an illusion — it only closes options relative to a background assumption, and when the background gets swapped out the "closed" option quietly reopens. Q should also press the harder point: even granting a position is genuinely dead, killing options isn't progress unless you're also building something. A graveyard isn't a city. Q can reach for the law analogy if it helps him — precedent accumulates and constrains without ever settling anything, and we don't call that progress toward truth, we call it a workable system.

Two more things I want them to fight about, because they're what actually bugs me:

(1) Sometimes what changes isn't the answer, it's the question — a question that couldn't even be posed cleanly becomes askable. Is that progress? Or is "becoming askable" just a change in what counts as a well-formed question, which is a fact about us, not about the world?

(2) In my field the loser has to explain why its prediction failed. In philosophy the loser seems to have to explain why the winner's argument begs the question — and that move seems to always be available. Does that mean the arguments are weaker, or just that the subject matter is different in a way that makes the move legitimate?

Have them actually answer each other — Q taking up P's specific case, P taking up the graveyard point — not two monologues stapled together. And then a short coda from you, not from P or Q: which side the exchange favors, and more importantly what would change your mind — what argument or case would flip you to the other side, and where your own reasoning is most likely to be shaky. Don't split the difference just to be polite.
