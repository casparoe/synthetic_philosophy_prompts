I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've spent much of the past year reporting on borderline cases — heaps of sand, minimum heights, statutes that turn on a single word, thresholds in regulations. Throughout, I've kept a running list of my own verdicts, partly to keep myself honest, and I've now reached the point where I can't tell whether the list adds up to a position or merely to confusion. I'd like you to tell me which principle, or small set of principles, best captures the list; what that principle implies about cases I haven't considered; and which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon.

My verdicts, stated as plainly as I can manage:

1. Ten thousand grains of sand piled up make a heap; three grains don't. I don't believe there is any number that is the first one that makes a heap, and I don't think that's a gap in my knowledge — I think there is no such number.

2. A woman who is five foot ten, in an ordinary American office, is tall. A man of five foot eight, in the same office, isn't. But a five-foot-eleven man at an NBA tryout isn't tall, and at a family reunion he is. For the office case, I want to say that "she is tall" is neither true nor false — not that it has a hidden truth value I happen to be unable to discover.

3. All the same, I take "either she is tall or she is not" to be true, and I take "no number is the first heap" to be flatly true rather than true-ish.

4. Take a city ordinance from the 1930s that bars "vehicles" from a riverside promenade, and ask whether a pedicab counts. That seems to me to have a right answer, fixed by what the people who wrote and used the ordinance meant, even if nobody alive could establish it. It feels unlike the heap case: I don't think we'd be free to settle it by stipulation.

5. I also don't believe there's a first number that is borderline-heap. "Definitely" seems to have no sharp edge either. Yet verdict 3 requires a definite/indefinite line somewhere, and I can't see where it falls.

6. A legal drinking age of twenty-one is not vague: a person one day short of twenty-one simply is not of age. That's stipulation, however arbitrary, and I don't want it swept in with the heap.

7. The shifting standards in verdict 2 don't feel like vagueness to me at all — they feel like the same word doing different work in different rooms — and I'd resist any account that treats the two as one phenomenon.

8. A worry rather than a verdict: when I write that there is no fact of the matter about the middle of the heap, I take that sentence to be correct, and "correct" sounds uncomfortably like there being a fact of the matter after all.

I have a tentative principle of my own, and I'd like you to test it rather than adopt it. Some predicates seem sharpenable: we could fix them with a stipulation and lose only convenience — "heap," and any threshold a regulation needs. Others seem load-bearing: fixing them would defeat the point of having them — "tall" as we use it about people, "child," "generous." My hunch is that the sharpenable ones really do have a fact of the matter (or would, once sharpened), while the load-bearing ones don't, and that this explains why verdict 4 feels to me like a hidden truth while verdict 1 doesn't. I notice, however, that this sits badly with verdict 1, since heaps look sharpenable and I've denied them a cutoff. If the distinction can be repaired, repair it and tell me what my list becomes. If it can't, tell me what does the work instead.

Please state the principle or principles precisely enough that I could set them out in the piece; say what they imply for at least two cases I haven't listed; and say explicitly which of my verdicts a candidate principle cannot accommodate, rather than rounding my list off to fit. Finally, tell me what would change your mind — which argument or consideration would move you to a different verdict — and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail. Write in American English.
