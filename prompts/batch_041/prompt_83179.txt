I retired some years ago after a long career in experimental science, and I have spent the years since trying to acquire the philosophy I never had time for. I have arrived at the problem of induction with the prejudices of a practitioner: it seems to me close to incredible that a method which predicts as well as ours does could be groundless, and yet I cannot find the flaw in the sceptical argument. What I would like from you is a number — your credence that the sceptical conclusion is correct, that is, that no non-circular justification of the reliability of inductive inference exists — together with the decomposition that produces it. If you judge the proposition as I have stated it to lack a determinate truth value, say so, give me the nearest version of it that has one, and put your number on that instead.

Please give the sub-questions your credence depends on, a rough probability and one line of justification for each, and then the combination. I expect some of the following to appear in the accounting, though you should add or drop terms as you see fit: (i) whether justification must be read in the strong, non-question-begging sense at all, or whether a pragmatic vindication — induction is warranted because if any method works this one will — ought to count as an answer; (ii) whether the subjective Bayesian response dissolves the problem rather than answering it, by denying that a probability can coherently be demanded of a principle of inference from no premises; (iii) what the no-free-lunch theorems actually establish, which I take to be that averaged over all possible target functions, under a uniform measure, every learner is exactly as good as every other, and whether the restriction to the kinds of functions that arise in nature, or to functions of bounded complexity, is an inductive assumption smuggled in at the back door or a claim that can be defended on independent grounds (if my gloss of the theorem is wrong, correct it, since the analysis turns on it); (iv) what to make of the online-learning results which guarantee that a predictor can be made to perform nearly as well, in the long run, as the best of a given set of prediction strategies, with no assumption whatever about the sequence being predicted — how much of the original problem those relative guarantees remove, and whether the residual assumption, the choice of the strategy set, is the whole of the problem in another guise; (v) whether one may consistently be a sceptic about the principle while treating the particular inductive inferences of laboratory life as legitimate, or whether the general and particular problems must receive the same answer; and (vi) how much weight the demonstrated predictive success of the sciences deserves, given that the argument from success appears to be circular.

My own credence in the sceptical conclusion is about 0.7. A colleague in the philosophy department, whose judgement I respect a great deal, puts hers at about 0.35. Tell me where our disagreement most likely lives — which term in the decomposition we are really disagreeing about, and whether one of us is making an error rather than a judgement call — and then tell me which term your own number is most sensitive to, and what argument or evidence, were it available, would move it most. I am not asking for a survey of the literature; I am asking for your number and the shape of the reasoning behind it.
