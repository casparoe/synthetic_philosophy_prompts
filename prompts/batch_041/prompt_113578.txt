When someone asserts something that turns out to be false, we fault the assertion; when someone asserts something true that she was in no position to say, we fault the speaker. These are different criticisms, and much of the recent work on assertion is an attempt to say which of them, if either, articulates the fundamental norm — whether one may assert only what is true, only what one knows, or only what one justifiably believes.

Please explain the conceptual structure of this issue and work it through on the following case.

A radiologist reads a scan and tells a colleague that a patient has a certain condition. The diagnosis is correct. What she does not know, and could not have known, is that a rare benign lesion produces an image indistinguishable from the one she has learned to read as diagnostic of that condition; her evidence would have been exactly the same had the patient been healthy. Her reading procedure is reliable in general and she applied it competently, but in this instance her belief is true by luck, and we may stipulate that she does not know. She has said something true and blameless, on evidence of a kind we ordinarily accept. (The stipulation is itself contentious: a reliabilist about knowledge might insist that she does know, since her method is reliable and she used it well. Say briefly how the dispute about knowledge bears on the dispute about assertion, but do not let it swallow the question.)

Against that case, please address the following.

First, distinguish three things a norm of assertion might be fixing: when the content asserted is correct (the thing said was true), when the speaker is entitled to say it (she is in a position to put it forward), and when the speaker is answerable for it (she is to blame if it goes wrong). Does a single norm have to do all three jobs? If the defender of a knowledge norm handles the radiologist by saying that she violated the norm but is not to blame — that she is excused rather than permitted — is the excuse doing independent work, or does it merely reimport a justification norm, leaving the knowledge norm idle? Say what the knowledge, truth, and justified-belief accounts each predict about this case, and where they agree.

Second, consider how we actually respond when an assertion is unmasked. When we learn that what we said was false, we withdraw it and allow that we were wrong. When we learn instead that we did not know, though what we said was true, we tend to say that we were justified rather than that we withdraw anything. Does that asymmetry tell in favor of a truth norm and against a knowledge norm, or can the knowledge account explain it — for instance, by distinguishing what made the original assertion proper from what makes its later retention improper? If the knowledge account explains the asymmetry, does the same explanation carry over to belief, or is assertion special?

Third, consider the hedging escape. The radiologist could have said, more cautiously, that the scan looks like the condition. That weaker claim is one she plainly knew, so the knowledge norm is satisfied; but the hedge seems to change what is at stake rather than to rescue the account. Does the knowledge norm govern the content of the assertion, the content of the speaker's commitment, or the force with which she puts it forward? And does allowing hedged assertion to satisfy the norm make the account too easy to satisfy, or is that simply what it is to have a permissive norm?

Finally, say what would settle the dispute — what pattern of ordinary criticism, retraction, or deference behavior would count for or against each account — and identify the parts of the issue that are underdetermined, where the rival accounts make the same predictions and the choice among them turns on something other than the data. I am less interested in who has held which view than in the shape of the problem and in the arguments that actually discriminate between the positions.
