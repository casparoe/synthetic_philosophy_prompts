I am a novelist, and I am stuck on a scene. A retired officer and his niece, a human-rights lawyer, are arguing over a war their country has entered; he closes the argument with "my country, right or wrong," and she takes the line as a confession that he has given up on moral judgment altogether. He insists it means nothing of the sort. I have come to suspect that the sentence is several quite different sentences wearing the same clothes, and that I cannot finish the scene until I know which one he is uttering and which one she is hearing.

Please lay out the distinct claims that slogan could be making — as a menu, not a verdict — ranging from the nearly empty to the nearly monstrous, and say of each whether it is a platitude, a substantive position a thoughtful person could hold, or something false or incoherent. Among the readings I would like you to consider, and to add to as you see fit: that it is a prediction about the speaker's own sympathies rather than a principle; that it is a claim about the particularity of obligation, that one's duties to one's own polity are not conditional on its deserving them; that it is a statement that loyalty is a practice requiring non-defection precisely when one disagrees; that it is the radical claim that whatever one's country does is right by definition; that it is a demand addressed to critics rather than a description of the speaker; and that it is a defensive bet made under uncertainty about one's own judgment. A familiar retort likens the line to professing loyalty to one's mother, drunk or sober; tell me which reading that retort is aimed at, and whether it hits it.

For each reading, I want to know what it would commit the speaker to: which of them would leave him free to work against the war he has just defended, which would make his niece's charge of abdication stick, and which the people who repeat the line usually have in mind. Finally, say what difference it makes what "my" is doing in the sentence — a nation, a state, a people, a constitution, a way of life — and whether the slogan becomes more or less defensible as that object changes. I am not asking for sources or a reading list; I would rather have the reasoning than the references.
