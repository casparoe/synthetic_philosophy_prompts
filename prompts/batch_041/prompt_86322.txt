Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about the trench truces of the First World War, and I want a written second opinion. His position is that the live-and-let-live system -- soldiers on both sides deliberately firing high or on fixed schedules, sniping quietly avoided, retaliation held in reserve for the units that broke the rhythm, and the whole arrangement coming apart when raids were ordered or fresh units rotated in -- shows that cooperation needs nothing but mutual benefit and a shadow of the future, and that the fuss about common knowledge is a philosopher's fetish. Mine is that the case cuts the other way: the arrangement held only while it stayed unacknowledged, and the moment it was forced into the open, by a raid, by a new officer, by anyone who had to be seen to be fighting, it collapsed. That suggests the thing sustaining it was a kind of shared knowledge that cannot survive being shared.

Analyze the episode through common knowledge of rationality and epistemic game theory, and adjudicate between us. I want the mapping done properly, not the standard gloss: who the players actually are (the relevant strategic unit is a small sector and its officers, not the belligerent states, and each side is playing a two-level game against its own command), what the options and payoffs are, and what the information structure looks like. Then say which common-knowledge assumption is doing the real work -- common knowledge of rationality, of payoffs, of the other side's expectations -- and whether the standard iterative definition of common knowledge can capture the case at all, or whether we need to distinguish knowledge that is common from knowledge that can be openly acknowledged. My hunch is that the second notion is unavoidable; tell me whether it can be made precise or whether it just collapses back into the first.

Bring to bear the result that coordination unravels once mutual knowledge is capped at some finite depth: it seems to predict the truce should never have gotten off the ground, yet it lasted for months in quiet sectors. Is that an anomaly for the theory, or evidence that the theory was modeling the wrong players? Finally, say where the framework misdescribes the episode -- norms, retaliation, the officers' incentives -- and give me a verdict: does the case confirm the theory, embarrass it, or force my friend's thesis to be restated in whatever form is actually true?
