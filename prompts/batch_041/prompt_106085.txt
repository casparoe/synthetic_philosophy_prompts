I am an undergraduate in a seminar on decision theory and its applications to finance, and we have just finished two weeks on growth-optimal betting: the rule that an agent repeatedly offered favorable bets should stake the fraction of wealth that maximizes the expected logarithm of final wealth, rather than the fraction that maximizes expected wealth. I understand the mathematics and I have already heard the standard arguments on both sides, so please do not rehearse the textbook version. What I want is a dialogue between two people who have thought about this far more carefully than I have and who disagree at the root.

One of them is a growth-optimal bettor. She holds that maximizing the expected growth rate of wealth is not merely one utility function among many but the distinctively rational criterion for any agent facing a sequence of multiplicative risks, and that its authority comes from something other than a taste for logarithms. Roughly: in a multiplicative process the typical trajectory diverges from the average trajectory, so what a single agent will almost surely experience over time is governed by the geometric mean rather than the arithmetic mean, and an agent who maximizes expected wealth thereby chooses a strategy whose likely outcome is poor even when its expectation is excellent. The other is an expected-utility theorist. He grants all of that as mathematics but denies that it settles the normative question: on his view, growth-optimality is just expected-utility maximization with a logarithmic utility function, the ergodicity-flavored argument either assumes what it sets out to prove or else conflates the time average of one agent with the ensemble average of many, and the real question is why a rational agent must have logarithmic risk attitudes rather than whatever attitudes she in fact has.

Have them argue on concrete cases rather than in the abstract. Useful material: a bet in which the amount staked is doubled with probability one half and halved with probability one half, considered first as a single wager and then as a hundred wagers in a row; a favorable even-money bet with a sixty percent chance of winning, where the disagreement becomes quantitative, since the growth-optimal bettor will name a specific fraction and defend it; and the case of an institution that can place many small uncorrelated bets at once, where ensemble-average reasoning seems to have real force in a way it does not for an individual. I would like them to press each other on four points in particular. First, whether the disagreement collapses once the growth-optimal bettor admits that she is simply risk averse. Second, whether the argument depends on an indefinitely long sequence of bets, given that no agent ever reaches the long run, and whether a single bet or a fixed final horizon leaves growth-optimality without its distinctive justification. Third, whether the ergodicity-flavored argument does any work at all for a one-shot gamble, or only for repeated ones. Fourth, whether the ability to diversify across many independent bets should make an organization behave like an expected-wealth maximizer while an individual does not.

Neither character should be a strawman, both should be equally competent, and each should answer the other's actual points instead of talking past them; they should argue from the mathematics and the cases, not from authorities. If they cannot reach agreement, I would rather the dialogue end with the disagreement narrowed to one sharp question than with a vague truce.
