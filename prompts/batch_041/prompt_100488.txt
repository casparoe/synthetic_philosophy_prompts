I just took over as the exhibit writer at our county historical society — I write every label, panel, and caption in the building — and my first big project is a permanent exhibit on a local figure who did some genuinely awful things, and also founded the town and built its first school. The board keeps reaching for some version of "well, he was a man of his time," and my gut says that's sometimes the right call and sometimes a dodge, but I can't tell which is which in any given case, and I don't want to either whitewash him or turn every panel into a lecture about how terrible everyone back then was.

What I need is a procedure I can actually run, panel by panel. I get maybe a paragraph of text per label, I'm not a trained historian, and whatever I write gets approved by a volunteer board that includes two of his descendants, so I have to be able to defend each call out loud. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, and the point where the procedure should just stop and say "this isn't something the evidence can settle" — because I suspect some of these calls are values, not facts, and I want those flagged as such instead of smuggled into a label as if they were neutral.

There are things I keep blending together and I want the procedure to force apart: whether we're judging him or judging the act; whether "everyone did it then" is even true, and whether it would matter if it were; the difference between someone who didn't know better and someone who knew better and did it anyway, or who wrote the opposite of what he practiced; and the fact that some people at the time already objected seems like it should matter a lot, though I cant articulate why.

Run it on three cases: (1) our guy, who enslaved people while writing in his own papers about liberty; (2) a later local figure who was a real hero on labor rights but also wrote nasty things about immigrants that read as plain bigotry today; and (3) a case where the procedure tells me something I won't want to hear — either that the defense actually holds for someone I'd rather condemn, or that it fails for someone the board loves. If the procedure gives a different answer for the person than for the act, say so out loud. And tell me where it runs out — the calls no procedure can make for me.

Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating what I asked. I'll be doing this for years, on dozens of figures, sometimes on a week's notice, so it needs to be something I can run fast and explain to a non-historian in a meeting.
