I run a causal inference reading group that has drifted into philosophy of science territory, and next month I have seventy-five minutes, a whiteboard and eleven people around the table: eight epidemiology and biostatistics doctoral students and three philosophers of science. My aim is that they leave able to say precisely what the interventionist account of causation adds to the counterfactual-dependence account, and why the two are not notational variants of one another. I want the session to exploit the split in the room rather than paper over it. The epidemiologists think of causation as whatever a randomised trial, or an imagined target trial, estimates; the philosophers have the counterfactual-dependence tradition at their fingertips and will be quietly convinced from the first minute that interventionism is that tradition with the notation changed. Plan the explanation for me rather than giving me the explanation.

Two decisions have to be made early, because everything else hangs off them. The first is the definition of an intervention itself: the conditions that make a variable an intervention variable on X with respect to Y, and in particular the clauses that exclude its being caused by Y and its being connected to Y by routes that bypass X. Tell me which clause carries which pedagogical weight, which one the audience will silently drop the moment they try to apply the definition to a case of their own, and whether the circularity worry — that an intervention is itself a cause, so the account appears to move in a circle — should be met before the definition reaches the board, immediately after it, or deliberately left to fester for twenty minutes. My instinct is to let it fester, but I want your view.

The second decision is the carrying example, and my candidate is not the usual one. I want to build the session on a case in which what is intervened on is the definition or the measurement of a variable rather than any process in the world: an administrative body changes the threshold at which a condition is diagnosed and recorded, so that a variable which appeared in all our datasets as a biological state now takes a different value for the same people, with nothing having changed in anyone's body. In my own work this is the case that makes me uneasy, because the epidemiologists will insist that this is exactly what an intervention is — it is a policy, it is something you could randomise — while the philosophers will insist that it cannot be an intervention at all if the account is meant to be about what makes a difference in the world. Tell me whether this example can carry the whole session or whether it is too clever by half; if it cannot, tell me what the replacement must have and must lack. I would rather not fall back on late pre-emption or the back-up assassin, and I want to know whether that reluctance is well founded or merely reluctance to get the details right.

Beyond those two decisions I need the following. The order in which the components must come, with the dependencies made explicit: what has to be in place before the charge of circularity can even be posed as a sharp question, and what has to be in place before the audience can see that interventionist causation admits of degrees — a relationship can be more or less invariant, holding up across a wider or narrower range of interventions — whereas counterfactual dependence as the philosophers know it is all or nothing. The misconceptions you expect, in the order they will actually surface, with the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off, and with the ones arising from the statisticians' background separated from the ones arising from the philosophers' background. For the former I anticipate: an intervention is a treatment assignment; an intervention must be something someone could actually carry out; a study in which the intervention could not be delivered is simply a confounded study. For the latter: this is counterfactual dependence in different clothes; an intervention is just a possible world with a name attached; the whole account is anthropocentric because it begins from what we could do. The single question I should put to the room at the end that would show whether they have grasped the distinction rather than merely followed it, together with what a wrong answer would look like. And what I should openly refuse to cover in seventy-five minutes, saying so out loud, rather than letting them leave with a half-understood gesture at structural equations or at actual causation for cases of pre-emption and overdetermination.

I do not want a literature tour or a reading list. They should leave with one distinction that they can state, defend and apply to a case I have not mentioned.
