I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy training — one gen-ed ethics course, remembered mostly as a sequence of trolley problems — and for about a year a friend of mine and I have been arguing about how pain and pleasure weigh against each other. It started when my partner, who gets chronic low-grade headaches, was having a bad stretch, and I said something stupid along the lines of preferring one hour of genuine agony to a year of that; I've been trying to work out ever since whether I actually meant it. What I want from you is not a survey of what other people have said, and definitely not a reading list, since I can't check anything and it wouldn't help me anyway. I want to know what my own answers commit me to. Here are my verdicts, reported the way I actually have them rather than the way I'd prefer to have them, all of them framed as choices between two worlds that are otherwise identical. (1) Ten people each with a mild headache for an hour, versus one person with a mild headache for ten hours: a wash; I genuinely don't care which world I get. (2) Everyone alive — call it eight billion people — with a mild headache for an hour, versus one person tortured for a full day: I prevent the headaches, and not close, and I don't feel like I'm being sentimental about large numbers. (3) One person tortured for a full day, versus that torture not happening but every other person on Earth forfeiting an hour of mild pleasure every day for a year: I prevent the torture, and if you keep raising the number of people or the amount of forfeited pleasure, I never arrive at a point where I'd let it happen. (4) Removing one person's hour-long mild headache, versus giving a thousand people an hour of mild pleasure each: I remove the headache, and it doesn't feel close — but I'd resist the description that I think an hour of pain is worth more than a thousand hours of pleasure. The two don't feel like they're on the same scale at all; being asked for the exchange rate feels like a category mistake, and I'm told the word for what I'm denying is that they're commensurable. (5) One person's mild, never-severe, low-grade daily headache for sixty years, versus one hour of agony for that same person today with no lasting effects, everything else equal: I take the hour of agony, which embarrasses me slightly. (6) One person's hour-long mild headache, versus one person's hour of the most intense pleasure of their life: I remove the headache. One person's hour-long mild headache, versus a billion people each getting an hour of the most intense pleasure of their lives: I take the pleasure, headache included. So there does appear to be an exchange rate after all, just a steep one, which sits badly next to what I said in (4).

My friend, who has read more of this than I have, says my answers sound like a threshold view: below some intensity of pain, pains and pleasures trade off against each other on a single scale, and above it, no amount of pleasure on the other side counts for anything. When I pointed out that I'd rather spare eight billion people an hour of mild headache than one person a day of torture, he said the threshold governs only the pain-versus-pleasure boundary, not pain against pain. I can't tell whether that's a real position or a repair job dressed up as one, and I'd like you to settle it against my cases rather than against whatever the literature says.

What I want is a principle, or a small family of two or three candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I could implement it: say exactly what gets summed, whether intensity and duration enter the same way or differently, whether persons are interchangeable units or whether the concentration of suffering in one life matters over and above the total, and where any cutoff sits and what it ranges over. For each candidate, tell me which of my six verdicts it saves, which one it forces me to give up, and how much that should bother me; tell me honestly whether the threshold idea is doing real work or merely re-describing my answers; and tell me whether the category-mistake talk in (4) can survive alongside (3) and (6) or whether I have to choose between them. Then run the best-fitting principle on four or five cases I haven't listed, chosen specifically to drive the candidates apart — include at least one where the principle implies something I'll find absurd, and one where two candidates that agree on all six of my cases finally disagree. If it turns out that no single principle fits and I'm simply inconsistent, say so plainly, and tell me which of my verdicts is doing the least work and would cost me least to abandon. Please write it as a memo to me, in plain words: no citations, no names of philosophers, no reading list. If some position has a standard label, just describe what it holds and move on.
