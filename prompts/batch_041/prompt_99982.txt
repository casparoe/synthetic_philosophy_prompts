I have been arguing about Bertrand's paradox with a friend who teaches probability, and we keep talking past each other, so I should like to commission a poem for a small anthology I am putting together. The paradox, as I understand it, is this: an equilateral triangle is inscribed in a circle, and a chord is chosen at random; we ask for the probability that the chord is longer than a side of the triangle. If we choose two random endpoints on the circumference, the answer is one third; if we choose a random point on a random radius and draw the perpendicular chord through it, the answer is one half; if we choose a random point inside the disk and make it the chord's midpoint, the answer is one quarter. Each method applies the principle of indifference, and each seems natural, yet they cannot all be right.

I should like the poem to be spoken by the circle itself, or by a chord that has been asked to be random and does not know which of its properties is supposed to be uniform. The form should be a sestina, and I want the six end-words to be endpoint, midpoint, radius, chord, longer, and random. The rotation should do the philosophical work: each term must take every structural position in turn, so that no single quantity can claim to be the natural parameter, and the reader should feel the principle of indifference sliding from one partition to another. In the envoi, please let the three answers stand together — one third, one half, one quarter — without choosing among them, perhaps by making the final tercet a single sentence that can be parsed in three ways. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who teaches probability would recognise the three methods, and good enough that someone who does not would still feel the vertigo of an infinity that will not hold still. I am not looking for a lecture in verse, and there is no need to cite anything; the form and the images should carry the argument.
