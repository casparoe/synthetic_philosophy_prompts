I have no academic training in philosophy or economics, but I have been thinking about Malthus on and off for a few years, and I have drafted an essay that I would like you to comment on. The essay is not about whether his numbers were right. It is about the move people make when a long-run prediction fails, which is to say that the theory was 'right but early'; I want to know whether that move is ever legitimate, and whether it is legitimate in his case.

My notes for the essay, in the order I mean to argue them, are these.

1. 'Right but early' covers at least three different things, and I think they are usually run together. (a) The mechanism was working, but a countervailing mechanism temporarily offset it, so the predicted effect was masked rather than absent. (b) The prediction was conditional in form — if nothing changes, then such-and-such — and something changed, so the antecedent was never satisfied. (c) The prediction had no date and no threshold, so no observation could ever have counted against it, and 'early' simply postpones the reckoning indefinitely.

2. I claim that only (a) is a genuine vindication. (b) is true but trivial: a conditional that survives because its antecedent is denied tells us nothing about the world. (c) is not patience, it is a rescue dressed up as patience.

3. Applied to Malthus: I think the interesting part of his argument was conditional in form. I have a memory that in later editions he softened it by allowing that people might marry later and have fewer children, so that the check could come from human choice rather than from famine, but I may be confusing the editions. If that is right, then the fact that England industrialised, imported food and went through a demographic transition does not falsify him, and that is precisely why 'right but early' is the wrong praise to give him: a theory that survives by having its antecedent denied is not ahead of its time, it is just saying very little.

4. Where I think he may be genuinely vindicated is in the weaker claim that populations grow until something checks them, which I suspect was never seriously at risk. If so, the real question is not whether Malthus was right but which of his claims were risky enough to be worth being right about.

5. The test I want to propose: a claim deserves the label 'right but early' only if we can describe, without using the theory, some state of the world that would have made the predicted effect visible, and that state did not obtain. Otherwise 'early' is just a way of never having to be wrong.

6. My worry is that this test proves too much. It seems to rule out cases I would want to count as vindication — a prediction of resource scarcity that was masked for decades by a wave of new technology and then reappeared, say. I need to know whether those can be kept apart from (b) and (c) above, or whether my test is simply too strong.

What I would like from you: first, tell me where my three readings are wrong, incomplete, or run together, since I suspect there are more than three and that I have merged some of them; second, tell me whether my reading of what Malthus actually claimed is historically defensible, as I am working from memory and may be misrepresenting him; third, tell me whether the test in point 5 is any good, and what it would rule in or out that I would not want it to; and fourth, tell me which parts of this are interesting and which are just me restating the obvious. I do not need references or a reading list — I need to know where the argument breaks. Please write in British English.
