In a methods seminar I'm taking, a computational neuroscientist I otherwise admire argues that the brain is a computer, and draws two conclusions from it: that because the same program can run on different hardware, cognition is multiply realizable and cognitive-level explanation is autonomous from neuroscience (you don't need to know the transistors to know the program); and that a mind could in principle be implemented in silicon, since nothing in the account ties it to carbon. Pressed on what kind of computer, though, she describes something more specific: a machine with no separation between memory and processing, whose program is partly written by its own hardware during development and learning, and in which the same circuit performs different operations depending on context — closer to a chip that reconfigures itself at runtime than to a stored-program machine. My worry is that the details she offers in defense of the analogy are the same details that undercut the conclusions she draws from it. Set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of hardware, software, program, memory, processor, and input/output — then say where the mapping breaks, which breaks matter for each of her two conclusions and which are idle, and whether either conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If some other analogy would do the work she wants, say what it is and why it does better.
