Question. In the framework developed in class, a lottery L that yields outcomes x₁ < x₂ < … < xₙ with probabilities p₁, …, pₙ is evaluated by its risk-weighted expected utility:

REU(L) = u(x₁) + Σ (from i = 2 to n) r(p_i + p_{i+1} + … + p_n) · [u(x_i) − u(x_{i−1})],

where u is a utility function and r, the risk function, is an increasing function with r(0) = 0 and r(1) = 1.

(a) Say what work r does in this representation, what expected utility maximization amounts to within it, and why an agent who maximizes REU can make the Allais choices — a certain $1 million over an 89% chance of $1 million, a 10% chance of $5 million and a 1% chance of nothing, together with the opposite choice in the pair where the 89% chance of $1 million is replaced by an 89% chance of nothing — while an agent who maximizes expected utility cannot.

(b) For L = ($0 with probability 0.25; $400 with probability 0.75), u(x) = x and r(q) = q², compute REU(L) and the certainty equivalent of L, and say what the two numbers show about this agent's attitude to risk.

(c) A rival textbook sets out a lower-tail variant that applies the transformation to the probability of doing at least as badly as each outcome rather than at least as well:

REU*(L) = u(xₙ) + Σ (from i = 1 to n−1) s(p₁ + … + p_i) · [u(x_i) − u(x_{i+1})],

with s increasing, s(0) = 0 and s(1) = 1. Does this variant enlarge the class of preferences that can be represented? Justify your answer.

Student's answer. (a) The probabilities that enter the calculation are not the probabilities of the individual outcomes but the chances of doing at least as well as each outcome, and they are passed through r before being used as weights. Writing q_i = p_i + … + p_n for the chance of getting x_i or better, the agent's value for the lottery is the utility of the worst outcome plus the utility increments above it, each increment being weighted by r(q_i) instead of q_i. So r is where the agent's attitude to risk is located: an agent whose r falls below the identity is giving the chance of the better outcomes less weight than the probabilities warrant, and that is what it is to be averse to risk. When r is the identity, each increment is weighted by q_i and the expression collapses to Σ p_i u(x_i), so expected utility maximization is the special case of REU in which the agent is neutral about risk. The Allais choices are available to an REU maximizer because REU does not build in the independence axiom: the weight carried by an increment depends on the lottery's own decumulative probabilities, so the common consequence is not automatically irrelevant to the comparison, and an agent can take the sure thing in the first pair and the gamble in the second. Buchak's conclusion is that such an agent is not making a mistake; instrumental rationality permits expected utility maximization but does not require it.

(b) With the outcomes ordered 0 < 400, we have q = 0.75 and a utility increment of 400, so REU(L) = 0 + r(0.75) × 400 = 0.5625 × 400 = 225. Since u is linear, the certainty equivalent is 225, which falls 75 short of the expected monetary value of 300. The shortfall is the risk premium, and it is worth noting that in EU the same premium would be traced to the concavity of u, so the curvature of r is best understood as Buchak's version of the curvature of u, written in the probability weights rather than in the utilities: there is only one source of risk aversion in the theory, not two.

(c) The lower-tail variant is more general. Applying s to the chance of doing at least as badly as x_i lets the agent register attitudes to downside risk directly, and some such attitudes — a convex s, for example — cannot be reproduced by any choice of r in the upper-tail framework. The two frameworks coincide only in the limiting case in which both transformations are the identity, where each reduces to expected utility maximization.
