I teach an upper-level epistemology seminar -- 14 students, 75 minutes -- and I want them to run straight into the dogmatism paradox and only hear the name at the end. Design me the activity. My rough idea, which you should improve or replace: each student gets a claim card plus a sealed envelope holding the answer, and can choose to open it; after that everyone publicly declares either I know this or I dont know this. Then we play evidence rounds where tokens get drawn that point against the claims. The catch is in the rules: anyone who declared knowledge can bin any evidence token that conflicts with their claim, free of charge and without justifying it, while anyone who stayed open-minded has to actually revise and loses points when the evidence pulls them off their claim. Scoring on final correctness plus a penalty for revising away from a true claim, no real money. The rules should make the smug dogmatic player win and the careful open-minded one get yanked around, so that when we stop they've basically lived the argument that if you know something you know any counter-evidence is misleading and can therefore ignore it. Then a debrief that maps each rule onto a step in that argument and lays out the real escape routes -- whether the knowledge survives the challenge at all, whether may-ignore actually follows, closure, that sort of thing -- plus a frank note on what the game can't show (I don't think it can show whether the knowledge is still there once the counter-evidence lands, so I'll have to supply that part myself). Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it -- I'm guessing somebody says the payoffs were rigged, and somebody refuses to declare knowledge even after opening the envelope.
