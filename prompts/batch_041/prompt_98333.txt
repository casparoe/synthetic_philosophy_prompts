Setup so we're on the same page. A two-person bargaining problem is: a feasible set S of utility pairs (closed, convex, and comprehensive — if a pair is in S, anything weakly worse for both players is in S too), plus a disagreement point d that both players get if they don't agree. A "solution" is just a rule that picks one point of S for every such problem. Two famous ones:

- Nash: the point maximizing (x1 − d1)(x2 − d2).
- Kalai–Smorodinsky (KS): find the "ideal point" (player 1's best feasible payoff, player 2's best feasible payoff), draw the straight line from d to that ideal point, and take where the line exits S.

Now a guy in my reading group swears this proves there's no fair solution to any bargaining problem, and I really don't buy it. Here's his argument:

(1) A fair solution must be Pareto optimal — it shouldn't leave a point on the table that's better for both.
(2) It must be symmetric — if the problem is symmetric between the two players, they get the same.
(3) It must be invariant to rescaling a player's utility axis (affine/scale invariance), since utility numbers are arbitrary.
(4) It must satisfy independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA): if you shrink the feasible set by deleting points that wouldn't have been chosen anyway, the chosen point doesn't move.
(5) It must satisfy individual monotonicity: if the feasible set grows so that one player can reach higher payoffs against every fixed payoff of the other player (d fixed, the other player's max unchanged), that player's payoff shouldn't fall.
(6) Classic result: (2)+(3)+(4)+ Pareto uniquely pin down the Nash solution.
(7) Classic result: (2)+(3)+(5)+ Pareto uniquely pin down the KS solution.
(8) And Nash ≠ KS in general. E.g. take d=(0,0) and S = convex hull of (0,0),(4,0),(1,3),(0,2): Nash gives (2,2) while KS gives (16/7, 12/7).
(9) So no solution satisfies (1)–(5) all at once. Therefore there's no fair way to bargain, period.
