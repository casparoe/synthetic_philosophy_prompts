I have read rationalist blogs and forums for years, and I have a settled set of gut verdicts about how much the long-run consequences of an action bear on its moral status. I would like to know what principle, if any, unifies them, and I am prepared to be told that one of my verdicts has to go.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

Voting in a close national election for the candidate I judge somewhat better: clearly worth doing, and I would feel a mild sting at skipping it — even though the chance my single vote is decisive is vanishing and the downstream effects are anyone's guess. Spending forty dollars on a well-made sweater I will wear for years: morally neutral; I do not owe anyone a justification, though the long-run effects on labor practices and the climate are unknowable. Giving a hundred dollars to a charity that distributes anti-malarial bednets, where the near-term effects are well evidenced: clearly good, and good because of its effects. Giving a hundred dollars to a group doing speculative work to make advanced AI go well: at least as good as the bednets, possibly better — even though I cannot trace any specific near-term effect and the long-run payoff is a lottery. Recycling one bottle: fine, morally negligible, not required of me. Having a child: permissible and good, but I do not think it is good because of the child's long-run consequences, which could be anything.

The pair I cannot square: the speculative AI donation and the sweater both have essentially unmeasurable long-run consequences, yet I call one strongly good and the other morally neutral. Voting and recycling both have negligible marginal causal impact, yet I call one nearly obligatory and the other not required at all. And the AI donation and the child have the same clueless long-run structure, yet I ground the goodness of one in its effects and refuse to ground the other that way.

Here is the principle I suspect is doing the work, which I will call the horizon principle: an act's moral status is fixed by the expected value of its consequences within the horizon over which I can assign non-trivial credences, and beyond that horizon acts inherit the moral status of their reference class. Please test it against the cases, repair it where it fails, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. Then say what the repaired principle predicts for a case I have not listed: choosing between two careers, one with clear and modest local benefit and one whose payoff is entirely speculative and far off. If no single principle can hold all the verdicts, give me the smallest set of candidates, name the case that motivates each clause, and say which candidate I should prefer once I see what it costs me.
