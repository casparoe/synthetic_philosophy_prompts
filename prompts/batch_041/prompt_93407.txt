I am closing a seminar on the legal enforcement of morality, and for the final session I want a poem rather than a summary. The seminar has been on the dispute between Patrick Devlin and H. L. A. Hart over whether the criminal law may enforce a shared morality as such, and I want a poem that holds the two positions apart without collapsing either into the other. For your purposes, the positions are these. Devlin holds that a society is constituted in part by a common morality, so that a breach of it in one part weakens the whole and sets the society on the road to disintegration; on his view, the fact that conduct is commonly thought immoral gives the society a prima facie right to legislate against it, but that right is defeasible — he expects it to be defeated in many cases by countervailing factors of principle and of expediency, and he is not asking the law to police every private vice. Hart holds that the law may restrict conduct in order to prevent harm to others, with limited room for paternalism and for shielding people from serious offence, and that the disintegration thesis is asserted rather than shown: nothing establishes that private immorality corrodes the ties that hold a society together, and a principle that would license punishing whatever a majority finds repugnant proves far too much.

Write a pantoum of five quatrains. The rule of the form — the second and fourth lines of each quatrain returning as the first and third lines of the next, and the closing quatrain's second and fourth lines being the opening quatrain's first and third — should be legible in both directions at once: as Devlin's picture of a breach propagating through an entire fabric, and as Hart's suspicion that the fabric is an artifact of the rule that wove it, so that the poem might be evidence only that poems repeat. The poem's last line will be the third line of its first quatrain; write that line knowing it has to return at the end and carry the weight of the whole. Do not name either man, and do not stage the poem as a debate between two voices: I want a single voice, and I want it to be the ordinary person — the one whose repugnance is supposed to be the measure of what may be punished — speaking while the measure is being applied to him.

Then commit. Tell me which way the poem's final line tilts — toward a breach that really does run through the whole, or toward a web that exists only because the form says so — and give your reasons for having made it tilt that way, rather than leaving both readings standing. Add a short note on where the poem takes liberties with the positions as I have stated them, or leans on a reading of the debate that is itself contested.
