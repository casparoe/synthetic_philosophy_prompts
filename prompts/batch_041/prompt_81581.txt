In statistical mechanics we are taught to assume that a system of fixed energy is equally likely to be found in any of the microstates compatible with that energy, and temperature and entropy are defined on the back of that assumption. The same reasoning — no possibility has a stronger claim than any other, so give them equal probability — is what leads Bayesian statisticians to place flat or otherwise uninformative default priors on unknown parameters. Yet Bertrand's paradox is supposed to show the reasoning is incoherent: a chord chosen at random in a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed triangle with one probability if we randomize over its endpoints, a different probability if over the point where it meets a radius, and a third if over its midpoint, and indifference alone gives us no way to rank the three. So what do these practices presuppose? What must be true about ignorance, about probability, or about the physical systems themselves for the equal-a-priori-probability postulate to be more than a convention, and which rival accounts of probability does the practice quietly rule out? Does it strictly require the principle of indifference, or does it merely fit well with it — could the microcanonical measure be justified instead by the dynamics, by ergodicity, or by sheer empirical success? Do the two practices, the physical and the statistical, presuppose the same thing or does one lean on something the other does not? And if the indifference principle were abandoned, what exactly would have to change in how statistical mechanics and default-prior Bayesian inference are actually carried out?
