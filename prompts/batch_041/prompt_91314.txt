I'm a second-year PhD student in early modern philosophy, currently writing a seminar paper on Montaigne's essay "Of Cannibals," and I keep running into a problem at the very beginning of the text that none of the standard relativism-versus-universalism readings seems to address. In the seventeenth-century translation I've been working from, the essay opens with a pair of anecdotes about foreign commanders who, confronted with the visible order of a Roman army, are forced to withdraw the label "barbarian," and then Montaigne draws this conclusion:

> When King Pyrrhus invaded Italy, having viewed and considered the order of the army the Romans sent out to meet him; "I know not," said he, "what kind of barbarians" (for so the Greeks called all other nations) "these may be; but the disposition of this army that I see has nothing of barbarism in it."—[Plutarch, Life of Pyrrhus, c. 8.]—As much said the Greeks of that which Flaminius brought into their country; and Philip, beholding from an eminence the order and distribution of the Roman camp formed in his kingdom by Publius Sulpicius Galba, spake to the same effect. By which it appears how cautious men ought to be of taking things upon trust from vulgar opinion, and that we are to judge by the eye of reason, and not from common report.

What I want help with is the inference in that last sentence. The anecdotes seem to show only that direct sensory exposure to a foreign practice can override a received prejudice — Pyrrhus sees the camp and revises his opinion. But the maxim Montaigne extracts from them is stronger and, I think, of a different kind: it installs "the eye of reason" as a faculty capable of adjudicating between opinions, not merely as a corrective to prejudice, and it treats "vulgar opinion" and "common report" as though they were the same thing when they plainly are not. I've been wondering whether the maxim is even meant as a doctrinal premise, or whether it is doing rhetorical work of the sort one finds in the Renaissance paradox tradition — where an opening commonplace sets up a thesis the essay will then complicate rather than defend — in which case the apparent gap between anecdote and maxim might be deliberate rather than a slip. Could you give me a close reading of the passage and its immediate context that takes this gap seriously, distinguishing carefully between what the anecdotes establish about perception and what the maxim claims about reason, and then say something about how that distinction bears on the essay's later treatment of the Brazilians? I'm not looking for a bibliography or a list of secondary sources; I want the conceptual reconstruction itself. Please write in American English.
