Essay draft:

The standard contrast is between outcome-based supervision, which rewards or penalizes the final answer, and process-based supervision, which rewards or penalizes the steps by which the answer was reached. The usual case for process supervision is that outcome supervision is blind to how the result was obtained: a model can get the right answer for the wrong reasons, or get the wrong answer while reasoning well. The usual case for outcome supervision is that process labels are expensive and that a process which looks good can be a rationalization generated after the fact.

I want to defend a third option, which I will call counterfactual process supervision. The idea is not to grade the trace the model actually produced, but to grade the trace it would have been likely to produce under small, controlled changes to the problem -- changes that should not alter the correct method, only the incidental details. If the model's success survives those changes, that is evidence that the process was robust; if it collapses, the original success was probably fragile. On this view, what we supervise is not the actual chain of thought but a disposition: a stable tendency to reach the answer by a method that would still work when the surface changes.

This is supposed to combine the strengths of the other two. Like outcome supervision, it does not require us to trust the model's own narration of its reasoning. Like process supervision, it is sensitive to how the answer was reached, because it tests the method rather than the answer alone. And it gives us a way to talk about cases where the outcome is right but the process is bad: those are cases where the success is not counterfactually stable.

The obvious objection is that this just pushes the problem back. If the model can detect the perturbations and adjust, then counterfactual stability is as gameable as a written rationale. My reply is that the perturbations need not be hidden from the model; they need only be drawn from a distribution that the model cannot reliably distinguish from the distribution of real deployment inputs. But I am not sure this reply is more than a promissory note.

The deeper worry is about what "the same method" means. Counterfactual process supervision seems to presuppose a way of individuating methods across changes in surface features, and I do not have a theory of that. Without one, the proposal may be circular: it tells us to reward the method that would succeed under perturbation, but the only way we know the method is by seeing what succeeds under perturbation.

So my tentative conclusion is that the distinction between process and outcome supervision is less fundamental than the distinction between supervision that is sensitive to counterfactual robustness and supervision that is not. If that is right, the real question is not "process or outcome?" but "which counterfactuals?" I would like to know whether this is a genuine advance or just a redescription of the standard trade-off, and where the essay's weakest link is.
