I retired from a zoology department about six years ago — thirty-odd years of population ecology, and since then I've been trying to teach myself philosophy with the time I used to spend writing grant reports, with mixed results. Last month I floated a puzzle at dinner at my neighbours' place and it turned into a fight I still can't referee, so I'm handing it to you.

The puzzle was a one-shot prisoner's dilemma with the usual numbers: 3 each if both cooperate, 5 if you defect on a cooperator, 1 each if both defect, 0 if you cooperate and get taken. No repetition, no names, no reputations, no way for either of them to affect what the other does later, and nobody knows who they're playing against. Everyone knows you defect in that setup. The wrinkle I added was this: both players know that each of them will decide by running exactly the same deterministic procedure on exactly the same description of the situation, so whatever the procedure spits out for one of them it spits out for the other, necessarily. I thought it was a cute bit of decoration. Ruth thought it changed everything, her brother Sam thought it changed nothing, and off they went. Roughly, over the evening:

(1) Ruth: the two choices aren't two choices, they're one choice. Since the same procedure produces both answers, the branches "I defect and they cooperate" (5 for me) and "I cooperate and they defect" (0 for me) aren't on the menu — the only reachable outcomes are (3,3) and (1,1). Defecting isn't dominant, it's just worse.

(2) Sam: that's sleight of hand. Dominance is a claim about what happens when you hold the other person's behaviour fixed. Hold it at cooperate and defecting gets you 5 instead of 3; hold it at defect and defecting gets you 1 instead of 0. Defecting wins in both cases, that's the entire content of the claim, and Ruth has quietly redefined "fixed" to get her answer. You can't make the other person's action a function of yours and still claim to be talking about dominance.

(3) Ruth: the counterfactual "I defect and they, running the same procedure, cooperate anyway" is incoherent — like saying "I'll raise my right arm and my reflection will keep both arms down." A rule for deciding has to rank the outcomes the agent can actually bring about, and if the two players' actions are literally the same event, then the choice is between the pair (3,3) and the pair (1,1), full stop.

(4) Sam: then it's a knife edge with no content, because "identical procedures" is doing all the work. Let there be any difference at all — different priors, one of them tired, one of them wondering whether the other is really running the same rule — and the outputs come apart and defecting is right again. Worse, the reasoning eats itself: if it's rational to run a procedure that says cooperate, it's equally rational for a copy of you to run one that says cooperate-if-and-only-if-the-other-cooperates-otherwise-defect, and now the two of you diverge, so one of you is being irrational and there's nothing in the setup to say which.

(5) Ruth: every argument in decision theory depends on the world being some particular way; that's not a special fragility. Nobody knows with certainty what the other person is doing, they have a credence, and once you're thinking in credences the correlation doesn't have to be perfect — it only has to be strong enough. And I don't need you two to be built from the same atoms; I only need the fact that you're the sort of creature who reasons this way to be evidence that they are too.

(6) Sam: grant the whole thing and it still explains nothing, because it can't survive selection. Suppose some lineages are wired to run the cooperate procedure. A mutant wired to run the defect procedure collects 5 whenever it meets one of them and 1 when it meets another mutant, while the cooperators get 3 against each other and 0 against the mutants. Do the sums and the mutant wins in any well-mixed population. Cooperation gets wiped out unless cooperators only ever meet cooperators — and if you have to assume that, you may as well have started there and skipped the clever part about identical procedures.

(7) Ruth: you keep changing the question. I never said anything about where the behaviour came from. I said what a certain kind of agent has reason to do in a single encounter, and whether that's stable over generations is a separate question you're welcome to answer however you like. Sam's reply to that was that a reason no lineage could ever act on isn't a reason, it's a curiosity, and that was about where they left it.

Could you adjudicate move by move — for each one, right, wrong, or right for the wrong reason, with a sentence or two of justification? I'd especially like to know (a) whether Sam's dominance argument in (2) actually answers Ruth's point in (1) or merely restates it, (b) whether her credence move in (5) rescues her, and if so roughly how strong the correlation has to be, given the payoffs I gave, before cooperating beats defecting, and (c) whether Sam's evolutionary round in (6) is a genuine objection or a change of subject. Don't split the difference just to be polite — if one of them is simply right I'd rather hear it — and please tell me what each of them is missing, because I have a strong suspicion it's the same thing in both cases and I can't put my finger on it.
