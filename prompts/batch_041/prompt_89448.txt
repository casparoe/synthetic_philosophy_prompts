I am currently taking a seminar on moral uncertainty, and I have been asked to write an essay on Ursula K. Le Guin's 1973 story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas. The story is familiar enough: Omelas is a city whose happiness depends on the perpetual misery of a single child; most citizens, once they learn of the child, accept the bargain, while a few quietly leave and are not seen again. Our seminar has spent several weeks on the standard decision-theoretic apparatus for moral uncertainty—expected choiceworthiness, intertheoretic comparison, whether one can treat moral theories as if they were credences over empirical hypotheses—and I have found that apparatus strangely unhelpful for this story. The ones who walk away do not appear to be maximizing anything, they do not announce a superior moral theory, and they do not seem to be weighing probabilities. They simply refuse. I want a reading of the story through the lens of moral uncertainty that treats it as a story rather than as a puzzle case, and that is honest about where the philosophical framework illuminates the walkers and where it distorts them.

Here is a distinction I have been trying out, and I would like you to test it against the story: theory-uncertainty, where one does not know which first-order moral theory is correct, versus competence-uncertainty, where one has reason to suspect that one's moral judgment is systematically warped by one's participation in a morally compromised arrangement. The citizens of Omelas may be uncertain in the second way even if they are confident in the first: they may know what utilitarianism and deontology each require and still suspect that their own acceptance is not a judgment at all but a symptom of the city's conditioning. If that distinction is real, then the walkers-away are not hedging against the possibility that utilitarianism is false; they are refusing to trust the version of themselves that Omelas has produced. But I am not sure the distinction survives contact with the story. Does it illuminate the walkers' choice, or does it let them off too easily by converting what might be a failure of moral courage into an epistemic predicament? Does Le Guin give us any reason to think the walkers are right, or does she deliberately withhold a verdict? Which scenes or structural features of the story support your reading, and where does the story resist it?

Please give me a committed reading rather than a survey of possible interpretations. Tell me which interpretation you think is strongest, defend it against at least one serious rival, and say what a philosopher working on moral uncertainty should take from the story that the decision-theoretic literature tends to miss. I would also like you to say where the story is genuinely ambiguous in a way that no theory can resolve, and whether that ambiguity is a defect or the point. Do not give me a bibliography or a reading list; I want the argument and the reading itself.
