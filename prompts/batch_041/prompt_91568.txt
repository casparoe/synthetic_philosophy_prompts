Not "usually bad" — always wrong, and the fact that it feels wrong to say that in certain cases is supposed to be a fact about us rather than about the rule.

I want to actually hold this view, but I'm worried it's the kind of view that can't lose: every time I think of a case that would sink it, there's a move where you say "ah but that wasn't a lie" or "ah but you didn't need to lie there". So what would actually count against it? What's the strongest case against it, and is that case decisive or just awkward? Sort it from "this really hurts" down to "the view shrugs this off", and I want to know which ones the defenders themselves would admit are bad, not just the ones critics like to bring up.

Two things I keep getting stuck on, and I think they're where the action is. First, one I came up with at work: we shipped a consent dialog that isn't false anywhere — every sentence is literally true — but it's laid out so nobody reads the second page and basically everyone clicks agree. Nobody lied. Is that a lie in Augustine's sense? If his view says no, is that a feature of the view or does it show the view is aimed at the wrong target entirely? Second, the case where what you say is false but you don't mean to deceive anyone (a joke, a story, a bit of roleplay), and the mirror case where you say something you believe is false and it just happens to be true. Where does the view actually cut on those, and does the answer there settle whether it's a view about the words or about the intent?

And the meta question: is this position definately falsifiable, or does it just relabel any apparent counterexample? If it does, is that a problem for a moral view, or is that just what a moral view is?
