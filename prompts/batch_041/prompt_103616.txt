I'm giving the welcome talk at orientation for this year's cohort of visiting grad students — about eighty people, mostly scientists and economists from a dozen countries, plus maybe five philosophers in the room who'll catch any reference I drop. I want to give them something better than "when in Rome." Specifically: a way to sort the local conventions they're about to run into — tipping, queueing in one line vs. crowding the counter, waiting for the walk signal on an empty street at 3am, standing on the right of the escalator — into the ones they're genuinely obliged to follow and the ones they can skip without owing anyone an apology, and why the two piles are different. The philosophy needs to actually do the work, not decorate it: I want the whole thing built around the difference between conventions that exist to coordinate us (so breaking them is a kind of taking-without-paying, a fairness problem) and conventions that are just local markers (so insisting on them is the thing that's rude), and I want it to be honest that the line between the two is often contested. Land it for the non-philosophers — concrete cases, no jargon — but dont water down the part the philosophers will be listening for, which is whether "it's the local norm" ever by itself creates an obligation, or whether it always needs something else to lean on. Five minutes, to be spoken aloud.
