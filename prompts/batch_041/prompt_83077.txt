I've been an architect for about twenty years, mostly restoration and retrofit work, and someone recently pushed me towards the Gould and Lewontin thing about spandrels — the argument that a lot of what looks designed in nature is really the space left over once you've solved some other problem, and that the stories biologists tell about what each feature is for are the same kind of story I invent when a client asks why the windows are that shape. I don't know any biolgy beyond school, but I know buildings, and I want a proper translation job, not a nice analogy.

So take the main moves in that critique — that a trait can be a byproduct rather than an adaptation; that some traits are just consequences of the body plan, the way a vault gives you a spandrel whether you wanted one or not; that drift and constraint do a lot of the explaining; that the habit of asking what each thing is for will generate a story about anything, including things that aren't there; and the distinction I've seen made between something being present because selection built it for a job and something being present because it hitched a ride on something else that was selected — and put each one into the vocabulary I actually use. Standard details. The module and the grid. Load-bearing versus applied ornament. Listed-building constraints. What happens when the supplier changes the sheet size. Services routes. The detail nobody can justify that's still in the manual because of a problem we had in 1998.

For each one, tell me whether it lands cleanly, lands only with a fudge, or has nothing on my side to land on at all. Then the part I actually want: the leftovers. My world is full of intentions — briefs, drawings, clients, somebody deciding — and yours isn't, or at least the whole force of the argument is that it isn't. So tell me what in the biologists' position can't be said in my vocabulary at all, and for each of those, whether the gap is my vocabulary missing something or the biologists quietly borrowing the thing my vocabulary has and theirs can't (a designer, a purpose, a reason that somebody had). If it's the second, say what the argument looks like once you strip that out, and whether it survives the stripping.

One test case so you can't stay abstract. Our practice has a detail we call the ghost gap: a 30mm recess above every window head, on every job, for thirty years. It exists because in 1998 a joiner was 15mm out on a lintel and someone hid it. Nobody has specified it on purpose since, but its in the standard set, clients now recognise our buildings by it, and one of our competitors has started doing it too. Is that a spandrel, an adaptation, an exaptation, all three at different times, or is the question itself malformed in my terms — and does your answer tell me whether to keep it on the next job? Be blunt about which of my translation moves is cheating.
