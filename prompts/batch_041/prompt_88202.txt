Right, I retired two years ago after 35 years doing ecology and population modelling, and I've spent most of retirement working through philosophy — decision theory and game theory mostly, since thats closest to what I did for a living. My reading group keeps telling me my views don't hang together and I want an audit before I present. Here's where I've landed.

(1) Rationality is just maximizing expected payoff given your credences. Get the beliefs right and maximize and you're rational; there's no further question about the will.

(2) You can't be rationally required to do something that's against your interest at the moment of doing it. A "committment" to carry out a threat or a promise you'd have no reason to keep when the time came is just cheap talk — it doesn't change what you should do, and it shouldn't change what anyone predicts you'll do.

(3) Backward induction is right for finite games where everyone knows the payoffs and the possible moves. In the centipede — the version where the pot grows every time we both pass, whoever takes first gets a bit more than they'd have got if the other took on the next move, and if we both pass all the way we both clean up — the answer is that I take on move one, and if I know my opponent is a maximizer who knows I'm one too, that's the end of it.

(4) But if I actually sat down to play centipede with a friend, I'd pass for a good long while, and I don't think I'd be being irrational. I'd be playing a person, not a proof.

(5) The surprise exam is different. The students who reason backwards to "so it can't be any day, so there's no exam" are fooling themselves; a teacher can announce a surprise exam and have it be one. Backward induction is a bad fit there.

(6) When I have to guess what another person will do, I start from the assumption that they reason roughly the way I do — same kind of mind, same kind of reasoning. That was my working method in science and it's my default in games too.

The group says (3) and (4) can't both be true. I think they can, because of (1): (1) makes rationality relative to what you believe, and against a friend I just have different beliefs about what he'll do than I do against a proof-machine. So (3) is the answer under one set of beliefs and (4) is the answer under another and there's no contradiction. What I want to know is whether that move actually works, and whether anything else in the list is pulling against something else. I have a feeling (5) is doing something funny but I can't put my finger on it.

Format please:

- Verdict up front — three or four sentences. Does the package hold, and which single pair is in the worst shape?
- The one I flagged — short paragraph. Does my (1)-based reconciliation of (3) and (4) actually work, yes or no, and if no, what exactly breaks?
- Tensions I didn't spot — the main event, and the longest section. Every pair that's genuinely in tension, worst first. For each: how bad is it, which of the two has to give, and what the repaired version looks like. If two of the items on your list are really the same problem, say so instead of counting them twice.
- False alarms — pairs that look like they clash but don't, one or two sentences each on why not.
- Cheapest fix — the one revision that cleans up the most, and what it costs me.

Don't just tell me pairs are "in tension"; I want to know which one bends.
