I am a novelist, partway through a collection of stories about how people live with their pasts, and I need one more piece for it. What follows is a commission brief; I would like you to write the story, and then a short note about the story addressed to me. Please take the role of a literary novelist with a serious amateur's knowledge of nineteenth-century German thought — the kind of writer who reads philosophy for images rather than for doctrine. Work carefully: before drafting, spend some time thinking on the page about whose story this is, what each character wants, and where the pressure between them actually sits, because the piece will fail if the ideas are pasted onto people instead of growing out of them.

The material I want you to work from is the second of Nietzsche's Untimely Meditations, from 1874, the one about the uses and disadvantages of history for life. You will know the three relations to the past he distinguishes: the monumental, which looks back for models and heroes and makes the past usable for action; the antiquarian, which reveres and preserves what has been inherited, down to the smallest local detail; and the critical, which sits in judgment on the past and tries to break with it. What interests me is the less quotable half of the argument. He insists that each of the three is healthy only insofar as it serves present life, and that each turns poisonous in excess: the monumental falsifies by assimilating the past to a present ideal, and so breeds fanatics; the antiquarian can decay into mere collecting, into a reverence that leaves a person incapable of acting at all; and the critical is self-undermining, because we are ourselves the product of the inheritance we are condemning, so that judging the past is also judging ourselves. He also holds that forgetting is not a failure but an active power, that a person's health depends on a plastic power of absorbing and reshaping what has been, and that art and religion offer a standpoint from which the whole historical fever looks beside the point.

Your brief:

- Set the story in a single institution with a real stake in its own past — a family firm, a village archive, a repertory opera house, a seed bank, a shipyard, a school. Your choice, but make it concrete and specific enough that it could not be swapped for any other.
- Do not use the words monumental, antiquarian, critical, plastic, unhistorical, or supra-historical anywhere in the story; do not mention Nietzsche or the essay; and do not have anyone explain the scheme. The three relations to the past must be embodied in three characters, each of whom is partly right and each of whom is self-deceived in a way the story shows rather than states, and none of whom is your mouthpiece.
- Structure the story so that a single past event reaches us in three incompatible accounts, but never in the event's own voice. The reader should be able to reconstruct what probably happened, and should also see that each account is doing work for the person who tells it.
- Include one scene in which a monumentalizing account of the past is falsified by the very act of telling it, and one in which the antiquarian's care for detail is given a genuinely good case, so that the reader feels the loss if it is thrown away.
- The critical character must aim their condemnation at something that constitutes them — a name, an inheritance, a professional formation, a debt — so that the hardest moment in the story is the discovery that the tribunal and the accused are the same person.
- Give forgetting a body: someone must be shown to forget on purpose, and the story must not treat this as simply a vice.
- End without settling which relation to the past is right. I would rather the reader be left with the cost of each than with a moral.

Length: roughly 1,800 to 2,400 words.

After the story, add a note of 300 to 500 words addressed to me rather than to a reader, in which you are explicit about your epistemic position. Separate what is settled about this text — what any careful reader of it would agree it says — from what is genuinely contested among people who study it, and say plainly when you are leaning on a contested reading. Among the contested points I would like you to address: whether the three modes are meant as three kinds of people or as three options available to anyone; whether the essay rejects historical inquiry or only its excess; how far it coheres with the genealogical work he did afterwards; and whether its nostalgia for a more unified culture can be detached from the politics it has sometimes been recruited for. Do not present any contested reading as the consensus, flag the places where the story takes liberties with the ideas rather than illustrating them, and where you are uncertain about the text, say so in the note instead of smoothing it over.
