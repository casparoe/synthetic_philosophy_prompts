I've been chewing on a puzzle that I suspect is under-theorized, and I'd like you to think it through with me rather than summarize the literature — which, as far as I can tell, mostly talks past the question.

There's a phenomenon psychologists have a name for: paltering, deceiving someone by means of statements that are literally true. You answer a different question than the one asked; you volunteer a true fact that invites a false inference; you let a wrong impression stand uncorrected. What interests me is how the surrounding institutions treat it, because I keep finding that the same legal system takes opposite positions on whether literal truth is a shield. Consider four familiar practices:

1. Perjury. A witness who says something literally true but calculated to mislead generally cannot be convicted of perjury; the doctrine places the burden on the questioner to pin the witness down. The crime requires literal falsity.
2. Fraud and securities regulation. Here the law does the reverse: it prohibits misleading statements outright, and an omission that makes what you did say misleading is itself actionable. Literal truth is no defense.
3. Advertising. Vague, subjective boasting is written off as non-actionable puffery, while an implied factual claim can be actionable even though it was never stated in so many words.
4. Defamation. Substantial truth is a defense, so a literally true statement is generally protected — yet a technically true statement can still lose protection if the overall impression it creates is false.

So we get, within one system, both the view that literal truth is a shield and the view that it is not.

I want to know what theory of the lying/misleading distinction each of these practices presupposes. Reconstruct the implicit theory behind each: does perjury's requirement of literal falsity presuppose that misleading is a lesser wrong, or merely that misleading is harder to adjudicate? Does securities law presuppose the opposite — that the wrong lies in the intent to deceive, so that lying and misleading are on a par? Say which rival theories each practice quietly rules out, and separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Then tell me whether the four are actually inconsistent or only appear to be, and where each is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories of the lying/misleading distinction would license the same rule. Finally: if we adopted the "intent to deceive" theory — treating paltering as a species of lying — what would have to change in each of these practices, and would anything be lost?

Give me the analysis as prose, then a structured summary.

First, a table with these columns: Practice; Domain; Implicit theory of the lying/misleading distinction; Rival theory it excludes; Strictly presupposed or merely compatible (with a one-line justification).

Then a JSON object with these fields: "presupposed_theory_per_practice" (object mapping each practice to a short phrase); "consistency_verdict" (one of: consistent, apparent-only, genuinely inconsistent); "underdetermination" (short string); "what_would_change_under_intent_theory" (array of strings); "residual_disagreement" (short string).
