I am a novelist, and I have been asked to run a single session at an interdisciplinary summer institute: sixteen participants, about half of them graduate students in philosophy or economics and the rest writers, journalists, and policy people, none of them specialists in welfare economics. I have one hundred minutes, one room with four tables, a whiteboard, a timer, and no computers. I want to build the session around a committee that has to decide something, because it is the scene I keep writing and failing to write: a group of decent people in a room, all being reasonable, all in possession of every fact anyone could ask for, who still cannot produce a defensible answer. What I want them to feel is the precise moment when an interpersonal comparison becomes unavoidable — when the group has to say that one person's loss outweighs another's gain, and has to notice that it has just supplied a fact it does not have. Not the arithmetic of it. The vertigo of it.

Design that session for me, and be concrete enough that a colleague could run it without me.

The scenario should be fictional, low-stakes, and textured — a town, a fund, a set of named characters with real situations — with four or five indivisible packages and six to eight characters, and no real money anywhere. Give me the full preference tables. Construct them deliberately so that no package is best for everyone, so that the intuitively obvious answer is not reachable from the information the group actually holds, and so that any tie-break requires comparing how much one character cares against how much another cares.

Rules and materials:

- One dossier per participant, naming a character and giving a complete, strict ranking over the packages, with an explicit note that intensities were never elicited and the characters cannot be re-interviewed.
- The group must return one recommendation plus a written justification; unanimity required; one recorder; two or three auditors whose standing job is to challenge any utterance of the form "his loss is worse than her gain."
- A warrant device: a small number of tokens the group may spend to have the facilitator read out a prewritten additional fact. Make each fact genuinely useful but never sufficient — the kind of fact that helps only if you have already accepted some scale. Tell me what the facts should say and why each one fails to close the question.
- A pre-commitment step: each participant privately writes, before any discussion, what they think the fair answer is; the reveal happens after the recommendation is written.
- Phase two: sealed cards go to four participants revealing interval-scale scores for their characters — numbers, with an arbitrary zero point and an arbitrary unit — while everyone else keeps only rankings. The group redoes the decision. Then the facilitator performs, live, the demonstration that decides the session: the same numbers, rescaled person by person, reverse the recommendation. Give me two specific rescalings that flip the result, and show me how to put that arithmetic on the whiteboard in under three minutes.
- A two-character swap case: two characters exchange packages, their rankings structurally identical. Show exactly what the group can and cannot say about that pair, and what the silence reveals.
- A debrief that maps what happened onto the substance: why the group's tie-break was covertly either a procedure or a dictator; the difference between comparing levels of well-being and comparing gains; the ladder from ordinal non-comparability through level comparability and unit comparability to full comparability, with one plain sentence each on what that assumption buys and what it costs; and the escape routes — refusing to rank at all, and handing the decision to a lottery, a bargain, or deliberation — including what each of those routes quietly assumes.

Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run and how to handle it. I expect at least six failure modes with fixes: someone will invent a zero-to-ten scale; someone will argue the trouble is subjectivity and the remedy is better measurement; the group will reach consensus too fast for the wrong reason; one participant will derail everything by insisting on procedure; someone will conflate utility with money; phase two will consume the debrief.

Close with an honest account of what the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must simply assert in words.

No citations, no reading list, no references to particular books or papers; if a named view helps you make a point, name it in a clause and move on. Write in American English.
