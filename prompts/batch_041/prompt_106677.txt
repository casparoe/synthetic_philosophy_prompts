My father was diagnosed with early-onset Alzheimer's a little over a year ago, and a question I had previously met only in philosophy seminars has become the thing I think about on the drive to the care home: in what sense is the person I visit still my father? I have written up notes for a short essay and would like your comments — chiefly on whether the metaphysics is stated correctly, and secondarily on whether the central argument is genuinely interesting or merely a picture dressed up as an argument. I am not asking for reading suggestions; I want the argument assessed.

The notes are these. The debate between endurantism and perdurantism is normally put as a question about how ordinary objects persist. The endurantist says that an object is wholly present at each moment at which it exists: the whole man is there at the wedding and the whole man is there in the care home. The perdurantist says that an object is a four-dimensional whole composed of temporal parts, so that at any given moment only a proper part of the object is present: the man at the wedding and the man in the care home are two parts of one longer thing.

My thesis is that the perdurantist picture, usually described as cold or alienating, is the one that fits what it is actually like to love someone through dementia, and that the endurantist picture, which sounds warmer, is the crueller of the two. Perdurantism lets me say two things at once: that the person in front of me is not now the man who taught me to fish, and that the man who taught me to fish has not been destroyed, because he is a part of a whole that still exists. Endurantism, by insisting that it is one and the same man throughout, forces me to say that the man who taught me to fish is now unable to recognise me, which makes the loss total in a way I do not believe it is.

I want to add two further moves. First, the stage view, on which we are not four-dimensional worms but momentary stages, and what makes it true that I am the same person as the child in the photograph is not identity but a counterpart relation between stages. Second, the claim that on the perdurantist view my father will still exist after he dies, since his earlier temporal parts remain part of reality; death ends the worm, it does not remove it.

Two things worry me. I have never understood whether the problem of temporary intrinsics is a real problem or an artefact of how the question is posed, and I suspect my argument may be doing no more than selecting the picture that consoles me and calling it the true one. Please tell me where the notes misstate the positions, where the argument actually has force, and where it collapses into preference.
