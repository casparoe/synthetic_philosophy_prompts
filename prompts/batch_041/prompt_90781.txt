I'm building out a set of fiction prompts for a philosophy-adjacent dataset, and I want one that's a bit off the beaten track -- no asteroid movies, no "scientists announce the end of the world" cold open.

Here's the philosophical seed. Don't name it in the story and don't let any character explain it. There's a thought experiment, I'm fairly sure it's Samuel Scheffler's, where you're asked to compare two futures: (a) you die soon, but humanity keeps going for a very long time; (b) you personally get to live out a full natural life, but human life ends early on -- long before you would have died anyway. Most people say (b) is worse. Worse than their own death. And the puzzle is why, because if (b) is bad only because of all the deaths it contains, that's just a bigger pile of the same badness, and people don't seem to be registering it that way. They register it as a different kind of badness, as though their work and their loves would be drained of point by the absence of any human future to inherit them. Scheffler's guess is that we can't value what we value except against the taken-for-granted backdrop of a continuing human life.

So: the story. Two things I care about.

First, set the ending far enough out that nobody alive will ever see it. Not a countdown -- something like three centuries away. The whole point is that everyone in the story was going to be dead by then regardless, and it still hollows out what they're doing. Give me a small, specific, unglamorous milieu where people are mid-project on something that only pays off if there are people after them: a herbarium, a repertory company, a seed bank, a dialect dictionary, an organ restoration, whatever you can make concrete. No scientists delivering exposition, no broadcasts, no montage of riots. The premise should arrive sideways, through the work itself and through the things people find they can no longer do.

Second, make it harder on them in one specific way: give them a live alternative that isn't plain extinction. Something that would carry on the *value* of what they're doing without being human -- a machine archive that will preserve the work perfectly and understand none of it, a lineage of non-human minds, an audience from somewhere else, something unsentimental and concrete. Then split the cast: one character thinks this is enough, that what matters is that the thing keeps being valued by someone; another thinks it isn't, that it was never about value in the abstract, it was about us. Don't resolve it. Let each be right in a way the other can't absorb.

Length 1200-2000 words, any POV as long as it stays tight. Then, separate from the story, a short note on where the story takes liberties with the idea, or leans on one reading of it where people who work on this genuinely disagree -- I don't want the story smuggling in one side as the settled answer. In that note be explicit about which parts of the picture are agreed on and which are up for grabs.
