Over the past few months I have been sitting in on a reading group on causal inference, and I keep encountering a distinction that I have come to suspect is not a real distinction at all. The way it is presented, there are two rival accounts of what it is for one thing to cause another. On the older counterfactual account, C causes E just in case E counterfactually depends on C: had C not happened, E would not have happened. On the newer interventionist account, C causes E just in case an ideal intervention that sets C to a different value would change E. The interventionist is supposed to have the edge because she need not talk about possible worlds and comparative similarity; she talks only about what happens when you intervene on C in the right way. My problem is that an intervention is itself characterized counterfactually. To say that some variable intervenes on C with respect to E is to say, among other things, that if the intervention variable had taken a different value then C would have taken a different value, and that the intervention leaves everything downstream of C undisturbed. So the interventionist appears to be helping herself to counterfactual talk at the foundation of a theory whose entire selling point was supposed to be that it dispenses with counterfactuals.

And it gets worse. It seems to me that any counterfactual dependence whatsoever can be reproduced by some intervention: enrich the model with enough variables, choose the intervention so that it holds fixed everything except C, and you will have manufactured exactly the counterfactual dependence the old account cared about. If the two frameworks are intertranslatable in that way, and if the interventionist's primitive is itself either counterfactual or causal — an intervention that breaks the arrows into C is, after all, a causal notion — then I cannot see how there is any substantive disagreement left to have. The whole thing begins to look like a verbal preference for saying surgical manipulation over possible world.

So my question is whether I should stop worrying about this. Is there a genuine, non-verbal disagreement between the counterfactual and the interventionist accounts, or does the observation that each can be cashed out in the other's vocabulary show that the distinction is spurious? If there is a real disagreement, I would like to see it on a concrete case rather than in the abstract — something like the example my group got stuck on, an early-life exposure such as season of birth that no one can actually manipulate but that appears to raise the risk of a later condition. I want to understand what the interventionist is committed to saying about a case like that, what the counterfactual theorist says instead, and why the difference matters rather than being a matter of taste.
