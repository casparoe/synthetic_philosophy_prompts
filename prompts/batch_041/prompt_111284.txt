I sit on the statutes commitee at my college, which every five years goes through the bye-laws and votes on which ones to scrap; most of what we're voting on is a century old or more and nobody has any idea why any of it exists. My colleague Ruth has Chesterton's fence on a mug and calls herself a Burkean. Her stated rule is that you don't pull a fence until you can tell her what it's holding up, and that once you can name the thing and see it's gone, you pull away — she calls the ones she keeps load-bearing. I think that's actually a slightly different rule from Chesterton's, which is about us not knowing why a fence went up at all, whereas Ruth wants to know what job the fence is doing, but either way her votes don't line up with it.

Here's her record. She voted to keep the bye-law requiring the head porter to fetch the front-gate key from the bursars office across the court each morning before he opens the gate; the reason for it is lost, the fellows queue in the rain while he walks over, and he gets paid an extra hour he doesn't mind. She voted to keep the bye-law, from the 1830s, that the accounts be read aloud at the fellowship's annual meeting — two hours, nobody listens, no record of why. She voted to keep the rule that the fellows process into hall in gowns on the first Sunday of term, which is in the 1880s statutes and which they all grumble about. She voted to scrap the 2003 rule that first-years wear gowns to formal hall, which did have a nameable purpose, marking them out so the older students would look after them, and plainly wasn't achieving it. And she voted to scrap the 1998 rule that the kitchen staff sign in and out of the lodge, which is the one that doesn't fit, because nobody could say what that book was for; when I pressed her she said its purpose was the bursar's record-keeping, and that five minutes of a kitchen porter's morning isn't worth the bursar's comfort.

So I can't tell whether she's running age (old things stay, new things go), or who pays (in every case she kept, the cost landed on people who get a vote; in every case she scrapped, it landed on people who don't), or just whoever complains.

The next item is the bye-law that the porters stand up when a fellow passes them in the lodge. It's in the 1880s statutes, the college historian thinks the deference reading is a Victorian gloss on something older, so nobody can say what it's holding up, and the porters, who are the lowest paid people here and have no vote on anything, have just filed a written complaint about it.

What will she vote, and which of those rules do you think she's really running? Say what each candidate predicts here and why you'd bet on the one you pick. Then tell me what else to put on the agenda to settle it — I want a case where the candidates come apart the other way round, so I'm not just reading one vote. And if she votes to keep it, tell me what she'd have to say about the 1998 sign-in book to stay consistent.

Write it as prose please, no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables — just an email.
