I'm the branch secretary for a care workers' union and I've got to give a speech at the picket line on Monday morning — first day of a two-week strike at the home. Four minutes, maybe five, so about six hundred words. I'm not counting on the day, so don't hand me an essay.

The crowd's a mix. Forty-odd members who voted yes. A handful who voted no and are still furious — one of them told me at the meeting that me asking her to come out is no different from the boss telling her to go in. Four or five agency staff bussed in to cover, standing across the road getting looked at like dirt. Two of the residents' families, who are frightened and haven't decided what they think of us. And a lad from the local paper who'll print whatever I say.

I don't want the usual rally speech. Not because I disagree with it, but because half the people standing there know it isn't that simple, and I'll lose them in the first thirty seconds. Everyone chants "we have the right to organise" as if that settled something, and it doesn't, not for the woman who wants to go in.

What I want instead is to make one distinction stick. I've honestly never heard anyone else put it this way: a strike is two things wearing one word. There's the refusal — me declining to sell my labour on those terms — and that's mine alone, I'd defend it to anyone, it's just me not doing a thing. And then there's the enforcement — the line, the pressure on the ones who'd rather go in, the fact that we make it cost the home something to run without us. Its a different kind of act, not the first thing done by more people, and it's where all the moral trouble lives. I think it's still justified. I want the speech to say so without lying about what it costs the people on the other side of it, including the agency staff, who I don't think have all chosen freely.

Keep it plain — no jargon, nothing that sounds like a lecture, nothing I'd have to explain twice.

Don't put any quotes or famous lines in it. I can't check anything from the car park and if I misattribute something the local paper will have a field day.

And be straight with me. If the refusal/enforcement split is just the old free-rider thing with a new coat of paint, or if it falls over the moment you push on it, say so and tell me where it breaks, instead of writing me a lovely speech around an idea that doesn't hold up. Same if doing this honestly means conceding something I can't concede in front of forty people on a Monday morning — then tell me that and I'll write something else. I'd definately rather know.
