I've been having a long, slightly ridiculous argument with my flatmate — she's finishing a PhD in philosophy of mind, I do cog sci — about whether dreams are experiences. Her line (she says it's Malcolm's): a report of having dreamt something is a retrospective judgement, the concept of dreaming is anchored in the waking telling, and the idea that there's an inner film running all night that we then recall badly is folk theory rather than an observation. My line, roughly the received view: something is happening in there, it's just that recall is terrible and the reports are reconstructions. We've been argueing in circles for months and I want us to do it properly — so write the joint statement we could both put our names to after a real adversarial collaboration. Give me a numbered section of agreed claims (what a dream report is and isn't; what the sceptic's argument from the unreliability of reports does and doesn't establish; what either of us would accept as evidence about a state occurring during sleep; what's definitional and what's empirical), then a disagreements table. In the table, for each remaining disagreement: the neutral statement of it, each side's own preferred wording of its own view (phrased so we'd each actually sign it), the crux underneath it, and for each crux a concrete test or case we both agree would bear on it, with both of our predictions written down in advance. If any of this turns out to be a verbal dispute — she suspects at least the bit about whether dreaming names a state or a practice is — say which part and why, and seperate that from whatever's left that's genuine. No references or lit review needed, we both know the literature; I care alot about the arguments and the tests.

Some material I want you to actually use rather than gesture at, because it's the stuff we fight about: lucid dreamers who signal with pre-agreed eye movements during REM and then, on waking, report doing the thing they signalled — is the signal a report made from inside the dream or just behaviour? The dream-enactment patients, the REM behaviour disorder ones who punch and shout in their sleep, and sleep-talkers whose utterances seem to line up with what they later describe — there an observer is getting dream content without waiting for a report. Stimuli delivered during REM, a spray of water or someone saying a name, turning up sometimes distorted in the report. And a test I'd genuinely like us to run: wake people and either take the report immediately or make them do a short unrelated task first, then see whether the narrative gets thinner, vaguer or reorganised — if the dream is largely composed at waking that should show up, if it's a memory of something that happened maybe less so.

One more thing: keep three things apart the whole way through, because we keep collapsing them — the telling, the memory, and whatever if anything was going on at the time. And if it turns out that Malcolm and Dennett aren't really on opposite sides of this at all, say so plainly, that would be useful to know.
