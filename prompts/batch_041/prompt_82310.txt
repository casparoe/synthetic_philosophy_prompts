I have no academic training of any kind. I repair and restore old machinery for a living — mostly antique machine tools, plus the occasional clock — and I have spent an unreasonable number of evenings reading popular accounts of evolutionary biology, which is where I ran into a dispute among biologists that I have not been able to put down since. As I understand it, the accusation is that the field has a habit of taking an organism apart into separate features and then telling a story about why each one is present because it helped its owner survive; that this habit functions less as a method than as a reflex, since when one story fails another is invented rather than the approach being questioned; and that the possibility a feature is present for no reason at all — because it is what you inevitably get when you build a body that way, or because it spread for causes that have nothing to do with doing a job — is the last thing anyone considers. The famous illustration, I gather, is architectural: triangular pieces of stone where a dome settles onto the arches beneath it, present because of the geometry of the structure rather than because anyone wanted triangles. The defense, as I understand it, is that the accusation is overstated, that asking whether a feature is an adaptation is perfectly respectable provided you are willing to test your answer, and that the critics never quite said what a better research programme would look like.

What I want from you is a translation of this whole dispute — the accusation and the defense both — into the vocabulary I actually think in, which is the vocabulary of a man who opens up machines he did not design. In my world, a feature I cannot immediately explain falls into one of four piles, and I would like to know what the biologists would call each pile and whether the correspondence is honest or forced. The piles are: first, it is there because it does a job, and what job can usually be read off from what wears against what; second, it is there because the process that made the part demanded it — a fillet where two surfaces meet, because a casting cannot have a sharp inside corner, a taper on a wall so the pattern can be drawn out of the sand, a thickened pad because a thread needs material to bite into; third, it is there because an earlier version of the machine had it, and the drawing, the tooling, or a customer's habit carried it forward long after whatever reason it once had had vanished; fourth, it is there because somebody wanted it or thought it looked right, and nothing has ever depended on it either way.

Then give me the residue report, and do not be polite about the translation. Tell me which of the biologists' claims pass through those four piles without changing shape, which have to be bent or fudged to fit, and which have no home in my vocabulary whatsoever. I am suspicious of three things in particular. First, every question of what a part is for, in my world, bottoms out in somebody's intention, even when the intention is long lost; the biologists have to get the same question answered with no designer anywhere, and I want to know whether my vocabulary can express their answer at all, or whether I am quietly smuggling a maker back in every time I say that something is there for a purpose. Second, everything I know is about one object in front of me, whereas their claims are about populations and frequencies across stretches of time I cannot picture; tell me what becomes of a claim like this spread for no reason when the only thing you can talk about is a single machine. Third, in my trade an explanation is tested by building the alternative and watching it fail — if I believe a rib keeps a casting from warping, I can pour one without the rib and see it warp — and they cannot do that with an organism, so I want to know whether their argument about whether these stories are testable at all survives the move into my terms, or whether it is a worry that exists only because their subject is unlike mine.

One thing nags at me and I want it addressed directly. I suspect the critics' central example belongs in my second pile, and I am not sure it does, because the reason my fillet exists is the mould, whereas the reason their triangular stones exist is, so far as I can tell, another feature that is itself supposed to be an adaptation. Whether that difference is a quibble or the whole point is exactly what I cannot work out on my own.

Finally, tell me what to conclude from the residue. If my four piles cannot carry their argument, is that because my vocabulary is missing something real — a way of speaking about things shaped over generations with no shaper — or is it because the argument is doing two jobs with one word, using the same term for a feature that is forced by structure and a feature that is merely unexplained? I would rather be told that my way of thinking is inadequate than be handed a translation that flatters it.
