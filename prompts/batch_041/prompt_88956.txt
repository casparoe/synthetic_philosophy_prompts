After thirty-five years as an experimental physicist I retired last spring, and I have spent much of the past year reading epistemology, largely in the evenings and largely badly. I have become stuck on an argument that a friend in my reading group reconstructed from a puzzle she tells me is well known in the literature, and I cannot decide whether it is a conjuring trick or a proof. I would be grateful if you would diagnose it. As she set it out:

(1) I know that P. Take P to be something I am as entitled as anyone to claim as knowledge — that the table before me is solid, say, or that water is composed of hydrogen and oxygen.
(2) If I know that P, then P is true.
(3) Any evidence that tells against a true proposition is misleading evidence; it points away from the way things actually are.
(4) So, from (1), (2) and (3), I know that any evidence telling against P is misleading.
(5) To know that a piece of evidence is misleading is to have a reason to disregard it.
(6) Therefore, by virtue of (1) alone, I already have a reason to disregard every piece of evidence that will ever tell against P.
(7) Nothing in the argument turned on which proposition P was, beyond its being known.
(8) Therefore, for any proposition I know, I have a reason in advance to disregard all future evidence against it.
(9) In other words, a person who knows something has, simply by knowing it, a standing licence to ignore whatever arrives later. Knowledge would be self-sealing.
(10) This is intolerable. Were (1) through (9) sound, a scientist who knows a theory would have no reason ever to run another experiment on it, and I have spent a career believing precisely the opposite — that knowledge is the thing new evidence is able to overturn.
(11) So either we never know anything, or knowledge is a species of dogmatism.

I am attached to (1), at least for mundane cases, and I am attached to (10). I would rather not end up a sceptic, and I would rather not end up defending dogmatism either. Tell me which premise or step is the one to give up, why it looked so plausible in the first place, and what giving it up costs. If more than one step can be sacrificed, compare the price of each rather than quietly choosing one. Then tell me where your own diagnosis is most likely to fail — what a better-trained philosopher would say to unsettle it, and which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict.
