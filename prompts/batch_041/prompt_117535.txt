My friend and I spent an evening arguing about this and I still can't see who won. She laid out the following case for giving up on consequentialism, and my instinct is that it proves far too much:

(1) A theory of right action earns its keep only if it sometimes ranks the options actually open to an agent.
(2) Consequentialism ranks options by their expected value.
(3) Expected value requires determinate probabilities for the outcomes of each option.
(4) For almost any option, its long-run consequences are the product of chaotic amplification of tiny differences — whether I answer this email tonight or in the morning cascades into who I meet, what they do, and what happens after that — and no determinate probabilities attach to those downstream consequences.
(5) So consequentialism assigns almost no option a determinate value.
(6) An option with no determinate value cannot be ranked against its alternatives.
(7) So consequentialism almost never ranks options.
(8) So consequentialism should be abandoned.

She then pushed further: the same reasoning applies to every option, so nothing is ever better than anything else, and the very idea of a normative ranking is a fiction. When I objected that this would wreck ordinary prudential deliberation too, she said that was fine — that it is a general truth about acting under chaos, not a problem specific to her target.

I can't accept (8), and I'm not prepared to say that rescuing a drowning child and buying a snack come out equal; I'd like to keep the thought that consequences matter at least sometimes. So tell me which step she should give up, and what giving it up would cost her. Some of these premises look innocent to me and I can't tell which one is doing the damage.
