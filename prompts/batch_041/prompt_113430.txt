Suppose I take a lump of clay and mould it into a statue. Later I squash it back into a shapeless lump. It seems obvious that the statue and the lump of clay are not two different things but one and the same thing described in two ways: first as so much clay, then as a statue. After all, they are made of exactly the same matter, they occupy exactly the same region of space, and at every moment at which the statue exists, the lump of clay exists too. If two things share all their matter and all their spatiotemporal location, what possible basis could there be for insisting that they are two?

But this comfortable answer runs into trouble the moment we ask about persistence. The lump of clay survives being squashed; the statue does not. The lump of clay existed before I began moulding, as a formless mass; the statue did not exist until the moulding was complete. So we have a difference in properties — being such as to survive squashing, having existed at an earlier time — and by Leibniz's Law, things that differ in their properties are not identical. So the statue and the lump cannot be one thing after all.

My suggestion is that we should not be panicked by this argument. What it shows is that the clay and the statue are two objects in the sense that they fall under different sortals, not that they are two separate occupants of space. The clay constitutes the statue; the statue is not identical with the clay but is made of it. Constitution is a relation of material dependence between a thing and the stuff or things it is made from, and it is not identity. Once we see this, the original puzzle dissolves: there is only one parcel of matter in the region, and it is correct to describe it both as a lump of clay and as a statue, depending on which sortal we are interested in. The apparent contradiction — that the same thing both survives and fails to survive squashing — was an artefact of confusing the object with the description.
