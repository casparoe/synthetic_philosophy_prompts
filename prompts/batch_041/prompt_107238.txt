I have been reading popular accounts of societal collapse — the end of the Bronze Age palace economies, the Maya, Rapa Nui — and I keep noticing a structure in the explanations for which I have no term. I should say at the outset that I am not after a reading list; I want the concept, if it exists, or else a well-made one.

The structure is as follows. When an account declares that a society collapsed, what has actually been shown to end is almost always some particular feature: a centralised administration, a monumental tradition, a script, a dense settlement, a trade network. The people themselves rarely vanish; they disperse, simplify, move, or continue under different arrangements, often for centuries. The 'collapse', then, seems to be indexed to whatever the investigator had been using as the mark of complexity in the first place, so that a change of mark turns the same archaeology into a story of continuity.

The part I cannot fit to any term I know is this. Sometimes the vanished feature was genuinely load-bearing: people had organised their lives around it, and its loss brought famine, violence, or a lasting fall in welfare. At other times the feature was little more than an artefact of the analyst's convenience — a pottery style, a class of monument — and its disappearance tells us nothing about how anyone lived. Both cases are filed under the same word, and that word carries a moral charge of hubris or overshoot. I suspect the charge attaches chiefly when we take ourselves to be the descendants or the successors, so that one and the same structural event reads as tragedy for one audience and as comeuppance for another.

My question is therefore twofold. First, is there an established concept for the distinction between the loss of a structure a society actually depended upon and the loss of the indicator by which we were measuring it — a distinction I take to be real, and which the single word 'collapse' obscures? Second, is there a name for the way the same event is narrated as decline or as transformation according to the narrator's stake in it? If neither has a settled name, please coin one for each, define it, and say where it sits relative to the nearest existing ideas — decline, transformation, resilience, adaptation — and what would count as a boundary case on either side.
