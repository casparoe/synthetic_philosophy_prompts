I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and over the past few years I've come to hold a thesis I now state without much hedging: famines are not natural disasters. Mass starvation, on this view, is always the output of human arrangements — entitlement systems, market behavior, relief policy, war, deliberate denial — and a drought or a blight is at most a trigger, never a cause. Amartya Sen's entitlement approach and his contention that famines do not occur in functioning democracies with a free press form the spine of it; the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the Great Leap Forward are the cases I reach for when I argue it.

What I want from you is an honest map of this thesis's exposure, not a defense of it. What would have to be true for it to be false? I'm after the specific kinds of evidence — historical cases, comparisons, the internal structure of the argument — that should move me, sorted by how decisive they would actually be rather than how often they get cited. I'd like you to work through the cases I'd expect a critic to raise and say what each one really cuts against: a famine in which a natural shock looks causally sufficient rather than merely triggering (the blight in Ireland; the cyclone and the severing of rice imports from Burma in Bengal; the droughts behind the Sahel and Somali famines); the 1974 Bangladesh famine, where the standard story is that food was available and entitlements collapsed anyway; Botswana's drought response in the early 1980s, where a severe drought produced no famine; pre-modern subsistence famines, where an entitlement framework built for market economies may simply not transfer; the Dutch hunger winter of 1944-45, with its embargo; the 1945 famine in Japanese-occupied Vietnam. I also want the modern politics of the label itself treated as part of the question: the technical criteria used to certify a famine, and the fact that declaring one is a political act with consequences for aid and for who gets blamed — which may mean the thesis is partly true of the word rather than the thing.

Then the rival political explanations: Alex de Waal's emphasis on violence and predation as against Sen's market-and-entitlement account, and the more mixed picture Cormac Ó Gráda draws — is that split within the political camp a problem for the thesis or a refinement of it? What does the thesis predict that a natural-disaster account does not, and have those predictions been tested? Is it falsifiable as stated, or has it been protected by quietly narrowing what counts as a functioning democracy and by treating any political ingredient in any famine as confirmation? If it turns out to be well insulated, say so and say whether that is a vice here.

Tell me what a committed defender of the thesis would themselves concede as counting against it, as distinct from what critics assert. For what it's worth, I think I would be most moved by a well-documented famine in which relief was available, requested, and refused for no strategic or ideological reason at all — that would break the crime story for me. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know famine history and famine economics, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward.
