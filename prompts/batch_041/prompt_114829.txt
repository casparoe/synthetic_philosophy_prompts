I retired from chemistry three years ago and I've been makeing up for lost time with philosophy — I run a monthly evening group for about a dozen retired scientists and engineers, sharp people, no formal philosophy training, and next month the topic is mine. I want to do: is death bad for you? Specifically the old Epicurean argument — that death can't be bad for the person who dies, because where death is, we aren't, and where we are, death isn't.

Don't explain the argument to me, plan how I should explain it. Seventy-five minutes, a whiteboard, and a room that will get impatient with anything that smells like word games. What has to be on the board before the question even makes sense to them? My guess is that the whole thing only gets going once they have some notion of a life being measured against the life it could have been, but tell me if that's the wrong place to start. Which single example should carry the session, and what does it need to have and to lack — I was going to lean on the symmetry between the eternity before I was born and the eternity after, and I want to know whether that's a case or a cheap trick. The misconceptions they'll bring and the new ones they'll form, in the order they'll actually surface — that Epicurus is doing therapy rather than metaphysics, that bad means bad to experience, that the argument only works if you're tired of living — and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off. Someone will ask me when the badness is supposed to happen; tell me whether to answer that or park it, and how to park it without looking evasive. Then one question I can put to the room at the end that tells me whether they've got the problem rather than just the punchline. And tell me what I should flatly refuse to get into at this level — and say so out loud.
