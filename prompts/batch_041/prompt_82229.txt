I'm grading for a second-year metaphysics course and I've got a stack of these to get through, so I'd like a second opinion on this one rather than agonising over it myself. The question I set was:

(a) What is a rigid designator, and what's supposed to be non-rigid about a definite description? (b) Kripke claims there are truths that are necessary but knowable only a posteriori. Explain how that's meant to work, using Hesperus/Phosphorus and water/H2O. (c) The descriptivist theory of names says a name is synonymous with a definite description. State the modal objection to that theory and say whether the descriptivist has any reply. (d) A collaboration announces a new particle and christens it 'Glion', glossed as 'the particle responsible for the excess events in the 3.5 TeV channel'. Two years later the excess turns out to have been a calibration artefact. On the Kripkean picture, does 'Glion' refer to anything in the actual history? And in the counterfactual history where the excess was real and came from a new particle? What does your answer show about how reference gets fixed?

Here's what the student handed in:

(a) A rigid designator is a term that designates the same object in every possible world in which it designates anything at all. Proper names are the standard case: 'Aristotle' picks out Aristotle whether we're considering the actual world or a world where he never met Alexander. Definite descriptions don't behave like that — 'the teacher of Alexander' picks out whoever happens to have taught Alexander in the world under consideration, which is a different person in different worlds.

(b) 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' are both rigid, and as it happens they name the same object, Venus. Because they're rigid, once they co-refer in the actual world they co-refer everywhere, so 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' is necessary; and since a rigid designator tracks its object across every world, Venus exists in all of them too. But the co-reference was discovered rather than deduced — it took the Babylonians noticing that the evening star and the morning star were one and the same. So the proposition is necessary but known a posteriori. 'Water is H2O' has the same shape: 'water' is rigid for a natural kind, and it took chemistry to tell us which kind, so again necessary a posteriori. The moral is that the necessary/contingent distinction and the a priori/a posteriori distinction are orthogonal, which a lot of people before Kripke assumed they weren't.

(c) If 'Aristotle' just meant 'the teacher of Alexander', then 'Aristotle might not have taught Alexander' would come out false, since it would be saying that the teacher of Alexander might not have taught Alexander. But it's obviously true — Aristotle could have died in infancy and never taught anyone. Names are rigid and descriptions aren't, so a name can't be synonymous with a description, and descriptivism is refuted.

(d) In the actual history there was never any particle doing the causing, so the baptism had nothing to latch onto and 'Glion' is empty, like 'Vulcan'. In the counterfactual history the excesses really were produced by a new particle, and that's the object the name would have been attached to, so there 'Glion' refers to it. So the reference of a name isn't secured by the description being satisfied — otherwise the name would refer in the first history and not the second, which is the opposite of what we want to say.
