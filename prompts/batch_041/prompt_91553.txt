I am in a foundations-of-cognitive-science seminar where I have been asked to defend the claim that the brain is a computer, and I have discovered that I cannot argue the point at all until I know what an account of computational implementation would have to deliver before it could be used to settle the question. Everything anyone says in the seminar — that the brain is or is not a computer, that a system merely as-if computes, that a description is 'just' a description — presupposes some standard for when a physical system really implements a computation, and nobody has stated it. So before I write anything, I would like the desiderata: the requirements that any adequate account of what it is for a physical system to implement a computation must satisfy, each one stated as a requirement and accompanied by the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, with a clear indication of which of them are non-negotiable and which are simply commitments of one camp rather than genuine constraints.

I want the list built against a particular set of cases, since those are the ones my argument will turn on and I do not want a list that only handles the easy ones. (1) The triviality cases: if any physical system with enough parts can be described as implementing any finite-state machine with few enough states, the account must explain why that is not fatal, and it must not do so by appealing to what an observer finds it convenient to say. (2) The analog and the dubious cases: a slide rule, an integrator circuit, a riverbed, a folding protein, a weather system — for each, the account should let us say whether the system computes a function or whether that is a projection of the describer's interests, and if the answer is that it depends on the describer, the account should say on what, precisely. (3) The lab cases: a spiking network in which no individual neuron carries a symbol and the putative computation lives in a population trajectory; a system whose dynamics are noisy, so that any state-to-state description holds only approximately; and a system whose computation is defined over a timescale far longer than the lifetime of any of its parts. (4) The case that set me off: two systems said to compute the same function whose internal states cannot be put into any useful correspondence with one another, so that the account must say what makes them implementations of one computation rather than two computations that happen to agree at the output. For each requirement, give the case it is answerable to; and if you think one of my four cases is not really a case, say so and say what it actually shows.

The part I most want is the negative result. Once the requirements are listed, tell me whether some subset of them is jointly unsatisfiable, and which one I should give up if so; my own suspicion is that nontriviality, the objectivity of implementation (its not being a fact about the describer), and the explanatory autonomy of computational posits in cognitive science (the thing that lets a modeler say the system is computing a particular function, not merely being described as if it were) cannot all be had at once, and that each familiar escape route buys one by paying for another — but I would rather be told that I have mis-drawn the space. Also tell me which requirement a reasonable person is most likely to deny, since that is where I expect the seminar to push. I do not need references or a survey of who has said what; I need the criteria themselves, and I will do the comparison against the positions we have read on my own.
