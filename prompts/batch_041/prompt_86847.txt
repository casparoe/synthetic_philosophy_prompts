I'm a science journalist, and for the past two months I've been reporting a feature, for a general-interest ideas magazine, on the recurring fight over what it means to say that some biological structure has a function. The ostensible peg is the long argument over how much of the genome is functional, but every interview slides away from DNA into cases about hearts and beaks and tails, and I've been leaning on one source in particular — a philosopher of biology who is unusually willing to rule on specific examples and who refuses, with what I take to be some pleasure, to say which of the textbook positions she holds. Her rulings, as I've collected them across three long interviews: the function of the heart is to pump blood; the peacock's tail is there to attract mates; the human appendix has no function, though the rabbit's does, since it ferments cellulose; the whiteness of bone is not a function of anything but a byproduct of how bone is laid down; the honeybee's waggle dance has the function of telling other bees where the nectar is; the capacity of adults in dairying populations to digest milk sugar has a function, being both recent and plainly favored; the human chin has no function. What I can't pin down is her test. When I asked about the peacock she said a trait's function is whatever it was selected to do. When I asked about the appendix she said it has no function because nothing in the body now depends on it. Those are not obviously the same criterion, and when I pointed that out she smiled and said that the interesting cases are the ones where the two come apart, which is exactly the sentence I want to be able to use in the piece.

Here is the case I need her on, and the reason I'm asking you rather than waiting for our next call. A colleague of hers has a paper arguing that a small patch of cortex in the left temporal lobe — the region that reliably lights up whenever a literate adult reads, and whose destruction selectively destroys reading while leaving object recognition, speech and everything else more or less intact — has the function of recognizing written words, in the same sense in which the heart's function is pumping blood. Nobody supposes that literacy is old enough to have been selected for; nobody supposes the patch is doing nothing; the disagreement is about what follows from that combination. If I put the case to her, what does she say, and why? I am less interested in her answer than in the machinery behind it.

So: lay out the candidate rules that fit everything she has said so far, say what each of them predicts about the reading case, and note any other case on which they come apart. Then tell me which rule you think she is actually running, how confident you are, and what would move you off it — including the possibility that her record is genuinely underdetermined by her verdicts and that only her rhetoric has been inconsistent, which I would rather you say plainly than paper over with an invented synthesis. Finally, name the single case I should put to her next: the one where the candidate rules give opposite verdicts, so that her answer would tell me which test she has been using all along rather than merely adding another data point to a record that already accommodates both. I file in three weeks and I'd like to walk into that interview knowing what question to end on. No need for citations or a reading list; I'll handle that side of it myself.
