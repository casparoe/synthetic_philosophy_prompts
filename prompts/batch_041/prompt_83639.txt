I teach a 300-level phil of mind seminar (18 students, one 90-min block, flat room, movable chairs) and I want to run a game that makes the physical/design/intentional distinction — and the 'real patterns' idea sitting under it — something they feel in their hands instead of a taxonomy they nod along to. They shouldn't hear the words stance, intentional, or Dennett's name until the debrief. Here's the mechanic I've been sketching; change it as much as you need:

Prediction game against black boxes. Four rounds, teams of 3-4. In each round I show them a system's observable behaviour only (positions, outputs, moves) and the teams have to file, for the next three outputs, (a) a prediction and (b) the rule they used to get it, written in 25 words or fewer. Scoring: +2 per correct prediction, +5 bonus if a rival team can regenerate your predictions from your rule alone after a 30-second read, -1 if you go over the word budget. No money, just points on the board.

Rounds I want to build cheaply, no special kit:
1. A deterministic physical process they can't see into — a laptop running a Game of Life pattern and showing only the generations, or a Galton board.
2. A machine with a spec — a vending machine, or a little program that sorts by a hidden rule.
3. A critter — a simulated animal, seen only as a trail of positions, whose hidden rule is something like 'move toward the brighter of two lights, coin-flip on ties'.
4. An imposter — something with no designer and no internal anything that still yields to goal-talk. My favourite is a moth spiraling into a flame because of a fixed light-orientation rule: 'it's trying to reach the flame' is short, predicts well for a while, and then fails badly.

What I want them to come out seeing: that the stance you take is a bet on which description buys the most prediction per word, not a claim about what's really in there; that the critter's preference is 'real' in the sense that the short description keeps earning its keep, supports counterfactuals, and beats every rival under budget; and that the moth has a goal-shaped pattern with nobody home.

Give me: rules and materials in runnable form, a timeline for the 90 minutes, the payoff scheme, and a debrief that maps each round onto the idea — including the bit where round 3 makes intentional talk feel unavoidable and round 4 makes it feel like a trick. Then the two honest sections: what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and which feature of the idea the activity can't reproduce so I have to supply it myself. My guess is it can't reproduce the normative/idealized side (we predict the rational agent, and all my systems are simple enough to be roughly rational) or the realism dispute itself, since the game only ever measures compression — but tell me if I'm wrong about that, and be blunt about anything in the design that's just going to be dead air or chaos.
