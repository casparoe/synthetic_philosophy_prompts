Okay, context first: I'm a novelist, three books in, the last two set in a made-up river town on a flood plain, and the one I'm writing now sprawls across about a hundred and fifty years — four generations of a family that ran the mill, a dam nobody agrees about, the river changing its mind about where it wants to be. My friend Dana, a historian (Ottoman Anatolia, don't ask), read the first hundred pages and said it was "very Braudelian," which she meant kindly but I've been chewing on it for a week. I read The Mediterranean maybe fifteen years ago, half of it, and I sat through a seminar on the longue durée that I remember mostly as people arguing about whether the sea is the protagonist, so what I've got is the gist: three speeds of time — the geography that barely moves, the slow decades-long drift of economies and habits of mind, and then the fast layer of wars and kings and coups, which he thought was basically froth, the least informative layer of the three; something about mountain people and plain people running the same quarrel for two thousand years; and a general hostility to great-man, one-damn-thing-after-another history.

Here's what I want. Take that and restate it, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually think in: scene versus summary, backstory, setting, what a chapter can hold, the gap between what my close third-person narrator can see and what the book quietly asserts over her head, arc, subplot, the world of the story versus the plot of the story, the stuff my characters never notice because it's the water they swim in. Don't smooth it over — if "structure" has to get bent into "setting" to fit, tell me it got bent, because setting is a thing I chose on purpose and my people can look right at it, and I don't think that's what he meant at all.

Then the part I actually care about, the residue report. Which of his claims come through clean, which come through distorted, and which have no counterpart in craft vocabulary whatsoever — and for that last pile, say whether it's my vocabulary that's missing something (there are ways a life gets shaped that I maybe don't have words for) or whether his claim was vaguer than it sounds once you try to write it down. The one I keep circling: is the longue durée even a claim about the world, or just advice about how to tell a story — and if it's advice, whose story, the historian's or mine? And does his demotion of the event survive translation at all, given that in a novel the event is basically the only thing I've got? Is the novelistic equivalent of his froth a vivid scene that teaches you nothing, or is there no equivalent because fiction just doesn't have his problem? Definately no reading list, I'm offline and I'd rather have the argument. And then, short: was Dana's compliment about my book, or only about its tempo?
