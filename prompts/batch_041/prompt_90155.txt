I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and over the last year or so I've settled into a position in decision theory that I would like you to attack as hard as it can be attacked.

The position is this: when I have to choose among options without knowing how the world will turn out, the correct choice is the one with the best expected outcome given that I choose it. I should treat my own action as one more piece of evidence about the world — the way a model conditions on a new feature — rather than as a cause operating independently of the world's other features. In practice: I should imagine having already decided on some option, ask how I now expect things to go, and pick the option that looks best from inside that supposition.

What pushed me toward this is a scenario I can't stop turning over. An assassin — call it Death — has left a sealed prediction of where I'll be tomorrow, and I can either stay in my city or take the last train to another one. The prediction is reliable, and Death goes wherever it expects me to be. If I stay, I can reason that the prediction must have said I stay, so Death is here and I die here; therefore I should leave. But if I leave, I can reason that the prediction must have said I leave, so Death is there and I die there; therefore I should stay. The evidentialist reading seems to say both options are equally fatal, and worse, whichever one I tentatively pick, the same reasoning shoves me toward the other. I genuinely cannot tell whether that looping is a defect in the evidential approach or merely an artifact of a case in which I am doomed either way.

Tell me what would count against the position I've described. I want the evidence, arguments, intuitions, and formal results that should move me, ordered from what would be genuinely decisive down to what would be merely awkward, and I want you to distinguish clearly between the objections the position's own defenders would already concede and the ones only its critics press. Tell me what this position predicts that its main rival does not — the view that I should ignore what my choice says about the world and act only on what my choice causally brings about — and where the two actually come apart in cases I can picture.

A few specific things I want addressed. Does the looping I described constitute a real objection, or can an evidentialist explain it away? Is there a version of the assassin case in which the evidential approach returns an answer that no one, on reflection, would accept? And I'm worried this might be the kind of position that cannot lose: if every awkward case can be redescribed so that the evidential answer comes out right, say so plainly, and tell me whether that is a fatal problem or an acceptable cost for a theory of this kind. Finally, since I work with predictive models for a living, I want to know how much of the puzzle's force depends on the predictor being perfect, and what becomes of the argument when the predictor is merely good — right, say, nine times out of ten — or when it is a model whose internals I partly understand and whose behavior I can therefore anticipate.
