Since I retired from experimental physics I have been reading history and philosophy in roughly equal measure, and I have found myself stuck on a conceptual problem that I would like you to explain to me carefully. It arose while I was reading about the long controversy over the origins of the First World War — the dispute, associated above all with Fritz Fischer, over whether Germany bore the primary responsibility for the war. What interests me is not who is right about the documents but a conceptual knot underneath the whole quarrel. It seems to me that at least three different things get asserted under the single phrase "Germany's responsibility": a causal claim (that the war would not have happened, or would not have happened as it did, but for German decisions), a moral claim (that those decisions were blameworthy), and a collective claim (that the bearer of the blame is a nation, a people, rather than one or more persons). I can see how the first is the kind of claim a scientist might in principle test by counterfactual reasoning; I cannot see how the second and third attach to the first, if they attach at all. Worse, the structural explanations that historians offer — the alliance system, the arms race, the domestic and economic pressures that Fischer's critics stressed — seem to dissolve the very agent to whom guilt is supposed to belong: if the war was the output of a system, where is the person, or the people, who is guilty?

Please explain this conceptual issue properly. I want to understand what it means, in the most defensible sense, to hold a nation responsible for a war, and how the three claims I have separated actually relate to one another — in particular, whether moral blame can survive an explanation that is mostly structural, and whether "national guilt" is a coherent moral concept or an explanatory metaphor that historians would do better to drop. Use the First World War debate as the working example, but I want the conceptual anatomy, not a narrative of the controversy or a survey of who said what. Treat the distinction I have drawn as a proposal to be examined rather than a framework to be filled in: if the three claims are not really separable, or if the line between the causal and the moral runs somewhere else, say so and redraw it.

I would much rather you take a definite position than lay out the options and leave the choice to me. Tell me which of the three claims you think is doing the real work in the phrase "war guilt," which of them I should give up, what a nation can coherently be blamed for if anything, and why — and then tell me where your own position is most exposed. I am a beginner in this field and would rather be corrected than reassured.
