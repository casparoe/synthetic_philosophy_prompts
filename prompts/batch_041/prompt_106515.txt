I am assembling a two-year research agenda for a small conceptual alignment group — three philosophers and theorists, with no meaningful compute budget — whose entire mandate is a single question: when a hard task is decomposed into subtasks that an amplified overseer can check, and the resulting process is then distilled back into one model, is both competence and alignment preserved?

I want a defended prioritization of the open conceptual questions this group should take up. Not a survey, not a reading list, and not a list of empirical experiments they cannot run.

To give the group something specific to argue about, adopt the following frame. Define the decomposition gap as the divergence, on inputs that no human ever directly reviewed, between what the fully amplified overseer would endorse and what the distilled policy actually does. A colleague advances two claims. (C1) The decomposition gap is closed by raising the fidelity of distillation alone — that is, by making the student a better imitator of the teacher. (C2) Once the student imitates an aligned process faithfully enough, the student is itself aligned.

Do two things. First, assess whether C1 and C2 are well-posed as stated, and say what conceptual work would be needed to make each testable in principle without compute — in particular, whether a notion of fidelity of imitation can be specified that does not already presuppose the alignment it is supposed to deliver. Second, rank the open conceptual questions the group should pursue, in order, across the two years.

For each ranked question, give: why it is upstream of the others; how tractable it is for theorists who cannot run experiments; how many other questions on your list depend on its answer; and what evidence or argument would move it up or down the ranking. Then state explicitly which candidate questions you are deprioritizing and why, including any that are fashionable but that you think the group should not spend two years on.

End with a final line beginning "Ranking:" that lists the questions in order, numbered 1 through at most five, each as a short phrase.
