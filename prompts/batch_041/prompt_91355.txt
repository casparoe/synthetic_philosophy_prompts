I'm putting together a session for our department's student-paper series, and the format is a 45-minute Q&A with a visiting philosopher. This terms theme is metaethics without truth, and our guest has spent a career defending a broadly Quinean line on moral values: moral judgments aren't true or false in the way that claims in physics or even in naturalized epistemology are, they're more like instruments or prescriptions that help us coordinate living together, and the study of them belongs on the engineering side of the ledger rather than the science side, i.e. norms relative to ends we happen to have rather than discoveries about a moral order. I've read alot of his stuff and I know he'll have polished answers ready, so I want questions that press on the seams instead of letting him restate the position. Could you draft me eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up that's keyed to the answer he's most likely to give?

A few things I specifically want covered, since these are the spots where I think the view gets uncomfortable. First, when he denies that moral judgments are truth-apt, how is that supposed to differ from the older emotivist and prescriptivist proposals he's definately aware of — what does the naturalist framing actually add, and what would he say to someone who thinks it's the same old non-cognitivism wearing a scientific-sounding coat? Second, the engineering analogy: if ethical norms are tools relative to ends, where do the ends themselves come from, and can he say anything about which ends are non-negotiable without smuggling in a truth-apt claim about what's good? Third, and this is the one I most want, the self-application worry. In his naturalized epistemology there's still a normative dimension — norms of theory choice, simplicity, conservatism, what counts as a good explanation — and he's been happy to call that a kind of engineering too. If the norms of inquiry are instrumental in the same way moral norms are, doesn't that make his own epistemic standards just as optional as any moral code? And if he resists that conclusion, what exactly is the disanalogy between the two cases?

Also work in something about moral disagreement and moral change: if there's no fact of the matter, what is a moral argument actually doing, and can his picture make sense of the thought that some moral changes are improvements rather than just shifts in what people happen to want? And I'd like one question about the awkward fit between this meta-view and the fairly conservative substantive positions he's defended at various points in his career — does the meta-view predict anything about those, or is it a separate layer that leaves him free to hold whatever attitudes he likes? The audience is grad students who've read a bit of Quine but not much metaethics, so keep the questions concrete and skip the jargon-dumps. And please don't hand me questions that could be asked of any non-cognitivist — the whole point is to make him talk about what's distinctive about doing this as a naturalist, and about what his own commitments end up costing him.
