I sit on the planning subcommittee of my residents' association, and at the last consultation on a scheme for 240 flats on a disused bus depot — eighteen per cent of them 'affordable' at eighty per cent of market rent, the rest for private sale, one access road, no new surgery, nine storeys where the tallest thing for half a mile is a church spire — a woman stood up and said that the developers had no right to the city. The room nodded. Nobody, myself included, could have said what she had just asserted, and I have been turning it over since. I hear the phrase everywhere now: at council meetings; in the campaign against the depot scheme; in a pamphlet arguing that our tenants' association ought to be a statutory consultee; in a column claiming that a city belongs to those who live in it rather than those who own it; and once, memorably, from a developer's own consultant, explaining why a gated block with a private courtyard would still be 'part of the city'. I am told the slogan has a pedigree — a French Marxist urbanist writing in the 1960s, though I have never met anyone who uses it and has read him — but that tells me nothing about what it is doing when it is used.

What I want is not a definition but a disambiguation. Lay out the distinct precise claims the sentence could be making, ordered from the trivial to the radical, and for each say what kind of claim it is (a legal entitlement, a moral claim, a claim about who ought to decide, a claim about what a city is for), whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or something false or merely rhetorical, and what it would commit its holder to. In particular, I want the carving at the edges. Does the right attach to individuals or to collectives, and if collectives, which ones — all residents of a city, or only those of a particular neighbourhood? Does it require inhabitance, or a particular tenure, or a minimum period of residence? Is it a right to a place (access, affordability, security, not being displaced), a right to a process (a say in what gets built), a right to a form (density, mixed use, public space), or a claim about use value set against exchange value? Can it be asserted against private owners at all, or only against the state, and is it in tension with property rights or parasitic on them?

The point that actually puzzles me is this: at my consultation the slogan was used to support refusing the development, but the people who would have lived in those flats are also inhabitants of the city, or would be, and their claims seem to run the other way. So for each reading, tell me whether it can be wielded by objectors of that kind, and whether it gives them the conclusion they took it to give them. Give it to me as a numbered list I can take to the next meeting, and if the most plausible reading turns out not to support the use it was put to, say so plainly rather than rescuing it.
