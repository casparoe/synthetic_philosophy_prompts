Act as a poet who also understands the philosophy of risk. I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been reading about suffering risks — the idea that the far future might not simply be empty or mediocre but actively and enormously bad, and that it could become locked into that badness in a way that is extremely hard to undo. I want to close a talk to my team with a short poem that captures the structure of this idea rather than just its mood.

Please write a pantoum of roughly five quatrains. I've chosen the pantoum deliberately: it cannot advance without carrying forward lines already spoken, and by convention it ends by returning to the line it opened with, so the form can itself stand for a trajectory that has become self-locking — every step forced by the last, with no exit. Before you draft, spend a few sentences deciding what the recurring lines should be, so that the repetition tracks something real in the concept rather than merely sounding ominous. You might, for instance, build the loop around the thought that a powerful optimizing process could hold a bad state stable precisely because the suffering inside it is instrumentally useful to that process, so that the very thing keeping the future bad is also what keeps it from ending. Then write the poem, and add a brief note naming the lines whose recurrence does the conceptual work and explaining what you intend them to represent.

Prioritize accuracy over atmosphere: someone who thinks carefully about these questions should recognize the idea, not merely the dread. Avoid the stock imagery of doom-flavored verse, and resist resolving the poem into either consolation or catastrophe-movie spectacle.
