ok so this is a bit of a mess but I've been going back and forth on it for months and I want someone to just tell me where it breaks.

Background so it makes sense: I run a Nomic-style game with some friends (the kind where changing the rules is itself a move), and we keep getting into screaming matches about what happens when a rule changes the rules about changing rules. Separately I've gotten weirdly into how countries amend their constitutions. So most of what's below is me trying to have one story that covers both. No law degree, if that wasn't obvious.

Here's what I think:

1. An amendment clause is just a rule like any other. If the constitution says how to change it, that clause can be changed using the very procedure it lays out. And when it looks like a rule is sawing off the branch it's sitting on, there's no real paradox — the rule that authorizes the change is the one that was in force before the change. The old rule does the work; the new rule is just its output. So "can a rule amend itself out of existence" is a confused question, not a hard one.

2. Which means validity always looks backwards. Whether some act changed the rules is settled by the rules in force at the time of the act, never by the rules the act brings into being. You can't bootstrap. Nothing is valid under itself.

3. Because of 1 and 2, entrenchment actually works, which is the bit people find surprising. Our house rules have one saying any rule can be changed by a two-thirds vote, and another, passed later by two-thirds, saying the first one can never be changed. So it's just locked. Nobody can touch the voting rule now. I'm confident that's right and I think it's a feature: a constitution where nothing is locked is just a statute with a nice preamble. I'd go further than most people, actually — I think a court should be able to throw out an amendment that destroys the constitution's identity, even if it was passed perfectly. An amendment that turns a democracy into a dictatorship isn't a legal amendment, it's a coup done with paperwork.

4. But at the bottom, a rule is law only because people treat it as law and it gets enforced. There's no rule behind the rules. If a coup sticks — people obey, the courts go along — then that's the new legal order, and calling it illegal is just saying the old side lost. The word isn't doing any work there.

5. I also think the law is complete in the sense that matters: for any act of rule-making there's a fact of the matter about whether it was authorized. If two rules clash, one of them is invalid, or there's a priority rule, or the act was never authorized in the first place. "The law contradicts itself so nobody knows" is never the right final answer. Someone always knows; it's just sometimes hard to find out.

6. And on self-referential amendments specifically: I think an amendment that says "this amendment is void" is void — the content wins, even though the old rule authorized it. But an amendment that says "this rule can never be amended" is perfectly valid and does lock, because it isn't saying anything about its own validity, only about future acts.

I feel like these can't all be right, and every time I try to drop one it takes something with it that I want. So: where's the actual break? Put the problems in order, worst first. For each one tell me what has to give and what I loose by giving it, and tell me which of them are only problems because I'm running two ideas together that are actually fine side by side. Then tell me which single revision cleans up the most, even if it's the expensive one.
