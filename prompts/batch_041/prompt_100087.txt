I'm taking a seminar on seventeenth-century philosophy, and I keep getting stuck on something about Hobbes that I can't resolve on my own. The story I've been given is that he wanted to do for politics what Euclid did for geometry: to begin from first principles that no one can deny and then deduce his conclusions with the same necessity and certainty the geometers achieve, so that his political philosophy would be a demonstrative science rather than a rhetorical persuasion. What puzzles me is what follows from that. If Hobbes really did derive his conclusions from self-evident axioms the way a geometer derives theorems, then his critics should have been forced to show either that one of the axioms was false or that some step in the deduction was invalid. Instead they went after his psychology, his theology, his picture of human nature — which strikes me as changing the subject rather than answering the argument. So either the geometry was doing far less work than everyone claims, or his opponents simply refused to play by the rules of a demonstration they could not refute. I'd like to know which it is.

I'm also trying to understand why geometry in particular, rather than arithmetic or logic, was supposed to be the model. My working assumption is that it's because the axioms of geometry are the most plainly self-evident truths available to us — the sort of thing you cannot deny without absurdity — so any chain of reasoning built on them inherits their certainty, and Hobbes's confidence that the commonwealth could be rebuilt on the same footing would then make sense to me. But something about this reconstruction feels wrong, and I can't tell whether the confusion is mine or belongs to the story I've been told about him. I'd appreciate it if you could sort out what Hobbes actually took the model to be and where my picture of it goes astray, rather than simply restating that he admired Euclid.
