I am writing a seminar paper on Ian Hacking's account of looping effects, and I want to test it against a specific episode in psychiatric classification rather than against the usual textbook examples. The episode is the recent history of grief in the DSM. Under DSM-IV, a major depressive episode was excluded if it arose within two months of the death of a loved one, unless certain severe features were present; this was the so-called bereavement exclusion. DSM-5, published in 2013, removed that exclusion, on the argument that bereavement-related depression is not clinically distinct from other depression. Then DSM-5-TR, published in 2022, added prolonged grief disorder as a recognized diagnosis, requiring persistent longing for or preoccupation with the deceased along with other symptoms, with a minimum duration of twelve months after the death for adults and six months for children and adolescents; the ICD-11 had already included a comparable diagnosis with a shorter minimum duration. The surrounding controversy was loud: clinicians, bereavement researchers, and patient advocates disagreed about whether ordinary grief was being pathologized, whether grief-related depression was being undertreated, and whether a new diagnosis would help or harm the bereaved.

What I want is an analysis of this episode through Hacking's framework, and then a candid assessment of what the episode does to the framework. In the first direction: who or what are the human kinds here, and what would it mean for the classification to loop? Does the removal of the bereavement exclusion, or the later creation of prolonged grief disorder, change how the bereaved understand and enact their grief, and if so, through what mechanisms? I am especially interested in the intermediaries that Hacking's own examples tend to leave in the background: diagnostic interviews, insurance codes, clinical trials, screening scales, support groups, and the ordinary conversation in which someone wonders whether they are grieving normally or for too long. Does the loop run through the self-understanding of the bereaved, through the clinician's recognition of a case, or through institutions that have their own incentives? And is grief an interactive kind in Hacking's sense, or does it sit awkwardly between interactive and indifferent kinds?

In the second direction: what does this episode show about the framework itself? I want you to say where Hacking's account earns its keep and where it is silent or misleading. For instance, the bereaved do not obviously constitute a community that reads the DSM and revises itself in response, the way some other classified groups do; the classification may be driven more by professional and institutional pressures than by the classified population's uptake; and the relevant feedback may take decades to appear, which makes the loop hard to observe and hard to distinguish from ordinary changes in clinical fashion. If the episode fits Hacking only loosely, say so, and say what a better account would need to add, whether that is a sociology of diagnosis, a theory of diagnostic cultures, or something else. Please keep the mapping disciplined: name the players, the classifications, the mechanisms, and the evidence, and be explicit about what the case cannot settle. I am not looking for a survey of the literature or for citations; I want the argument.
