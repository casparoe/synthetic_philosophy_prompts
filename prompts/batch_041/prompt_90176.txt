So I retired from the geology department three years ago and, having finally run out of rocks to look at, I've been reading philosophy — Dennett, some of the cognitive science of religion, a couple of those big anthologies — which is a roundabout way of explaining the spot I'm in. My oldest friend Ray died in February at 79. He played the organ at St. Bridget's for 44 years, was at morning mass basically every day of his adult life, and was the most honest man I ever knew. His family has asked me to give the remembrance at the reception in the parish hall afterward: forty or fifty people, mostly parishioners who knew him longer than I did, plus his grandkids, plus Father Alvarez, who's doing the actual funeral mass. I'm an athiest and everyone in that room knows it, Ray knew it, and I'm not going to stand up there and pretend otherwise.

What I want is for the speech to honor him through the one idea that's actually helped me make sense of the whole situation — that religion is a natural phenomenon, something people do because of the kind of creatures we are: our hair-trigger instinct for seeing an agent behind events, the need to do something with a body we've lost, the way a shared ritual binds a group together regardless of whether the story attached to it holds up. I'd like that view to do the structural work rather than get name-dropped, so that I can say true things about what his faith was and what it did for him, and about how he and I got along for sixty years while disagreeing about all of it, without me either claiming his beliefs were correct or explaining them away as a delusion. I think the by-product picture is what lets me pull that off, along with the gap between actually believing a thing and valuing belief itself — a gap I notice in myself at funerals, of all places. He'd have hated a speech that dodged, but he also had a low tolerance for sentimentality, so keep it warm and a little dry, five minutes max, and nothing that would make Father Alvarez squirm or make Ray's sister think I was using the funeral as a seminar. Please begin with the speech itself, no preface and no commentary, and don't restate any of the above.
