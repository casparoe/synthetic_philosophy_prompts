I'm putting together a small anthology of philosophical fiction and I need one more piece, this one on Zhuangzi and death. The bits I keep coming back to: his wife dies and he's squatting there drumming on a tub and singing, and when Huizi tells him off he says she's just gone through one more change, like the turn of the seasons, and that wailing would mean he didn't understand fate; and then when he's dying himself he tells his friends to skip the fancy burial, since the crows and kites will get him above ground and the ants below, so they'd only be robbing one to feed the other. Plus the butterfly dream, which I think is doing more work here than people give it credit for — he can't tell if he's a man who dreamed he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming he's a man, and the same uncertainty hangs over which side of death is the dream.

Write me a story that takes all that seriously without being reverant about it. Set it somewhere the transformation is literal and in view — a village, or a near-future town, your call — where the dead are composted and the soil goes back into the orchards, so the metaphysics ends up on the dinner table. What I'm after is the friction: somebody who actually belives it, and somebody who can't, or better, one person who believes it and then hits the one loss where it won't hold. Don't let it collapse into a debate and don't make either side a straw man. Around 1500 words, and you don't have to say the name anywhere in it.
