Two people in my seminar have been going at each other for about three years now. One of them thinks the Holocaust is unique in a way that makes comparison to other genocides either false or morally obscene; the other thinks uniqueness-talk is mostly rhetorical, and that the interesting questions are about comparability and about how much of it comes out of ordinary modern state capacity. Bauman comes up, the Historikerstreit comes up, and by now there just repeating themselves. I want to force them into an adversarial collaboration and produce the joint statement they could both actually sign.

Don't let them rehash the usual stuff though. Impose a taxonomy and make them commit in advance. Every uniqueness claim, I say, is one of three things, and they have to tag each of their own claims: (a) membership uniqueness — the event is the sole member of a class picked out by a feature-set, so the real fight is over which features are in the set; (b) causal-role uniqueness — take the event out of the causal story of the twentieth century (or of modernity, or of the international legal order) and the rest of the story changes in a way no substitute event could match; (c) address uniqueness — the event makes a kind of moral demand on us that other mass atrocities don't make, or don't make in the same way. My suspicion is that the singularity person needs (a) but can only defend (c), and the comparativist is attacking (a) while quietly granting (c), and if that's right I want it said out loud.

They also have to agree, together, on a grain-of-description test: any two events are unique under a fine enough description, so when is a uniqueness claim doing real explanatory work rather than just conjoining features until the class has one member? Have them write that test jointly, then have the comparativist say which of the other's claims survive it.

Sections I want:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims — about what modern states actually did, how the killing was organized, what comparison is even for, and what neither of them is claiming (be explicit about the things they both reject, including anything that would give aid to a denier; they're serious people, so fence that off).
2. A disagreements table: neutral wording of each disagreement, plus the wording each side would accept for its own position. If a row turns out to be a verbal dispute, mark it and say which part is verbal and why.
3. Cruxes — at least three sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each: a case or argument both agree bears on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance. I'd guess the cruxes include whether the targeting was definitional (a people as such, rather than a people because of territory or politics), whether the industrial and bureaucratic character is essential or incidental, whether it matters that the perpetrator got no geopolitical or economic payoff, and whether "modernity" is a cause or just a setting.
4. A short closing section: what would actually move the debate, what wouldn't, and each of them naming the weakest point in their own position — something they wouldn't normally say in public.

No citations, no reading list, no bibliography, and don't put anything in quotation marks, including anyone's famous formulations — paraphrase or leave it out. Around 1200 words, and write it in British English (I'm sending it to a friend in Leeds).
