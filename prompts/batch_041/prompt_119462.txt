I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about rationality across time, going by my gut on cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, is behind it.

Take someone whose pairwise preferences cycle: they prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, and they'll pay a dollar to swap whatever they hold for whatever they prefer. A broker offers them a sequence of swaps that walks them through the cycle and lands them back at A, three dollars poorer. If they saw the whole sequence coming and took it anyway, that seems flatly irrational to me. If they didn't see it coming, the broker's a swindler, but I'm honestly not sure the agent did anything wrong. If they saw it coming and refused the package deal, I think they're fine. But now the case that won't sit with the others: someone whose tastes just drift, vanilla Monday, chocolate Tuesday, no new information, no trades, no money lost. I don't think that person is irrational at all. Same with the person who'd rather have the shot tomorrow than today and says the same thing every day, so it never happens -- I do think something's gone wrong there, but I can't say what, if the taste-drifter is fine.

Then the temptation cases. Someone buys the cigarettes, knows they'll want to quit, and at the later moment lights up; I feel like the later self is the irrational one, except I also believe people are entitled to the preferences they actually have. And the dial: each notch up adds a bit of pain and a bit of money, each single notch is worth it at the time, and they end at the top setting in agony holding cash they'd hand over to undo the whole thing. I keep flip-flopping on whether that person was irrational at the start, only at the end, or never.

The principle I keep reaching for is just: a choice is rational if it's the one the agent most prefers at the moment of choosing. But that makes the pump and the dial come out clean, which I don't believe, so either the principle's wrong or my verdicts are.

Is there one account of diachronic rationality -- about whole sequences of choices, not single choices -- that gets all of this, or close to all of it? If it has to give up one of my verdicts, tell me which one and why. And say what the account predicts for a case I haven't listed: someone with a stable cycle who'd turn down every one of the swaps if offered them one at a time, but happily signs a contract that performs the entire cycle automatically.
