Okay, I'm writing a thing on the paradox of tragedy and im tired of every account grabbing one pet example and declaring victory. Before I read any more of them I want the actual spec. What would an adequate theory of why we seek out and seem to enjoy art that makes us sad, scared, or miserable have to deliver? Give me the desiderata -- the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it cant collapse, the constraints it has to respect -- with the case behind each one, not just a bare list.

For the cases: people who sob at a film and then tell everyone to watch it; horror fans; the sad song on repeat; the fact that nobody enjoys this way when the grief is their own and real. For the distinctions: enjoying vs. valuing; liking vs. wanting; the emotion felt in the moment vs. what you'd report about it afterward; real vs. as-if emotion. And a few things I keep running into that I want you to treat as data the theory has to fit rather than as rhetorical flourishes:

- the second-order thing -- call it being meta-moved -- where the pleasure isn't the sadness at all but catching yourself being moved, the little glow of noticing your own response. Does that need its own explanation, or does it just dissolve into the standard ones?
- people with aphantasia who can't picture a scene at all and still cry at books. If your theory needs the audience to vividly imagine the events, it has a problem here.
- the person who enjoys a sad work more when they're already down. Same phenomenon, or a different one?

Then tell me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of a camp (the catharsis crowd, the safety/control crowd, the meta-emotion crowd, whatever else you think is in play). And the part I actually care about most: flag any pair of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, because if some of them are in tension then the list itself is the result. Skip scoring the usual suspects, I'll do that later.
