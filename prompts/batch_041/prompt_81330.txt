I build static analyzers and Datalog-style fixed-point engines, so my working vocabulary is: relations and their current extensions, monotone operators on lattices, least fixed points, semi-naive evaluation, stratified versus non-stratified negation, well-founded and stable-model semantics, the chase, and confluence arguments of the Newman's-lemma kind, where local confluence plus termination yields a unique normal form. My game theory is one undergraduate course's worth: normal-form games, Nash equilibrium, and the vague memory that you can iteratively delete dominated strategies. I would like the second thing restated entirely in the terms of the first, and then I want to hear what the translation lost.

Treat the surviving-strategy set as a relation saturated by a deletion rule, and be explicit about the rule's structural properties. Is the deletion operator monotone in the current extension? Is it a rewrite system admitting a critical-pair analysis? Does deletion under strict dominance behave like a confluent system while deletion under weak dominance fails local confluence, so that the final set depends on the schedule? If so, what plays the role of stratification — the thing that buys uniqueness when the operator stops being monotone — and if nothing does, is the honest reading that the object of study is the family of maximal deletion sequences rather than a canonical fixed point? Note that when the dominator may be a mixed strategy, the side condition ceases to be a finite syntactic pattern over the relation and becomes a linear-programming feasibility query against the current extension; tell me what that does to the confluence and stratification stories, and whether the relation ought to carry mixed strategies alongside pure ones, given that a mixture of already-deleted pure strategies might still be available as a dominator.

Then the residue report. Which parts of the game-theoretic discussion — order dependence, the strict/weak distinction, the status of the surviving set as a solution concept, the epistemic reading in terms of what players know about one another's rationality, the relation to Nash equilibrium and to rationalizability, the normal-form versus extensive-form gap — translate cleanly, which survive only as distortions, and which have no counterpart at all in a fixed-point-and-rewriting vocabulary? Keep the translation honest: do not quietly import game-theoretic assumptions and present them as structure the framework already had. One contrast I especially want addressed. In my world a static analysis is judged sound or complete relative to a concrete semantics specified independently of the analysis. Tell me whether the deletion procedure has anything playing that role, and if it does not, whether the gap is a defect of my vocabulary — which only knows how to be sound with respect to something given in advance — or a sign that the textbook presentation of the procedure is tidier than its foundations.
