I am delivering the opening address at a small, invitation-only symposium on neuromorphic engineering and the philosophy of mind, and I want the talk to do real conceptual work rather than warm up the room. The audience is deliberately mixed: roughly a third are hardware people who build spiking silicon and speak casually about the brain as a computer; a third are neuroscientists and cognitive scientists who are tired of that metaphor and will be listening for whether I treat it as an identity claim or as a heuristic; and the rest are philosophers, plus one historian of mid-century mathematics who has read von Neumann's Silliman Lectures closely and will notice immediately if I attribute to him a tidier position than he actually held. The occasion is the annual lecture our group holds in von Neumann's name, and the text I want to build it around is The Computer and the Brain, the short, unfinished, posthumously published set of lectures in which he compares the nervous system with digital computers.

The speech should be organized around a single conceptual tension rather than a survey. That book is usually remembered as an early statement of the brain-is-a-computer thesis, but its more interesting structure is that von Neumann uses the comparison to locate its own breaking points: the discreteness of the all-or-nothing spike set against the graded, timing-dependent, partly continuous conditions that produce it; the possibility that the neuron is the wrong unit of analysis and that the synapse or some dendritic region is the right one; the way a system built from slow, noisy, failure-prone components achieves reliability through sheer numbers and parallel organization rather than through exact symbols; and the suggestion, offered as mathematically guided speculation rather than as settled doctrine, that the brain's internal language may not be the language of conscious mathematics. I want the address to make that tension audible to the engineers without letting the philosophers hear a slogan, and to leave the hardware people with something to carry back to their architecture and the philosophers with something to argue about at the reception.

Some constraints. Do not quote von Neumann directly anywhere in the speech: the text is unfinished and posthumous, and I would rather paraphrase his positions than pretend I have his sentences in front of me. No equations, nothing that requires a slide, and it has to be sayable aloud in about eighteen minutes. It should not resolve the tension in favor of either side. After the address, append a short note to me, clearly marked as not part of the speech, saying which interpretive claims in it are most contested, where the reading is most likely to fail (for instance if the historical evidence points to von Neumann treating the analogy as a literal identity claim rather than a heuristic, or if the remark about the brain's language turns out to concern precision and noise rather than form), and what specific textual or historical consideration would make me revise the central framing.
