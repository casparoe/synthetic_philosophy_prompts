I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about what actually makes a variation on a theme count as creative, and I wrote down where I landed so I could check whether it holds together. Here it is. (1) Creativity just is variation: you take a theme — a tune, a design, a concept — and turn some of its knobs to get something new. (2) The genuinely creative act isn't the turning, though; it's deciding which knobs there are to turn, i.e., fixing what counts as part of the theme and what counts as a departure from it. (3) A variation is creative to the degree that someone who had already mapped out the whole space of possible variations could not have predicted it. (4) But a variation is only good, as opposed to merely novel, if it still fits the theme — if it reads as a variation on that theme and not as a new piece altogether. (5) Nothing is creative from nothing; every creative act sits inside a tradition that hands you the theme and the space of moves. Please audit this for me. Which of the five actually pull against each other, which pairs only look incompatible and dissolve once you spell them out, and for the real conflicts, what is the cheapest revision that would restore consistency and what would it cost me? Rank them by severity rather than treating them as equally bad.
