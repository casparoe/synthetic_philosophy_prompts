Some friends and I have been arguing about a distinction that keeps surfacing in recent writing on how large-scale systems go wrong: the contrast between a failure that comes from a system seeking influence and a failure that comes from slow value drift. On the first picture, the system never abandons its stated goal; it simply becomes steadily better at acquiring the power, resources, and discretion needed to pursue that goal, until the accumulation of influence is itself what explains its behaviour and the original aim has become decoration. On the second, the aim itself is quietly redefined, one defensible step at a time, so that no single decision looks like a betrayal and there is no moment you could point to and say: there, that is where it turned. The two are supposed to be genuinely different failure modes, and the unsettling part of the claim is that we may never get a clean signal that either one has occurred.

I want to try that apparatus on a case I know well enough to argue about: the English East India Company. It starts as a chartered trading venture, becomes a territorial sovereign in Bengal in the middle of the eighteenth century, and ends up administering an empire until the Crown takes over in the wake of the 1857 rebellion — with the Bengal famine of around 1770, the Regulating Act of 1773, and the successive charter acts that stripped away its monopolies and finally its commercial functions altogether marking the stages along the way. My instinct is that both of the failure modes above fit, which is exactly what worries me, so I want you to tell me whether that is because the distinction is doing real work or because it is too coarse to survive contact with the case. Who or what is the "system" here, given that we are talking about a joint-stock company with a board in London, servants in India enriching themselves, and shareholders who mostly wanted dividends? What was the original goal, and at which point, if any, does the influence-seeking reading explain more than the drift reading, or the reverse? Note that the transformation was loudly denounced in its own time — Burke's prosecution of Warren Hastings is the famous instance — which seems to cut against the idea that this kind of failure is invisible until it is too late; tell me what that does to the theory. And tell me whether "failure" is even the right word for a drift that made a great many people very rich, since the verdict presumably depends on whose valuation we are using, while the theory seems to assume a single evaluator.

Then run it back the other way: what does the case show about the distinction — a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case it was never built to handle? I would rather have an honest map of where the theory is silent than a forced fit. Be explicit about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on the Company's history or on institutional drift, and do not present a live scholarly dispute as though it were the consensus; where you are unsure of a historical fact because you cannot check it, say so rather than smoothing over it.
