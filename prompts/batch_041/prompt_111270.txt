I'm a junior in high school and I help run a small philosophy club that meets on Thursdays after class. Last week our faculty advisor, who teaches the ethics elective, offered a distinction I've been turning over ever since and still can't decide whether it's profound or just a bit of verbal bookkeeping. She said that when people call a choice "hard" because the values involved are incommensurable, they might mean either of two quite different things. On the first reading, it just means there is no single scale on which both options can be placed — the way it makes no sense to ask whether a novel is longer or shorter than a song, even though each has a length of its own. On the second reading, it means something stronger: that the two options can indeed be compared, and the comparison simply returns no verdict — neither is better, neither is worse, and they are not equal either. Her provocative claim was that the first reading is true almost everywhere and philosophically boring, while the second is the one that is supposed to turn a hard choice into a genuine problem rather than a scheduling annoyance. A friend in the club then built the following argument, and now he can't find the exit, and neither can I. I would rather keep the second reading of incommensurability, and keep the thought that some ways of resolving a hard choice really are wiser than others, so I want to know which step is doing the damage and what it costs to cut it.

(1) A choice is hard in the philosophically interesting sense only when its options are incommensurable in the second, verdict-denying sense. (2) If two options are incommensurable in that sense, then neither is better than the other and they are not equal in value. (3) If neither is better and they are not equal, then there is no respect in which one ought to be chosen over the other. (4) If there is no respect in which one ought to be chosen over the other, then either may be chosen. (5) If either may be chosen, then no reason for choosing one rather than the other is any better or worse than any other reason. (6) So in a hard choice, the person who agonizes for a week, the person who flips a coin, and the person who simply picks whatever suits them personally are all doing equally well; none of them can be criticized, and the very idea of practical wisdom is empty. (7) But that is absurd — some resolutions of a hard choice are wise and others are shallow or callous, and that difference is real. (8) Therefore (1) is false: either hard choices are never really incommensurable, or incommensurability does not have the consequences claimed for it.

To make it concrete: the older sister of another club member has to choose between staying home after graduation to help care for their grandfather, whose care has fallen entirely on the family, and taking a scholarship to study abroad that she has wanted for years and may never be offered again. I think either choice could be the right one, and I would resist anyone who said she was simply wrong to go — but I also think a version of her who went purely because the weather abroad is nicer would be missing something, and that this is not nothing. Tell me which premise to reject, why it looked so plausible in the first place, and what each alternative costs. Please lead with your verdict in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
