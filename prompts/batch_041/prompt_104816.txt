I'm taking a moral philosophy course and I've got stuck on Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism — the one where the man who saves his wife rather than a stranger is supposed to have one thought too many if he thinks that she is his wife and that partiality is permitted in cases like this. I've ended up holding a view I'd like you to stress-test. The view is this: the objection is not really about how demanding utilitarianism is, nor about whether agents should deliberate in utilitarian terms; it's about the kind of reason that can figure in the agent's action. A theory alienates you when it requires your deepest commitments to be held at arm's length, as things whose weight is always conditional on their contributing to an impersonal total. But I also think the objection only lands if there's a defencible line between an agent's projects and her moral reasons — and I'm not sure there is.

So: what would count against this reading? I want the defeaters ranked, not just a list. Which cases would show that the alienation is real but harmless, or that it isn't alienation at all? What would a utilitarian have to say about the wife-saving case, or about a case I've been chewing on — a doctor in a mass-casualty triage who treats her own child first, not because children come first, but because it's her child — for the objection to lose its grip? And which of these would Williams's defenders themselves concede as counting against them, as opposed to dismissing as a misunderstanding? I'm especially interested in whether the view has been qualified into something unfalsifyable: if every theory can be described as either alienating or not depending on how we individuate the agent's projects, is that a problem for the objection, or just a sign that it's a moral-psychological claim rather than a criterion of rightness?
