ok so here's what i want. write me an internal note from a fictional insurance syndicate that has, a bit recklessly, started selling 'national responsibility cover' — policies that pay out when your country gets officially blamed for some historical catastrophe. this file is about whether they can quote a premium for the blame currently sitting on Germany for the First World War. one internal note, maybe 700-800 words, with the usual dull headings ('insured peril', 'exclusions', 'basis of premium', 'open items'). deadpan actuarial prose the whole way. no winking, no lol-isnt-insurance-funny — the comedy has to come from the history being done properly, so that someone who has actually read the Fischer controversy laughs instead of wincing.

stuff the note has to actually work through, because the file is a mess:

- the Versailles clause everyone calls the war-guilt clause. it never uses the word 'guilt' at all — it has Germany accept responsibility for the loss and damage caused by the war, which is a liability formula, not a moral verdict. the underwritting team has spent decades treating a reparations hook as a confession, and the syndicate's lawyers keep quietly exploiting the gap. the germans read it as a moral condemnation regardless, which is the genuinely interesting part and should be in the note somewhere.
- the counterfactual problem. the actuary needs a baseline and there isn't one. if the alliance system, the mobilization timetables and the blank cheque to Vienna were going to produce a great-power war anyway, what exactly is being attributed to the insured? the historians won't hand her a baseline and she can't price the peril without one.
- three tiers, because the file keeps getting re-rated: wanting a war, being willing to run the risk of one, and merely letting one happen. different perils, different loadings. Fischer's thesis — that the Reich deliberately instigated a war to become a world power, with the aims drawn up in Berlin in September 1914 as the evidence — puts the client in the top tier, and it has survived most of the counterattacks, so it can't be dismissed as a fringe loading. but the objection that he read the 1914 aims backwards out of 1939 is a real methodological exposure and the pricing model has no field for it.
- the primacy-of-domestic-politics line (elites choosing a foreign adventure to manage conflict at home) as an alternative causal route, which changes who is even insured — the state, the elite, or the nation — and therefore changes everything about the policy.
- succession: does the Federal Republic inherit Weimar's premium, or is it a new entity? the file contains two incompatible actuarial tables and nobody has taken either one out.
- hindsight: the note should concede that they are rating a decision taken by people with no archives, no after-the-fact knowledge, and about six days.

somewhere in the margins, a note from the senior partner who thinks collective guilt is a category error, that only individuals can be responsible for anything, and that the whole line of business should be cancelled. he's not a strawman and he doesn't win either.

dont end with a verdict on who caused the war. end with the syndicate declining to quote, and let the reason for declining be that it can't work out who actually holds the policy. keep it flat. and get the historiography solid enough that a historian of 1914 wouldn't wince.
