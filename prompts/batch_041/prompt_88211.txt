**The money pump is an argument about time**

The standard money pump is supposed to show that cyclic preferences are irrational. If you prefer A to B, B to C and C to A, a trader can sell you B for C plus a penny, then A for B plus a penny, then C for A plus a penny, leaving you where you started, three pence poorer. Since the loss is foreseeable, you should not have cyclic preferences.

I claim that this argument proves much less than it is usually taken to prove, and that what it does prove concerns diachronic rather than synchronic rationality.

First, the pump is unavailable if the trades are offered as a package. Offer a cyclic chooser the whole sequence at once — you will end up exactly where you are now, minus three pence — and she will refuse, because the endpoint is worse than her present holding. The pump therefore requires the trades to be offered one at a time, and it requires the chooser to assess each against the status quo immediately before it rather than against the sequence as a whole. It works only on a chooser who is, in this respect, myopic.

Second, myopia is not the same thing as cyclicity. A chooser with perfectly transitive preferences can be led step by step into a ruinous total, as in the self-torturer case, where each step is worth taking on its own terms and the series is not. So the pump does not isolate cyclicity as the defect; it isolates the combination of cyclicity with myopia, or perhaps myopia alone.

Third, the remedy is itself diachronic. The chooser must treat a sequence of decisions as a single object, which means either refusing to enter the cycle or entering and treating her earlier decision as binding on her later self — the picture sometimes called resolute choice. But whether an earlier decision can bind a later self, and whether it must, is precisely what is at issue between those who assess rationality time-slice by time-slice and those who assess it over a whole life. The money pump appears to presuppose an answer.

My uncertainty is whether this makes the argument circular in any damaging sense. A defender may reply that the pump is indeed diachronic, but that the diachronic norm it presupposes — that one's choices over time should answer to a single ranking — is independently plausible, so the argument is not circular, merely not self-contained. I do not know how to assess that reply.
