My flatmate has an argument he busts out everytime anyone says Malthus was right but early, and I can't get out of it. Here it is:

(1) "Right but early" means: the mechanism is real, the outcome just hasn't arrived.
(2) For any predicted outcome that hasn't arrived, you can always name something still in play that's holding it off — a new resource, a new technology, a lag, a bubble. There is never a shortage of candidates.
(3) So that defense is available to any long-run prediction that hasn't come true yet, no matter how much has happened that the theory said couldn't happen.
(4) A defense that's available to every theory, whatever the evidence, doesn't distinguish between theories. And a claim that distinguishes nothing isn't an empirical claim.
(5) So Malthus's theory is empty — and so are Marx's immiseration thesis, the Club of Rome, peak oil, all of it. Anything that lives on "it's coming" is not a theory.
(6) But that has to be wrong, because we obviously do distinguish a theory that was merely early from a lunatic prediction that the world ends next Tuesday, and the argument above can't tell them apart. Which makes me think the trouble is in (2) or (4).

His fix is that a theory gets to say "early" only if it can name what would have to happen for it to be too late — he calls this its "lateness condition" — and if that thing has already happened, the theory is just wrong, no deferrals allowed. He reckons Malthus's lateness condition is a durable rise in living standards that fertility doesn't eat up, and that this has happened, so Malthus is dead and buried.

I'd rather lose (2) than (4). So: which step do I give up, why did it look fine until now, and what do I pay for dropping it? Then give me a one-line test for "early" vs "wrong" that I can actually apply next time this comes up. Under 500 words.
