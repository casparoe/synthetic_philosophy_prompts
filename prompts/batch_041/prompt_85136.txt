Consider the following reconstruction of the thesis that ours is the best of all possible worlds. Let W be the set of possible worlds and let the value-order be the relation on W of being at least as good as. Call a world maximal if no world is strictly better than it, and a maximum if it is at least as good as every world. In a total order these two notions coincide; in a partial order they come apart, so that there can be maximal worlds, no maximum, and pairs of worlds that are simply incomparable in value. The Leibnizian God cannot be satisfied with a merely maximal world: on the view in question the choice of a world is explained by that world's superiority over the alternatives, and the principle of sufficient reason is what guarantees that such an explanation exists.

What is your credence that the principle of sufficient reason, together with God's perfect goodness, is by itself decisive for the existence of a best possible world — that is, that no further assumptions about the structure of value are needed? I want the number and its decomposition, not a verdict. Assign a rough probability and one line of justification to each of the following, plus any further term you think belongs:

1. That the principle of sufficient reason, properly stated, demands a comparative sufficient reason for God's choice — a reason of the form that this world is better than every alternative — rather than merely some sufficient reason or other.

2. That God's perfect goodness is best read as maximizing (God must choose the best), rather than as satisficing, or as an aesthetic fittingness that selects a world fully expressive of God's nature without requiring it to be best.

3. That the value-order of possible worlds is total, so that incomparability never arises and the maximal/maximum gap is closed.

4. That, conditional on totality, a maximum exists — that is, your credence against the density objection, on which for any world there is a better one (one more happy creature, one fewer gratuitous pain, an arbitrarily small improvement), so that the values of worlds have a supremum but no maximum. Fold into this term your estimate of how far the objection survives the reply, usually attributed to Leibniz, that worlds are individuated by complete concepts and so cannot be varied one feature at a time.

5. That, if there is no maximum, the principle of sufficient reason can be satisfied by a reason of another kind — God's free decree, the completeness of a created system of worlds rather than the optimality of any single one, the full expression of God's nature, or the creation of nothing at all.

Then combine the terms into your final credence and tell me which one the number is most sensitive to and what argument or consideration would move it most. Finally: holding those terms fixed, what credence do you end up with that there is a best possible world, and how does it differ from your first number — that is, do you think the best-world thesis could be true even if the principle of sufficient reason does not force it, or false even if the principle does? Say briefly what would count as settling the question.
