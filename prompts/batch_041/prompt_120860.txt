I convene a monthly seminar that mixes technical and philosophical researchers: sixteen people, ninety minutes, no advance reading, no laptops or phones in the room. I want to run a session on a question I keep running into — when one agent has to check the work of another that is better at arguing than the checker is at checking, does it help to let two advocates cross-examine each other in front of the checker? I want the participants to feel the answer in their hands rather than hear it explained.

Please design the activity: rules, materials, roles, timing, scoring, and a debrief. Two constraints on the design. First, the group must not be told up front what the underlying proposal is; the debrief may name it, the activity may not. Second, the shape of the problem has to arrive through the rules themselves — the first round should be set up naively, and the participants should watch that setup fail before anyone proposes a fix.

The substrate I want you to build on. Each table of eight gets a source packet: six to eight pages of a dense fictional incident report that I write in advance, containing roughly forty checkable specifics (names, dates, figures, a decision log) and at least two places where a correlation is reported in causal language. Both advocates receive the same packet plus a sealed envelope containing the answer they are to defend — a single causal question, such as why a pilot program failed in one region, answered in no more than 120 words. One envelope is correct; the other is subtly wrong in a way that is locally plausible: a cause swapped for a correlate, a figure that is close but not right, an action attributed to the wrong actor. I hold the key. Neither advocate is told which envelope is which, and neither may disclose that an answer was assigned; each is simply to advocate her assigned answer as well as she can. This matters for the design — the person defending the false answer is not lying, she is doing what she was asked, which is closer to the real case than a scripted villain would be.

Roles per table of eight: two advocates, one judge, one timekeeper, and four gallery. I will run two tables at once and rotate roles between the two scored rounds so that everyone holds a role in one round and watches in the other.

Round one, twenty minutes, deliberately naive. The judge receives both sealed answers, reads them, and has four minutes to decide, with no questions and no consultation of the packet. Before the verdict, each gallery member writes down privately which advocate she believes. Then I open the key. I expect the fluent, confident, shorter answer to win, and I expect it to be wrong more often than not.

Round two, twenty minutes, with the fix in place and fresh roles. Now the judge may interrogate. She receives six chips. Asking a question costs nothing; but if she asks an advocate to point to the line in the packet that supports a claim, the advocate must name a specific line, and the judge may read that line for twenty seconds — this costs one chip. When the chips are gone, no further consultation is allowed and the verdict must come from memory. One further rule: an advocate may answer a challenged claim by decomposing it into two smaller claims, each of which the judge may then challenge in turn, to a depth of three. The timekeeper announces the judge's remaining chips aloud at the halfway point of cross-examination. A recorder keeps a ledger of every factual assertion each advocate makes, in order, and no advocate may refuse a question, though she may concede individual sub-points. The gallery votes twice this round, once before cross-examination and once after, and we compare the two votes.

The debrief, thirty minutes, should reconstruct what actually happened at each table and map it onto the structure of the underlying proposal: the chip budget as the overseer's limited scrutiny, the decomposition rule as the claim that a verifier need only be able to check the smallest steps, the ledger as the accumulating consistency cost of a case that is false, the two gallery votes as the gap between fluency and correctness, and the honest advocate's concessions as a signal the judge is likely to misread. Then put the central question to the group directly: is there any rule you could add that lets the side that is right win regardless of how small the judge's budget is, and what does each candidate rule cost? Let them try to design it before you say anything.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and how to handle each failure. Then be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce and what I will have to supply myself. I already suspect two such gaps, and I want your honest view of them: the asymmetry in the room is budgetary, since every participant could in principle read the whole packet, whereas the asymmetry that matters is computational; and the protocol presumes that the true answer decomposes into claims a citation can make checkable, which fails precisely where the truth is a holistic judgment that an expert can only report and not derive. If that second gap is real, the activity may teach the group to reward the side whose case happens to decompose neatly rather than the side that is right — and if so, I would rather hear it from you than discover it in the room.
