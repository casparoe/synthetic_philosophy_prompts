I cover math and computing for a science magazine, and I'm about six months into reporting a long feature on the idea that simpler explanations are more likely to be true -- the whole line that runs from Solomonoff's universal prior through Kolmogorov complexity to the minimum-description-length machinery that engineers actually use. The reporting was going fine until I sat in on a two-day workshop last month and realized that the people who work on this don't agree about what the field's live problems even are. One person told me the invariance theorem already settles the important philosophical question and that everything since is basically engineering. Another said the framework is dead in the water because it measures simplicity as program length relative to a universal machine, and the choice of machine only buys you an additive constant -- which is fine asymptotically and close to useless when you have a few million bits of data and a finite lifetime, since that constant is where all the interesting action is. A third said the real gap is that these theories treat hypotheses as black boxes: they can rank one program that outputs the data against another program that outputs the data, but they have nothing to say about why a hypothesis with visible structure -- a law, a mechanism, a symmetry -- should beat a lookup table that happens to compress well, which is the thing scientists are actually doing when they say one theory is simpler. And one guy argued that the whole enterprise penalizes the wrong cost: what you should be charging a hypothesis for is how long it takes to run or to test, not how many bits it takes to write down, and once you switch to that the ordering of hypotheses changes.

So here's what I want from you, and I'd rather have an argument than a survey. Suppose you had three or four strong students with good math and CS backgrounds, two years of their lives, and no obligation to produce anything immediately useful -- which of these open questions, or one I haven't listed, should they actually go after, and in what order? Tell me which questions are upstream of which (does the finite-data/constant problem have to be settled before the black-box problem is even well posed, or can they be worked independently?), which ones are tractable in two years by people who aren't going to reinvent measure theory, and which ones would actually change something outside the field -- engineering practice, how ML people think about regularization, what philosophers of science should be saying about simplicity. I also want to know what would move an item up or down your list: a result, a counterexample, an argument that some of this is a pseudo-problem in disguise.

The reason I care about the ranking and not just a catalog is that the piece has to have a spine, and right now I can't tell whether the honest story is "the theory works and we're filling in details," "the theory is elegant and empirically empty," or "the theory was aimed at the wrong target from the start" -- those are three completely different articles, and they point me at different people to interview. So please defend the order, be explicit about the criteria you're using, and flag any place where you think my four workshop complaints are secretly the same complaint wearing different hats. Skip the citations and the reading list -- I can't check any of it from where I'm sitting and it won't help me decide what the piece is about.
