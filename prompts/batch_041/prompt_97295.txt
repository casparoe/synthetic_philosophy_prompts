I am in my third year of an undergraduate degree in philosophy, and I am currently taking a module on decision theory and its paradoxes. Each student on the module has to design and run a single fifty-minute session for the rest of the group — roughly eighteen people, one seminar room, no computers — and my assigned topic is the two-envelope paradox. I would like you to design the session for me.

What I want above all is for the group to feel the pull of the switching argument before anyone names its flaw: they should reconstruct the expected-value reasoning themselves, commit to it, and only afterwards discover what it costs them. Please give me the whole apparatus — the rules, the materials, a payoff scheme that runs on tokens rather than real money, any roles, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that ties each moment of the activity back to a particular step in the paradox. The core of the session should be a two-player staging in which one participant holds an envelope and reasons aloud about whether to pay a small price to swap it, while the partner is free to keep offering further swaps; I want the holder to argue themselves into paying, and then to find that the same reasoning licenses paying again, and again.

In the debrief I would like the group to be able to separate at least the following: the move that treats the observed amount as though it were equally likely to be the smaller or the larger envelope; the assumption of a uniform or scale-invariant prior over the possible amounts and why no such prior can exist; the difference between a rule of always switching and a rule that simply ignores the contents of the envelope; and the sense in which the argument can be repaired, or not, once the generating process is fixed. Please also include a second, shorter round in which the envelope contents are produced by a mechanism the participants are told about in advance, so that they can watch the switching argument become defensible for some observed amounts and plainly inapplicable for others. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it, and be candid about which feature of the paradox the activity cannot reproduce and that I will therefore have to supply in my own words.
