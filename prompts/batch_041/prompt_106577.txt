I'm trying to understand a passage from Kant's Perpetual Peace (old translation, the one on Wikisource) and I keep going back and forth on what work it's actually doing. Here's the bit:

"A state of Peace among men who live side by side with each other, is not the natural state. The state of Nature is rather a state of War; for although it may not always present the outbreak of hostilities, it is nevertheless continually threatened with them. The state of Peace must, therefore, be established; for the mere cessation of hostilities furnishes no security against their recurrence, and where there is no guarantee of peace between neighbouring States—which can only be furnished under conditions that are regulated by Law—the one may treat the other, when proclamation is made to that effect, as an enemy."

A friend in my seminar, Daria, has this half-formed reading: she says the interesting thing is that Kant starts with "men who live side by side" and only later slides to "neighbouring States." Her claim is that if you take the first phrase seriously, then border exclusion isn't a neutral exercise of sovereignty — it's a way of keeping some people in a state of nature with respect to others, because the people who are kept out are still living side by side with the insiders in the relevant sense (same planet, overlapping possible lives, etc.). So a right to exclude, on her view, is itself a standing threat of war, not a stable legal settlement. She also says this makes Kant a better ally for open borders than people think, which sounds backwards to me.

What I want help with: can you walk me through what Kant means by "state of Nature" and "state of War" here, and then assess Daria's move? Specifically, is there a principled way to decide whether "men who live side by side" is meant to pick out individuals or already constituted peoples? And if it's individuals, does that really put a right to exclude in tension with the demand that peace be established, or can a state legitimately say "we are establishing peace among ourselves, and the exclusion of outsiders is part of that establishment"? I'd like the answer to distinguish between (a) a right to exclude as a condition of internal peace, and (b) a right to exclude as a perpetuation of the state of nature. Maybe there's a third option I'm missing. Don't worry about citing anything; just help me see the structure of the argument and where Daria's reading either holds up or breaks.
