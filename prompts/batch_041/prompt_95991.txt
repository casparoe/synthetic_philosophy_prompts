Bit of an odd request, so let me set it up. I'm a retired physical chemist — thirty years of spectroscopy, and a decent amount of statistical mechanics before that — and since I packed it in I've been sitting in on, and now half-teaching, a graduate seminar in philosophy of physics. I've been handed the Maxwell's demon / physics-of-information slot for the final: 90 minutes, closed book, no internet, essays run 800 words or so. I need you to write the question, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

Here's my worry. Every one of these students can produce the party line: the demon has to keep a record of what it did, sooner or later it has to erase the record, erasing a bit costs at least kT ln2, and that pays back exactly what the demon stole from the gas. Two paragraphs, done. That's recitation, not an answer, and I'm not even sure it's the whole story. I've read philosophers of physics arguing that Landauer's principle is a theorem about particular models rather than something you can read straight off the second law, and that logical and thermodynamic reversibility can come apart. I gather the cost of the demon has been relocated over the years — it used to be the measurement, then it became the erasure — and I'm not convinced the relocation was free. There's also the move where the demon simply doesn't erase: it keeps its records in a reversible computer and defers the bill, or it's only finite so it can't run forever. And someone pointed out to me that the erasure accounting quietly assumes a division of the world into "the memory" and everything else that the physics itself doesn't hand you. Where the entropy actually gets paid seems to me the crux, and I want the question to land there.

So: the question has to be unanswerable by reciting the erasure story, but it also has to be self-contained, since they can't look anything up and I don't want them guessing at citations or inventing references. Put whatever setup they need inside the question itself. I'd like it to include a short passage in a student's voice — the confident two-paragraph version above, written out — that they have to audit: which parts survive, which assumptions are smuggled in, and what would actually have to be shown for the demon to be laid to rest by information-theoretic bookkeeping rather than by the boring fact that any closed cycle has to come back to where it started.

Then a model answer of about 700 words, pitched so a mixed room of physicists and philosophers can follow it, and a marking scheme: five must-hit points, 5 marks each, 25 total, with one line for each on how candidates typically get that point wrong. Finish with a sentence or two on what separates an A from a B — what the B answer contains and what the A adds. Don't have them quote anyone or name papers; names of arguments are fine, names of articles are not. And half the room are philosophers who are shaky on the physics, so make the model answer spell out the thermodynamics rather than assuming it.
