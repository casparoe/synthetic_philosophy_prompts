**The Ass Was Never the Problem**

The puzzle is usually put like this. An animal is placed exactly between two identical bales of hay, with no ground for preferring either. If it must have a reason favoring one over the other before it can move, it never moves, and it dies. Three replies are standard. First, perfect ties do not occur in nature: there is always some asymmetry, a scent, a shadow, a hair's breadth, so the case is a fiction. Second, the will is free, and freedom means it can simply opt for one; no reason is required. Third, the case is incoherent: a creature with genuinely no reason to move in either direction would not be moved, and that is the end of it.

All three replies accept the same assumption, and that assumption is what I want to reject: that a rational agent acts only when it has a reason that discriminates between the options, a consideration telling in favor of this one and against that one. That is too strong. What an agent needs is a reason to act and a reason to stop deliberating, and neither is the same thing as a reason that discriminates. When two options are level in the respects that matter for the decision, deliberation has nothing left to buy: further inquiry cannot improve the outcome, and the time spent is pure loss. The tie is therefore not a reason to do nothing; it is a reason to bring inquiry to a close. Call the first sort of consideration a discriminating reason and the second sort a terminating reason. The ass starves only if we insist that terminating reasons do not exist.

Once terminating reasons are admitted, a second distinction becomes available, between choosing and picking. Choosing an option is answerable to discriminating reasons: I chose this because it was better on some dimension that mattered. Picking is what one does when the options are level and something must nevertheless be done. Picking is not a degenerate or defective form of choosing, and it has norms of its own. One should not pick in a way that covertly reintroduces a preference one does not have, as when a supposedly arbitrary selection is steered by a name, an order, or a first impression. One should not re-pick afterwards, since re-picking is not a second attempt at a question that has no answer but an attempt to have it both ways. And one should be willing to describe what one did as picking, rather than dressing it up as a discriminating reason one did not have.

Here is the part I am least sure of, and the part I most want you to attack. After I have picked, the picked option has a property it did not have before: it is the one I picked. That property is a genuine reason for what I do next — a reason to stay with it, to invest in it, to stop treating it as revisable. This is not rationalization, because the reason is real and was created by the act rather than falsely reported as having been there all along. What would be rationalization is the pretense that the reason preceded the pick. So the structure of picking is peculiar: it is not justified by a reason that comes before it, and it generates a reason that comes after. An arbitrary act can be the beginning of a rational commitment rather than a failure of one.

This suggests a test for whether a tie was genuine, which is the second thing I want you to attack. If, after picking, I find myself wishing I had taken the other — not because new information arrived, but because a difference I had suppressed now seems obvious — then the tie was not genuine, and I picked where I should have chosen. If no such wish appears and the pick settles into commitment, the tie was genuine. On this view the tie is partly retrospective: it is confirmed by the absence of regret. I can already hear the objection that this is close to unfalsifiable, since an absence of regret can always be redescribed as successful self-deception.

The ethics of picking is a further question and I only gesture at it. Where the choice affects others — a committee choosing between two applicants it cannot distinguish on the merits, a scarce good going to one of two equally needy claimants — a coin is fair, but fairness is not the only value in play. A coin may be worse than a tie-breaker that is substantive: need, willingness to bear a cost, a policy announced in advance. Flipping is then not a permissible pick but an abdication, and the interesting question is what makes the options level in the first place, which is itself a substantive question about what matters for the decision.

The moral I draw is that the ass is not a counterexample to rational agency. It is a counterexample to a theory of rational agency that recognizes only discriminating reasons. Our theories should distinguish the reasons that discriminate from the reasons that terminate, and should say what picking is, rather than treating it as choosing with the lights off.
