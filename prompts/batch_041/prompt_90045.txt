I'm a science journalist working on a feature about how hospitals and insurers draw the line between therapy and enhancement. Many hospitals and insurers effectively use what could be called a baseline-restoration rule: an intervention is covered when it returns a patient to a previous or species-typical level of functioning, and is presumptively excluded when it would push someone above that level. So growth hormone is covered for a child with a diagnosed deficiency but not for a short healthy child; antidepressants are covered for major depression but scrutinized for mood enhancement; and the same institutions will authorize preventive interventions, such as statins or vaccines, that don't restore any prior baseline at all. What philosophical commitments does this policy presuppose about disease, normal function, and the aims of medicine? Does it require a naturalistic, value-neutral account of disease, or does it covertly rely on a normative ideal of the healthy person? Is the therapy/enhancement line doing independent work, or is it a proxy for worries about fairness, authenticity, and medical hubris? And which parts of the policy are strictly licensed by that line rather than merely compatible with it—what would have to change if the committee replaced it with a straightforward welfare or capabilities criterion?
