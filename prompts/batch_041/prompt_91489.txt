I have been in a reading group on Bayesian confirmation for the better part of a year, and we keep returning to the problem of old evidence without ever getting past the point at which the discussion began. What I want from you is a diagnosis of the impasse, not another solution to the problem.

The shape of the difficulty is familiar. An agent is certain of some proposition e; a hypothesis h entails e; since P(e) = 1 for that agent, conditionalising on e cannot raise her credence in h, and the likelihood ratio does no work. Yet scientists evidently treat evidence of this kind as strong support, and the stock historical cases are ones in which a theory was accepted largely because it accommodated a fact that was already known. The responses we rehearse fall into a few families, and each seems to us to fail for a reason that is itself contested. One family says the agent should conditionalise on a counterfactual credence function, one in which she does not yet know e; the objection is that this function is either undefined, where e follows from background knowledge she is certain of, or else is the credence function of a different agent with different evidence, so that what has been computed is not the confirmation of h by e at all. A second family says the agent is never fully certain of e — there is always some residual doubt, some possibility of having misdescribed or misderived it — so that P(e) < 1 and the ordinary machinery applies; the objection is that the problem then dissolves only for agents whose grasp of the evidence is imperfect, and it was posed for the idealised agent of Bayesian theory. A third family distinguishes the question whether e confirms h from the question whether the agent was entitled to her confidence, or whether the acceptance of the theory was rational; the objection is that this concedes the problem rather than solving it, since the second question is the one we cared about. The group has divided along these lines and nothing any of us says moves anyone.

My own suspicion — and I would like you to treat it as a hypothesis to be assessed rather than a position to be elaborated — is that the framing is at fault in a specific way. In each family the question is taken to be how the agent should conditionalise, given e and h. But in the historical cases, and in the idealisations we build from them, h was not sitting there waiting to be tested: it was constructed with e in view, selected from a space of alternatives precisely because it entailed e. If that is right, then the evidence that matters is not e but the agent's evidence about the process by which h came to be on the table, and no amount of care with the counterfactual credence function will help, because the missing datum is not the prior probability of e but the selection of h. The statistician's analogy would be a fit computed on the same data used to choose the model's form, where everyone agrees that the remedy is to condition on the selection rather than to imagine the data unobserved. I want to know whether something like this is the right diagnosis, and in particular whether it makes the dispute substantive, verbal, or a case of two parties answering different questions under a single heading.

So diagnose the disagreement. Are the three families disagreeing about the facts of confirmation; about what a probability function is supposed to represent — an agent's actual degrees of belief, an ideal logic of evidential support, or something in between; about which cases count as data; or about an unstated starting intuition, for instance that confirmation must be a relation between propositions rather than between a proposition and a history of inquiry? Is a hidden formal question doing the work — whether conditional probability is well defined when the conditioning proposition is certain, or whether confirmation is a synchronic or a diachronic notion? Or is the problem ill-posed in something like the way that questions about what the agent would have thought had she not known e are ill-posed when her epistemic state is partly constituted by knowing e? If the last of these is right, say so plainly and say what remains of the problem once it is granted.

Then tell me what would count as progress under your diagnosis: a distinction we have been running together, a case that one family cannot accommodate, a formal result about conditionalisation on certain evidence, or a decision that the question should be replaced by another one. If you think nothing would move it — that the parties are separated by a starting intuition about what confirmation is for — I would rather hear that, with reasons, than a survey of the literature. Please do not give me a reading list; I want the diagnosis, the argument for it, and an honest account of what could change my mind.
