You are a philosopher of decision theory with a specialization in infinite decision problems, the foundations of expected utility theory, and the philosophy of probability. I want you to construct and defend an argument for a specific position. Please begin by brainstorming the structure of the problem, the strongest objections to the position, and your replies to those objections. Then present a clear, organized argument. Do not merely survey the literature or report what others have said; build the case yourself.

The Pasadena game is defined as follows. A fair coin is tossed until it lands heads for the first time. If the first heads occurs on toss n, the player receives a payoff of (-1)^n × 2^n / n dollars. The probability that the game ends on toss n is 2^−n. The expected value of the game, computed by summing the probability-weighted payoffs in the natural order n = 1, 2, 3, ..., is the alternating harmonic series, which converges to −ln 2 (approximately −0.693). The series of absolute values, however, is the harmonic series, which diverges. The expectation series is therefore only conditionally convergent: by rearranging its terms, one can obtain any real number as the sum, or make the series diverge. Consequently, the game has no order-invariant expected value, and standard expected utility theory assigns it no value at all.

Your task is to argue for the following position: The Pasadena game has a determinate rational value, and that value is −ln 2. The game is not a case of genuine indeterminacy or rational incomparability; it has a definite fair price, and the fact that other summation orders yield other numbers does not undermine this.

In defending this position, address the following:

1. Motivate what I will call the Sequential Realization Principle: when a gamble is generated by a sequential process whose stages are ordered in time, the value of the gamble is determined by the order in which the outcomes are realized, and the expected value is the sum of the probability-weighted payoffs taken in that order. Why should the temporal order of a sequential gamble be value-relevant? Is the principle a constraint on rationality, a feature of the metaphysics of value, or something else?

2. Address the strongest objection to your position: The game is fully specified by the probability distribution over payoffs. The order in which we list the outcomes is a notational artifact, not a feature of the game. If we reorder the outcomes, we get a different sum, but the game itself is unchanged. Therefore, the natural-order sum cannot be the game's rational value; at best it is the value of one particular description of the game. How do you reply? Is there a principled distinction between the game and its representation that survives this objection?

3. Compare the Pasadena game with the St. Petersburg game, in which a fair coin is tossed until the first heads and the payoff is 2^n dollars if the first heads occurs on toss n. The St. Petersburg game has an infinite expected value. Does your Sequential Realization Principle assign a determinate value to the St. Petersburg game? If so, what value, and does it resolve the St. Petersburg paradox? If not, why not? What does the comparison reveal about the nature of the Pasadena problem?

4. Draw out the implications for expected utility theory. Does your position show that EU theory can be extended to handle the Pasadena game, or does it show that EU theory must be supplemented by an additional principle about the order of summation? Is the resulting theory still recognizable as expected utility theory, or does it amount to a different theory of rational choice?

Be mathematically precise and philosophically explicit. Acknowledge the weakest points in your case, and explain why you think they do not defeat the position.
