A colleague in a forecasting seminar offers what I take to be the standard defense of cliodynamics, and I would like you to audit the analogy rather than restate it. His claim is that cliodynamics stands to history as Newtonian mechanics stands to the motions of the planets: once the relevant state variables are identified, the equations governing their evolution written down, and the initial conditions measured, the subsequent trajectory is a matter of computation rather than interpretation. On that basis he argues that the structural-demographic forecasts of a coming peak of political instability — elite overproduction, the immiseration of the general population, and a state too fiscally strained to buy off its rivals — should be treated as predictions in the same sense as an ephemeris, and that macrohistory ought therefore to be funded and taught as a hard science.

Give me a full audit. Set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the positions and velocities, of the force law, of the initial conditions, of the integration step, of the prediction and of its observational check — and then say where the mapping fails. I am particularly interested in which disanalogies bear on his conclusion and which are merely incidental: the absence of anything like fundamental forces or conserved quantities; the fact that the parameters are fitted to the same record they are then used to explain; the possibility that a secular cycle is partly an artifact of how one detrends and aggregates the data; the reflexivity of a system whose members read the forecasts; and the difference between predicting a future and postdicting a past.

Then give me the verdict: does the Newtonian analogy carry the conclusion that these forecasts deserve the epistemic standing of an ephemeris, or was the analogy doing the work all along? If some weaker comparison — statistical mechanics, seismology, epidemiology — does better, say which and why.

Finally, state how confident you are in the central claims you make, and single out the point you are least sure of.
