Ok so I've been roped into running next month's session for our department's ethics reading group and the topic that landed on me is procreative beneficence -- the idea that when you're choosing between embryos you should go with the one you expect to have the best life. I can defend it badly in a pub argument but what I need from you isn't the argument, it's a plan for teaching it in an hour to a room of about fifteen people: mostly clinicians and public health folks, one lawyer, two philosophers who will be annoying, and nobody who has read anything on this. So what has to come first, second, third, and why that order rather than the obvious one -- the obvious one being define beneficence, then define eugenics, then argue. I suspect that's wrong, because by the time the word eugenics is out everyone has already sorted themselves into camps and the rest of the hour is just people defending their camp.

The thing I want to hang the hour on is the embryo screening that's actually on the market now, where a clinic hands you a polygenic score for each embryo -- a number for heart disease risk but also numbers for height, for how many years of schooling, that sort of thing -- and the parents pick one. I think that's the right carrying example because it kills the comfortable assumption that selection is only about disease. But tell me if it's the wrong one, and either way say what the example has to have and what it has to lack to be load-bearing here; my worry is that any version of it where I say "the second embryo would have had a worse life" is doing philosophical work I haven't earned yet at that point in the hour.

Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually arrive, with the specific moment in the sequence where I should head each one off. The ones I can already see coming: that this is just medicine so long as it's about health; that if the principle holds then parents are obliged to pick the best embryo and are doing something wrong if they don't; that the child who isn't selected is being harmed by not existing; that the principle is about making the world better rather than about the child's own life; and the one I've started calling the shop window mistake -- people keep reading it as "pick the objectively best-scoring embryo off the shelf," when I think the point is only that it ranks the options you happen to have. If that last one is really the principle and not a mistake, say so and I'll drop it. Also tell me what to leave out at this level and say out loud that I'm leaving it out. I need one closing question for the room that will show me whether they've got the structure of the principle rather than just having opinions about designer babies. No reading list please, I'm not assigning anything. Keep it under 700 words and give me the sequence as a numbered list with a line or two per step, not long paragraphs.
