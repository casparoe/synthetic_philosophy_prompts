I teach a 90-minute session for about 18 graduate students drawn from economics, statistics, and philosophy, and I want them to feel — in their own bankrolls — why repeatedly taking a favorable gamble at the stake that maximizes expected value is a reliable way to go broke, and why the proposed fix is to maximize the growth rate of one's wealth instead of its expected value.

Design the game. Participants should start with a common bankroll, know the per-round odds and the win probability, and choose each round how much of their current bankroll to stake; I want the arithmetic of a favorable even-money bet to look compelling enough that most of the room stakes recklessly at the outset. Build in whatever structure is needed — parallel worlds, a public ledger, a borrowing rule, several rounds of play — so that the group runs into the gap between the ensemble average and the typical outcome before anyone names the growth-rate criterion. Include rules, materials, a round-by-round timeline, a payoff scheme that requires no real money, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the log-optimal stake, what overbetting past it does to the growth rate, and the contrast between the time-average and the average across players.

Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to keep a lucky early run from teaching the opposite lesson. Be frank, too, about what the game cannot reproduce: I suspect it can dramatize the growth-rate argument but not settle whether maximizing geometric growth is normatively required of a rational agent, as opposed to one defensible policy among others. If that is right, say so plainly and tell me what I have to supply in the debrief myself, rather than papering over the gap.
