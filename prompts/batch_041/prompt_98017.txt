I've been reading a lot of the older rationalist material on base rates, reference classes, and the outside view, and it's left me with a nagging problem I can't resolve on my own. I'm a data scientist by trade — I spend my days fitting hierarchical Bayesian models, so partial pooling, exchangeability, shrinkage, and the bias-variance tradeoff are my native vocabulary — and I keep running into the same argument whenever the topic of national character comes up. One camp insists that there are real, stable, causally efficacious differences between peoples, that refusing to name them is just refusing to look at the data, and that a properly estimated group-level parameter is as legitimate an object as any individual-level one. The other camp says the whole notion is a reification, that it's the ecological fallacy dressed up in aggregate statistics, and that even where the numbers hold up, generalizing about peoples is morally off-limits in a way that has nothing to do with sample size. I want you to take the debate as it actually stands — realism about population-level dispositions versus the reification critique and the moral objection — and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use: group-level priors and hyperparameters, partial pooling, exchangeability and its failures, the reference-class problem, the distinction between estimating a population parameter and predicting an individual, and the difference between a latent summary and a causal mediator.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophical dispute translate cleanly — I suspect the ecological fallacy and the small-sample worry have exact counterparts, and that the bias-variance framing of partial pooling is precisely the "don't over-update on one person" intuition — and which parts have to be distorted to fit? In particular I want to know whether the concept of "character" survives at all: in a hierarchical model the group parameter is a convenient compression of a distribution, not a thing the group has and transmits, so does the realist's "character" have any counterpart in my vocabulary, or is the realist smuggling in a causal claim the statistics never licensed? And the normative half of the question — when, if ever, it is acceptable to generalize about peoples — plainly has no statistical counterpart, since my models can tell me the expected error of a prediction but not whether it is just to make it. Is that residue a defect of my framework, or a sign that the philosophical debate was always two debates, one empirical and one ethical, that the word "character" keeps fusing together?

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. And be careful to separate what is settled among people who actually work on this from what is genuinely contested — don't hand me a contested view as though it were the consensus. I would much rather have an honest map of the disagreements than a tidy verdict.
