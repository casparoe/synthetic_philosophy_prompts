I am a novelist at work on a short story, and I want it to make a reader feel, from the inside, what it is for a word to hold together by family resemblance rather than by definition — the story should never name Wittgenstein or use the phrase. The setting is an invented island where a single term covers a scatter of practices: hailing a stranger across the reef, settling a debt in fish, teaching a child to hold her breath, keeping watch beside the dying. Any two of these share something, but nothing runs through them all, and the islanders have never needed a definition. The plot turns on a case no one has seen before — a young woman keeps vigil for a drowned stranger she never met — and the community must decide whether the term reaches it, arguing only in the idiom available to them: precedent, custom, who taught whom, how a thing is properly done. Give me the story, roughly fifteen hundred words, in close third person, with a mainland lexicographer present who keeps demanding the one rule that would settle it. I want the reader to feel at once that there must be a fact of the matter and that no one can produce the rule — and I do not want the question resolved in the last paragraph by anyone explaining it.
