I'm a backend engineer with no training in philosophy, and I've gotten into a long-running argument with two friends about the three-way contrast people keep drawing between cultures of honor, cultures of dignity, and cultures of victimhood — roughly, a world where insults are answered personally, a world where everyone is owed equal respect and offenses are routed to impersonal institutions, and a world where people are ranked by how much harm they've suffered and how well they can document it. My friends treat this as a straightforward ranking exercise, and I've noticed that all three of us are quietly grading the cultures by whichever one we were raised in. I'd like you to actually do the grading, on a scale I'll specify, and to be explicit about where the scale itself is doing the work.

Here is what I'd like. For each of the three cultures, give a score out of ten on each of the following five dimensions, with two or three sentences justifying each score: (1) false-alarm cost — how much damage the culture inflicts when an accusation or an insult is mistaken or maliciously invented; (2) reinstatement — whether someone who has been disgraced has a recognized route back to standing, and how costly it is to take it; (3) scale — how well it functions among strangers who will never meet again and have no shared history to appeal to; (4) distribution of enforcement — who actually does the punishing, and how much it depends on the aggrieved party's private resources; and (5) hostage-taking — how easily an outsider can weaponize the culture's own rules against a member of it. Then collapse the five scores into a single composite per culture and give me the tier list, best to worst. If you think one of the three doesn't belong on the same list as the other two — or that a fourth culture (something like an audit culture, where the currency is documentation and everything is permanently on the record) belongs in the ranking — say so and place it, rather than forcing the three-way comparison.

Two things I'd like you to do on top of the scoring. First, I've read that sociologists sometimes sort the ways communities respond to wrongdoing into a few styles: punishing the offender, making the offender compensate the victim, treating the offender as someone to be fixed, and reconciling the parties so the conflict simply ends. Map honor, dignity, and victimhood onto those styles — which style each culture defaults to, and which it cannot use at all — and tell me whether that mapping changes any of the composite scores. Second, I suspect my five dimensions aren't neutral: several of them presuppose that keeping the peace among strangers is the point of a moral culture, which is exactly what a culture of honor denies. Tell me which of the five are loaded in that way, whether the load is fatal to the exercise, and what a dimension set built from an honor-culture starting point would look like instead.

Please be clear about how confident you are in each part of this — the individual scores, the composite ranking, and especially the claims about what my dimensions presuppose. Single out the one judgment in your answer you are least sure of, and say why.
