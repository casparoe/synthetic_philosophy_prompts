I want a consistency audit of my own epistemological commitments, not a survey of the literature. Here is where I have landed:

(1) Evidentialism: whether a belief is justified depends only on how one's total evidence bears on its content.
(2) Higher-order evidence counts as evidence: facts about how reliable I am at a given kind of reasoning bear on the proposition that reasoning delivers. If I have good grounds to think my process is unreliable here, that tells against the conclusion, not merely against my confidence in it.
(3) Conciliationism about disagreement: when I find myself in a recognized disagreement with someone I take to be as good as I am at the relevant reasoning, and we have fully disclosed our grounds to each other, we should both move toward the middle, however compelling my own reasoning seemed.
(4) No rational akrasia: no one rationally believes p while rationally believing that their belief in p is epistemically ill-founded. Believing something while judging that believing it is a mistake is always a defect, never a coherent position to occupy.
(5) Self-trust: I am entitled to treat my own basic faculties as trustworthy without first establishing that they are; my inability to answer the global skeptic does not render my ordinary beliefs unjustified.
(6) A verdict I am confident in: if I learn that I worked through a long calculation while on a drug that I know makes people prone to error on precisely this sort of task, and I recheck and can find no mistake, I should abandon the conclusion.

Which of these genuinely pull against each other, which only appear to, and how severe is each real tension? Rank the real ones, and for each say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost. Then tell me which single revision would relieve the most tension, and what it would leave standing.
