I have been asked to give the commencement address at the small liberal-arts college where I have taught philosophy for thirty-one years, and it will be my last one, since I retire at the end of the summer. The room will be the usual mixture: several hundred graduates who are hot and restless and would rather be at lunch, their parents and grandparents, a board of trustees with half an eye on the endowment, and a row of my own colleagues, who have read the same books I have and will hear in anything I say the errors I have been repeating for three decades. I want to spend my eight minutes on a single question that has organized my whole working life and that I have never answered to my own satisfaction: why should anyone prefer knowledge to true belief? I do not mean the difference between a careful belief and a careless one. I mean the plain fact that two people can both be right about the same thing — one because she has understood it, the other because she guessed — and we treat the first as better off in a way that has never been easy to explain. Someone who merely believes, correctly, which road leads to Larissa will walk there as reliably as someone who knows; in a single case, the true belief guides action just as well, and it is hard to say what the knower has that the lucky one lacks. The answers I can give — that knowledge stays put while true belief wanders off, that knowledge can be explained and handed on, that knowledge is something you have done rather than something that happened to you — each seem to me to prove too much or too little, and I want the speech to say honestly which of them survives and to admit that none of them quite closes the gap. I also want it to face what these graduates are about to discover: correct answers are now free and instantaneous, and a machine will always tell them the road to Larissa and always be right. If true belief has become cheap, every defense of knowledge that rests on its usefulness is in trouble, and I would rather name that trouble than paper over it.

What I need from you is the speech itself, written for the ear and in my voice — a retiring teacher, plain-spoken, affectionate toward the students, a little impatient with my own profession. It should run roughly eight hundred to a thousand words, and it should carry its argument by example rather than by taxonomy. Build it around one concrete scene rather than a survey: I have in mind a student in my introductory logic course who produced the correct answer to a problem on the final and, when I asked her to say why it was correct, could only tell me that it was the answer at the back of the book, alongside a second student who got the same problem wrong, understood exactly where she had gone wrong the moment I pushed her, and repaired it on the spot. I want the distinction between knowing and merely being right to emerge from those two students rather than from a definition, and I want the speech to turn on the difference between them in a way my colleagues will recognize instantly without the graduates or their parents needing a single technical term. You may mention Plato once, since everyone has heard of him, but do not quote him and do not cite anything at all — no titles, no page numbers, nothing that would sound like a seminar. End by leaving the question open in a way that is generous rather than coy: I do not want a rousing finish that claims education makes people better, and I do not want a shrug either.
