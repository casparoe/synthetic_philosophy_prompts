For three semesters my metaethics seminar has been circling a single question, and I would like an honest diagnosis of why we cannot get past it rather than another round of the usual arguments. The topic is the evolutionary challenge to moral realism: the claim that because our moral beliefs were shaped by natural selection for their adaptive effects, and because the content of moral truth would have played no role in shaping them, those beliefs are not responsive to the moral facts and so do not amount to knowledge. The realists in the room concede the evolutionary premise in full and contest only the inference. Their standard move is to posit a third factor that would have disposed our ancestors both to hold the beliefs they held and to get the moral facts right, so that the correlation between moral belief and moral truth is explained without the truth itself doing any causing. The debunkers reply that this either assumes the very moral facts it is meant to secure or quietly swaps the debunker's causal premise for a different and much stronger explanatory one.

What I find striking is how much the two sides share. They accept the same evolutionary biology, the same rough picture of what it would take for a belief-forming process to be reliable, and even the same list of which moral beliefs are in play. Yet the realists hear the debunking argument as an ambitious epistemological thesis that must clear a high bar, while the debunkers hear it as a modest, nearly trivial application of ordinary genealogical reasoning, no more exotic than explaining why we crave sugar. My hypothesis — and I would like you to test it rather than grant it — is that the impasse is neither a disagreement about the biology nor a merely verbal dispute about the word knowledge, but a compound of at least three separable disagreements: first, about what the debunker's premise actually asserts, whether it is a causal claim about particular belief tokens, an explanatory claim about the best account of our moral dispositions, or a modal claim about counterfactual sensitivity; second, about which epistemic relation is at issue, whether reliability, tracking, safety, or some defeasible entitlement of another kind; and third, about the intended scope of the conclusion, whether it strips us of moral knowledge only, or equally undercuts the realist's own third-factor explanation, and if the latter whether that is a reductio of the argument or a bullet the debunker should simply bite.

I want you to diagnose the standoff. Tell me whether my three-part hypothesis is right, wrong, or incomplete, and where you would draw the lines differently. Show me which of the apparent agreements are genuine and which are concealing a deeper divergence — in particular, whether the two sides mean the same thing when they call a belief responsive to the facts. Test the argument against a parity condition of the sort the participants keep invoking but rarely state: a debunking argument is only as strong as the least plausible belief it would also debunk if its form were applied uniformly, so ask whether the debunker's reasoning, extended to perception, mathematics, or logic, is a consistent extension or a reductio, and whether the realist's third-factor reply is available with equal force in those neighboring domains. If the disagreement proves substantive, say which of the three sub-disagreements is doing the most work and why the others are downstream of it. If it is better described as two parties answering different questions under one heading, name those questions and say whether either side would accept the other's question as the right one to have asked. Then tell me what, concretely, would count as progress from here: a distinction that dissolves one of the sub-disagreements, a formal result about the scope of the argument, a concession one side is entitled to treat as decisive, or a demonstration that none of these is possible because the whole dispute rests on starting intuitions about the evidential force of genealogy that neither side can argue for. I am not asking for a verdict on whether the debunking argument succeeds; I am asking what kind of disagreement this is, and what would move it.
