I retired out of a biology department a few years back — thirty years of insect physiology, nothing glamorous — and since then I've been trying to patch the holes in my education, philosophy especially, which I somehow dodged entirely as an undergrad. I've drifted into a reading group that's slowly working through the evolutionary psychology stuff, and there's one fellow in it, a philosopher, who has what he clearly regards as the conversation-ender. Whenever somebody floats an adaptationist story — fear of snakes, step-parent effects, whatever — he says: but evolution is a tinkerer, not an engineer. His gloss is that selection doesn't design toward an optimum, it just grabs whatever parts are lying around and bolts them together, so you should never expect a well-made trait, and so any story that presents some behavior as a finely-tuned adaptation is a just-so story. From there he concludes the whole feild is storytelling.

I don't buy it, and I honestly can't tell whether that's my biologist's reflexes or just me not knowing the philosophy. So I'd like you to take his analogy seriously and audit it. Start with the correspondence table — what's supposed to line up with the tinkerer, the engineer, the scrap parts, the workshop, the finished gadget, and the person who wanted the gadget in the first place — then tell me where the mapping actually gives out. The part I most want is the sorting: which of the disanalogies matter for the conclusion he's trying to reach, and which are just scenery? Does "tinkered" even imply "not optimal," and does "not optimal" imply "untestable"? If the analogy can't carry him from tinkering all the way to just-so story, say so, and tell me whether there's a better comparison available or whether the whole genre of engineering talk should be dropped. One thing I'd ask you to be scrupulous about: separate what's actually settled among people who study this — about whether adaptationist hypotheses can be tested, for instance — from what's genuinely contested, and don't serve me a live dispute as though it were the consensus. I'd like the research program to survive, but not by pretending it's above criticism.
