Bit of an odd one. I'd rather you just reason this out from first principles than go hunting for names, because I dont think the names are the point here.

You know the result that gets quoted at people on the blogs constantly: for a Bayesian with a fixed prior and a fixed utility function who's maximizing expected utility, the expected value of free information is never negative, so knowing more can't leave you worse off in expectation, as long as you're allowed to just ignore what you learn. I think its Good, or maybe somebody earlier, I always mix that up. Anyway, it gets used to settle arguments about whether you should read the comment section, take the test, open the envelope, look at the polls before committing to a plan, and I've always suspected it's being asked to carry more weight than it can. But every time I sit down and try to build a case where knowing something hurts, I end up deciding I've smuggled in a non-EU decision rule, or a utility function that quietly shifts when I learn things, and then the theorem was never about my case in the first place.

So give me a ranking. Take the main families of "actually, information can be bad for you" that come up and order them from most to least threatening to the actual claim, where threatening means: if this one goes through, a perfectly ordinary Bayesian with an ordinary prior and utility function really is worse off for knowing, not someone who's already left the framework. For each one, a short paragraph on why it lands where it does, and say straight out whether it's a genuine counterexample or a case where one of the assumptions fails and the result was never about it. Cover at least: utility functions that change with what you know, infinite or undefined expected values, cases where other agents can observe what you do so your knowing changes their behavior, the version where information isnt free because you pay in money or in thinking time, and the version where you can't commit to not acting on what you learn so you know you'll do something stupid with it. Add anything I've missed, including the weird-probability angle where conditional probabilities don't behave (I half remember an argument that the value of information can go negative for certain finitely additive priors, but I can't reconstruct it and might be conflating it with something else).

Two things I care about most. Be ruthless about duplication, because I suspect half of these are one objection wearing different hats, and I'd rather have four real distinctions than nine that collapse into each other. If two of the families I listed are secretly the same thing, say so and merge them. And at the end, tell me which single one you'd bet still looks like a live problem in fifty years, and whether the original slogan has quietly been narrowed into something weaker than the version people actually repeat.
