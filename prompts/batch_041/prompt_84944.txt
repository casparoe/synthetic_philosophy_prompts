My reading group is doing Frankenstein next month and I got volunteered to kick off the discussion. Everyone always runs the "is the creature a person, does he have rights" thing and I'd rather come at it sideways, through the philosophy of biology literature on what counts as life, because I think that's the stranger question the book is sitting on and nobody in the group has read any of that stuff.

My angle, tell me if it's any good: Victor never tells us how the animation works. He says he found the cause of generation and life, got as far as "bestowing animation upon lifeless matter," and then clams up for Walton, so we never learn whether there's a metabolism in there, whether the tissue is just dead tissue that moves now, whether he ages or heals or sleeps, or whether he can reproduce at all — except that he demands a mate, and Victor seems convinced the two of them would breed a new race, which would make him the founder of a lineage and alive under an evolution-style criterion. Meanwhile a sterile mule is plainly alive and flunks that same criterion, a virus flunks the metabolism criterion unless you go with the virocell line that a virus is alive in the form of the cell it hijacks, a prion is infectious with no nucleic acid at all, the giant viruses blur the line further, and people run self-replicating programs that evolve in silico with no chemistry anywhere in sight. I also half-believe the line that "life" isn't a natural kind yet, that we can pick out instances without a definition the way people picked out water before anyone knew about oxygen, and the definition may come later or never.

So give me a reading of the novel through all of that: where these definitions actually get a grip on the creature, where they slide off, what the borderline cases predict about him, and where the book resists the whole exercise and I should just admit it. Format it as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up, since its going straight onto a handout.
