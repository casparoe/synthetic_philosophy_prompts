I'm in a grad seminar on nationalism and I need to bring something to the workshop next Thursday — the prof keeps saying she's bored of close readings and wants us to "do something with the material," and after an argument with my flatmate (who thinks Hobsbawm and Ranger's *The Invention of Tradition* and Anderson's *Imagined Communities* are basically just cynicism dressed up as history) I've decided to write a satire. Can you just write it for me? No citations, no reading list, I'll handle the footnotes.

The bit I want: a deadpan internal strategy memo, about 700 words, from a fictional brand consultancy to the steering committee of a client — an invented small country that's maybe thirty years old, has an anxious second generation, a flag nobody salutes properly, and a ministry of culture with a budget line for "heritage." The firm has been retained to manufacture the country's national traditions from scratch, and the memo is the phased plan: a folk dance with a plausible peasant origin, a national dish, a costume, a founding battle, a national day, a "timeless" funeral custom, the lot. Voice should be exactly that of a real consultancy deck — confident, numbered sections, slightly soulless, bullet points, phasing, deliverables, risk register — and the comedy should come from the actual mechanics of the thing rather than from jokes as such, so that someone who's read the literature laughs instead of wincing. In particular I want the memo to register, without ever coming out and saying it: that the product only works if everyone believes it's ancient, so there's a whole section on backdating and on "rustication" (worn edges, one deliberately garbled variant, a version in a regional dialect that contradicts the official one); that the previous client tried to invent ex nihilo and it died on contact with the villages, so the firm's standing rule is now to always graft onto some pre-existing local practice and just extend its pedigree, and the memo treats this as a technical constraint rather than a philosophical embarrassment; that the ministry needs it to feel like it welled up from the villages while the villages are the intended audience; and that the same technique is billed to a biscuit manufacturer under a different account code, which the memo mentions as a scheduling conflict. There should be a confidentiality clause and a note on document retention, because the memo existing at all would destroy the thing it describes — and I want the consultants to be aware of that, in a flat procedural way, without any of them breaking character and winking at the reader.

Two more things. First, the client's liaison keeps asking whether the late-nineteenth-century European cases the firm cites as precedent were really any different, and the memo's answer has to be slippery but not an outright lie — let the reader supply the uncomfortable thought. Second, the census, the maps, and the museum should show up only as an unglamorous appendix item, logistics on the critical path, the statisticians and the curator holding things up, since the memo's authors obviously have no idea they're standing in for something enormous. Keep it dry throughout: no italics, no nudging, no "little did they know." Assume the reader is smart but don't assume they've read anything — it should still be funny to someone who's only ever thought about this stuff at the level of "wait, tartan is fake?" And end on a final line that reads exactly "Bottom line:" followed by one sentence summarizing the firm's actual recommendation.
