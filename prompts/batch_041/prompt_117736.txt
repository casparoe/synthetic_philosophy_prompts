Last term, in a seminar on the ethics of agriculture, another participant — a just-war theorist by training — laid out the following argument about insecticide use, and I have been unable to decide whether it is a sound argument with a monstrous conclusion or a broken argument. The difference matters to me, because I sit on a committee that advises growers, and the whole shape of our advice turns on it.

Background. The decision we advise on is whether a grower keeps spraying a broad-spectrum insecticide on a calendar schedule or switches to threshold-based spot treatment, where the threshold is set in money: treat when the expected value of crop loss exceeds the cost of the application.

The argument.

(1) If a benefit can be secured without imposing serious suffering on a sentient creature, it is wrong to secure it by a method that imposes such suffering.

(2) Where there is a substantial probability that creatures of a given kind are sentient, their possible interests may not be discounted to zero; the weight given to a possible interest should scale with the probability that the interest is real.

(3) A broad-spectrum application kills on the order of ten million invertebrates per hectare — my colleague's figure, which I have not checked — and a large fraction of the survivors are left for days with impaired locomotion, feeding and grooming.

(4) The yield gain is genuine but modest; the human benefit per hectare, generously measured, comes to something like a few person-days of food.

(5) The suffering caused, summed over creatures and over days, exceeds the human benefit by several orders of magnitude, and no defensible weighting of how much a creature's suffering counts can close a gap of that size unless that weight is set at zero, which (2) forbids.

(6) So the application is wrong.

(7) Nothing in (1)–(6) turns on the particular chemistry: the same reasoning condemns botanical insecticides, microbial agents, inert dusts, flame weeding, cultivation, mowing, harvest itself, and the conversion of wild land to cropland.

(8) The obvious escape fails. It does not help to say that these invertebrates would have died soon anyway, or that they die in comparable numbers in the wild: what (1) forbids is suffering imposed by an agent who could have avoided imposing it, and the alternative to spraying — lower yields, more land under the plough — does not abolish those deaths, it relocates them.

(9) And if the lives of invertebrates contain on balance more suffering than contentment — a possibility that cannot be excluded, and that the conditions of wild and farm life make credible — then (1) applies to the continuation of those lives as well, and the argument yields a further conclusion: the greatest benefit available to invertebrates is a reduction in their numbers, and in the end their elimination.

My colleague accepts (9). I find it intolerable, and I suspect it is the symptom of a mistake upstream, since a plea on behalf of the insects ends as a proposal to exterminate them. But I cannot locate the mistake: (1) through (8) each seem to me more plausible than their denials, and I cannot say which one I would rather surrender. If I must choose, I would rather keep (1) and (2) than accept (9). Tell me which step fails, why it looked acceptable, and what rejecting it costs — including what becomes of the practical conclusion about spraying once that step is removed.
