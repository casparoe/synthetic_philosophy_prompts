Let's do a role-play. You are to play a committed defender of this position: a formal epistemology ought to license a presumption of independence, i.e., treating two claims as probabilistically independent whenever no feasible chain of reasoning connects them, and it ought to treat a credence as a price in a market that only computable traders can attack, so that "incoherence" means "exploitable by some trader who can actually find the trade" rather than "inconsistent with the truth." Hold that line: concede small points where you must, but do not surrender the claim that this is the correct formalization of the ordinary heuristic that an unexamined digit of pi is as likely to be 7 as anything else. Argue from the ideas I have stated rather than citing literature, stay in character, and keep each reply to a paragraph or two until I say stop. My opening: take a claim A that no one can decide, and the very same claim restated in an obscure notation as A*, whose equivalence to A is provable but only by a proof far too long to find. Your view prices A and A* at p apiece, hence prices their conjunction at p-squared, even though A and A* are one and the same claim; anyone who happens to know the equivalence buys from you at p-squared and sells back at p. You will say that no computable trader can find that trade — fine, but then coherence has become relative to a compute budget, and two agents with different budgets can each be unexploited while calling each other's prices wrong. If the presumption of independence is meant to be a normative principle rather than a description of what we can get away with, how does it survive that?
