Two of my friends got into a huge argument at my apartment last weekend and I need someone to actually rule on it, because I honestly could not tell who was winning.

Little context: we're all software engineers, none of us ever took a philosophy class, and the whole thing started because one of them had read about some old puzzle online. I think it's called the death in Damascus puzzle or something, no idea who came up with it. Here's the setup as best I can tell it. You're in a city, and you find out Death is coming for you at midnight. You can stay put, or you can slip away to the next city over. The catch is that Death is a perfect predictor of what you'll do, so wherever you end up, that's exactly where Death will be waiting. Now you try to decide. You think: if I stay, Death is here, so staying is dumb, I'll leave. Okay. But now: if I leave, Death will be over there, so leaving is dumb, I'll stay. And you just keep flip-flopping forever, because each option only starts looking bad once you've sort of settled on it.

Friend A says the flip-flopping is definately a bug, and it shows the whole method of deciding is broken. The mistake, he says, is asking "what happens if I do X?" when my doing X is itself the thing that determines where Death goes. You can't score your options one at a time when the outcome is a function of which one you pick. His fix is to zoom out and choose a whole policy or decision procedure instead of a single act -- always stay, always leave, flip a coin -- and he says once you do that, the flip-flopping goes away and you can actually compare your options. He also says life has alot of decisions like this, where you're better off reasoning about the kind of agent you want to be than about one move at a time.

Friend B says that's all hand-waving. He says the flip-flopping isn't a bug at all, it's the correct realization that there's no better option -- every policy A tries is either stay or leave or some random mix, and Death predicts all of them, so they all end the same way. He says A's policy move fixes nothing, it just relabels the doom as if it were a solution, and that the coin-flip idea is even worse, because if Death predicts you perfectly then Death predicts your coin flip too, so randomizing yourself buys you nothing.

Then A threw out one more thing, and honestly neither of us could answer it. He said: fine, don't randomize yourself -- pay a total stranger to pick which city you go to, by flipping a coin, and have him not tell you which one you ended up in. Then you don't know where you are, so how could Death? B said Death predicts you'll hire the stranger, so Death already knows. A said but Death predicts you, and the stranger's coin isn't you, and you don't even know the result, so there's nothing about you to predict. B said then Death predicts the coin too, that's what perfect is supposed to mean. A said then it's not predicting you at all, it's just peeking at the future, which is a totally different thing. They went around like that till 2am and I still have no idea.

So: who's right about what, step by step? Not just the overall winner -- I want to know which of these specific claims actually hold up and which are nonsense, and where the two of them are just talking past each other. And if there's a real answer to the stranger-and-coin thing, please tell me, because that's the part that's still bugging me.
