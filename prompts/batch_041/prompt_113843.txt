I teach the probability unit on a second-year philosophy of science module — 15 students, one 90-minute seminar, whiteboard and a bag of poker chips, no laptops — and every year the three interpretations of probability (the frequency view, the personalist/Bayesian view, propensities) come out of their mouths sounding like three flavours of the same thing. I want an activity where they have to actually operate as each of the three for forty minutes and run into the specific place where their own view jams, rather than hearing me list the standard objections afterwards. Here's the setup I've half worked out, but please build the rules properly, I'm not convinced it holds together.

Before class I fill an opaque bag with 100 chips and settle the black/white split by a coin flip: heads it's 30 black, tails it's 70 black, and I don't tell them which way it went. The draws are exchangeable in the sense that my beliefs about any ordering of the draws are the same, which I think is doing some work here. I split the class into three teams, each of which has to post a number every round plus a one-line statement of what the number is a number *of*: a Betting Desk that posts a two-sided buy/sell price in play money for a token paying 1 if the next chip is black; a Frequency Office that has to state its number as a long-run frequency and name the reference class it's a frequency in; and a Propensity Lab that has to state its number as a physical disposition of the setup and say what would count as that disposition changing. Then we draw chips round after round and everyone reposts. I need the full rules, the materials list, the round-by-round timeline, and the two or three injections that stop the three views quietly collapsing into each other. The injections I've half thought through: (i) after a run of blacks I announce that the bag gets re-prepared by the same coin-flip procedure every five draws, so the Propensity Lab has to say whether the chance changed while the setup looks identical; (ii) a reference-class auction, where the Frequency Office is handed several classes that are all defensible — all draws so far from this bag, draws from bags prepared this way that showed at least six black in the first ten, draws made in this room on a Tuesday — and has to pick one while another team argues for a different one off the same data; (iii) a sealed bag on the table that will never be opened, with each team asked for the chance that it holds 30 black chips. My guess is the three numbers agree for most of the game and split exactly at (iii), and that's the moment I'm after.

What I most need from you is the debrief and the failure modes. I want the debrief to walk through the specific moments of the game and say which bit of which view each one is, including the objection that the Betting Desk wins by construction because everything here ends up being a bet — I don't know how to answer that honestly, since the other two teams have to bet as well to play at all. And be straight with me about what the game can't show: I suspect it can't reproduce the serious case for frequentism (the actual work it does in sampling, testing, error rates) or the metaphysics of propensity as an irreducible dispositional property, and that five minutes of me talking has to carry those instead. Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time — teams peeking in the bag, the Frequency Office refusing to post a number and being right to refuse, seperate two-sided prices that I then can't police in real time (I'd like the pick-off rules pre-computed if that's possible). 15 students, 90 minutes, no prep from them.
