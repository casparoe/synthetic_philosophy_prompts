I'm a science and ideas journalist — long-form features, mostly where social science bumps up against philosophy — and I've been roped into running a session at our newsroom's ideas festival in the spring. 75 minutes, one room, about 25 people: a mix of reporters, a few academics who'll be around anyway, two or three policy people. No screens, no prep reading. I want it to be a thing they do, not a panel with me talking.

The theme is path dependence, the whole "was it always going to turn out this way" question. There are three things I want them to feel rather than hear, and honestly the session is only worth running if the activity delievers these:

First: "path dependent" gets used for at least three seperate claims that come apart on inspection. One is contingency — the outcome hinged on some early accident that could easily have gone the other way. One is inefficiency — a better option was available and we're stuck with a worse one. One is entrenchment — switching now would cost more than it's worth. I want them to live through a case where they get one or two of those without the others, so they stop treating the phrase as a single thing.

Second, the token-versus-type thing. Even if which thing won was basically a coin flip, the fact that some single thing wins and everything else dies might be overdetermined. So when they ask afterwards "would the same outcome have arrived anyway," I want them to notice they have to say whether they mean the specific winner or the shape of the whole outcome — and that the answer is different depending on which.

Third, I want a rewind. Halfway through I'll reset them to the start and let that early accident fall a different way, so they can watch whether the same outcome re-forms or a different one locks in. Then I'll ask them to bet on what "would have happened" in a world we can't actually run, and I want them to feel how little they can honestly say about it.

Practical stuff: no real money or meaningful stakes, so the payoffs have to be points or pride. Give me the rules, the materials, roles if you need them, a minute-by-minute timeline, the payoff structure, and a debrief that maps what just happened onto those three claims and the token/type point — but nothing gets named before the debrief, they should walk into the trap cold. Also tell me what will predictably go sideways the first time I run it, and be straight with me about the part of the idea the game can't reproduce, because I'd rather know that now than have the debrief quietly overclaim.
