For the past year a seminar I run has been stuck on what is generally called the paradox of deontology, and I would like a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another attempt to settle it.

The shape of the problem as we have been working with it is this. A deontological restriction forbids an agent to do a certain kind of thing to one person — kill him, lie to him, confine him, use him — even when doing it is the only available way to prevent several instances of the same kind of thing. If the reason not to do it is the badness of the thing done, the restriction appears to require the agent to treat that badness as less weighty precisely in the case where honoring the restriction multiplies it. So either the restriction is grounded in the value of persons, in which case it seems self-undermining, since it fails to honor the very value supposed to support it; or it is not so grounded, in which case it looks like an intuition that has been given no reason and perhaps cannot be given one.

Three positions have hardened in the room. First, the consequentialist: a restriction must be justified by what it protects, and if following it predictably leaves more people killed or used, it has failed by its own standard; the only defensible versions are indirect — rules whose general acceptance does the good — which concedes the point. Second, the Kantian-minded: the restriction is not a device for producing outcomes at all; it registers the fact that a person cannot be requisitioned, and the demand to minimize violations is not a demand an agent can be under without treating persons as a stock. Third, a colleague who holds that the impasse dissolves once we see that the badness of harm supplies an agent-neutral reason while the restriction is agent-relative — two kinds of reason, no contradiction, only a pun on the word.

Two attempts to break the deadlock failed in the same way, and it is this that I find diagnostic. We constructed cases meant to isolate the two rationales — a public health officer who can stop an outbreak only by confining one healthy carrier against his will for a month, or only by deceiving the public about a risk in a way that will cause panic and some deaths — and the consequentialist read them as displays of the restriction's cost while the Kantian read them as displays of whose standing as a person is at stake on either side of the choice. Then a colleague proposed a distinction that everyone accepted: between a restriction that protects persons from being used and one that constitutes the agent's relation to what she values, arguing that the paradox bites only on the first. The consequentialist replied that the second is a refusal to give a reason dressed up as a kind of reason; the Kantian replied that this reply simply commits the mistake the distinction was introduced to expose. The distinction did not resolve the dispute; it became a new site for it.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the parties are not disagreeing about cases or about facts but about what a justification of a moral requirement is supposed to accomplish: whether it must show the requirement answerable to a value the agent can be asked to promote, failing otherwise, or whether it may show the requirement to be constitutive of the agent's standing toward others, in which case the demand for a further showing is itself what is being denied. If that is right, the dispute is not verbal — the word is not doing the work — but it is also not empirical, and each side's standard of adequacy may be internal to its own view.

Tell me whether that diagnosis is right, and if not, what the disagreement actually is. Take seriously the alternatives I may be underestimating: that it is verbal after all, in the sense that the two sides use the same terms with different extensions; that a hidden formal question is doing the work, such as whether the structure of practical reason admits constraints that are not themselves maximizable, which a theorem or a counterexample might settle; that the sides are answering different questions under one name — what makes an act right, versus what it is to be an agent standing in a certain relation to others; or that the impasse shows the question is ill-posed. Ground the diagnosis in what each position actually commits its holder to, not in what it is popularly taken to say. Then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a case, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected, and why.

Tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis you give, and identify the single point in it about which you are least sure. Answer in at most 800 words.
