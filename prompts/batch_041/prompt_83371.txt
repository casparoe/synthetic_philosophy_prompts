A friend of mine prices gambles for sport, and I have been trying to reverse-engineer the policy behind her quotes. Her verdicts so far are these. First, the classical St. Petersburg gamble, a fair coin tossed until the first head, paying 2^n dollars if that head first appears on toss n: she says the expected value is infinite, that this is a defect of the gamble rather than a sum of money it is worth, and that she would pay at most about ten dollars for a ticket. Second, the same gamble with the payoff capped at one million dollars, so that you receive 2^n for n up to 20 and one million thereafter: she quotes about twenty-one dollars, which is the expected value, and treats the question as closed. Third, a lottery with probability 10^-100 of paying 10^100 dollars and otherwise nothing: she quotes one dollar, again the expected value, and again treats the question as closed. Fourth, a gamble that pays a thousand dollars with probability one per cent and otherwise loses five dollars, whose expected value is a little over five dollars and whose median outcome is a loss of five dollars: she quotes five dollars.

Now the case I want to put to her, which she has not seen. A fair coin is tossed until the first head; if the head first appears on toss n, you receive (-1)^(n+1) · 2^n / n dollars, so the payoffs alternate in sign while growing in magnitude: +2, -2, +8/3, -4, +32/5, and so on. Summing the expected payoffs in the natural order gives the alternating harmonic series, which converges to the natural logarithm of 2, about seventy cents; but the convergence is conditional only, and a rearrangement of the same terms can be made to converge to any real number at all, or to diverge. I have deliberately not told her any of that, and I do not intend to; I want to know what she would say.

I am less interested in the number than in the policy. Give me the candidate pricing rules that fit her four verdicts, say what each rule predicts about the new ticket, say which rule you think she is actually running and why, and name one further gamble I could put to her that would best tell the surviving rules apart. Be explicit both about where the rules make contact with the mathematics, that is, whether the relevant series converges, whether an expectation exists, whether a truncated version of the gamble has a limit, and about where they make contact with live disagreements among people who work on decision theory, such as whether a bounded utility function is a principled answer or an arbitrary one, whether declining to price a gamble whose expectation is undefined is a considered rule or an evasion, and whether an agent who refuses to rank such a gamble is thereby irrational. State plainly which parts of your analysis are settled and which are contested, do not present a contested position as the consensus, and mark clearly where you are giving your own view rather than reporting the state of the discussion.
