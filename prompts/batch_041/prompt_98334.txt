I have recently started spending a few evenings a week in a social virtual-reality platform, and the experience has left me with a puzzle I cannot resolve on my own. The platform behaves, in one respect, as though what happens there is entirely real, and in another respect as though none of it is real at all, and I cannot tell whether the two halves of that behavior rest on a single coherent view or on two incompatible ones.

On the first count: when users report being groped, cornered, or threatened by another avatar, the platform treats this as a genuine harm to a genuine person. It suspends and bans accounts, and it has introduced protective affordances — an invisible bubble that keeps other avatars at a fixed distance, for instance — precisely on the reasoning that unwanted virtual contact is a wrong that warrants a real sanction. No one at the company tells the complainant that it was only pixels and nothing actually happened to her.

On the second count: the same terms of service insist that the virtual currency, the items, the land, and even the account are licensed to me rather than owned by me, that they have no monetary value, that I may not sell them outside the platform, and that the company bears no liability if they are lost, stolen, or wiped. So the company will punish someone for harming my avatar while simultaneously denying that my avatar, or anything attached to it, is mine.

What I want from you is the implicit theory here. Does this pattern presuppose a particular view of what makes a harm real — that it is the subjective experience that counts, that being made to feel violated is sufficient — and if so, does that same view not also make the virtual sword real, since my attachment to it and my distress at losing it are equally subjective? Or does the practice instead presuppose an asymmetry between harm and property, so that harm is grounded in experience while ownership is grounded in social or legal recognition, which would let a platform acknowledge the first while withholding the second? Reconstruct whichever theory best fits what the platform actually does, and take care to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit. Where is it underdetermined — which rival theories would equally well license everything we observe?

Then tell me what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced. If we took virtual objects to be genuine property, as some courts reportedly have when virtual goods are stolen, or if we took virtual harms to be mere play, what in the platform's conduct — the bans, the protective affordances, the disclaimers, the prohibition on outside trading — would have to be given up or redesigned?

I would rather you push back than perform compliance. If the inconsistency I am pointing at is not really an inconsistency, if there is a principled distinction between the two halves that I have simply failed to see, or if the practice is too underdescribed for any theory to be read off it, say so plainly instead of assembling a tidy reconciliation. And if my framing smuggles in an assumption — that a platform's terms of service and its moderation practice must express a single view at all, rather than being a bundle of expedients — tell me where.
