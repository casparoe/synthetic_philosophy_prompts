Right, so our decision-theory reading group has spent a few weeks on the Kelly criterion — the idea that when you're betting a bankroll over and over you want to maximise the expected log of your wealth, i.e. the geometric growth rate, rather than the expected wealth — and the last session is meant to be about the bigger claim some people make on the back of it, that this is a general picture of rationality (some call it geometric rationality) rather than a trick that only works for a long run of independent, repeatable, small-stakes bets. I want to hand out a decision tree that takes someone from "yes, I agree you shouldn't take every positive-expectation bet" to a named position, so people can see where they actually land instead of arguing about labels.

Ground rules for the tree: every branching node should be a question someone could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and phrased as a concrete commitment or case, not "do you believe in log utility?". Don't give me reading lists or citations, just build it out of the ideas — assume everyone in the room knows what a random variable and an expected value are but nobody has the literature memorised. Eight-ish nodes, six to nine leaves.

The distinctions I care about, roughly in the order I'd want them:

- what's being maximised: expected money, expected utility, or the growth rate of whatever it is that compounds;
- whether the decision is a one-off or one draw from a long series of similar decisions;
- if the average across possible worlds and the average along your own single trajectory come apart, which one you think is authoritative for what you ought to do, and whether you think that's a fact about value or a fact about the dynamics;
- whether ruin matters because it's absorbing (you don't get to bet again) or just because it's bad;
- whether log utility is a claim about what you actually value or a claim about what any sensible agent must do given compounding;
- and whether the whole thing stretches to things that aren't money and can't be split into small stakes — health, trust, attention, years of life — or whether it's parochial to gambling.

Leaves I want to see, in whatever order the tree puts them: the full-Kelly growth maximiser; fractional Kelly because you don't know the true edge; fractional Kelly because you're just risk-averse over wealth; the unrepentant expected-wealth maximiser who takes any favourable bet at any size; the person who says the real argument is about time averages vs ensemble averages in a non-ergodic process and has nothing to do with utility; the pragmatist who thinks log is right for a long series of bets but expected utility is right for a genuine one-shot; the generalist who extends the geometric picture to anything that compounds (don't risk ruin in your health, your relationships, your reputation); and the deflationist who says this is all just expected utility with log utility and the disagreement is entirely about what people value.

Cases to run through it, please actually use these: a coin you know is biased, bet repeatedly a hundred times, where betting everything wins an astronomical amount in the lucky world; a single favourable bet that risks everything you have and that you'll only be offered once; a bet where you have a good but not certain estimate of the edge; a bet that's favourable but where the outcomes are correlated rather than independent; and a non-money case where you can't stake 3% of the thing (you can't risk 3% of a friendship, and there's no second run).

Two things I'd like you to do at the end rather than fudge. First, flag any pairs of leaves your tree can't actually seperate — I suspect the two fractional-Kelly leaves look identical from the outside, and that the ergodicity person may be behaviourally indistinguishable from the log-utility expected-utility maximiser while telling a completely different story about why — and say briefly why the tree can't tell them apart. Second, note any branch combinations that look coherent but that nobody seems to occupy, and say whether you reckon that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Build the tree from the logical space first and only then hang the names on the leaves, and if a leaf has no defender, label it as such instead of inventing one.
