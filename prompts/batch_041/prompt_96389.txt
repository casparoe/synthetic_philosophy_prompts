I belong to a small reading group on the historical basis of famous stories, and we keep re-running the same disagreement. We have worked through three cases — whether a war at Troy stands behind the Iliad, whether any single figure stands behind the Arthur of the romances, and whether the Exodus narrative rests on any real migration — and one member of the group answers every doubt with the maxim that there is no smoke without fire. His use of it is not casual. He treats the sheer existence of a detailed, old, widely repeated story as evidence that something happened, and he holds that the burden of proof lies wholly with anyone who says a story was invented from whole cloth; when a tale preserves details that fit an older world better than the world of the people telling it, he takes that as a real memory distorted in transmission. I am not sure he is simply wrong, but I am fairly sure the proverb is doing several different jobs at once, and that the job he needs done is not the one most of those readings perform.

What I would like is a menu rather than a verdict on the proverb as such. Set out the distinct precise claims 'there is no smoke without fire' could be making when it is applied to a traditional story — from the nearly empty (the story has a cause; its existence raises the probability of something, perhaps only of the belief in it), through the middle readings (some name, place, or institution in it picks out something real; it descends by some causal chain from a real event; a real person or episode survives inside it in distorted form), up to the strong ones (its structure or sequence reflects real events; the memory is continuous and popular rather than literary and late). For each, say whether it is trivial, defensible, or false, and say which of them would actually license his conclusion — that the story's existence shifts the burden of proof onto the skeptic — and which would leave his conclusion untouched. Then tell me what the readings that survive predict about the three cases, and about one I would add: a story that first appears late, grows more detailed with every retelling, and is eventually treated as confirmed by a shrine or a site that later generations built to match it. My suspicion is that the metaphor runs backwards in a case like that one — that the story is the fire and the smoke it makes is the evidence people later point to — so I would also like to know whether any reading of the maxim can accommodate it, or whether the maxim has to be abandoned there; and, since he will ask, which reading he most likely means when he says it.
