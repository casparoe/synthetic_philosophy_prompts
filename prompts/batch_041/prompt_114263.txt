Since retiring from the physical sciences I have been sitting in on a reading group on decision theory, and two of the regulars recently fell into a disagreement I could not adjudicate. The theory under discussion is one in which a gamble is evaluated by transforming cumulative chances rather than the utilities of outcomes: one ranks the possible outcomes from worst to best, applies an increasing "risk function" to the chance of doing at least as well as each outcome in turn — a function fixed at zero for impossibility and at one for certainty, and convex when the agent is averse to risk — and then sums the resulting reweighted increments against the utilities. When the risk function is the identity, the formula collapses into ordinary expected utility.

The exchange went roughly as follows.

A: This is not a new theory of rationality, only a notational variant of expected utility. Every risk-averse pattern it recommends can be reproduced by an expected-utility maximizer with a concave utility function; the risk function is diminishing marginal utility under another name.

B: That cannot be right. The theory violates the independence axiom — the sure-thing principle — which every expected-utility maximizer must satisfy. A preference pattern that violates independence is not representable as expected-utility maximization under any fixed utility function, so the two theories deliver different verdicts, not different words for the same verdict.

A: Independence is doing no work here. If all the theory says is that risk aversion is permitted, that permission was already granted by concave utility; and the risk function is a free parameter fitted to whatever choices one likes, so the theory predicts nothing and forbids nothing.

B: The free-parameter complaint proves too much, since utility functions are fitted in the same way. The real difference is normative. The theory denies that the sure-thing principle is a requirement of rationality, so someone who declines a fifty-fifty gamble over a fixed sum and then declines a scaled-up version of it is not thereby convicted of inconsistency. It also keeps the attitude to risk separate from the utility of outcomes, so it does not have to explain a dislike of gambling as a diminishing taste for money.

A: Even granting that, the theory remains purely consequentialist, judging acts only by the outcomes they might produce, and it saddles the agent with a single global risk function across every context. That is both psychologically implausible and normatively unmotivated — why should my attitude to a coin flip about pocket change govern my attitude to a gamble over something I cannot afford to lose?

B: The global-function objection is fair against the simplest version, but it is exactly the objection that can be raised against a single global utility function, and nobody takes that to refute expected utility. As for the consequentialism, many of us want precisely that: a clean separation between a person's attitude to risk and everything else they value.

I would like a point-by-point adjudication rather than a verdict on the whole. For each of the six moves above, say whether it is clearly right, defensible but overstated, or clearly mistaken, and briefly justify the rating. Then say what, if anything, both parties are missing. Please be explicit about how confident you are in each judgment, and single out the one point in your answer about which you are least sure.
