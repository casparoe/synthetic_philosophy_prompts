I read a lot of the rationalist blogs, and this is exactly the sort of dispute they chew over endlessly, but for once I genuinely can't call it, so I'd like you to referee. It concerns the three-stances picture of explanation — physical, design, and intentional, the picture usually associated with Dennett — and the real-patterns criterion that is supposed to keep the intentional stance from collapsing into mere instrumentalism. As I understand that criterion, a pattern is real when the description that captures it is a better compression of the data than the data itself: if a short description naming the pattern lets you predict a system's behaviour, the pattern has earned its keep, and there is a fact of the matter about which descriptions are shortest.

Two friends of mine, Dana and Marcus, argued about this at a reading group, and I have reconstructed the exchange as faithfully as I can. I am reporting it as a bystander; I did not take a side, and I would ask you not to assume I have one.

Dana: The real-patterns criterion is precisely what saves the intentional stance. Beliefs and desires are real because an intentional description is the best compression of behaviour — nothing at the level of neurology predicts as much with as few bits. And since "best compression" is an objective matter, the intentional stance is tracking structure that is really there, not merely a convenient way of talking.

Marcus: Compression cannot be the criterion, because compression is always relative to a coding scheme, and for any body of data you can rig a scheme that makes some gerrymandered pattern cheap. What makes a pattern real is not that it compresses but that it is causally load-bearing: you can intervene on it, and the behaviour changes in the way the pattern says it will. A set of beliefs that predicts behaviour perfectly but that you could delete without changing anything is a shadow, not a real pattern.

Dana: The intervention test is just more prediction — you are predicting what happens under an intervention — so if those predictions are good, that is more data compressed, and I can absorb your test into mine. Besides, demanding intervenability would make most respectable scientific posits non-real: the temperature of the early universe, historical facts, and even the physical stance's own patterns, since you cannot intervene on individual electrons at will.

Marcus: Then you have either trivialised your criterion or made it circular. If "best compression" turns out to mean "the compression that survives counterfactuals," you have renamed causal structure as compression and quietly conceded that invariance, not compression, is doing the work. If it does not mean that, then it cannot separate real patterns from gerrymandered ones, since both compress. Either way, the compression story is not carrying the weight you want it to.

Dana: Then let me be explicit. The compressions that count are the ones that stay stable when the system is perturbed — a good compression survives being knocked around. That is invariance, it is objective, and it is what I meant all along. We agree on the criterion; I simply insist on calling it compression, because compression is the part that can actually be measured.

Marcus: If we agree on the criterion, then the interesting question is what it implies about the intentional stance, and I think it implies something you will not like. Take an organism whose behaviour is fully predicted by an intentional description, but whose posited beliefs and desires are causally idle: the real causal story is a design-stance mechanism, and the beliefs are a summary that could be swapped for a different summary without changing a single behaviour. Your criterion — compression that survives perturbation of the behaviour — says the beliefs are real, because the summary keeps predicting. Mine says they are a useful fiction. This is a case where the two criteria come apart, and you owe an answer about which one is right.

Please adjudicate this move by move. For each of the six turns, say whether it is right, wrong, or partly right, and why; I do not want a summary verdict that skips the steps. Then tell me what both of them are missing, and who, if anyone, has the better of the exchange overall. I am particularly interested in three things: which of the two criteria — sheer compression, or causal load-bearing — the intentional stance's posits actually satisfy, if either; whether Marcus's idle-summary case is even coherent, or whether a description that survives every perturbation of behaviour is by that very fact causally load-bearing; and whether this is a genuine disagreement or has quietly become a verbal dispute over the word "real." Rank the considerations you take to be decisive accordingly.
