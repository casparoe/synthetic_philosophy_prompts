So I retired last year after 30 years of counting birds for a living, and I've spent the last two years reading philosophy of mind, and I've got myself into a bit of a pickle. I want to write a short research proposal — the kind of thing you'd send to a small foundation or a departmental pot of seed money for a six-month pilot — and I need help turning my half-baked idea into something that actually has a question in it.

Here's the background as I understand it. There's a method for studying consciousness where you don't take a person's report about their experience at face value, as a direct window into their mind. Instead you treat the report as data about what the person believes or claims about their experience, and then you try to explain, in third-person terms (brain, behaviour, whatever), why they say those things. I beleive this is called heterophenomenology and I think Dennett came up with the name, but dont hold me to that. What bugs me is that everyone argues about whether this is just behaviourism in a nicer coat, and nobody seems to argue about the thing that struck me immediately, which is: this is an occupancy problem.

For 30 years I did camera-trap surveys. You never see all the animals. You get detections, and a detection is not the same thing as an animal being there. You get false negatives (the animal's there, the camera missed it) and false positives (a blurry shape you misidentify). So you build a model with two layers: the ecological process (is the species present at this site?) and the observation process (given it's present, do we detect it?). And here's the clever bit — you can estimate the thing you can never see directly, occupancy, from repeated surveys, because the pattern of detections and non-detections constrains it.

Now, a verbal report of an experience looks to me exactly like a detection. The phenomenal state is the occupancy. The report is the detection event. And the report can miss (I have the state but don't or can't report it) or misfire (I report something that isn't quite what was there, or I report from expectation rather than from what's actually going on). So the interesting question isn't "is heterophenomenology behaviourism?" but something like: what does heterophenomenology have to assume about the report-generating process in order to license any inference from reports to experience at all, and is that assumption the same kind of assumption as the one in occupancy modelling?

I want the proposal to make this sharp. My gut says the analogy breaks somewhere important, because with a camera trap I can independently verify the detection process against animals I already know are there, and with a person reporting their experience there's no independent access to the phenomenal state to calibrate against. But maybe that's too quick — maybe there's a kind of repeat-survey structure (same subject and stimulus over many trials, or many subjects on the same stimulus) that does some of the calibrating work, and the proposal should figure out exactly how much.

So what I need from you: a short proposal, call it two pages, with (a) the question stated so it has a determinate answer, (b) why the existing debate doesn't settle it, (c) what I'd actually do month by month for six months — I'm not running a lab, it's me, a desk and a library, so the method has to be conceptual and argumentative rather than new data — (d) what would count as a result and what would count as the project failing, and (e) whether this is too big or too small for the time. Don't give me a reading list or citations, I'll chase those down myself, I just need the argumentative skeleton. And tell me honestly whether the camera-trap analogy is a cute dead end, because I genuinely can't tell anymore.
