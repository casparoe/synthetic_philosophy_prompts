Last night I spent two hours arguing with a close friend about whether evolutionary explanations of human behavior — mate preferences, xenophobia, religious belief, the whole adaptationist repertoire — deserve to be called science or are just storytelling with a biological vocabulary, and he ended the argument by producing Popper. On his telling, Popper demonstrated that Darwinism is not a testable scientific theory but a metaphysical research programme, because the principle of natural selection collapses into the claim that those who survive are the fit and the fit are those who survive: it excludes nothing, predicts nothing, and therefore cannot be falsified. When I pressed him, he added that Popper later conceded that natural selection can be tested after all, but he says this concession was a late embarrassment and does not undo the original point, which he treats as the settled verdict of philosophy of science on the entire Darwinian enterprise.

I do not know enough to answer him, and I suspect that the one sentence he keeps repeating is doing several different jobs at once without his noticing. What I want from you is a disambiguation rather than a verdict on evolution. Lay out the distinct precise claims that the assertion that Darwinism is a metaphysical research programme rather than a testable scientific theory could be making, ranging from the near-trivial to the radical. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something actually false, and be explicit about what kind of testability is at issue: the bare principle of natural selection considered on its own; selection taken together with auxiliary assumptions about heredity, variation, and ecology; particular adaptationist hypotheses about particular traits; or the research programme as a whole. Also distinguish readings that concern the logical form of the theory from readings that concern the actual practice of its practitioners and from readings that concern its metaphysical presuppositions, since I think he is sliding among all three.

Then tell me which of these readings his argument actually needs in order to reach the conclusion he wants, namely that adaptive explanations of human behavior are not science at all; which readings would deliver a much weaker conclusion than he thinks he has; and which readings would, if true, embarrass his position rather than support it — for instance by making metaphysical-research-programme status a feature of every mature science rather than a defect peculiar to Darwinism. He also keeps treating Popper's authority as decisive while waving away Popper's alleged later change of mind, and I would like you to say which readings on your menu would make that combination coherent and which would expose it as picking and choosing. Give me the whole thing as a numbered menu with a one-line verdict on each entry, ordered from the reading that makes the slogan most innocent to the one that makes it most damaging, so that I can take it back to him and see which entry he actually meant.
