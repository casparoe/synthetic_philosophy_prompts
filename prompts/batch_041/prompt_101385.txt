I teach a 90-minute session on the definition of death to a mixed group of about 16 medical and philosophy students, and I want them to feel, rather than merely hear, why the neurological and circulatory criteria cannot be folded into one tidy account. Design a role-played hospital scenario in which a patient who has met neurological criteria for death — no brainstem function, an isoelectric EEG, a ventilator doing the breathing — is simultaneously an organ-donor candidate whose heart is still beating. Assign roles (intensivist, transplant surgeon, bedside nurse, family member, ethics consultant, and perhaps a hospital lawyer), give each a private brief with its own interests and constraints, and stage the sequence: confirmatory testing, the declaration, the family conversation, the procurement request, and a final decision under time pressure. The rules and briefs should make participants collide with the conflict themselves — the relative who asks whether the patient is really dead yet, the surgeon whose protocol needs the heart still beating, the nurse who observes that the patient "looks alive" — before anyone names the whole-brain/higher-brain distinction or the dead-donor rule.

Then give me the materials (role briefs, a case timeline, a decision rule), a debrief that maps each moment onto a specific piece of the problem (the gap between the neurological and circulatory criteria; why irreversibility and permanence come apart; whether "death" is functioning here as a biological kind or as a legal and social status; why the transplant motive is both irrelevant and impossible to ignore), and an honest note on what the role-play cannot reproduce — especially what it cannot show about a body on which the two criteria would deliver different verdicts. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle the student who tries to dissolve the whole exercise by saying that death is just a definition.
