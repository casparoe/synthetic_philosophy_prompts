And here's the script, typed up as the student wrote it:

(a) The two complaints are (i) that expected value maximisation tells you to take a tiny chance of an astronomically large payoff over a certain modest one, which is the mugging case, and (ii) that it tells you the far future swamps everything in the present, because of how many people might exist later. These are really the same complaint, because in both cases the numbers get out of hand — in one it's the probability, in the other it's the value. Both are complaints about the decision rule rather than about value, since the axiology is just whatever the agent's utility function says.

(b) The reply says the rule is fine and the inputs are the problem: nobody knows the probability of a one in a googol event, and nobody can put a number on a galaxy of future lives. Stop pretending to know these things and the fanatical verdicts go away, so expected value maximisation survives as the correct rule. This works, because in the cases people actually worry about — mugging, the far future — the estimates really are garbage, so the fanatical verdict is an artefact of the garbage and not of the rule.

(c) I think this is a strong point. If you avoid fanaticism you will sometimes turn down a tiny chance at an enormous good, and turning it down is choosing the modest over the enormous, which is the same move the fanatic is accused of. So the anti-fanatic has no principled ground to stand on and the objection collapses.

(d) Clearly right: taking a one in a billion chance of saving a million lives over certainly saving one life. Clearly wrong: handing your wallet to a mugger who threatens to torture you in a way you cannot check unless you pay up. The difference is that in the second case the probability is made up by the mugger, whereas in the first it is just small.
