I am a graduate student in political philosophy finishing a seminar paper on the ethics of markets, and I would like you to act as a demanding but constructive referee — someone who works on commodification and the ethics of exchange and who is willing to tell me flatly that a distinction I have drawn is either empty or already sitting in the literature. Assume I know the standard writers in this area (Sandel, Satz, Radin, Roth) and do not give me reading lists, citations, or a neutral survey of possible positions: I want you to think carefully about the argument below and then take a definite stance on whether its central claim survives, with reasons. Please start by brainstorming the two or three strongest objections you can see, and only then commit to a verdict.

The paper argues as follows. The literature on repugnant markets tends to treat the objection to a market in a good as an objection to the good itself — to its meaning, its proper sphere, its value. I claim this mislocates the objection in a large class of cases. What people find repugnant is not the presence of money around a good but a relational property of the transaction: that the price is a function of one party's lack of acceptable alternatives. Call this the Dependency-Rent Account. On this account a market is objectionable to the degree that the gain one party takes from the exchange is generated by the other party's desperation; the closer the burden-bearer is to having no alternative but to transact, the more surplus the counterparty captures, and the more the exchange looks like extraction rather than trade. Three consequences are supposed to follow. First, the account predicts that repugnance should track market structure rather than the good: a public monopsony that buys at a fixed, publicly financed price, assigns the burden by lottery, and decouples any individual's payment from her own desperation should be markedly less repugnant than a free market in the same good, even though the good and the transfer are identical. Second, the direction of desperation should not matter: price gouging for ice, water, or a lifesaving drug after a disaster is objectionable for the same reason as kidney sales, even though in the first case the desperate party buys and in the second she sells. Third, the account explains why the objection is often weaker when a payment is framed as compensation or insurance rather than as a price — compensation is indexed to the burden borne, a price to the buyer's willingness to pay.

The account then runs into vote selling, and I cannot decide whether this is a refutation or merely a boundary. The vote seller is not desperate in the relevant sense; her alternative to selling her vote is simply keeping it. Yet selling votes is, if anything, more repugnant than selling kidneys. So I have bolted on a second and apparently independent clause: some goods are attached to roles rather than owned by persons, and roles carry a constitutive non-transferability condition, so that alienating the good does not merely degrade it but destroys the very thing that made it worth having. Voting looks like this; so, arguably, do jury service and the exercise of public office. But now the paper looks disjunctive and ad hoc, and the second clause may have nothing to do with commodification at all — it may be a claim about the corruption of roles that would hold even if no money changed hands, as when a vote is traded for a favour in kind. There is one further piece of evidence I find suggestive but cannot place. Consider a gradient: paying a citizen to vote for the candidate she already supports seems least objectionable, paying her to abstain seems worse, and paying her to vote for a candidate she opposes seems worst. The Role-Alienation clause predicts that even the first case is wrong; the Dependency-Rent Account predicts that all three are roughly on a par, since none involves desperation. So the gradient seems to be evidence for the second clause and against the first.

What I want from you is a verdict rather than a menu. Tell me whether the Dependency-Rent Account survives as a single thesis or should be split into two independent theses; whether the Role-Alienation clause is genuinely about commodification or is a separate claim about roles that I have dressed up as one; and whether the voting gradient is as damaging to the first clause as I fear, or whether it can be explained within it (for instance by the thought that what is sold in all three cases is not a vote but a standing capacity to be a reliable instrument of someone else's will). Along the way, flag anything I have got wrong about the real-world cases — kidney markets, disaster price gouging, the one country that has run a government-organized donor-compensation scheme for decades — or about where my view sits relative to the existing positions. Be blunt about the parts that are merely redescriptions of the obvious.
