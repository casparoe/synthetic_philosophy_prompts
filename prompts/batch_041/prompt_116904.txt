Question 3 (20 marks)

Classical hedonism holds that pleasure is the only intrinsic good and pain the only intrinsic bad. The heterogeneity objection is commonly described as the most serious difficulty facing the view. (a) State the objection as precisely as you can, and explain why it is a difficulty for hedonism specifically rather than an observation about how experiences happen to feel. (6) (b) Consider two hedonist replies: one that revises the account of what pleasure is, and one that appeals to our ability to compare or rank pleasures. Assess how far each reply succeeds. (10) (c) Say what the objection, so assessed, shows about hedonism's prospects as a theory of welfare. (4)

Student's answer

(a) The objection begins from the fact that the things we call pleasures have almost nothing in common. The taste of a ripe peach, the easing of a headache, the satisfaction of finishing a proof, the pleasure of a friend's company — introspect these and you find no single quality running through all of them. What you find is a mixed bag of experiences, held together only by the word we use for them. So "pleasure" names at best a nominal kind, and the hedonist's central term is equivocal. This is a difficulty for hedonism in particular, and not merely a fact about experience, because hedonism needs one property that explains why every pleasure is good; if there is no such property, there is nothing for the theory to assign value to, and the claim that pleasure alone is good collapses into a list.

(b) The first reply revises the account of pleasure. It concedes that the introspected contents of pleasures differ, but holds that they share a certain feeling-quality, a pleasantness present in each and varying only in intensity. Feldman's attitudinal hedonism is the modern form of this reply: on his account all pleasures do share a distinctive introspectable quality, namely the quality of being enjoyed. This is at least a reply, since it offers a property that could do the unifying work, though it is open to the complaint that the alleged feeling-quality cannot be found by introspection, and that the same sensation can be pleasant on one occasion and unpleasant on another.

The second reply appeals to comparability. Mill's distinction between higher and lower pleasures, together with his appeal to competent judges, shows that pleasures differ in degree rather than in kind and that the judges' preferences supply the common measure in which all pleasures can be ranked. If pleasures can be ranked on a single scale, they have something in common after all, and the hedonist keeps his single good while allowing that pleasures are various. This is the stronger of the two replies, since it does not rest on a contested claim about introspectable feeling.

(c) The objection therefore does not sink hedonism. Once pleasure is understood as a way of taking something to be enjoyable rather than as a sensation, the heterogeneity of sensations is no obstacle, and hedonism survives as a theory of welfare at least in its attitudinal form. The only real cost is that hedonism must give up the idea that pleasure is a single feeling, which is a cost most hedonists have been willing to pay.
