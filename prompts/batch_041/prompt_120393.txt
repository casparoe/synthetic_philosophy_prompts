Clever Hans, the horse exhibited in Germany in the opening years of the twentieth century by Wilhelm von Osten, a gymnasium mathematics teacher, was said to add, subtract, multiply, work with fractions, tell the time, keep a calendar, read, spell, and understand German, answering by tapping a hoof or nodding; a commission of thirteen appointed by the German board of education concluded in 1904 that no trickery was involved, and it fell to Oskar Pfungst, testing with blinders, with questioners other than the owner, and with questioners who sometimes did not know the answer, to show that the animal answered correctly only when the questioner both knew the answer and could be seen. Read this episode through the framing that treats Darwin and Turing as having performed matching inversions of reasoning — Darwin substituting undirected process for a designer, Turing substituting the question of how a mechanism produces competent behaviour for the question of whether it comprehends. Do not recite the framing; make it precise, then test it against the case.

Begin by separating three claims that the formula competence without comprehension can carry: a claim about the mechanism, that no state in the causal process represents what the performance is about; a claim about explanation, that comprehension need not be invoked in order to explain the performance; and a claim about the world, that comprehension is nowhere present in the causal chain at all. Say which inversion supports which claim, and whether either entails the third.

Then do the mapping. Identify the system whose competence is in question, say what the horse's performance was evidence for, and assess rather than assume the following claim: that the striking feature of Hans is not the absence of comprehension but its displacement, since the behaviour was genuinely competent and comprehension was genuinely present, only lodged with the questioner rather than the animal, with the consequence that the inversion tells you to look for a mechanism but not where in the loop to look. Say precisely what Pfungst's controls establish — that the horse had no comprehension of arithmetic, or only that the questioner's cues were sufficient to produce the answers — and whether the horse's sensitivity to postural and facial cues is itself a competence that stands in need of explanation, and whether it should be explained by training, by the animal's natural social cognition, or by both. Note the Darwinian face of the case as well as the Turingian one, and ask whether the mechanism that actually produced the answers was designed by anyone at all.

Finally, run the analysis back onto the framing. Does Hans vindicate the inversions, embarrass them, or leave them untouched? Is competence without comprehension a thesis about explanation rather than about minds? Does the familiar methodological moral — that the expectations of the observer can constitute the performance observed — follow from the analysis, or is it a separate lesson resting on different grounds? Close by saying where the framing falls silent, and end with one short paragraph on what the analogue of the questioner's cue would be in the evaluation of a contemporary language model.
