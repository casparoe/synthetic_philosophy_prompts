In my school's economics club we run a play-money prediction market, and right now the contract on our basketball team making the state tournament is trading at 0.62. Two of the seniors who run it, Maya and Devon, have been arguing for weeks about what that number means, and they've asked me to referee because I'm the one who keeps the ledger.

Maya says the price is a piece of knowledge: no single trader on the floor knows much, but the price at any moment reflects what all of us together know, so the best available answer to "will the team make state?" is simply to read the board. Devon says that's mysticism. If the price really did contain everything the traders know, then nobody would ever have a reason to trade — to buy at 0.62 you'd have to think the price is too low, and if the price already contains what you know, there is nothing for you to disagree with. Trade happens only because people believe they know something the price doesn't, which means the price never contained everything after all. Maya replies that this proves too much: information enters prices exactly through the profits traders earn by acting on it, so a market in which no one ever had a private reason to trade is a market no one would bother to inform. Devon answers that this is the contradiction restated rather than resolved — the more accurate the price, the less anyone is paid for making it accurate, so the perfectly accurate market is the one where nobody does the work.

Neither will budge. Can you sort out who is right about what, point by point? Specifically: (1) Is Devon's no-trade argument sound as he states it, and what does it quietly assume about the traders? (2) Does Maya's reply rescue the claim that prices aggregate knowledge, or does it concede Devon's point? (3) If traders can reasonably hold different beliefs, as we plainly do, does that help Maya or hurt her? (4) Given all this, what should I, the person reading the ledger, actually conclude about what the 0.62 knows?
