I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been building a small knowledge-base agent on the side. To decide how the thing ought to represent what it "knows," I read around in the standard way of modelling belief as a set of possible worlds, and a friend who did a philosophy degree helped me turn it into an argument. The argument looks airtight to me, but its conclusion is absurd, and I can't locate the bad step. I'd like you to find it.

Here is the argument. (1) An agent's doxastic state can be represented by a single set W of worlds — intuitively, the worlds the agent has not ruled out. (2) A proposition belongs to what the agent believes exactly when it is true at every world in W; I intend this as an account of what the agent's state represents, not as a one-way test. (3) Every logical truth is true at every world, hence true at every world in W. (4) So for any logical truth L, L is true at every world in W, and therefore, by (2), the agent believes L. (5) Suppose the agent believes p and believes p→q; then p and p→q are true at every world in W, so q is true at every world in W, so by (2) the agent believes q. (6) Hence the agent believes every logical consequence of anything she believes, and nothing in this reasoning referred to her memory, her reasoning speed, her attention, or what she has actually considered. (7) Now consider a first-year student who believes the usual axioms of arithmetic and believes the usual inference rules are truth-preserving. She has never heard of Fermat's Last Theorem and would say, if asked, that she has no opinion about it; and she plainly does not hold infinitely many distinct beliefs, one per theorem. (8) Yet (6) says she believes Fermat's Last Theorem and each of the infinitely many other theorems of arithmetic. (9) So either she does believe all of them, or (2) is false.

The conclusion in (8) is the one thing I can't accept; everything else I'd defend if I could. I already tried one patch and it didn't help: I said the agent believes p only when p is true at every world in W and p is expressible in the agent's own vocabulary, but Fermat's Last Theorem is perfectly expressible in arithmetic vocabulary, so the patch leaves (8) untouched. Tell me which numbered step I should reject, why it looked so plausible, and what rejecting it costs me — in particular, whether I can keep any version of the idea that belief is closed under logical consequence, and whether the possible-worlds representation can be salvaged by adding something to it (I have a vague sense that people talk about "awareness," or about letting impossible worlds into W, or about a distinction between explicit and implicit belief, but I have no idea what any of that is supposed to buy) or whether I have to give it up outright. If more than one step can be defended as the culprit, rank them by how much each would cost me.
