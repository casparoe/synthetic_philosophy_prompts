I sit on the board of a small charitable foundation — I'm a software engineer by training and the only member with no background in law, history, or philosophy — and next month I have to persuade the other trustees that the foundation itself owes something for a wrong committed by the business that created our endowment: in the 1890s it ran a works that relied on forced prison labor. My argument is that an institution which keeps claiming its past as its own — the name, the buildings, the endowment, the reputation — cannot disclaim the wrongs of that same past, and that what we owe is a matter of genuine guilt rather than merely an inherited debt we could decline. I'm told this is roughly the ground Karl Jaspers covered when he wrote about German guilt after the war, though I have not read him.

The board is nine people: four who think in terms of fiduciary duty and legal liability, two historians who will be skeptical of treating an institution as a continuous moral agent, one retired clergy member, and two who will want to know what any of this costs and who gets to decide.

Draft me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually face from these people, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that person would put it, with a short reply and a note on where the reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Assume I know no philosophy and cannot cite anything, so argue from first principles. Under 900 words.
