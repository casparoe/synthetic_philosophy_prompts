Kant’s practical philosophy accords animals no direct moral standing. They are things rather than persons; our duties concerning them are indirect duties towards humanity; and the ground of this, in his system, is the restriction of moral personality — and with it the domain of duties of right — to beings capable of autonomy. Take that reading as given.

Suppose Kant were alive now and had to respond to three developments that postdate him. The first is industrial animal agriculture: animals reared at scale under conditions few would defend as humane, within an economy that treats the practice as unremarkable. The second is a proposed legal framework that would abolish the property status of animals and place them in a third category, neither persons nor property, under a regime of guardianship: sentience would trigger protection, public authorities would be empowered to enforce duties of care, and animals would hold legally recognised interests while remaining incapable of claiming them. The third is the marginal cases argument, which observes that many human beings lack the very capacities Kant made the ground of moral personality, yet are treated as persons.

Predict Kant’s considered positions. In doing so, distinguish three things: where he would hold fast, and whether his conclusions follow from his principles or merely from his empirical beliefs about animal minds; where he would be pushed to revise, and what the revision would cost him systematically — in particular, whether he could admit duties of right towards animals, or enforceable duties of care, without abandoning the person/thing distinction; and where his framework would be silent or indeterminate. Say explicitly whether he would treat industrial farming as a wrong done to animals or only as a failure of human self-respect. Consider also whether his account of organisms as natural ends in the Critique of Judgement supplies resources his practical philosophy withholds, and whether that tension is real or merely apparent.

Give a verdict on each development, rank your predictions by confidence, and identify the textual or structural considerations that would most change your mind. No citations or reading list; argue from the shape of the position. Around 1,000 words.
