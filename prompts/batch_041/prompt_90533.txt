Two people in my lab got into it over drinks last week about how we should be building the safety case for a system we're shipping, and I've been chewing on it since. I'm the bystander here, I honestly don't know who's right, so don't read anything into the way I tell it.

A is from the formal-methods side. He says we should only ship things we can give worst-case guarantees about. Anything weaker is gambling with other people's lives, and he thinks that's the whole ballgame — you pick an adversary class, you prove the system holds against everything in it, done. When B pushed back he said: at least worst-case is honest. Average-case needs a probability distribution over how the thing actually gets used, and nobody can hand you that distribution. It's a story you tell yourself so you can sleep at night. And the stakes are asymmetric anyway — a small chance of catastrophe isn't paid off by a good expected value, because the loss is unbounded and you don't get a second run.

B is an empiricist. Her first reply was that the guarantee is only ever as good as the adversary class, and choosing that class is where all the real work and all the quiet dishonesty happens: make it tight and the guarantee is vacuous, make it loose and you've just written down a list of the failures you're willing to eat. Second, she said the unbounded-loss point cuts both ways — if you refuse to ship until you have a worst-case proof, you might be locking in the worst case by default, since the thing you don't ship is replaced by something else with its own tail risk. Not shipping isn't the safe move, it's just a move with its own outcome distribution.

A came back that fine, then treat shipping-or-not as a decision under uncertainty too, he can put "don't ship" in the action set — that's not an argument for average-case, it's an argument for making the decision explicitly, which he can do in a worst-case frame. He also said her move proves too much: if every refusal is itself risky, then the precautionary instinct is dead, and so is the whole safety mindset.

B said the precautionary bit is right but doesn't rescue worst-case. Her point: even a worst-case analysis over an adversary class needs some weighting over how likely each adversary is to actually show up before you can decide anything. "Assume the worst" just assigns probability one to the worst element — that's a prior too, a maximally paranoid one, and it isn't more objective than any other prior. So the real disagreement isn't worst-case versus average-case, it's which prior, and A picked his by fiat.

A said the prior isn't arbitrary, it's chosen to be robust — the point is to be insensitive to it, and a bound that holds across a whole family of distributions is strictly stronger than one that holds for a single distribution. B said sure, but the family is your choice and it's usually sized to be tractable, and robustness to the family only means robustness to reality if reality is inside the family. She said the track record is that these systems fail on inputs nobody in the threat model imagined, which is exactly what a too-narrow adversary class hides. A said then widen it. B said then the guarantee becomes "nothing bad ever happens," which is either trivially true or trivially false.

Go through this and adjudicate it point by point — who's right at each step, where each of them is overreaching, and what both of them are missing. Two things I'd especially like separated, because I think they kept sliding between them without noticing: worst-case over inputs given a fixed system, versus worst-case over which system you choose to build. And at the end, tell me whether the whole worst-case-versus-average-case framing is a real dilemma or a false one.
