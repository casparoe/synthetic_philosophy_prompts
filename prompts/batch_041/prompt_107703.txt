You are a philosopher of action and of causation, with expertise in the metaphysics of omissions and in the criminal law's distinction between acting and omitting. Think carefully before you answer, and open with a brief brainstorming section in which you lay out the candidate theories and their costs before you commit to any of them.

Consider a pattern that runs through the criminal law of most common-law jurisdictions. A stranger who stands by while a child drowns in a shallow pond -- who could have saved the child at no risk to herself -- commits no crime; the law recognizes no general duty to rescue. Yet the same system holds a parent liable for the death of a child she fails to feed, holds a lifeguard liable for a swimmer he fails to help, holds liable anyone who has begun a rescue and then abandoned it, and holds liable anyone whose own careless conduct created the peril, so that if you knock a pedestrian into the road you must at least try to help. Some European legal systems, by contrast, do impose a general duty to rescue. Meanwhile the same common-law system permits a physician to withdraw a ventilator from a patient who will then die, and describes this as letting the patient die rather than killing, while a lethal injection is killing. And in tort, a failure to warn of a danger can ground liability, whereas a bare failure to act generally cannot.

Set aside for now whether any of this is morally attractive. I want the theory of causation, and the theory of agency, that the pattern presupposes. Specifically: does the practice presuppose that an omission -- the absence of an act, a nothing -- cannot stand in a causal relation at all, so that the parent's liability must be grounded in duty rather than in causation? Or does it presuppose a counterfactual or quasi-causal account on which an omission counts as a cause whenever the omitted act would have prevented the harm? Reconstruct the implicit view as charitably as you can, then say which rival views the practice actually rules out and which it merely happens to fit. Identify the places where the practice is underdetermined, where several incompatible theories of causation by omission would license exactly the same doctrine.

Then tell me whether the parts of the practice presuppose consistent things. Is the law's treatment of the drowning stranger coherent with its treatment of the parent, the abandoning rescuer, the ventilator-withdrawing physician, and the failure to warn? If the pattern rests on a single theory, say what it is; if it rests on several that do not fit together, say where the seam is. Finally, suppose we replaced the implicit view with a straightforward but-for account, under which any failure whose occurrence would have prevented the death is a cause: what in the practice would have to change, and what could stay as it is?

Be precise about the difference between what the law strictly presupposes and what it finds convenient, and flag clearly any point at which you are reconstructing a theory rather than reporting one the practice commits to. Aim for an answer that a legal theorist and a metaphysician could both read without wincing.
