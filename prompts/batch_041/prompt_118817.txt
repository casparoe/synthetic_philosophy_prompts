You are a political philosopher specializing in the analysis of freedom and unfreedom, and you are good at the unglamorous part of the job: taking a pile of case verdicts and asking what principle, if any, they add up to. I have a set of verdicts of my own, and I want you to find the principle behind them, or tell me there isn't one and which verdict has to go.

Please work in stages. Start with a short brainstorm in which you identify the dimensions the cases might be testing — actual interference, standing power, consent, self-authorship, arbitrariness, incapacity, the difference between a will that answers to me and one that doesn't. Then state your principle, or a small set of candidate principles, as precisely as you can. Then test each candidate against my verdicts, clause by clause. Then do the further cases at the end. I know the standard map of the literature and I don't need citations; what I want is a decision, not a survey. Where the cases pull in different directions, say so plainly rather than smoothing it over.

My verdicts:

1. A woman lives in a town where one man owns the mill that employs everyone, the only grocery, and the only bus line. He has never refused her anything and interferes in nothing; he is fond of her. She knows that if she campaigned against him, or moved in with her boyfriend, or asked for a raise he disapproved of, he could ruin her without breaking any law. She asks for nothing he would disapprove of. She is unfree, and badly so.

2. A climber joins a club whose safety officer has the right to veto any route she plans. She joined for that reason, would be alarmed if the veto were abolished, and has never had a route vetoed. She is not unfree.

3. A man arranges an automatic transfer that moves most of his pay into an account he can only reach at a branch in another city, to stop himself gambling. He is not unfree; nobody is doing anything to him.

4. In a village, women do not speak at public meetings. There is no rule, no punishment, no enforcer; a woman who spoke would meet silence and, afterwards, a slight cooling in everyone's manner toward her. A woman there has absorbed this so thoroughly that she does not experience the silence as a constraint and says she has no wish to speak. I want to say she is unfree, but I cannot name whose will she is under, and the moment I try to name a dominator the verdict slips away.

5. A prisoner serving life has a comfortable cell, more say over his daily schedule than I have over mine, and no wish to escape; the warden decides everything else and could lawfully change all of it tomorrow. He is unfree.

6. A driver obeys traffic laws she voted for and thinks just, and would keep obeying them if they were repealed. She is not unfree.

7. A hiker is trapped in a canyon by a rockfall. Nobody is involved. She is not free to leave, but I don't think this is the same thing as being unfree at all — I suspect the word is doing two jobs here.

8. A man was raised in a small community that deliberately shaped his desires from infancy. He wants nothing but to serve its leader, would refuse an offer of escape, and suffers no coercion as an adult. He is unfree, deeply so.

Cases 2 and 8 look alike — an endorsed constraint — and I give them opposite verdicts; that is the pair I most want explained. Cases 1 and 4 also pull against each other: in one I find unfreedom with no interference, in the other I hesitate because I cannot locate a will.

The principle I keep reaching for, and suspect is too crude, is this: a person is unfree to the extent that what she can do is settled by a will that is not hers, whether or not that will is exercised, and whether or not she welcomes it. It gets 1, 5, and 8 and seems to wreck 2, 3, and 6. If it can be repaired rather than replaced, tell me the smallest repair and what it costs me.

What I want from you: the principle or principles, stated so that each clause is tied to the case that forces it; your verdicts on at least three cases I have not listed — a feed that quietly shapes what a user is offered, though no one designed it to constrain her; a son who decides when his agreeable mother eats, whom she sees, and what she watches, with her consent and his genuine care; and a woman priced out of her own city by thousands of strangers each lawfully bidding for housing; then an honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether the best outcome is one principle or a principled distinction between two kinds of unfreedom — and if the latter, what the two are and why one word covers both.
