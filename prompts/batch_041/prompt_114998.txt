I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of two decades, which is long enough to have acquired a reflex: when an analogy starts carrying an argument's weight, ask for the correspondence table before conceding anything.

The analogy I want audited is the one that gets produced whenever the countermajoritarian objection comes up — the objection that entrenchment and judicial review let a dead majority, or a panel of unelected judges, tie the hands of living ones. The standard reply is that this is just the Ulysses problem: the polity binds itself in advance, the constitution is the rope, the Sirens are the passions of the moment (panic, faction, the temptations of the emergency), and judicial review is the crew that has been ordered to ignore the prisoner's pleading. On this reading entrenchment is not a limitation on self-government but a particularly clear instance of it, and the countermajoritarian objection is a confusion about who is doing what to whom.

The specific occasion: I proposed, half-seriously, a two-stage amendment rule — anything passed during a session of unusually high mobilization would have to be re-enacted by a freshly elected legislature before taking effect. The reply was that this is Ulysses with a longer rope, and therefore unobjectionable.

I want the audit, not another round of assertion. Give me the mapping explicitly — what plays Ulysses, the mast, the ropes, the Sirens, the crew, and the calm morning after — and then where it breaks. I am particularly interested in four joints:

1. Ulysses is one agent with a later self that can ratify or repudiate the binding; a polity is not, and there is no moment at which the bound party wakes up and is glad the rope held. Is the missing ratification moment a detail or the whole thing?
2. The crew is a third party ordered to disobey the protagonist, and its loyalty is the mechanism. In the political case, who is the crew? If the crew turns out to be one of the factions — that is, if the ones holding the rope have their own views about the Sirens' song — is that still the same story?
3. Ulysses binds himself against a known, temporary, and subsequently regretted state; entrenchment binds against contingencies nobody foresaw. How much of the analogy's force depends on knowing in advance what you will later wish you had not done?
4. The binding is temporary: it lapses once the ship is past the rocks, and the same crew that held the rope unties it at the far end. If a determined majority can always amend, is the constitution a rope or a speed bump — and does that make the analogy self-undermining, in that a rope that can be slipped does not bind and one that cannot leaves the polity unfree?

Then tell me which of these disanalogies actually bear on the conclusion the reply is drawing — that entrenchment is an instance of self-government rather than a limit on it — and which are merely colorful. Say what the analogy quietly imports that the target lacks (a unified popular will? a single, time-inconsistent preference ordering? the identity of binder and bound?), and whether the conclusion survives once those are on the table. And say what the two-stage rule I floated would look like if we designed it by taking the disanalogies seriously rather than by waving the analogy at it. If there is a better analogy for the same job, give me that instead.
