I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy, working at the seam between pragmatics and normative ethics, and next month I am presenting a paper at a one-day workshop that my department is co-hosting with the linguistics institute. The paper defends a claim I have not seen put quite this way, and I want to be ready for the discussion rather than blindsided by it. The claim is this: the familiar contrast between Dutch directness and Japanese indirectness is best described neither as a difference in how much the two speech communities value honesty nor as a difference in how much they value politeness, but as a difference in where each community places what I call the interpretive burden — who is expected to do the work of recovering the speaker's meaning and to absorb the social risk of an utterance — and, connectedly, in who is taken to have standing to demand an accurate belief from the speaker. On this account a white lie is wrong not in the first instance because it misrepresents, but because it presupposes that the hearer lacks standing to be told the truth; and because standing is fixed by a community's conventions, the very same sentence can count as a white lie in one setting and as an ordinary courtesy, or an ordinary statement, in another. I expect this to rub several people the wrong way, and I would like a prep sheet for the question period.

The audience is small and I know roughly who will be there. There is a senior pragmaticist who will insist that everything I am calling a cultural difference falls out of universal conversational maxims plus local calibration, and who will want to know what my notion of an interpretive burden adds that a Gricean account does not already supply. There is a linguistic anthropologist who did years of fieldwork and is a thoroughgoing relativist; she will resist any move that sounds like grading one community's speech norms against another's, and she will be suspicious of my moral vocabulary. There is a Kantian who holds that lying is wrong without exception and will go straight for the white-lie argument, contending that my account quietly licenses deception whenever the local conventions are permissive. There is a virtue ethicist who will reframe everything in terms of honesty and trust and ask whether a standing-based account can even explain why the betrayal of trust feels like the core of the wrong. And there are two graduate students who will ask the blunt methodological question: how would anyone measure an interpretive burden, and is the whole proposal unfalsifiable? I want the objections I will actually get from these particular people, in their own idiom, not a generic list of possible criticisms.

Please give me the following. First, a short section titled "The claim in one paragraph" (roughly 100 words) stating my thesis in the cleanest form I can actually defend. Second, a section titled "Objections, in the order they will come," containing six to eight objections, each headed by a one-line version phrased the way the relevant audience member would phrase it and labelled with who it comes from, followed by a reply I could deliver in about two minutes and a candid sentence on where that reply is weakest. Third, a section titled "Concessions to make up front," listing what I should give away before anyone has to pry it out of me. Fourth, a section titled "The question I am hoping nobody asks," giving the question itself, a note on why I dread it, and my least-bad answer. Keep the whole thing tight enough to use at the podium; no bibliography or citations are needed.
