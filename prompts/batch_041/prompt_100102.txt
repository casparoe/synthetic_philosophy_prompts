I am trying to formulate a principle for when learning something is good for the learner. My verdicts: a free, reliable test that tells you which of two job offers will make you happier, before you choose, is good; the same test arriving after you have signed a contract is bad. A free test for a treatable disease is good; for an untreatable disease that will only cause dread, bad. A free test that tells you the exact date of your death, which you cannot change, is bad for most people but good for someone who prefers knowing the truth. A free test that tells you the outcome of a coin flip after you have bet is bad if you lost and good if you won, even though it cannot change your wealth. A free test that tells you what you will choose tomorrow in a decision you have not yet made is bad, because it undermines your sense of agency. A free test guaranteed irrelevant to any decision and without emotional effect is neutral. I suspect the principle is: information is good iff it is received before the relevant decision and can be used to improve the outcome, or iff the learner has a stable preference for knowing the truth. But that seems to get the death-date and coin-flip cases wrong, and leaves the after-the-fact cases mysterious. Is there a principled account that captures all of these, or which verdicts must I abandon? What does it say about a free test that tells you whether you would be better off not knowing the result of the test? Please state your confidence in the account you propose and identify the point you are least sure of.
