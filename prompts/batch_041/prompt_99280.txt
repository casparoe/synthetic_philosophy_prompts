I'm putting together an episode for a small student-run podcast — philosophy of death, no budget, mostly undergrads listening — and my guest next month is a philosopher who's been defending a reading of Zhuangzi that I half-agree with and half-suspect is a magic trick. Her line is that the famous scenes aren't arguments. The one where his wife dies and Hui Shi comes to console him and finds him squatting by the door banging on a tub and singing; the one where he uses a skull as a pillow and then asks it how it ended up like that; the friends who sing over a corpse and scandalise Confucius's disciples; the deathbed scene where he tells his disciples not to bother with an elaborate funeral because the sky and earth will serve as his coffin and the whole of things as his grave-goods. On her view we've all been taught to read these as a doctrine — death is one more transformation, like the turn of the seasons, so grief is a category mistake — and that gets the direction of explanation backwards. Grief is a practice with a script, and what Zhuangzi does is run a different practice in the same slot: the drumming does the work a lament would have done. The philosophy is in the substitution, not in a premise. She also thinks the seasonal-transformation line is a red herring that only ever gets trotted out for the deaths we're already comfortable with, and that it does no work at all for a child's death or a stupid, violent one — which is where I think the whole thing has to earn its keep.

So write me the questions I should put to her. I want at least one that pins down what 'counter-ritual' is supposed to mean when the audience is the other mourners rather than the dead — is the performance addressed to them, and if so what is it meant to do to them; at least one on the passage in the same chapter as the butterfly dream where lifespans get scrambled (the long-lived Peng Zu dying young, the infant who died outliving everyone) and on the 'how do I know' argument built on the girl taken from the border country to a king's palace who wept at first and later was glad — does that argument belong to the counter-ritual reading, or is it a rival, more argument-shaped strategy the text can't seem to do without; and at least one that makes her actually spend the seasonal analogy on a death that doesn't complete a cycle. And one question she can't escape by saying 'that's the therapeutic point' — if grief is just a script, why does the text work so hard to talk the reader out of it? A counter-ritual doesn't need a treatise.

Give it back in four labelled sections, in this order: 'The view, plainly' — two questions that force her to state the counter-ritual reading without leaning on the word 'therapy', each with the follow-up I'd ask if she just repeats herself (about 150 words); 'Where it has to earn its keep' — three or four questions on the hard cases above, follow-ups included (about 350 words); 'The self-undermining worry' — two questions about why a mere substitution needs so much argumentative scaffolding (about 150 words); and 'The one I'll close on' — a single question, no follow-up, the one she'd have to answer without falling back on her favourite move. Keep the questions in her vocabulary rather than mine — she says 'script' and 'slot', so use those — and dont pad them with setup.
