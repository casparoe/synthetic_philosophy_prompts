ok context first: I'm a 4th-year in HPS, and I've somehow been drafted as the neutral note-taker for a workshop where two people in my area have been arguing past each other about incommensurability for what feels like two years now. What I need is the joint statement they'd both actually sign — and I keep drafting things that quietly favour one of them, which is why I'm handing it to you. Please dont give me a literature review or a reading list, I know the literature. I want the document.

The two positions, in a form each of them would accept as their own:

The Deep camp: a term's meaning is fixed by its whole inferential role, so when a theory is replaced wholesale the old terms don't co-refer with the new ones in any translation-invariant way. There's no theory-neutral observation language and no neutral meta-language either — any attempt to state the comparison already imports one side's categories. Rational theory choice across a genuine revolution is a reconstruction we perform after the fact, not something the participants could have carried out.

The Localist camp: meaning variance is real but local and piecemeal. There's always a shared core — pointing, measuring, intervening, shared practical problems — and that's enough for a rational comparison. The meta-language isnt theory-laden all the way down. Calling something incommensurable turns a difference of degree into a difference of kind.

The thing they keep not agreeing about, and what I want the document to prise apart, is a three-way distinction we hammered out in the reading group: (A) reference drift, where the term keeps tracking the same stuff but the theory assigns it different constitutive properties; (B) taxonomic re-carving, where the extension itself changes and old predicates have no clean image in the new scheme; (C) criteria drift, where what counts as a good explanation, a decisive test, or a relevant anomaly changes. Deep says A, B and C come as a bundle in a real revolution and you can't get C without the others. Localist says C happens on its own all the time and A and B are always bridgeable by what he calls a bridge lexicon — a shared operational gloss that fixes enough co-reference for the two sides to argue with each other.

There's also a sub-fight about comparison versus measurement. Localist's line is that you don't need a common unit to compare two things rationally, you need a shared practical problem and shared success conditions. Deep's reply is that shared success conditions are exactly what a revolution changes. I think that one's the real crux and I want it named as such.

And there's a toy case we invented for the workshop that I want the document to lean on, because both of them can argue about it without either claiming the history is on their side: two traditions study the same class of systems. The first models them with a single global temperature field and predicts instrument readings. The second models them with a set of local kinetic variables and says the global temperature is a coarse-grained fiction with no referent. When the second tradition wins — does the first tradition's successful prediction count as evidence for the second theory, or does it get re-described as a claim about the coarse-grained average and thereby stop being the same claim? And does the first tradition's term pick out anything the second tradition's term also picks out?

Deliverable, and I'm being fussy about the shape of it:

1. A numbered section of claims both sides can sign. Non-vacuous ones — about what incommensurability would have to amount to, about the toy case, and about what could in principle settle the crux. No weasel wording that just restates the disjunction.
2. A disagreements table. For each crux: a neutral formulation of the question; then Deep's position in wording Deep would put his name to, and Localist's in wording he'd put his name to (don't let either column smuggle in the other side's vocabulary); then the case or argument both agree bears on it; then each side's prediction, recorded in advance.
3. A short section flagging which parts of this dispute are merely verbal and what would make them substantive.
4. Each side's defeaters — what each would count as evidence or argument against its own view — and, honestly, whether there's anything both agree would actually move the debate.

If a crux can't be given a shared test, say so rather than faking one.
