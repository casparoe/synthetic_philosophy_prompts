Write a short poem about Thomson's lamp for a graduate reading group in metaphysics. Set the puzzle up inside the poem: a lamp is switched on and off at the one-minute mark, then at half a minute before the end, then a quarter, and so on, so that infinitely many flips fall inside the minute. Let the form carry the mathematics — open with an eight-line stanza and halve the line count in each stanza that follows (eight, four, two, one), so that the poem narrows on the final instant the way the intervals do — and let the single closing line refuse to say whether the lamp ends on or off. Make that refusal the right kind: not "we cannot work it out", but "the instructions do not settle it", since the same infinite sequence of flips is equally compatible with a lamp that ends on, one that ends off, and one wired so that by the end it is no longer a lamp at all. Along the way the poem should let the reader feel the wrong demand — that it must be one or the other — and then the correction. A philosopher who knows the puzzle should find the ending exact; someone who does not should still find it a poem. Give it a title.
