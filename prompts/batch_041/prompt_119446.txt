For a piece I'm writing on literary translation, I've been sitting in on a competition in which translators were asked to render the same short Renaissance French poem into English, each entry obeying a different formal constraint — one keeps the rhyme scheme, one the syllable count, one is a lipogram, and so on. The exercise is in the spirit of Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot, which collects dozens of renderings of a single poem by Clément Marot. Twice in one afternoon I heard the Italian saying traduttore, traditore invoked to do opposing work. A translator used it to argue that any English version that preserves the rhyme must be falsifying the sense; a critic used it to argue that the literal, unrhymed versions are the real betrayals, since they jettison the very thing the form was doing.

I left unable to say what the saying actually claims, and I suspect both of my sources would have floundered if pressed. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims it might be making — what exactly is betrayed, by whom, to whom, and by what standard — ordered from the trivial to the radical? For each reading, I'd like to know whether it is a platitude, a substantive thesis worth defending, or plainly false; which readings would actually license each of the two uses I've described; and which readings sit in tension with one another. I'm also curious whether the saying can be restated so that it doesn't indict itself: its punch depends on a rhyme that the English "translator, traitor" loses, so if the charge holds against all translation, it holds against the English form of the charge as well. Please give me a numbered menu rather than a single verdict, with the case for and against each entry. I don't need citations or a reading list — I'm after the arguments, not the literature.
