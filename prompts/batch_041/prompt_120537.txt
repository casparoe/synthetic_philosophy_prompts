What I want is a real two-way reading: what the play dramatises that the theory gets right, where it complicates the theory, and where the reading stops working and you have to say so.

Some things I'm curious about. First, the play hands the usurer the argument from nature. Shylock defends interest with the Jacob and Laban story — the ewes and rams breeding, the profit being his by right — and when Antonio asks whether his gold and silver are ewes and rams, Shylock answers that he makes them breed as fast. That's a direct shot at the sterility thesis: if generation is the most natural thing there is, and money can be made to generate, then either usury is natural or nature isn't the standard. Does the play know its handing the anti-Aristotelian premise to the villain, and does it ever answer it? I suspect it doesn't, but tell me if I'm wrong.

Second, the bond. The pound of flesh is a contract that specifies exact equivalence, and the trial is about whether exact equivalence can be honoured at all — flesh but not blood, the letter of the bond and nothing beyond it. That looks like a dramatisation of the incommensurability problem: the contract tries to make the two sides fully commensurable and what it produces is an impossibility, so equity has to come from outside the bond, from Portia's interpretation. Is that reading too neat? Aristotle's own answer is that need is the measure, but Venice is a place where need is manufactured — Bassanio needs three thousand ducats, Antonio needs to help his friend, Shylock needs his bond — so I don't think the Aristotelian measure survives contact with the play, and I'd like you to test that.

Third, and this is the bit I actually care about, the caskets. Three suitors choose between gold, silver and lead; the one who takes what he desires and the one who takes what he thinks he deserves both lose, and the one who hazards everything wins. On a straight Aristotelian reading the gold-chooser is the chrematist and the lead-chooser is the household manager. But Bassanio is a spendthrift who borrowed the money to come and try, and he wins; and Portia's own fortune is what makes the whole Belmont world possible. So the play seems to want to condemn money-getting while its happy ending runs on it. Does it? Or is there a way of reading Belmont as genuinely outside the Venetian economy rather than just its funded wing?
