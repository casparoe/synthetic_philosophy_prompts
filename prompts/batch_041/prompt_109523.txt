Consider two inquirers, A and B, stipulated to be identical in every respect that bears on action or practical success. Each holds the same true belief about a matter with no practical upshot whatsoever: the exact number of grains of sand in a particular distant desert that neither will ever visit, be affected by, or have any reason to care about. A arrived at her belief by a method she can articulate, test, and defend, and that method would deliver the same verdict on any relevantly similar occasion; B arrived at the same belief by a guess that happened to land on the truth, and can say nothing in its defence beyond a shrug. Neither will ever act on the belief, be asked to justify it, communicate it to anyone who cares, or learn anything that depends on it.

Your task is to give a credence, and to show your work. The proposition is this: A's state is epistemically better than B's — better from the standpoint of inquiry itself, and not merely a reflection of the practical advantages that articulable, reliable belief usually confers. (This is the Meno problem sharpened by removing the practical payoff that normally does the explanatory work.)

Decompose the credence. Assign a rough probability to each of the following, with one line of justification each:

1. that 'epistemically better' names a genuine, non-deflationary relation here, rather than a projection of practical or social concerns onto a case from which those concerns have been stripped;
2. that the value of knowledge over true belief is purely instrumental — reliability, future usefulness, communicability — in which case the stipulations should force the answer toward no difference;
3. that reliability, or the capacity to give and defend one's grounds, is intrinsically valuable, so that the difference survives the removal of practical payoff;
4. that knowledge carries a distinct achievement or credit value — value in virtue of the believer's own contribution to getting it right — which B's lucky guess lacks;
5. that the ordinary concept of knowledge is unified enough for a single answer to be correct, rather than a bundle of overlapping concerns with no fact of the matter;
6. any further term you think belongs in the decomposition.

Then state how the terms combine into a single number, name the one sub-question your number is most sensitive to, and say what argument or piece of reasoning would move it most. Present the decomposition as a table. For reference, my own credence is about 0.4; if yours differs, say which term accounts for most of the gap.
