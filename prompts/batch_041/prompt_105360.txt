I teach philosophy part-time in the BA program at a medium-security prison — one intro ethics section a term — and every spring the program director ropes a faculty member into giving the commencement speech at the graduation. It's a small affair: visiting room, folding chairs, maybe forty graduates in caps and gowns, families on one side, a couple of unit staff and the associate warden up front, the university dean, and the donors who actually pay for the books. I got tapped this year. Normally this is easy — you tell people they earned it, they deserved it, the future is open — but I've been sitting in a reading group on the Caruso/Dennett fight over free will and just deserts, and it's kind of wrecked my ability to give that speech with a straight face. Half the room is doing serious time, and two or three of my students are up for parole within the year, so "you deserve to be where you are" is not a sentence I'm willing to say, and neither is the professor-ish version where I imply that what they did wasn't really up to them — that's both false to the way they talk about their own lives and pretty insulting coming from someone with a car and a parking spot. But Caruso's alternative, where we lock people up the way we quarantine the sick, because of the risk and not because they deserve it, sounds gentler until you notice it hands everything to the actuarial risk score, and if you're in that room and your score is bad then "we're not punishing you, we're just managing your risk" can mean you never come home. Dennett's move, as I understand it, is to keep desert but deflate it — answerable to the community rather than to some cosmic ledger — and he says something like stripping desert out of praise is what makes praise condescending. That one lands hard for me, because this speech is pure praise by definition. It's a commencement address.

So what I want from you is the speech: five to eight minutes, in my voice, to that room. Make the disagreement do real structural work — I don't want the Caruso/Dennett material as a quoted aside or a "some philosophers say" hedge; I want it shaping what the speech can and can't say, so that if you cut the philosophy the speech falls apart. Don't name either of them in the speech itself, partly because the warden is sitting right there and partly because I want the ideas absorbed rather than cited, and keep the speech itself jargon-free — if you use a word like desert, it has to be audible to somebody who has never read philosophy. There's one thing I specifically want you to face instead of dodging: the asymmetry. If nobody deserves blame in the basic sense, then "you deserved this diploma" is in the same boat, and I want the speech to say what's actually left of praise at that point rather than doing a fake pivot where everybody claps anyway. I don't care which way you land — if the skeptic has the better case for this particular room, land there, just don't cheat to make it comfortable. I'm closer to Dennett than to Caruso personally, but I'd rather have the honest version than the flattering one.

Then give me a short seperate note afterward, a couple of paragraphs and clearly not part of the speech, telling me where the speech is taking a contested position and being explicit about which parts of this debate are actually settled among people who work on it and which are genuinely up for grabs — don't let one side of a live fight get presented as the consensus, and flag any place the speech leans on a reading of either guy that a specialist would push back on, or on an empirical claim about incarceration or risk prediction that I'd be embarrassed to have asserted from a podium. American English throughout.
