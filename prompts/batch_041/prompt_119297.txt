I'm a fourth-year history PhD student working on early modern state formation, and next month I have to present a chapter to our department's history seminar. The chapter argues that the whole debate about presentism is framed backwards. I don't think historians can escape the standards of their own time, and I don't think they should try; the interesting question is which present-day commitments are doing the work and whether we're honest about them. I break presentism into three kinds: selection-presentism (which past questions we bother to ask — e.g., I care about tax farming rather than peasant mentalities), evaluation-presentism (which past conduct we praise or blame — e.g., whether we call a massacre a crime against humanity), and explanation-presentism (which causal categories we use to make the past intelligible — e.g., saying the early modern state 'failed' to become modern). My claim is that selection-presentism is unavoidable and harmless, evaluation-presentism is sometimes unavoidable but has to be owned rather than hidden, and explanation-presentism is the one that really distorts, because it turns the past into a failed version of us. The ideal of escaping presentism entirely is itself a product of our own methodological moment, so the discipline's self-image is incoherent. I'm not saying anything goes — I'm saying the usual way of policing presentism is confused.

The audience is mostly early modernists and a few modernists, plus two philosophers of history who come to these things. They're not hostile to theory, but they're allergic to anything that sounds like relativism, and several of them have been accused of presentism in their own work and are still sore about it. They care a lot about archival rigor, about not writing history as a story that culminates in the present, and about the difference between anachronism and presentism. One of the philosophers will probably ask whether my three-way distinction is stable or whether it just redescribes the hermeneutic circle. The historians will worry that I'm giving people permission to read the present into the past, and someone will definately bring up slavery or the Holocaust and ask whether my view means we can't condemn it. A couple of them think the real sin is anachronism, not presentism, and that I'm conflating the two. Another will say that if presentism is unavoidable, that's exactly why we need stronger methodological guardrails, not a relaxed attitude. I also expect someone to ask whether my view is just the old line that all history is contemporary history, and I never know what to say to that.
