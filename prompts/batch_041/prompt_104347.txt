I teach a seminar on probability and induction, and I keep getting tangled up in my own gut reactions to indifference arguments, so I want to lay out my verdicts and see whether there's a principle behind them or whether I'm just inconsistent. Here they are. (1) A friend hands me a coin from a novelty shop, I have no idea whether it's fair, I flip it once and it comes up heads; I'm perfectly happy to say the next flip is 1/2. (2) Same story with a six-sided die from the same shop; before rolling I'd say each face is 1/6. (3) A shuffled deck: fine, I treat all orderings as equally likely, because I know the shuffle is what makes it so. (4) But a factory makes cubes and all I know is that the side length is somewhere between 1 and 2 cm; I refuse to say the side length is equally likely to fall in any equal-width subinterval, and I equally refuse to say the volume is uniform. I just don't think there's a fact of the matter there. (5) A chord is drawn "at random" in a circle and I'm asked for the probability it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Depending on whether the chord is picked by its midpoint, by its endpoints, or by its distance from the centre, I get 1/4, 1/3, 1/2. I say the question is broken, not that one of those is the right answer. (6) Yet when I've got an urn of unknown composition and I draw balls with replacement, I'm perfectly happy with the Laplace answer, (n+1)/(n+2), after n successes — the "unknown composition" doesn't bother me there the way the cube factory does. (7) A friend rolls a die behind a screen and tells me the result is odd; I say P(it's a 3) = 1/3 and feel fine about it. (8) I'm told a number was selected "at random" from the positive integers with no other information, and I say there's no such distribution and no probability that it's even.

Here's my problem. (1), (2), and (6) all look like pure indifference from ignorance, which is exactly what I'm rejecting in (4) and (5). The difference I want to point to is something like: in (1), (2), (6) there's a physical set-up that makes the outcomes exchangeable — a coin is a coin, an urn is an urn — whereas a cube isn't symmetric under reparametrizing side length as volume, and the circle has no privileged random-chord mechanism. But that feels like I'm smuggling in knowledge of the mechanism, and if I'm allowed to know coins are roughly fair, why am I not allowed to know that a factory makes cube side lengths roughly uniform? Or maybe the real distinction is whether the symmetry lives in the physical process versus in my description of it, in which case (1) isn't as innocent as I think it is. Can you tell me what principle, or small set of principles, actually captures my verdicts? State it precisely, tell me which clause each of my verdicts is pulling on, what it predicts for a couple of cases I haven't listed — say, an unknown-bias coin I've already flipped 100 times and gotten 60 heads, is the next flip still 1/2? and a uniformly random point on a sphere, is it fine to say its latitude is uniform? — and be honest about which of my verdicts the principle forces me to give up. I suspect (1) and (6) can't both survive, but I'd rather be told than keep guessing.
