Since retiring from the laboratory I have been trying to teach myself logic and the philosophy of mathematics, and I have run into a puzzle that a younger colleague calls a cousin of the MU-puzzle. The system is this: the alphabet is {A, B, C}; the sole axiom is AB; and the rules are (i) if a string ends in B you may append C, (ii) if a string begins with A you may double the substring after the A, (iii) you may replace any occurrence of BBB with C, and (iv) you may delete any occurrence of CC. The question is whether AC is derivable. I can see that it is not, by tracking the number of B's modulo 3: the axiom has one B, rule (i) leaves the count alone, rule (ii) doubles it, rule (iii) subtracts three, rule (iv) leaves it alone, and AC has zero B's, which is a multiple of three, while no sequence of doublings and subtractions of three starting from one ever reaches a multiple of three. But the proof feels like a step outside the system—a younger colleague calls it jumping out of the system—because I am using arithmetic to say something the system cannot say about itself.

A friend in our reading group then offered the following argument, which I cannot refute but which seems to prove far too much. (1) The only way to establish that AC is underivable in the system is to step outside it and reason in a metatheory, here arithmetic. (2) Any metatheory is itself a formal system, or can be formalized as one. (3) To establish the limits of that metatheory, one must step outside it in turn. (4) This yields an infinite regress: no formal system can establish its own limits. (5) Therefore, the claim that AC is underivable is not established by any formal system; it is known only by an informal jump. (6) If a mathematical claim can be known only by an informal jump, then mathematical knowledge is not formalizable. (7) The underivability of AC is a mathematical claim. (8) Therefore, mathematical knowledge is not formalizable. (9) But if mathematical knowledge is not formalizable, then the metatheory used in (1)-(5) cannot be trusted to establish the limits of the object system either. (10) So the argument undermines itself. I would rather keep (1) and (6) than (8) and (10), but I want to know what I am paying. Which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am particularly interested in whether the regress in (3)-(4) is vicious, and whether there is a principled difference between stepping out of a system and merely using a stronger system.
