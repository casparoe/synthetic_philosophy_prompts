I want to know how exposed a view I hold really is. It concerns the gradual-replacement thought experiment: replace a person's neurons one at a time with silicon units that reproduce their input-output behaviour, and ask whether the person survives. My view is that what carries the person across is neither the material nor the pattern taken as an abstract object, but the fact that the substitution proceeds as a single continuous causal process — one system becoming another by degrees — so that building a duplicate and switching it on preserves the pattern but not the process, and therefore not the person.

Map what would count against this rather than surveying the literature. Is there a way to specify the relevant causal process that does not already presuppose the personal identity it is meant to explain? If the criterion can only be stated by pointing to an unbroken history, does it collapse into the bare claim that identity is just the spatiotemporal continuity of a living body? What observation or argument, if any, should move me — could the answer turn on something in principle testable, such as whether a partly replaced subject's reports of its own past become systematically confabulated, or whether two half-replaced systems can be shown to diverge in what they can access? And which of the standing objections — fission, the arbitrariness of counting one process when the replacement runs in overlapping waves, the possibility that biological and silicon units exchange signals only with a delay — would a defender of the view concede as counting against it, as opposed to absorbing by qualification? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say plainly whether the view risks anything at all.
