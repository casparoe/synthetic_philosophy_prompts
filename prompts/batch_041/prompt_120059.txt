I am working on an essay about Rousseau's Letter to M. d'Alembert on Spectacles, the 1758 response to d'Alembert's article on Geneva in the Encyclopédie, in which d'Alembert had suggested that Geneva might benefit from a theater. I have a draft of the central argument below, in compressed form, and I would like detailed comments on it. I am not looking for copy-editing or for a summary of the Letter; I want to know where the argument is wrong, where it is unoriginal, and where it is too quick.

My thesis is that Rousseau's objection to the theater is not primarily an objection to the moral content of plays, and not primarily an objection to the moral character of actors, but an objection to a structural feature of theatrical representation. The theater places an audience in a passive relation to a depicted action that is sealed off from the audience's own life, and it converts moral responsiveness into a feeling that is discharged in the moment rather than carried into practice. The content-based arguments — that Molière's Misanthrope makes the honest Alceste ridiculous while the manipulative Philinte is presented as superior, that tragedy excites passions it does not teach us to govern — are illustrations of this deeper point rather than the point itself. On this reading, the problem with the theater is not that it shows us the wrong things but that it shows us anything at all in the mode of representation: it makes virtue an object of admiration rather than a practice, and it makes vice an object of amusement rather than a thing to be avoided.

I take three features of the Letter as evidence for this reading. First, Rousseau wrote plays and an opera himself; if the objection were to the moral content of drama, his own practice would be harder to explain. Second, he confines his conclusion to Geneva, a small republic, and does not call for the abolition of theaters in Paris; if the objection were to theater as such, this restriction would be arbitrary. Third, the alternative he proposes is itself a kind of spectacle: public festivals and balls in which the citizens are themselves the spectacle, and in which there is no separate class of performers and no separate audience. If spectacle as such were the problem, this alternative would be incoherent. So the problem must be with a particular structure: representation at a distance, professionalized, confined to a dedicated place and time, with a passive audience.

The critical question, and the part of the argument I am least sure about, is whether this distinction can be sustained. The festival is supposed to differ from the theater because the citizens are both actors and spectators, so that the image they see is their own life rather than an alien fiction. But this seems to presuppose that the community's self-image is not already mediated by the opinion of others in a corrupting way. Rousseau's own account of amour-propre — the form of self-regard that lives on the esteem of others — suggests that any spectacle of oneself before others is precisely the occasion for amour-propre. If that is right, the festival either presupposes the absence of the very thing it is supposed to cure, or it is just as dangerous as the theater, only with a different cast. The distinction would then be not between two structures of representation but between two audiences, one healthy and one corrupt, which is not a principled distinction at all.

One way to save the distinction would be to say that in the festival the representation is not of fictional persons but of the citizens themselves, so there is no gap between appearance and reality for amour-propre to exploit. But even then the citizens see themselves as others see them, which is the structure of amour-propre. Another way would be to say that the festival does not simulate feeling but expresses it, so that the actor's paradox — that the actor's art is to appear to feel what he does not feel — does not arise. But if everyone is now an actor, the paradox seems to be universalized rather than dissolved.

I am inclined to conclude that Rousseau's position is better read as a diagnosis of the social conditions under which theater is corrupting than as a coherent alternative to theater. On that reading, the festival is not a real institutional proposal but a way of specifying what the theater would have to be in order not to be corrupting — namely, a community transparent to itself. And the interest of the Letter is that Rousseau cannot quite bring himself to say whether such a community is possible or whether it is a memory of something that never existed.

What I would like from you:

1. Is the central interpretive claim — that the content arguments are subordinate to a structural argument about representation and the discharge of moral feeling — accurate to the Letter, or am I overreading? Where does the text push back?

2. Is the claim about amour-propre a fair extension of Rousseau's view, or am I importing a concept from elsewhere in his work in a way he would not accept here?

3. Is the festival/theater distinction as unstable as I claim, or is there a reading on which it holds? If it holds, what does that reading have to say about the actor's paradox?

4. Is the thesis interesting, or is it a standard reading of the Letter in different vocabulary? If it is standard, what would be a more surprising claim in the same vicinity?

5. Where is the argument too compressed, or where does it lean on an assumption I have not defended?
