Next Thursday I have to give the opening remarks at a two-day workshop where my agency is supposed to adopt its first-ever gift policy, and I need help writing them. The room is split down the middle and both halves will be taking notes. Half are donor people, consultants, and a couple of journalists in the back, and they talk the way you'd expect — zero tolerance, declarations, the usual rankings, corruption is corruption wherever you are. The other half are district officers and two traditional leaders I've known for years, and for them a basket of yams or quietly covering a visiting inspector's fuel is not a bribe, it's what a decent person does, and they'd tell you the real rot is the minister's nephew getting the road contract while a village gets nothing.

I dont want a speech that splits the difference and bores everyone. I want to actually argue something: that whether a transfer counts as corruption isn't settled by its size or by whether cash changed hands, and that two tests tend to get run together. One is whether the person on the receiving end could have refused without losing face, their post, or their standing in the community. The other is whether the transfer actually changed the decision, or only sped up a decision that was going to go the same way anyway. Those two tests come apart, and I want to work through what follows from that for a rule we could actually enforce.

The hard part: whatever I say has to make the donor half feel their concern is real and not just a colonial hangover, and it can't let the local half hear 'anything goes.' And I have to end with something concrete to adopt, knowing full well that the draft in front of us (declare anything over a token value, hand it over to the state) would in practice only ever be used against the government's enemies. Please write the speech. Ten, twelve minutes max.
