I retired from geology a couple of years ago — mineralogy and a lot of time at the microscope, so I'm used to asking what a material actually is down at the grain and molecule level — and I've been teaching myself philosophy since then with more enthusiasm than skill. I've bogged down in the old statue-and-the-clay problem (material constitution, I think it's called) and I want you to write me an essay on it that takes a real position and defends it. No survey of who-said-what, no reading list — I don't have reliable internet and I'm not going to chase down citations anyway — just the argument, and it should end somewhere rather than telling me all sides have merit.

What bugs me about the standard setup is that it quietly assumes the material is one fixed stuff that just hangs around while the shape changes. In my experience that's often not true. Fire a lump of clay and the clay minerals lose their water — you get metakaolin, and hotter still you get mullite and glass — so the little ceramic bird is arguably not made of the same material as the lump I started with, even though the only thing that left was water. Or the petrified logs I picked up as a kid: the wood is mostly gone, silica has come in and taken its place, sometimes right down to the cell walls, so what's in your hand has the shape and the grain of wood and practically none of the original stuff. Or a pseudomorph — a pyrite crystal that has slowly weathered into limonite but kept the cube. Nobody calls the limonite pyrite, yet the form survives the substance.

So here's what I want: an essay that answers whether a thing is identical to the material it's made of — give me a verdict and justify it, don't end on both-sides-ism. Then tell me whether the classic puzzle even survives contact with the chemistry, or whether the philosophers were solving a problem about an idealized stuff that doesn't occur. If your answer is that it depends on the case, fine, but then say precisely what it depends on. And commit on two things in particular: first, is the petrified log the same object as the tree that fell, and is that the same question as the statue one or a different one? Second, when my fired bird and the fired lump of material sit in one place, is there one object there or two, and what does your earlier answer force you to say about that? Please don't let the whole thing collapse into "it's just a matter of how we choose to talk" — if you go that way, earn it. Maybe 1200 to 1500 words.
