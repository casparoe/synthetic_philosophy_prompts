Last night a friend and I argued for two hours about A Beautiful Mind, and I want to know whether either of us came out of it with anything. His thesis is that the film is a feature-length argument for the argument from illusion, and therefore an embarrassment for disjunctivism. His case runs roughly like this: Nash's imaginary roommate Charles, Charles's niece Marcee, and the Defense Department agent Parcher are, for long stretches, rendered to us exactly as his real colleagues are — same light, same ambient sound, same ordinary back-and-forth — and Nash himself cannot tell them from the genuine article until he is cornered into trying. If a hallucinatory experience and a veridical one are indiscriminable in that way, he says, they must be experiences of the same fundamental kind, differing only in what causes them; and that is exactly the common-kind assumption that the disjunctivist denies, so the film is a counterexample with a runtime. I think this gets both the film and the philosophy wrong, but I could not say why at the time, and I would rather have the argument worked out than win it by volume.

Here is the reading I want, and I want it to be honest in both directions. Take the disjunctivist's side seriously — the claim that a perception and a hallucination can be subjectively alike yet belong to different kinds, so that the argument from illusion trades on a premise it has not earned — and then ask what the film actually commits itself to. My suspicion is that the indiscriminability the film relies on is retrospective and belongs to the audience rather than to Nash: the reveal retroactively reclassifies an episode, and what changes in Nash is not the character of the experience but his stance toward it — he learns to route around people who never age, to check the doorway, to notice that a little girl is always where the plot needs her. If that is right, the film is less a proof of the common-kind assumption than a study of what it costs to hold a belief against an experience that will not cooperate, and the disjunctivist might have more use for it than my friend does. So I want you to say which scenes carry the weight — the reveal and its immediate aftermath, the insulin treatments, the pen ceremony, whatever you judge central — whether the film's own narration presupposes that Nash's hallucinations and his perceptions are one kind of thing or two, and whether it dramatizes something the standard debate between the disjunctivist and the sense-datum theorist leaves out. If the film is incoherent on this point, or if the reading I am pushing distorts it, say so rather than rescue it. I have no interest in a bibliography or a list of further reading; the argument is the deliverable.

Finally, be candid about how confident you are in the reading you give, and single out the one claim in it you are least sure of — the place where a better-informed critic would most likely push back.
