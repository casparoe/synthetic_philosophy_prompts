I wrote this thing about karma and I want someone to actually pick at it instead of telling me its nice. It's notes-level, not a finished essay, so feel free to tell me the whole framing is wrong.

Here's the argumant. People defend karma by saying it makes the world just: if you suffer, it's because you did something, and if you do something, it'll come back to you. But I think that's a slide. Two claims get mashed together:

(A) Outcomes are causally dependent on prior actions — your situation is downstream of what you did.
(B) Outcomes are deserved — your situation is something you merit.

(A) doesn't give you (B). A hangover is causally dependent on last night; that doesn't make it a punishment. So the interesting question isn't "is karma true," it's "what would have to be added to (A) to get (B)."

I want to say desert needs two things, maybe three. First a ledger: some accounting where a deed is held and a later outcome is issued against it. Second an attribution: the outcome has to be issued to the same agent who did the deed, in a way that agent can own as theirs. Call the law-only version thin karma and the ledger-plus-attribution version thick karma. Thin karma is just a claim about character and causation — act this way long enough and you become the kind of person who... Thick karma is what people actually want when they say the world is just.

Third thing I keep tripping over: even with a ledger and an attribution you need the outcome to be the right kind of thing to answer the deed. A currency. If I steal your ox, is the karmic counterpart losing an ox, or being stolen from, or being unable to trust anyone again? Nothing in (A) tells you. I suspect the answer is always supplied by whoever's doing the accounting, which is awkward for anyone claiming karma is a law of nature rather than a local custom with cosmic pretensions.

Then the thought experiment I've been chewing on. Imagine a world where thick karma is literally true — the books are real, deeds are held, outcomes are issued — but the person who receives the outcome has no memory of the deed and no continuity of character with the doer. Call it the amnesiac ledger. My claim: even there, nothing is deserved. The outcome is still just an event that happened to someone; it's been routed through a bookkeeping system. It's like being billed for a debt that someone with your name ran up in a city you've never been to. The ledger makes the causation traceable, but traceability isn't ownership.

The obvious pushback: desert doesn't need memory, it needs identity — if it's the same person, memory is neither here nor there, and I'm smuggling in a psychological criterion of personal identity when the traditions are working with something more like a continuum. That's probably the strongest objection and I don't have a clean answer. Maybe the answer is that the continuum view has to say what makes the later stage the *same* agent for desert purposes, and every candidate (causal continuity, character continuity, a soul) either makes memory relevant again or makes desert collapse into "the universe has a causal structure," which nobody needed karma for.

What I actually think karma is doing, once you strip the justice talk, is a claim about moral causation being non-local and non-legible: what you do has moral effects you can't see, can't trace, and won't cash out on your own timescale, and the moral order is not readable from inside one life. That's a real and interesting claim. It's just not a claim about desert, and I think the desert framing is doing emotional work the causation can't cash. Maybe that's fine — maybe a theory of moral causation is more useful than a theory of desert, since desert tells you what people owe and causation tells you what your actions are already doing.

- Is the amnesiac ledger actually doing work, or is it the standard problem of evil in a costume? The usual objection to karma-as-justice is "bad things happen to good people"; mine is "even if they didn't, it wouldn't be justice." I want to know if that's a real distinction or a nicer-sounding version of the same thing.
- Does thin karma survive as anything distinct from "character is shaped by habit"? If not, say so — I'd rather cut the word karma entirely than keep a label with nothing under it.
- Where am I just asserting the conclusion?
