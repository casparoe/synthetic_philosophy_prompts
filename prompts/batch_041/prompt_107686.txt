I chair a small city historical commission—we review plaques, statues, museum labels, and the odd school curriculum supplement. I keep coming back to Nietzsche's three ways history can serve life—monumental, antiquarian, critical—and his warning that too much history can paralyze action, plus the need for some unhistorical and suprahistorical air and the question of how much plastic power the community actually has to grow out of its past. I want a decision procedure my board can actually run on a proposal, not a lecture. Treat each mode as both a resource and a failure mode: the procedure should make us name which mode is doing the work, then run the check for that mode's characteristic distortion. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because it's values all the way down. We have twenty minutes per item, no Nietzsche scholars on the board, and the mayor's office can overturn us. Then run it on two cases: a proposal to remove a statue of the city's founder because he was also a slaveholder, and a proposal to add a plaque about a forgotten labor strike that ended badly. Make one of those come out in a way I won't like, and tell me what the procedure can't do. Start with the one-sentence rule the procedure is trying to implement, then the steps. No preamble, no restating the question. Separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who know Nietzsche—don't present one reading as the consensus. American English.
