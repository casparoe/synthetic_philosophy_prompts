You are a philosopher of science with a strong grounding in the history of ideas and in the sociology of scientific knowledge. Please think carefully and at length before you answer, beginning with a short brainstorming pass over the space of possible questions before you commit to a ranking.

Three months ago I published what I took to be a novel theoretical result in my field. Six weeks later, a group on another continent published the same core result, with a strikingly similar proof strategy and even a similar title. I have since learned that at least two further groups were within months of the same thing. I had built a good deal of my professional identity around the belief that this was mine, and the episode has left me with questions I cannot shake. I have now been asked to chair a small working group — five people, roughly two years, a modest budget — whose brief is to think rigorously about priority, credit, and the apparent inevitability of discovery, and I need to decide what we should actually work on.

I do not want a survey, and I do not want a reading list. I want a defended agenda. Rank the open questions and tell me which two or three deserve the group's two years, and why. Use explicit criteria: how much downstream work depends on the answer, how tractable the question is for people with historical and philosophical training rather than quantitative skill, how neglected it is, and what evidence or argument over the coming year would move it up or down your ranking. Where the criteria pull against one another, say which you are weighting and why.

So that we are talking about the same thing, here are two framings I have been playing with. Treat them as live but contestable proposals rather than settled vocabulary. The first is a delay metric of credit: the credit due to a discoverer is proportional to how much later the discovery would have occurred had that person never existed, so that a genuinely indispensable contributor receives more than a member of a large simultaneous cluster. The second is a readiness picture of inevitability: a discovery is inevitable to the extent that the field's conceptual and instrumental preconditions were already sufficient, so that the result was in the air and only its articulation and labelling were contingent. I am aware that both are crude, and part of what I want from you is an account of where they break down.

Among the questions I would like you to weigh: whether credit should track causal contribution, moral deservingness, or something else entirely; whether the counterfactual delay framing remains coherent when the field would have produced the result regardless; whether a priority norm does genuine work for science or is largely a status game that could be redesigned; and whether the historical record of simultaneous discovery supports any general law or is merely a collection of cases with no common structure. Add anything I have missed if it is upstream of these.

Finally, be explicit about the epistemic status of what you say. Separate what is settled among historians and philosophers of science from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus. Where you are offering your own view rather than reporting the state of a debate, flag it as such. Please write in British English.
