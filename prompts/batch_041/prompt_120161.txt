I'm a philosophy professor who just got handed a small internal grant — enough for one postdoc for two years, some summer money for a good PhD student, and maybe a day a week of my own time — and I get to pick the topic. I want to spend it on money pumps and diachronic rationality, partly out of long-standing irritation with how that discussion stalls. You know the setup: an agent whose preferences run in a cycle can be walked around the cycle, paying a small toll at each step, and ends up exactly where she started, only poorer. The familiar escape hatches each seem to blunt the pump at a different point — refuse to start, resolve in advance to keep what you've got, have preferences over whole histories rather than terminal states, or simply not be the sort of creature who trades whenever a trade looks locally good — and I've never been convinced any of them settles whether the pump is really an argument about the coherence of the preferences themselves or something much weaker, like the agent's capacity to bind herself, or the fact that she's standing in an environment rigged against her. I also suspect the whole genre inherits unexamined baggage from the Dutch-book case for probabilism: that exploitablity by a clever counterparty is the mark of irrationalty, and that a disposition exploitable somewhere is therefore defective everywhere. Then there's the question I can't get past, which is whether any diachronic requirement can be derived from synchronic ones at all, or whether the honest view is that rationality is a matter of what you do at each moment and the pump is just a fact about what happens to people who can't commit. Two more things I want on the table: cycles among options that are incommensurable, or merely on a par rather than rankable, where it isn't obvious that any toll is small enough to be worth paying; and whether anything survives if the offers arrive as a single take-it-or-leave-it package instead of a sequence of local choices, since then there's nothing to be resolute about.

So what I want from you is a prioritization, not a survey. Given two years of one postdoc, one good grad student, and me at a day a week, which open questions here are actually worth the effort, and in what order? I care about which questions are upstream of which — if we settle X, does Y become easy, or does it just evaporate? — about which are tractable with tools philosophers already have (decision theory, formal epistemology, a little game theory) rather than needing new mathematics or a lab, and about which are neglected because everyone assumes they've been settled. The end I have in mind is two or three papers that change how people read these arguments, not a survey chapter.

Give me your actual ranking and defend it: the criteria you're using, the trade-offs between them, and what argument or result over the next couple of years would make you reorder it. Please don't hand me the balanced both-sides version — commit to an ordering, and tell me why the items below the top one lose.
