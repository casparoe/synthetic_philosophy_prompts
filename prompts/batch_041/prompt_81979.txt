For the past year or so I've been reporting on the naturalistic study of religion — the cognitive and evolutionary work that treats gods, rituals and doctrines as things a mind and a culture produce, rather than as things revealed — and I can follow the adaptation-versus-byproduct argument well enough to write about it. What I can't place is a smaller pattern that keeps surfacing in my interviews and in the ethnographies I read, and for which I have never heard a term. The most durable elements of a tradition are usually the ones that are hardest to explain and least convenient to perform: a fast whose original occasion is forgotten, a posture or a diet or a pilgrimage that no one, including the most devout, can say the point of, kept on a schedule nobody negotiates. These opaque and costly elements outlast the doctrines meant to justify them; the sermons change, the fast does not. And then the opacity itself seems to start doing work — precisely because the practice can't be rationalized, it is taken as evidence that something real must lie behind it, so the inherited gesture ends up shoring up the belief rather than the other way round. When a reformer tries to make the practice transparent, either by supplying it with a sensible purpose or by dropping it as superstition, the streamlined version is usually the one that fails to hold a community together, and the practice either creeps back or the community splits.

What I want to know is whether this loop — opacity lending fidelity, cost sustaining the practice, and the unexplained practice then feeding back as a warrant for belief — has a name, or whether it is merely a redescription of things that already do. I know it sits somewhere near costly signaling, near the idea of credibility-enhancing displays, near the byproduct account of religion, and near the claim that religious representations are memorable because they are only slightly counterintuitive. But each of those, as I understand them, explains one link in the chain — why a display is credible, why a belief sticks — and none of them seems to be about the circularity, or about the specific claim that a practice's opacity is load-bearing rather than incidental. So what I'm after is the carving, not the label: if there is an established concept for the loop, which one is it, and where does my description overshoot or undershoot it; if there isn't, I'd like the phenomenon named and defined, with its edges marked — in particular, whether it is supposed to be special to religion or also shows up in secular inherited practice (military ceremony, legal formality, academic ritual), and whether the opacity is genuinely functional or just the residue of how things get transmitted. A bare name would leave me exactly where I started.
