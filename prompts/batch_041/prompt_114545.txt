I'm a novelist who teaches an upper-level seminar, 'Character and Mind,' that I run jointly for MFA fiction students and philosophy undergraduates taking it as a writing-intensive elective. The final is a single essay question, ninety minutes, closed book, and I want it to get past a recitation of eliminative materialism — the thesis that our talk of beliefs and desires will be dropped by a mature science rather than reduced to it — and to land on the thing I actually care about as a writer: my entire craft consists of attributing beliefs and desires to invented people and getting readers to predict those people from the attributions, and I want students to say whether that practice counts as evidence against eliminativism, evidence for it, or neither.

To keep them from drifting into the standard summary, build the question around a small invented scenario I'll put in front of them: a writer drafts the same scene twice, once in belief-and-desire vocabulary and once in sub-personal, mechanistic terms, and finds that the second version, though it describes everything, no longer lets a reader anticipate what the character will do next. Students should say precisely what that failure does and does not establish. The crux I want them to reach is the difference between eliminativism proper and instrumentalism about the intentional stance, and whether the predictive success of fiction can be turned into an argument for realism about the attitudes rather than merely a demonstration that the vocabulary is useful.

Please write the question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme. In the model answer I want a committed position, not a survey of the options — take a side on what the novelist's practice shows and defend it. The marking scheme should name five must-hit moves, the marks each is worth out of twenty, and for each one the specific error that typically costs a candidate that mark.
