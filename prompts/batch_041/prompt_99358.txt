I have been arguing with a friend about whether information can be bad for you. She is a decision theorist and keeps invoking a result she calls Good's theorem: for an agent who maximises expected utility and updates her beliefs by conditionalisation, the expected value of receiving information cannot be negative, so a rational agent should never pay to avoid a free look at the evidence, and should not regret having looked. I accept the theorem in the abstract, but my verdicts on cases keep pulling away from it, and I want to know what principle actually captures them. Here are the cases and my gut verdicts. (1) A sealed envelope contains the exact date and manner of my death; I would pay a small amount not to open it, even though knowing could not change anything I do. (2) A medical test would tell me whether I have an untreatable condition whose course I could prepare for; I would pay to know, even though the only actions it changes are practical arrangements I could make blind. (3) I have recorded a match and avoided the score; I would pay a small amount to keep from being told, and I think the person who blurts it out has done me a minor harm. (4) I would pay to know whether a close friend secretly despises me, even if I would not confront them or change my behaviour. (5) I would pay not to know whether my partner once had a fleeting unkind thought about my work, even though it would not change how I act. (6) If a reliable oracle offered to tell me the exact number of atoms in my body at this moment, I would not pay a penny either way; it seems to me to have no value at all, though the theorem says it cannot be negative. (7) If the oracle offered to tell me whether a stranger I will never meet has a benign or malignant tumour, I would rather not know, and I do not think that is irrational. (8) But if the oracle offered to tell me whether the same stranger will be in pain tomorrow, and I could send anonymous aid, I would pay to know, and I would pay more than for the date of my own death.

What principle, or small set of principles, best captures these verdicts? I am tempted by this: information has positive value when it either changes what you can do or is itself part of what you care about, and negative value when it causes suffering you cannot use. But that seems to make (1) and (4) both non-action-guiding and yet opposite, and (5) and (7) non-action-guiding and negative, while (4) is non-action-guiding and positive. Perhaps the distinction is between information that is constitutive of a good you already have and information that is merely descriptive of a world you cannot affect, but I cannot make that precise. Please state the principle precisely, giving the case that motivates each clause; say what it predicts for cases I have not listed—information that is bad news but lets you savour the time remaining, information that is good news but spoils a surprise, information about a past event you cannot change, information that is intrinsically trivial but completes a theory you care about; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate. I am especially interested in whether Good's theorem survives as a special case, and if so under what restriction—say, that utility is defined only over actions and consequences, not over the state of being informed. If the theorem must be given up, say which verdict I should abandon and why. Please answer in British English.
