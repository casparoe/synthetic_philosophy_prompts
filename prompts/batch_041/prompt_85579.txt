I've been going back and forth with my roommate about the old "do the numbers count" thing (the Taurek debate, though I've only read about it secondhand) and I've realized I have pretty firm gut verdicts on a bunch of cases but no idea what principle they add up to. Here they are, all of them assuming I'm the only one who can act, everyone's a stranger unless I say otherwise, and I can't ask anyone's permission. One healthy stranger on the left, five equally healthy strangers on the right, both groups die otherwise: save the five, no doubt about it. One vs two: save the two, but weakly, it barely registers as a reason. One vs a hundred: save the hundred, obviously. One vs five where the one is my daughter: save my daughter, and I don't feel guilty about it at all. One person who'll die vs five people who'll each lose a hand: save the one, five hands don't add up to a life for me. One healthy twenty-year-old vs one eighty-five-year-old, everything else equal: coin flip, and I get uneasy when people start talking about life-years. Two six-year-olds vs five eighty-year-olds: I lean toward the kids, and this is the one I keep flip-flopping on, some days it feels like a straight coin flip and other days the kids feel obviously right. And then this one: I can save one person for sure, or try something that works half the time and saves five if it works and saves nobody if it doesn't. I save the one. I won't gamble with people's lives even when the arithmetic says the gamble is definately better. I'm leaving out the cases where saving the five means using somebody as a means, that's a different fight.

Here's the closest thing I have to a principle: save the greater number when everyone has roughly the same amount at stake; when they don't, save whoever has the most at stake; when one option is certain and the other is a gamble, take the certain one; and I'm allowed to weight my own people heavily. I suspect this is just four ad hoc patches stitched together, and I want to know whether there's one thing underneath it or whether the patches are the honest answer.

So: is there a single principle that gets all my verdicts, or nearly all, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without asking you again? Where does it bite the bullet against me, i.e. which verdict do I have to give up? And please apply it to two or three cases I haven't listed that you think would stress it most, especially any where my numbers-count verdict and my don't-gamble verdict pull in opposite directions. If one of my verdicts is just a mistake, say so plainly. I'd rather drop a verdict than keep a theory with a patch for every case.
