I spent my working life in the natural sciences — I was a physical chemist — and since retiring I have been reading philosophy with more enthusiasm than discipline. The sorites paradox is the puzzle I keep returning to, and I find myself puzzled less by the paradox than by the people who write about it. For decades the literature has been divided among those who hold that vague predicates have sharp but unknowable boundaries, those who hold that the boundaries are genuinely indeterminate and that truth is a matter of supertruth, those who hold that truth comes in degrees, and those who hold that the extension of a vague predicate shifts with context. Each position has been on the table for a long time, each has well-known costs, and as far as I can tell nobody converts anybody. I would like a diagnosis rather than another survey.

My own suspicion — offered as a hypothesis, not a conviction — is that the parties are not, despite appearances, answering the same question. Some seem to be after a compositional semantics that keeps classical logic and the disquotational schema intact; some seem to be after an accurate description of what we are doing when we judge a borderline case; some seem to be after a metaphysics of vague boundaries in the world. If that is right, then much of the disagreement may be about which phenomenon a theory of vagueness is even meant to explain — a hidden disagreement about the explanandum rather than a clash of answers. I am aware that my scientific habits may be misleading me here: in my own field a sharp transition, a boiling point or a critical point, emerges from continuous underlying behaviour, and we do not ask whether the sharpness is an unknowable fact about each molecule or a mere artefact of description; the coarse-graining does the explanatory work. I mention this only because it shapes my suspicion that the sorites debate may be importing a false dichotomy.

Please diagnose the stall. Is it verbal, a disagreement about the target phenomenon, a disagreement about what counts as a datum (the intuition of tolerance, the apparent absence of a cutoff, the felt absurdity of the conclusion), or a clash of starting commitments — say, to bivalence or to classical logic — that neither side can argue for from neutral ground? Engage the positions concretely enough that I can see which move you think each side is really making. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. Throughout, separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as if it were the consensus. Write in British English.
