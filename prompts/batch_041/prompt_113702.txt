I recently left a doctorate in logic for a research-ethics position at a technology company, and I have been taken aback by how often Gödel's incompleteness theorems are invoked in our internal arguments — to show that no machine can match human mathematical insight, that no formal model could capture consciousness, and even that a completed physics is out of reach. Our group has invited a logician whose own work is in the proof theory of arithmetical theories, and who is known for the austere view that the theorems are philosophically inert outside proof theory, nearly every non-mathematical appeal to them being a category mistake. I am to interview him before an audience of engineers and scientists, and I want eight to ten questions that keep the discussion on the content of the results rather than on the slogans.

Please include at least one question on each of the following, with a follow-up for each keyed to the answer he is most likely to give: first, what the two theorems actually assert and under which conditions — effective axiomatizability, consistency as against ω-consistency, how much arithmetic a theory must be able to represent — and where the popular claim that there are mathematical truths we can never prove goes wrong; second, the Lucas–Penrose argument that we can recognize the truth of a Gödel sentence for a system that stands in for our own minds, together with the standard mechanist rejoinder and the disjunction it leaves us (either the mind is not a machine, or it is one whose consistency it cannot establish), and which horn he would take; third, the one foundational program the theorems genuinely unsettled, Hilbert's, and what survives of it — consistency proofs by means other than the finitistic ones he demanded, and the partial rescues of his aims.

I would also like questions that press his austere line rather than let it stand: whether there is any legitimate non-mathematical application of the theorems, since the arguments about self-trust and self-justification in epistemology appear to turn on their content rather than merely borrowing their prestige; whether incompleteness is a defect at all, given that arithmetic without multiplication and the theory of real closed fields are both complete and decidable; whether the notion of a sentence being absolutely undecidable — not merely unprovable in this or that system — is even coherent; and why these results, rather than the halting problem or Tarski's undefinability theorem, exert such a hold on the imagination. Close with a question about what would have to be the case for him to concede that some non-mathematical use of the theorems is sound.
