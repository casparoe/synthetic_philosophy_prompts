For the last several weeks I have been having the same argument with a friend of mine, a retired actuary who is otherwise a very careful reasoner, about whether either of us should bother voting in our ward election this spring. His move, every time the subject comes up, is the one-liner 'my vote doesn't matter', delivered as though it settles things; and when I point out that the last two races in our ward were decided by margins small enough to count on the fingers of one hand, he slides over to the near-synonym 'one vote never decides an election' and treats the two sentences as saying the same thing. What I want from you is a careful breaking-open of that one sentence, so that I can go back to him with something better than annoyance. What are the distinct precise claims that 'my vote doesn't matter' could actually be making? I expect the interesting work is in two places. The first is the word 'matter', which might mean that the outcome is causally different from what it would have been had I stayed home, or that the outcome is not counterfactually dependent on my act in the but-for sense, or that my act leaves the margin of victory essentially unchanged, or that it makes no difference to anything I can plausibly be credited with, or that it does nothing for the legitimacy or the mandate of whoever wins, or that it fails to move other people's turnout — and these come apart. The second is the probability claim, which might be the literal claim that the chance my vote is decisive is zero, or the weaker claim that it is nonzero but so small that it can be ignored, or the still different claim that it is small relative to the cost to me of voting, or the claim that once we assume I care only about my own narrowly self-interested payoffs it drops out of the calculation entirely — and the last of these is a claim about the shape of my preferences rather than about elections at all. I would also like the level of the claim made explicit, since a statement about what my single act accomplishes is not the same as a statement about what the rule 'people like me stay home' would accomplish, nor about whether the outcome is in some sense constituted by the votes rather than caused by them.

Please lay the readings out as a menu rather than settling on one and defending it, ranging from the readings that are flat platitudes, through the ones that are substantive and defensible, to any that are simply false, and then tell me which of them my friend actually needs in order to get from 'my vote doesn't matter' to 'so it isn't worth your time to vote' — since my suspicion is that only one or two of the readings do that work, and that they are not the ones he would accept if they were stated plainly. Say as well whether his switch from 'my vote doesn't matter' to 'one vote never decides an election' is a change of claim or only a change of emphasis, and whether the readings that make his conclusion follow are the same ones that survive contact with a race decided by a handful of votes. One thing I would rather not have: a lecture on whether people ought to vote anyway, or on civic duty generally. I want the disambiguation and the verdicts on each reading, and I want to see exactly where the equivocation is if there is one. Write it as continuous prose, please — no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italics, no numbered lists and no tables. A letter is the right shape for this, even though I do need to be able to pick each reading out separately, so signpost them in the sentences themselves rather than formatting them.
