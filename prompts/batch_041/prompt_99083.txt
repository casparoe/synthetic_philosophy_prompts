Build me a historical case that isolates overdetermination from ordinary joint causation, for a seminar on causal explanation in history. Spec: two processes, each on its own sufficient to bring the outcome about within the same window, that are genuinely causally independent of each other (neither caused by the other, no common cause, no coordinator pulling strings), and that run on different timescales -- one a slow structural development, one a short-lived event -- so that a Braudel-minded historian and an events-minded one would each want to point at a different one. One paragraph, a real-ish setting (a regime, a firm, a city council -- not a fantasy), no science-fictional devices.

Then walk me through the verdicts. What does the but-for test say about each process? What does a NESS/INUS-style account say? And what does a probabilistic account say -- and make sure the second process does not raise the probability of the outcome once you've conditioned on the first, so that account has to deny it's a cause. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the overdetermination if the two processes turn out not to be independent (say the slow decline is what caused the short-lived event), and how a defender of difference-making theories of causation would try to defuse the whole thing by calling it a puzzle about causal selection rather than about causation.
