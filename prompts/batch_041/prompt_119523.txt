Two jurists sit on an advisory commission drafting the charter of an international tribunal that a victorious coalition will convene to try the leaders of the state it has just defeated. They agree that the new tribunal will be measured against Nuremberg and Tokyo; they disagree about whether it should be modeled on them, and their disagreement runs back to what they think those tribunals were. The first jurist maintains that the offenses on the draft list were already unlawful when they were committed — that the planning and waging of aggressive war violated customary international law, and that atrocities against civilians violated the laws and customs of war — so that a tribunal which declares this law is applying it rather than making it. The second maintains that no treaty or custom then in force attached individual criminal liability to the waging of aggressive war; that a defendant convicted on that charge is punished for conduct that was not criminal when he engaged in it; and that the tribunal, however salutary its purpose, is legislating for the occasion and applying its legislation backward.

Both jurists accept, for the sake of argument, a threefold distinction among forms of retroactivity: retroactive definition, in which conduct previously lawful is declared criminal; retroactive jurisdiction, in which conduct already criminal under some domestic law is made triable before a new international forum; and retroactive attribution, in which a mode of individual liability — the responsibility of a commander who fails to punish his subordinates, say, or liability for participation in a common plan — is applied to conduct that predates the codification of that mode. They disagree about which of these, if any, is fatal to a tribunal's legitimacy, and about whether the first and the third are really distinct.

Write their dialogue. In the course of it, the second jurist should press a difficulty of self-presentation: the tribunal must justify itself to the defeated, who will call it the vengeance of the strong, and to the future, for which it must stand as a precedent; yet the more it presents itself as declaring pre-existing law, the weaker its claim to lay down law that will bind future wars, while the more it presents itself as laying down such law, the more it concedes that the defendants were tried under law that did not exist when they acted. The second jurist should also observe that the bench, the charter, and the choice of charges are the victors' own work, and should raise the objection that the victors have themselves done things resembling what they now charge; he may add that a frankly political reckoning would at least be honest about what it is. The first jurist must answer these points rather than evade them, and may argue that the legal form was what kept the reckoning from becoming an execution and made a record that outlasts the victors.

Requirements: neither jurist is a caricature, and neither may win by assertion; each must engage the strongest form of the other's position and concede whatever seems to him genuinely right in it; neither may settle a question by appealing to what the Nuremberg or Tokyo tribunals held, since both know who wrote those charters. By the end the two should have located precisely what divides them — whether it is a disagreement about the content of customary law, about the concept of law, or about what a tribunal may permissibly do — and should have said what each would have to give up for them to converge. It is not required that they agree. Aim for a sustained exchange, with several substantial turns on each side, rather than a sketch.
