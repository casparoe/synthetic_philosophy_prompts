ok so I've been reading about cases where two people land on the same idea at basically the same time — Darwin and Wallace on natural selection, Newton and Leibniz on the calculus, Bolyai and Lobachevsky on non-Euclidean geometry, Mendeleev and Lothar Meyer on the periodic table, Scheele and Priestley on oxygen, Bell and Gray on the telephone. And everyone always wants to know two things: was the discovery inevitable, and does it matter who got there first. I wrote up an argument that both questions are confused, and I'd like some comments on it. Here's the argument.

Think of any discovery as having two seperate parts. There's the scaffold: all the prior results, techniques, notation, and well-posed problems that make the discovery reachable at all. The scaffold is almost always the work of many hands over decades, and it's shared — anyone working in the area in that period inherits roughly the same one. Then there's the last step: the specific move that actually closes the gap. The last step is usually small, often idiosyncratic, and it's the part we name after a person.

Now, the "inevitable" question is almost always answered at the scaffold level. By, say, the 1850s the field had everything it needed, so someone was bound too do this. And then, with no extra argument, that gets read as a claim about the last step: so someone was bound to take the last step. I want to call this the finish-line fallacy. A crowd converging on a finish line tells you the course led there, not that the last stride is easy. Reachability makes the field converge; it doesn't make any particular stride trivial.

There's a probabilistic version that makes the point cleaner. Suppose N people are positioned to take the step and each has probability p of taking it in a given period. The chance that someone takes it rises fast with N; the chance that a particular person takes it first is roughly 1/N, i.e. close to arbitrary. So "it was inevitable" (someone will) and "does it matter who" (which one) come apart exactly when N is large and p is small — which is precisely the situation in the famous cases. The discovery is inevitable and the discoverer is a coin flip, and those aren't in tension, they're the same fact seen twice.

One more wrinkle I can't decide how much to make of: the cases aren't all the same shape. Newton's fluxions and Leibniz's differentials are different last steps on a shared scaffold; we've retroactively merged them into one discovery called the calculus because they sit on the same scaffold and give the same answers. So sometimes "simultaneous discovery" is really shared scaffold, divergent last steps that we later unified. That makes "who discovered it" worse rather than better, because the question presupposes a unification the people involved didn't have.

Which brings me to credit. Our naming conventions track the last step, so fame goes to whoever happened to be standing closest when the coin landed. Meanwhile the scaffold-builders — whose contribution is genuinely hard to duplicate, since it requires being early and being right for a long time — get nothing. So my answer to does it matter who made it: it matters, but mostly as evidence that our credit conventions are pointed at the wrong part of the discovery. If we cared about the hard part, we'd name the scaffold after people.

I know this is close to some standard points (standing on the shoulders of giants; multiple discovery as a sociological phenomenon) and I'm worried it's just those in new clothes. So what I want from you: is the scaffold/last-step split doing real work, or does it collapse into the giants point? Is the finish-line fallacy a real fallacy or just a fancy restatement of "overdetermination doesn't imply individual inevitability"? Does the credit conclusion actually follow, or am I smuggling in a value judgment about which part deserves the credit? Where does the framing break — I can think of cases where the last step really was the hard part and the scaffold was thin, and I don't know what to do with them. Give me the strongest objection, and be blunt about the weakest part of the whole thing. If I've got a historical example wrong, say so, but don't hand me a reading list — I want the argument, not the bibliography.
