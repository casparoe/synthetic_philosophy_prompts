Ok, I need a careful comparison of two ways of cashing out the idea that a rational agent defers to objective chance, and I want the comparison run on a specific case rather than in the abstract.

Version 1, the textbook one: if you know that the chance of A is x, and the rest of your evidence is admissable — meaning it tells you nothing about A over and above the chance — then your credence in A should be x. You don't second-guess the chance; that's what deferring means. (The obvious question is what counts as admissable, but hold that thought.)

Version 2, which is mine, and which I half suspect is incoherent: you don't defer to a bare chance value, you defer to the whole chance setup. You condition on the chance function being the one it is and on the history up to now, and then your credence in A is the chance of A given that same setup — i.e. the chance of A conditional on the chance theory itself, not the unconditional chance of A. So when the setup hands out a nonzero chance to a future that would falsify the setup, version 1 tells you to plug that number in, while version 2 recomputes and lets the undermining future drop out.

Now the case. A lump of radioactive material with a half-life, where the chance theory itself assigns a tiny but nonzero chance to a long run of decays that, if it happened, would mean the chance function was never the one we assumed (say chances are fixed by the total pattern of events, so a weird enough future just is a different chance setup) — the future would undermine the theory that assigned the chances. Walk me through my credence in that undermining future under each version, before and after conditonalizing. Is version 1 straight-up inconsistent here, or only awkward once you say what admissable means? Does version 2 still deserve to be called deference to chance, or is it ordinary conditionalization wearing a lab coat — and if it's the latter, what work is the word chance still doing? Does version 2 make chance explanatorily idle? And is there a version 3 that keeps the deference and dodges the undermining problem, or do you have to surrender one of the two?

Be straight with me about how confident you are in your answer, and name the single step you're least sure about.
