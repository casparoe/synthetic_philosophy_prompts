I've spent the past several months reporting on the quiet work that the common prior assumption does in economics, decision theory, and parts of philosophy, and I keep colliding with the same unresolved argument about what kind of claim it is. The assumption—often called the Harsanyi doctrine—holds that rational agents' differences in belief are to be attributed to differences in information rather than to genuine divergence in prior credence, and it is what licenses the agreement theorem, the no-trade theorem, and much of the machinery of Bayesian games. I have come around to a fairly strong version of it, and I would rather have that version tested than flattered. The position is this: the common prior assumption is not a technical convenience or a convention adopted for tractability but a genuine constraint on rational belief. Rational Bayesians who share the same information should not persistently disagree about probabilities; where two competent and informed agents do disagree, either one of them is making a mistake, or the disagreement is traceable to a difference in information, including information about how the other is reasoning. Principled, information-invariant disagreement—the kind we observe between experts who have examined the same evidence—is therefore something to be explained away rather than taken at face value.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure. What would count against it—which observations, arguments, formal results, or possible cases—and how decisive would each of them be? I am especially interested in how the answer shifts depending on which version of the claim is on the table: as a thesis about rationality, as an empirical hypothesis about how belief differences actually arise, as a modeling convention, or as the premise of a representation theorem. Which defeaters would the position's own defenders concede as counting against them, rather than dismissing as a misunderstanding of what the assumption is for? I would also like to know what the position predicts that its rivals do not: that persistent public disagreement under common knowledge of one another's opinions should dissolve, that speculative trade driven purely by divergent beliefs should be absent, that the set of belief profiles inconsistent with a common prior should be negligible on some natural parameterization. And if the position can absorb every anomaly by adjusting the auxiliary assumptions—common knowledge, perfect Bayesianism, the absence of ambiguity-averse agents, the absence of noise traders—does it become unfalsifiable, and is that a defect for a claim of this kind, or simply what one should expect from a constraint on rationality rather than a descriptive hypothesis?

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. If the standard defenses are themselves disputed—the claim that the anomalies reflect failures of the auxiliary assumptions rather than of the assumption itself, the claim that the doctrine is a convention rather than a theorem of Bayesian decision theory, the equivalence between the existence of a common prior and the impossibility of a bet that every agent would regard as profitable in every state—then say so, and say who is likely to remain unconvinced and why. I would rather have a verdict with the confidence calibrated than a survey that refuses to land.
