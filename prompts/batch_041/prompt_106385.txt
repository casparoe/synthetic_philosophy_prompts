I'm in a seminar on population ethics and I've got a chain of reasoning about the utility monster that I cant get out of. The monster, in case you've somehow avoided it, is a being who gets vastly more welfare out of any given resource than a person does, so a view that says "maximize total welfare" ends up saying we should hand it everything.

I tried to build an argument that the monster is a paper tiger, and I think it proves too much. Here's the chain:

(1) A total-welfare view says an outcome is better the more welfare is in it, and every being's welfare counts the same.
(2) The monster is defined by a comparison: per unit of resource, it gets more welfare than a person does.
(3) Take that at face value — per unit, always, the monster wins.
(4) Then any given unit of resource produces more welfare in the monster's hands than in a person's.
(5) So total welfare is maximized by routing every resource to the monster.
(6) So the view says: feed the monster, starve the rest of us.
(7) That's absurd, and it's the whole point of the example.
(8) So the total-welfare view is false.
(9) But notice what (8) costs. If the view is false, it's because not every being's welfare can count the same — some being's welfare has to be worth more per unit than another's, or we don't get the verdict in (7) that we wanted.
(10) A being whose welfare is worth more per unit, for no reason at all, just is a monster, so the objection has reinstated the very thing it was trying to kill.
(11) So the monster objection to the total view only succeeds by turning into a monster itself.

I'd rather keep (7) than (1) — I'm not giving up on the thought that numbers count for something — and honestly my money is on (9)-(11) being where it goes soft. My friend says I'm attacking a strawman, that I've got the monster's definition wrong, and she won't tell me how, which is driving me up the wall. So: which step goes, and what do I pay for dropping it? If it's (2) or (3) that's rotten, don't just tell me to restate it — tell me what the fixed version does to the rest of the chain, because I suspect the honest version of the monster is a much stranger beast, and I want to know whether it's even a coherent thing to stipulate or whether the whole example dies right there.
