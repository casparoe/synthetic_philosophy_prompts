Write a short story about two rival theories of the same phenomenon and the people who hold them — invent the phenomenon, the setting, and the science. The richer theory entails everything the sparser one entails and much more besides: wherever the sparser theory is right, the richer one is right too, and the richer one also stakes itself on further claims the sparser theory never makes. A character nearing the end of a career spent on the sparser theory defends it on grounds of verisimilitude: if a theory's closeness to the truth is measured by adding up the true consequences it entails and subtracting the false ones, then any theory that says more than its rival will thereby be committed to more falsehoods as well as more truths, so the ledger can never rank two theories that differ in content at all — the only theories it can treat as equally close to the truth are ones that say exactly the same thing. The story should turn on what it costs to accept that, and on why someone might go on using the measure anyway. Let a younger colleague work the argument out and be unable to tell whether it exposes a defect in the measure or a discovery about what getting closer to the truth could possibly mean, and let at least one character keep faith with the older theory for reasons the reader can respect. Do not name real philosophers, texts, or episodes from the history of science, and do not quote anyone.
