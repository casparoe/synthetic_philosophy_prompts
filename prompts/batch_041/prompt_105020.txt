I'm running a single session of my decision theory seminar on the St. Petersburg paradox and I want the students to feel the actual pinch of it, not hear me narrate it afterwards. 18 students, 80 minutes, they've all had expected value and they've all seen the paradox named somewhere, so describing it again gets me nothing. Design me the runnable activity. I have a rough shape in mind and I want you to build it out properly, with rules, a timeline, and the debrief.

What they should walk out with: the felt collision between an argument that looks airtight (the expected value is infinite, so any finite price is a bargain and no finite price is enough) and their own flat refusal to pay more than pocket change; and then the second thing, that the standard fixes dont dissolve the paradox, they quietly swap it for a tamer game that has no paradox in it.

The shape is casino night. Everyone gets a bankroll of tokens and the house offers the one bet: flip a fair coin until it lands heads; if the first head is on flip k, you win 2^k tokens. I want the bidding and the arithmetic to do the work, not me.

Round 1, everyone privately writes the most they'd pay to play one round, out of their own pocket, and I want the tokens framed as standing in for real money so it stings a little. Collect the slips, read the bids out loud. They'll cluster low.

Round 2, build the expected value together on the board, term by term, and watch it add 1+1+1+... and not stop. Then I, as the house, make every student a take-it-or-leave-it pair of offers: I'll sell you a ticket at twice your bid, or I'll buy your ticket at your bid. If you're maximising expected value you buy at double and you never sell. Count the flinches. The contradiction is now in their hands instead of in a book.

Round 3 is the part I actually want the session built around, so give it room. Reveal the house only has a finite bankroll, say 1024 tokens, so the bet is capped there. Ask what the ticket is worth now. Have two or three of them derive it or brute-force it, and here's the fact you'll want: for a house that can pay out at most W tokens, the fair price is log base 2 of W plus 1, which for 1024 is 11 tokens, and a few hundred simulated plays land near that. The paradox is gone. Then take the cap away, house infinitely rich, and ask what actually changed. Nothing about the bet changed, only the thing we assumed away. That's the moment I'm chasing: the finite bankroll didn't solve the paradox, it replaced it with a game that has no paradox.

Give me roles so it isn't just me narrating: a banker who holds the 1024 and physically refuses to pay past it, two auditors running the many-plays simulation and reporting the running average, everyone else as punters. Materials: bid slips, a board, tokens or a spreadsheet, one coin or a phone RNG.

Then the debrief, which is where I most need you. Pin the moments in the activity to the real positions: the diminishing-marginal-utility move (a claim that the fair price should be computed in utility rather than tokens, which the activity only half fits because tokens carry almost no utility curvature), the bounded-bankroll move, and the uncomfortable point that both are ways of saying not this game. I want them to see the fork clearly: either expected value is the wrong rule and you owe a replacement plus a reason to trust it, or the game is unplayable because no finite house can offer it and the paradox was never about a game you could be offered. Mention only in passing, as an extension I might add if the session runs hot, that there's a line of thought where the fair price turns on whether you play once or play repeatedly and compound, a time-average rather than ensemble-average thing. Dont let that eat the session.

Two things I want you honest about in the write-up: what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it, and which feature of the paradox the activity cannot reproduce so I have to supply it myself. My own guesses are that the tokens kill real risk aversion so some students bid absurdly and the whole thing looks silly instead of forceful, that someone short-circuits it with expected value is just the wrong rule and deflates the room, that the simulation variance means most rounds end at flip 1 or 2 and nobody ever sees the tail, and that the finite-bankroll reveal feels like a bait and switch if I dont make them derive it first. Tell me what I'm missing and how to handle each.

Keep the whole thing runnable inside 80 minutes and dont let round 3's arithmetic swallow it.
