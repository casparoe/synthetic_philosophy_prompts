I am a novelist, and I have come to a scene I cannot yet write. Two of my characters are at dinner, and the argument they fall into is one I want to be worth the reader's attention — not the usual vegetarian-versus-carnivore set piece that everyone has heard a hundred times, but something that goes a layer or two deeper and leaves both of them standing.

I would like you to draft that dialogue.

The first of them has reached a conclusion that unsettles him because it seems to follow from his own principles. He cares, above all, about the number of sentient creatures who suffer and die so that he may eat, and he has come to believe that the least harmful diet available to him is one built on pasture-raised beef, and that the worst is one built on chicken and eggs. His reasoning is arithmetic, and I want it laid out rather than gestured at: crop agriculture is itself a machine for killing, since the combine brings in mice, rats, rabbits, ground-nesting birds and numberless invertebrates along with the grain, so every plant calorie carries a body count; and when a calorie is routed through an animal, that crop-field cost is multiplied by how much feed the animal needs to yield a pound of meat. A cow on grass turns roughage that people cannot digest into food and is slaughtered once; a chicken is a small furnace that burns a great deal of grain to produce a little flesh. By this ledger, the omnivore who eats beef and shuns poultry may be responsible for fewer deaths than the vegan who lives on wheat, rice and soy — and if bivalves are not sentient at all, as some argue, then perhaps the truly conscientious plate is one of oysters and beef, and no vegetables at all.

The second character is a vegan who accepts the same consequentialist premise and refuses the conclusion. I want her objections to be the strongest available, not a recitation of slogans. She might ask whether a field mouse and a cow are to be weighed on the same scale at all; she might point out what a pasture costs in wild habitat and in the wildlife displaced to make room for it; and she might spring the thing her companion has not yet reckoned with — that the suffering of wild animals in nature may be the largest and most overlooked moral fact there is, which would make the whole comparison of diets a quarrel over loose change while a fortune burns. I want her to have the sharper line if she has one, and I want him to be able to answer it.

Let them genuinely engage: when one of them makes a move, the other must answer that move and not an easier version of it. Neither is a fool, and neither is a caricature; each should be able to grant a point without losing the thread. I do not need them to settle anything, but I do not want the fog of a polite draw either — by the last line, a reader should be able to say precisely where the disagreement has come to rest: whether it is an empirical dispute about numbers, a moral dispute about what may be counted, or a dispute about which facts are even relevant to the question. Please write it as a scene, with enough stage business to keep it alive, but do not let the room and the wine swallow the argument.
