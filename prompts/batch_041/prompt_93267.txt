I work with two groups who have been talking past each other about the reference class problem for years: modellers who build risk and pricing models, and philosophers of probability. The modellers treat it as a pseudo-problem — you condition on the variables you have, you check calibration and discrimination out of sample, and where two reference classes assign different frequencies to the same individual you keep whichever partition earns its keep in validation. The philosophers say this changes the subject: the question was what probability the individual's outcome actually has, and validation tells you only which model predicts well on average, not which class the individual really belongs to; and because the competing classes are typically not nested, there is no finer partition to fall back on. They add that conditioning on everything relevant collapses the probability to zero or one, so the real question must be what to leave out, and nothing in modelling practice answers that. My hypothesis is that this is less a disagreement about probability than about what a probability is for — a guide to decision, or a fact about the world — and that the two sides are answering different questions under one name. Diagnose the impasse: is it verbal, is it a hidden empirical or formal question, are the sides answering different questions, or is the underlying question simply ill-posed? Argue from what each side actually claims, and say what would count as progress — a distinction, a theorem, a change of question — or why none should be expected. Tell me how confident you are in your diagnosis and which part of it you are least sure of.
