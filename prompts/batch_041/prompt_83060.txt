I want a short story built on the following apparatus. It comes from an old thought experiment in the philosophy of mind, but it is set out here in full: nothing needs to be looked up, nothing should be cited, and no prior knowledge of the literature should be assumed.

The apparatus is an arena — call it the careenium — a shallow, walled billiard table with no pockets, whose rims are slightly raised and whose segments can be made more or less bouncy. Inside it are several thousand small, strongly magnetized balls, which I will call simms. Nothing else is in the arena; the simms move only by colliding with one another and with the rims. The rims are the apparatus's only contact with the world outside: as things happen out there, different segments of rim grow more or less bouncy, and the simms are nudged accordingly. The simms are magnetized in such a way that they cohere in transient clumps — groupings that form, persist for a while, break apart, and recombine — and over time the pattern of clumping comes to track regularities in the outside world, much as a set of marks on a page can track facts. Call these recurring higher-order groupings simmballs. The apparatus is used: people watch it, read it, adjust it, and rely on what it does.

Two complete descriptions of the careenium are always available. One is in terms of the simms: positions, magnetizations, collisions, the bounciness of rim segments. The other is in terms of simmballs: which formations arise, which recruit which, how they merge and dissolve, and how the whole dance corresponds to what is happening outside. The question I want the story to hold open is whether the simmballs push the simms around, or whether all the pushing is done by the simms and the simmball story is only a convenient summary of what the simms were doing anyway.

Write that story. Roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, third person, no epigraph. It should do the following work:

1. Give the apparatus a practical stake: a character who needs the careenium to start doing something it does not yet do, or to stop doing something it does, and whose plausible interventions fall on one side or the other — rearrange the simms and the rims, or arrange conditions so that a new kind of simmball forms and recruits the rest.

2. Follow one and the same short interval at both levels. First the simmballs: a legible dance in which formations stand for things that are not present and are used in their place. Then the same seconds as simms: an unmanageable blizzard of collisions, complete and useless. The narration must not tell the reader which of these is the real one.

3. Let someone try to settle the question by experiment — an intervention designed to show whether the simmballs do any pushing of their own. The experiment should fail to settle it, and the failure should be a fact about the events, not a remark by the narrator.

4. Include two competent characters who disagree, and treat both fairly: one who holds that once you have the simms and the rims the causal story is finished and the simmballs are bookkeeping; one who holds that a system usable at the higher level must be doing some of its work there, since otherwise no one could ever act on it in that way. Their quarrel should be about this machine, not about philosophy.

5. Resolve nothing. End at the point where the question is sharpest — ideally on an intervention that works, and that each character can fully account for in their own terms.

6. Keep the philosophical vocabulary out of the narration. If a character names the problem, let them name it in the idiom of their trade, and let them be partly wrong.

It is not a story about the careenium waking up, and nothing should turn on whether it has experiences; if some simmball seems to stand for the careenium itself, that is a detail, not the point. Nor should anything license treating simmballs as little agents with purposes of their own — if a character talks that way, it should be visibly a manner of speaking. No coda, no author's note.
