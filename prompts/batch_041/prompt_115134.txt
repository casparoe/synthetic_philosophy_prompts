Ok so I've been lurking on the rationalist blogs for years and I basically think of myself as a Bayesian — I update, I try to stay calibrated, the whole deal — and lately I've been sitting in on a seminar on epistemology because a friend in the department keeps dragging me to it. She's really into this literature on 'higher-order evidence' and 'epistemic akrasia' and the more she explains it the more I suspect it's a confusion that Bayesians sorted out decades ago. Here's my reasoning, tell me where it's wrong. 'Higher-order evidence' is just evidence about a fact — namely the fact that my reasoning process is unreliable. I took a pill that I know makes me bad at math, or I've had three beers, or I'm emotionally invested in the conclusion. But that's still evidence! It has a likelihood ratio! Bayes' rule tells me exactly how far to move my credence in the mathematical claim, and if I want to be fancy I can condition on the joint distribution over (claim, reliability of my process). So why does anyone talk about it as a seperate kind of thing with its own puzzles — 'undercutting' vs 'rebutting', whether it's 'screened off' by the first-order evidence, all of that. And 'epistemic akrasia', believing something while believing you shouldn't believe it — that's just ordinary weakness of will, like eating the cake while believing you shouldn't. Nobody writes papers about 'dietary akrasia' being a deep paradox. If I believe my belief that p is unjustified, I should just... not have it? Obviously? And yeah I know people say 'you can't check your reasoning without using your reasoning', but that's just the general problem of induction for cognition, not a special puzzle. So is there anything actually here, or is this one of those areas where smart people have invented a problem that dissolves the moment you write down a probability distribution and refuse to double-count evidence?

What finally set me off was a case she gave me. You take a drug that you know degrades your ability to evaluate proofs, and then you carefully work through a proof and it seems completely convincing — you can't find any flaw, it feels exactly like every proof you've ever gotten right. She says the interesting question is what you ought to believe, which, fine, I can at least see that there's a question there about whether the reliability evidence outweighs the apparent proof. But then she said something I think is just incoherent: that it might be rational for you to believe the conclusion *and* rational for you to believe that believing it is irrational, and that this 'level splitting' isn't a contradiction but the correct answer. That sounds to me like saying a square can be round. Either the total evidence supports the conclusion or it doesnt. She also kept saying 'akrasia' as if the interesting claim were about what's *rationally permissible* rather than what's psychologically possible, which I think is a motte-and-bailey — nobody ever doubted that people can believe things they think are unjustified. So: is there a version of either puzzle that survives being stated in plain Bayesian terms, or is the whole thing a case of people refusing to conditionalize because they don't like the answer? I want the steelman here, not the pop version.
