I retired a few years back from behavioural ecology -- thirty-odd years of watching dominance hierarchies in birds and mammals, and enough time refereeing ESS papers that I know the machinery cold. Since then I've been in a reading group with two other ex-scientists and we've drifted into Hegel, and I keep getting the uncanny feeling that the master-slave business in the Phenomenology is just a dominance hierarchy in fancy dress. Could be the classic novice's overreach, but I'd definately like you to test it properly.

Take the master-slave dialectic and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually have: dominance and subordination, ritualised aggression, submission signals, winner and loser effects, the hawk-dove game, that bourgeois strategy where the stable play is hawkish if you're the owner and dove-ish if you're the intruder even though ownership is an arbitrary asymmetry, reconciliation after fights, coalitions, and the cost accounting of who can afford to escalate. Go claim by claim and label each one: translates cleanly, translates only with distortion, or has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. And please don't fudge it by quietly restating Hegel's claim in my words while keeping his assumption -- if something only goes through because you smuggled in a notion I don't have, say so.

Three things I'd especially like you to press on, because they're where the hunch either gets interesting or dies.

The fight to the death. In every system I've studied escalation is rare and lethal fights are rarer still; the loser usually gets to keep living and the relationship settles into something cheaper. Hegel seems to need the death stakes, since the slave is supposedly the one who preferred life and servitude to dying, and that's what makes his position defective. Is that a modelling error on Hegel's part, or is the biological version doing something different in kind? And what happens to the dialectic if you rewrite it so escalation is conventionally capped, the way it actually is in real contests -- does it still work?

The hollowness. The master is supposed to end up badly off, because the recognition he gets comes from someone he doesn't recognise as an equal, so it doesn't count for what he wanted; meanwhile he's the dependent one, needing the slave's work and submission to remain master. Is there anything like that in my world? Best I can do is a dominant who leans on the tolerance of his subordinates or on coalitions -- some primate alphas hold power through allies, cooperative breeders depend on helpers -- but that reads to me as a fact about power, not a defect in the relationship. Is there any ethological sense in which dominance has to be endorsed by the subordinate to be real, rather than just complied with?

Self-consciousness needing another self-consciousness. My instinct is that this is just the true-but-cheap observation that social animals need conspecifics to develop normally. Am I missing a distinction there, or is that really all it comes to?

Then the part I actually care about -- the residue report. What doesn't survive at all? I suspect its the whole normative side: recognition as a subject, as someone whose claims count, and the idea that a relation can be defective rather than merely unstable or costly. If that's what falls out, tell me honestly whether the residue is a gap in my vocabulary -- a real feature of social life that behavioural ecology simply has no language for -- or whether it's a sign that Hegel is running a normative conclusion through what looks like a description, in which case the residue is fog rather than content. Don't be polite about it.

Also: no reading list, no citations, don't tell me to go read Kojeve or whoever. I want the translation and the residue, not the bibliography.
