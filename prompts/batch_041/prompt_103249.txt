You are giving the best man's toast at the wedding of two close friends from graduate school, both of whom went on to teach philosophy; roughly half the room, however, will be relatives and old friends with no philosophical background at all. You have decided the toast should be built on G. E. Moore's ethics rather than on the usual sentiments, and in particular on his position, in the final chapter of Principia Ethica, that the greatest intrinsic goods are personal affection and the appreciation of beauty; that these goods are objective rather than a matter of what any particular person happens to enjoy; and that the value of a whole — what he called an organic unity — can exceed the sum of the values of its parts.

Write the four-to-five-minute toast that this view makes possible and that this audience can receive. Moore's commitments should do structural work rather than decorate: the toast cannot rest on the thought that the couple is good because they make each other happy (Moore was no hedonist), nor on the thought that their bond is valuable merely for them (he held value to be objective), and it should put the organic-unity idea to real use in saying what the two of them are together rather than merely separately. It has to be warm and legible to the relatives, not a seminar for the philosophers — but the philosophers are listening, and they will notice if you quietly put into Moore's mouth a claim he would have rejected.
