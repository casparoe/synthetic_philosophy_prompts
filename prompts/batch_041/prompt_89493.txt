In my graduate seminar on the infinite, a mathematician colleague kept objecting to Hilbert's hotel with the remark, 'Infinity is not a number.' He used it both to deny that a hotel with infinitely many rooms can take in infinitely many new guests and to deny Cantor's result that the even numbers can be paired one-to-one with the natural numbers. I want a disambiguation of the slogan, not a verdict on my colleague. Set out the distinct precise claims it could be making: that infinity is not a natural number; that it is not a real number; that it is not a cardinal or ordinal; that it is not a quantity at all; that it is only a potential process rather than a completed totality; that there are many infinities rather than one; and that the sentence is a grammatical remark about ordinary usage rather than a mathematical claim. For each reading, say whether it is a trivial truth, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false, and say which readings would actually license the two uses to which he put it. Then tell me which reading someone would have to adopt for the slogan to support finitism, and whether that is the reading ordinary speakers of the slogan are likely to have in mind.
