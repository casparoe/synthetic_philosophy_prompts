I'm a novelist, and I've agreed to write a longish piece for a literary quarterly on why the extinction of humanity would be a loss beyond the deaths it involves. The essay is built around Samuel Scheffler's argument in Death and the Afterlife — that what he calls our collective afterlife, the ongoing life of other people after we are gone, is a condition of our valuing anything at all. I've drafted it from memory, working from notes, and I'd like your comments before I commit to the prose. I'm not asking for sources or a reading list; I'd rather be told where I have him wrong and where my own argument fails.

1. The set-up. The puzzle I open with: our own deaths, for most of us, don't drain the point out of what we do, but the death of everyone does. Scheffler's two scenarios — doomsday, a collision thirty years after our generation has ended, and the no-children case, in which we live out our lives comfortably but no one is born after us. His conjecture, as I've written it up, is that our confidence in a human future underwrites our confidence that anything matters, so that the loss is not the sum of goods forgone but the loss of the conditions under which anything counts as a good.

2. My addition, which is the reason I think a novelist should write this essay at all. The novel is the art form of the unverifiable addressee: it is a two-place relation whose second place is a stranger who will outlive the writer, and the form carries a claim about the future in its grammar of address. If that is right, extinction damages the work itself and not merely its would-be readers. I want to claim this is a fourth kind of loss, alongside the deaths and the never-conceived, the goods that would have happened, and the loss of the conditions of mattering.

3. My test case, which I've called the Last Readers. Humanity has two hundred years left, and they are the best two hundred years of reading there have ever been. Against that, a variant of the no-children case in which every book survives and the present generation goes on reading for another fifty years, and then no one. Which is worse, and does either satisfy the conjecture? I think the first does and the second doesn't, but I can't give the reason without circularity.

4. An objection I can't shake: the argument proves too much. If value depends on an indefinite future, every finite future is worthless and my confidence in the finished work was always counterfeit. I answer with a distinction between value achieved and value sustained — the poem written in 1300 is good whether or not anyone reads it now — but I don't know whether that distinction rescues the argument or quietly abandons it.

5. A second objection: the argument may be true of novelists and false of people. It may be occupational narcissism. Someone who values things by doing them may have no stake in a posterity.

6. I notice that the classic illustration of the no-children scenario is itself a novel. I don't know what to do with that.

Please comment in order of usefulness, but starting with accuracy: tell me plainly if anything I attribute to Scheffler isn't his. Then: is 2 a genuine addition or a restatement in literary dress? Which of 3, 4 and 5 is doing real work and which is confusion? Where does the piece sag, and what should I cut? If the Last Readers case is muddled, give me one cleaner version of it. No citations needed.
