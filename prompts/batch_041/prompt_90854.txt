Question 3 (20 pts). In lecture we split "rigidity" into two things, and you have to keep them apart all the way through:

(R) repertoire-rigidity — the behavior is emitted by a fixed, stimulus-triggered routine that runs to completion whether or not it is getting the animal anywhere; change the triggering stimulus and the routine restarts instead of adjusting.

(S) success-rigidity — the behavior keeps missing the agent's own goal, and the agent keeps doing it anyway, with no revision.

(a) [6] Which of R or S does the classic digger-wasp (Sphex) burrow-inspection case, as it's usually told, actually illustrate? Justify your answer. State explicitly what the experimenter has to do to produce the repetition.

(b) [6] Explain why R and S can come apart: give one case of a system that is R but not S, and one that is S but not R.

(c) [8] Dennett and others use the wasp in arguments about free will. Once the case is sorted into R and S, does it support the conclusion that the wasp is not a free agent? Does it support anything about human free will? Say what the case can and can't establish.

Student's answer:

(a) The case illustrates R. The wasp drags the paralyzed cricket to the burrow, leaves it at the entrance, goes inside to inspect, comes back out — and if the experimenter has moved the cricket a few inches while she's inside, she doesn't pick up where she left off; the sequence resets, so she hauls the cricket back to the mouth and inspects again from the top. Fabre's account has this repeating dozens of times and the standard telling says she'll do it forever. To get the repetition you just have to displace the prey during the inspection; the cricket itself doesn't have to be altered in any way. That's why I say R and not S: the routine is keyed to a stimulus (prey at the entrance) rather than to the goal (prey in the nest), and it's the stimulus that has to stay put for the sequence to finish. (You can get the same reset by cutting off the cricket's antennae, which the wasp insists on using as a handle — either way the program starts over.)

(b) R is a claim about the mechanism, S is a claim about the outcome, so they can obviously pull apart. A thermostat is R but not S: it does the identical thing every time the temperature drops, and that's precisely what makes it work — it isn't failing at anything. For S without R, take a gambler who switches systems at roulette every few spins: he's as flexible as you like, never runs the same routine twice, and never gets anywhere. Or a reinforcement-learning agent with a reward function that's mis-specified — it keeps exploring, tries genuinely new policies, and keeps doing the wrong thing. So the wasp is R; and I'd say she's S as well, since the inspection routine never actually gets the cricket into the burrow.

(c) Dennett's point is that if a creature's behavior is just a chain of stereotyped routines triggered from outside, there's no room in it for the self-monitoring that freedom requires, so the wasp is a machine — and since we're made of the same kind of stuff, our confidence that we're different is an illusion. The R/S split doesn't rescue the wasp here, because R is all Dennett needs; he's a compatibilist, so for him freedom is a matter of how the behavior is produced, not whether it's caused. The problem comes when the example is stretched to cover humans. Being describable as sphexish at some level is true of anything — you can describe someone's whole morning routine that way — so "sphexish" can't be doing the work of separating free agents from machines, and the term (which Dennett coined, for what it's worth) is doing rhetorical work, not explanatory work. Keijzer has shown the wasp isn't even R: with enough repetitions she does eventually adapt, so the stereotype is a myth and the case collapses. So the honest verdict is that the wasp case gives us no reason to deny the wasp free will — she may well be a free agent after all, just a small one.
