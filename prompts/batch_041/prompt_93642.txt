I have been reading rationalist and effective-altruist blogs for the better part of a decade, and there is a pattern in my own practical reasoning that I have never found a name for — every nearby label turns out to be wrong in a way I cannot quite articulate, and I would like the concept carved properly rather than simply handed to me. Here is the pattern. When two of my own interests are at stake, I price them off against each other without the slightest difficulty: an hour of mild boredom against a slightly better dinner, a worse commute against a cheaper flat — the exchange rate is unremarkable, roughly proportional, and if you make one side a little worse I shift to the other. But every so often the pricing stops altogether. A friend once asked whether I would accept a small humiliation in order to spare a stranger a considerably larger but still entirely ordinary inconvenience, and my answer was an immediate no; when he pointed out that the same humiliation would spare a great many strangers the same inconvenience, the answer was still no, and it did not move as we multiplied the strangers. Nothing about the humiliation had changed except its quantity, and quantity had stopped mattering. It is not that the rate is bad. It is that past some point the rate ceases to exist, and two things that were trading at par a moment ago are suddenly not on the same scale at all.

Two things sit next to this and may or may not be the same phenomenon. First, the break seems to track who is on which side rather than how much is involved: when both the cost and the benefit land on me, the same two quantities trade off at an ordinary rate, and the moment the cost lands on someone else the trading stops, with the magnitudes unchanged. Second, there are cases that produce the same feeling of a hole in my ordering for what seems like a different reason — I am choosing between two ways to spend the next few years, and I cannot say that one is better, I do not think they are equally good, and I would be irritated if you told me to flip a coin, yet I also would not say that the two are so alien to each other that comparison is a category error. I have seen incommensurability offered as the answer to that second case, and to the first, and it seems to fit neither exactly.

So: is there an established name for the abrupt end of tradeability in the first case, where below some magnitude the two goods are commensurable and above it they are not, with the same two goods throughout and nothing qualitative changing at the break? If it is a known thing, I want its boundaries more than its name: how it differs from ordinary diminishing returns, from a value I simply refuse to trade on principle, and from the second case above. If it has no name, coin one and define it so that it does real work, and tell me which of the two phenomena I described deserves it more. I would also like to know whether the break is best understood as a fact about the values themselves or a fact about my psychology, and, either way, what it implies for the habit of adding up a great many small goods until they are supposed to outweigh one large bad.
