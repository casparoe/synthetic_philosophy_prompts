I'm giving a talk next month at a small decision theory workshop, and I need to prepare for the question period. The audience is about twenty people: roughly half are committed causal decision theorists who work on counterfactual semantics and expected utility, a handful are evidential decision theorists who think the smoking lesion problem is decisive against CDT, and two or three work on causal graphical models and interventions in the Pearl tradition. The thesis I'm defending is that the Death in Damascus problem does not show what it is usually taken to show. In that problem, you are in Damascus, Death has an appointment with you tomorrow, and you can either stay or flee to Aleppo. Death is a perfect predictor of your choice, so if you stay, Death will be in Damascus, and if you flee, Death will be in Aleppo. You die either way, but your choice is evidence about where Death is. The standard reading is that CDT says you are indifferent, while EDT says you should flee because fleeing is evidence that Death is in Aleppo. My thesis is that this reading is wrong: properly formulated CDT also says you are indifferent, and the real lesson of the problem is that decision theory cannot recommend an action when the agent's own decision is perfectly correlated with the state of the world and the payoffs are identical. I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a summary of my talk.

The CDTers in the audience will likely press me on whether my formulation is ad hoc and whether it can handle imperfect predictors; they will want to know why I don't just say that CDT recommends staying, and they will ask about the role of counterfactual dependence when the predictor's accuracy is less than perfect. The EDTers will argue that I am ignoring the evidential value of the act, and that fleeing is rational because it raises the probability that Death is in Aleppo, even if the causal structure is fixed. The causal modelers will ask how I represent the predictor's prediction in a causal graph, whether the agent's action should be treated as exogenous, and whether my account collapses into a form of evidential reasoning under a different name. I also expect a question about self-undermining recommendations: if CDT says the agent is indifferent, does that not mean CDT fails as a guide to action? Please give me the eight or so objections I am most likely to get, ordered by how much damage they would do to my thesis, each phrased the way that particular part of the audience would phrase it, with a two-minute reply for each and a note on where the reply is weakest. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks.

I am particularly worried about two things. First, a CDT sympathizer might object that my view makes the agent's decision procedure irrelevant, because if the agent's choice is perfectly correlated with the predictor's prediction, then no matter what the agent does, the outcome is the same, and this seems to undermine the whole point of causal decision theory. Second, an EDT sympathizer might object that my view is just evidential decision theory in disguise, because I am treating the agent's choice as evidence about the state of the world, and that this is exactly what EDT says to do. I want the prep sheet to help me navigate these objections without conceding the core of my thesis. The audience is sophisticated, so the objections should be in their vocabulary: the CDTers will talk about counterfactual dependence, causal expected utility, and the distinction between acts and predictions; the EDTers will talk about news value, conditional probability, and the evidential role of the act; the causal modelers will talk about interventions, do-calculus, and the difference between conditioning and manipulating. I want to know what to concede up front, what to hold firm on, and the one reply that has to land for my thesis to survive. Please write the prep sheet.
