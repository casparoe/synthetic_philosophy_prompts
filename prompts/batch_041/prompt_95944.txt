I'm a postdoc in a trapped-ion quantum computing group — we cool the ions, run gates, measure, and then at the end of every run we dump the trap; the ions are just gone, maybe a few hundred times a week, and I had never given that a second's thought until about two months ago, when I got drafted onto our institute's responsible-research subcommittee. It's five of us, we meet once a month, we see maybe six proposals a year plus whatever complaints get filed, and our director signs off on everything we write — so it has to be something a physicist can read in ten minutes and a philosopher won't wince at, and my name goes on it either way. Here's the thing that's eating me: one of our philosophy affiliates has filed a formal objection to a proposal we're sitting on. Their claim, roughly, is that if something like panpsychism is true — experience or proto-experience goes all the way down, so there's some tiny "what it's like" bound up with fundamental physical entities — then our experiments aren't ethically neutral, and neither is the boring stuff we do every day. They've started using a phrase for it that's now stuck in everyone's head: an experiment's "experiential footprint." The rest of the committee wants to wave the objection off as absurd, but I spent the summer reading about this and I honestly can't tell weather the objection is deep or just confused, and "we don't find it plausible" is not a procedure.

So what I actually need from you is the procedure: a checklist we can run on a proposal, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the call to a human because the question is about values and not facts. It has to hold up against the obvious pushback — that experience isn't automatically suffering, so panpsychism might have no welfare implications at all; that we have no idea whether micro-experiences ever combine into some subject who could be harmed, or whether they stay unowned and dumb; that "the same entity" may not even be well-defined once you're talking about indistinguishable particles or entangled states; and that a rule which takes all of this seriously might forbid everything we do, including the instruments we'd use to study it. I'd rather it admit where it's guessing than fake a precision it doesn't have. Then run it on three cases: the proposal under review (a decoherence measurement that destroys the very state it's measuring), our everyday practice of dumping the trap after each run, and one case where it comes out as a flat stop — and I'd bet the middle one is going to make me miserable, which is fine, I just need to know. Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do, especially whether it can ever tell the difference between "an entity had a bad moment" and "there was no one there to have a moment at all." No literature review, please — I don't need a reading list, I need something the three non-philosophers on my committee can actually apply.
