I am assembling a diagnostic handout for a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I want it to sort readers by their actual commitments on the question of whether the brain is a computer. Please construct a decision tree for me, in which each internal node is a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and each leaf is a labeled position on whether the brain computes and in what sense.

Fix the starting commitment as follows, since I take it to be shared by nearly everyone in the dispute: at some level of description, neural activity can be modelled as the processing of information, and the model is doing more than decorating a redescription. The tree should begin there and show where readers come apart downstream.

I want the following distinctions encoded as branch points, though you should order them as the logic requires and add any you think I have missed:

First, the triviality gate. Consider the familiar construction in which an arbitrary physical system — a bucket of water, a storm — is mapped onto the transition table of some finite-state machine by an arbitrary assignment of physical states to computational states. If a reader holds that this construction makes the claim that the brain computes no more substantive than the claim that the bucket computes, that is one leaf, and it should be reached by an explicit question about the bucket rather than by naming a doctrine.

Second, for readers who take the claim to be substantive, what the mapping must respect: nothing beyond the existence of some mapping (which collapses back into the first leaf); the causal and functional organization of the system, so that the transitions the computation requires are caused by transitions the system actually undergoes; or, in addition to that, the content of the states — whether they are about anything, for the system itself.

Third, whether the claim is constitutive or explanatory: does the brain compute in the sense that computation just is what cognition consists in, or only in the sense that computational description is the best available model of it?

Fourth, whether computation is medium-independent, so that the same cognitive function could be implemented in silicon, or whether the specific physics of the substrate is doing irreducible work.

Fifth, whether the relevant computation must be digital and discrete or may be continuous and analog, since a reader may accept that the brain computes while denying that it is a computer in the everyday sense.

Use concrete probes at the nodes wherever you can: the thermostat, the abacus and the person using it, a system whose states are matched to a computation's states by a lookup table with no causal connection between them, a trained network whose internal states nobody can interpret, a continuous analog device, the nervous system of a worm. Do not let any node be answerable by asking the reader which label they prefer.

For each leaf, give the name of the position where a standard name exists and mark it unnamed where none does, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Note any combinations of answers that the logical space leaves empty, and say briefly whether they are empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to occupy them. Flag explicitly any pairs of positions my tree cannot separate — I expect at least one such pair to concern the format of the computation rather than the question of whether the brain computes at all, but tell me where the real failures of discrimination lie. Close with a short note on which single node does the most work in dividing the space, and why.

Aim for eight to twelve leaves. The handout should be self-contained: no citations, no reading list, and no reliance on the reader's recognizing a proper name to follow the tree.
