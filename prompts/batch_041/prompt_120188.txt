Drawing Hands All the Way Down

Start with the lithograph everyone knows: two hands, each holding a pen, each drawing the other. Escher's joke is that the picture is impossible, but the deeper joke is that it isn't quite — the hands don't draw themselves into existence, they're drawn by an artist on a sheet of paper that never appears in the frame. Hofstadter's phrase for this, in Gödel, Escher, Bach (chapter 20, I think), is the inviolate level — below every tangled hierarchy there's one, and the tangle only runs because something outside it is holding still.

Gödel's proof is the cleanest case. Arithmetic can make statements about arithmetic only after you've built a coding scheme, numbers standing in for symbols and proofs. The code is boring, mechanical, and completely outside the loop it makes possible. Nobody calls the coding scheme clever. It's just the floor.

My argument is that this is a useful diagnostic for the systems we're now tangling up on purpose, and that we keep skipping the question of whether the floor is real or just painted on.

Take AI training. The loop is models learning from text that models wrote, and then judging it. The inviolate level is supposed to be human-generated text and human judgment — the raters whose preferences the model gets tuned to match. But that level is being colonized: raters work alongside models, synthetic text goes into the training pile, and the human signal thins out or starts to look like the model's own reflection. I don't know how bad this gets. My point is narrower: you can't run the loop without a level that isn't in it, so the interesting question is always where that level is and whether it's still holding.

Science has the same shape. Peer review and replication are supposed to make the enterprise self-correcting, and the inviolate level there is nature — an experiment that can say no. When the currency becomes citations, and the citations are handed out by the tangled system itself, the level gets swallowed and self-correction starts to look like self-reference.

Then the awkward one: a constitution with rules for changing itself. The inviolate level is the people, which isn't an entity sitting outside politics but a fiction that does the work of stopping the regress.

Which is where I get nervous about my own essay. For Escher and Gödel the inviolate level is real and external. For my human cases it's often posited, and positing it is exactly what Agrippa said you'd end up doing: the regress runs forever, or you go in a circle, or you plant your flag. Maybe Hofstadter's insight isn't that there's always a floor but that a tangle can't see its own floor — which would make the flag-planting not a failure but a precondition. I think that's the real claim, but I'm not sure it's his.

So the question I want to leave readers with isn't whether an institution is self-correcting but what its inviolate level is, and whether it's actually inviolate or just pretending. My worry: every institution I admire — science, markets, a free press, and now the labs — describes itself as self-correcting, and each has a level it would rather you not inspect. Maybe the mark of a healthy loop isn't the loop. Maybe it's whether the thing will point at the floor beneath it.
