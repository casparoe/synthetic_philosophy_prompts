I teach a third-year seminar that sits between the metaphysics of causation and the philosophy of responsibility, and I'm putting together the take-home final now. One of the questions has to be on causation by omission, and I want to avoid the two answers I get every year: the counterfactual analysis of causation, plus the objection that if omissions count as causes then infinitely many of them do, plus a shrug. So this term I've been pushing a home-made criterion on them to argue with. I've been calling it the standing-option account. Roughly: an omission counts as a cause of an outcome only if the omitted action was a standing option for the agent at the time -- something she could have done, where doing it required no new decision or fresh undertaking, because the option was already live in whatever she was already doing. Not scanning the pool while you're on duty as a lifeguard is a standing option; not wading into the pond next door while you read in your garden is not, on this account, because acting would have meant taking on a whole new course of action. I'm not sure it survives contact with hard cases, which is sort of the point.

Here's what I want the question to do. It should make them state the standing-option criterion precisely enough to test, and then say whether it's a criterion of causation at all or really a criterion of something else -- responsibility, culpability, blame -- that's been smuggled in under a causal label. Then I want three applications that pull in diffrent directions: (i) a night nurse who skips a scheduled check and the patient dies; (ii) a company that inherits an ongoing discharge of effluent from a predecessor and never installs a filter, where nothing the current management does amounts to a decision not to filter, they just carry on; and (iii) an automated system whose default is to take no action, which is what it does when a sensor never fires, and the resulting inaction floods a town. And then the part I actually care about: I want them to try to produce a counterexample to the criterion -- a case where an omission intuitively causes the harm but was never a standing option, or a case where something clearly was a standing option and the omission plainly isn't a cause -- and to say what the criterion would have to give up to accommodate it. I also want a paragraph on why accident investigators at an engineering firm will happily list an unperformed inspection as a cause of a crash, while a tort court will usually say a pure omission isn't a cause at all, and whether the standing-option account predicts that split or just redescribes it.

Write the question (it should be answerable in about 90 minutes), a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves an A answer has to make and the specific moves a B answer will have instead -- including which wrong turns should cost marks, e.g. treating the criterion as a definition of what an omission is, or quietly re-labelling counterfactual dependence and calling it a day. Also flag anywhere the question is ambiguous enough that I should tighten the wording before I hand it out.
