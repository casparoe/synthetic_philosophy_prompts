I'm doing a unit in my phil of sci seminar on frequentism vs Bayesianism and I want to use the Bem precognition thing from 2011 as the running case: the paper with nine experiments, eight of them hitting p < .05, published in a top social psych journal, then Wagenmakers and co.'s Bayesian reanalysis saying the evidence was essentially nil, then the failed replications a year or two later. Take that whole arc and run it in both directions for me. Be precise about the mapping first: what did the frequentist machinery actually license here, and what should it have licensed once you account for multiple comparisons, optional stopping, and the fact that eight of nine "significant" results is itself a suspicious pattern? Then do the same for the Bayesian side: was the reanalysis a fair reading of the data or did it smuggle in a skeptical prior about psi, and does it matter which, given that the verdict comes out the same either way? I want the minimum-Bayes-factor bound in there (you know the one -- how much a p of .05 can possibly move a likelihood ratio), and I want you to say honestly where each framework is just silent, say about the incentive structure that produced the studies in the first place. Then the interesting bit: what do the failed replications actually do to the analysis? Does the episode vindicate the Bayesians, or does it show that both sides were answering "how strong is the evidence in this dataset" when the question that mattered was "will this come back the same way next time" -- and if the latter, whose framework, if either, was even addressed to that? End with a verdict on what the case shows about the frequentist/Bayesian dispute itself: a confirmation, an anomaly, or something neither side anticipated. Dont let it turn into a p-value-bashing exercise or a Bayesian victory lap.
