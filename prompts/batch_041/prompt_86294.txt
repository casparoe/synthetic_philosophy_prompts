In my epistemology seminar this term we spent the last two weeks on the dogmatism paradox, and for the final paper I have chosen to argue for a verdict on it rather than write another survey of positions. The paradox, as I understand it and as I would like you to address it, runs roughly as follows. Suppose I know that P. Now suppose that evidence E comes to light which, considered on its own, would give me good reason to think that not-P. Since I know P, P is true, and so E cannot be a genuine sign that not-P; it must be misleading evidence. But then it looks as though I am entitled to set E aside without even examining it, and, more generally, to treat the acquisition of knowledge as a licence to ignore whatever later evidence turns up, on the ground that it is bound to be misleading. Knowledge, on this picture, is dogmatic by its very nature, which is a strange result: it suggests that the better my epistemic position with respect to P, the less responsive to evidence I need to be about P. It also appears to refute itself, since if I am entitled to ignore evidence against what I know, I am entitled to ignore the evidence against this very conclusion.

I want the essay to defend one diagnosis and to say why the others fail. Four candidates seem to me worth weighing, and I would rather you engage them as positions than as names attached to them; I am not asking for a literature review, and there is no point in citing work we cannot check. First, the argument may be sound and the conclusion acceptable: knowledge is dogmatic, and this is a feature rather than a bug, because the point of knowing is to close a question. If you take that line, you owe an account of why the appearance of self-undermining is only an appearance. Second, the argument may fail because a knower cannot use her own knowledge as a premise in this way: the step from "I know that P" to "P" may be available from the outside but not to the believer herself as a step in her deliberations, and without it she has no entitlement to pre-empt E. Third, the argument may mistake the level at which the question is asked: what is really at issue is not what a single act of disregarding evidence may do, but which general policy of belief management a believer ought to adopt, and such a policy has to be judged by its effect on inquiry as a whole rather than by the permissibility of one instance. Fourth, the argument may trade on running together two things — what entitles me to form the belief in the first place need not be what keeps me entitled to it later — in which case the paradox shows something about the fragility of retained knowledge rather than about dogmatism as such.

Along the way, the essay should isolate exactly which premises the argument needs (that knowledge is factive; that a knower may rely on what she knows in working out what to believe; and some particular account of what makes evidence misleading) and say which of them could be dropped without losing the paradox. It should also say whether the puzzle, if it bites at all, bites on justified belief and not only on knowledge. Finally, it should state what would have counted as evidence against the verdict you reach. End with a definite verdict and defend it; "each response captures something important" is not an acceptable conclusion.
