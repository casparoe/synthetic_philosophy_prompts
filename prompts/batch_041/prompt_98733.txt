I write about science and ideas for a general audience, and I'm working on a peice about the endurance vs. perdurance thing — whether an object is wholly present at each moment of its life, or is really a four-dimensional entity with temporal parts. To give it some historical texture I went back to Hume's Treatise, and this passage stopped me cold:

"We ought to examine apart those two questions, which are commonly confounded together, viz. Why we attribute a continu'd existence to objects, even when they are not present to the senses; and why we suppose them to have an existence distinct from the mind and perception."

Right after that he says he wants to figure out whether it's the senses, reason or the imagination that produces this opinion, and that "we may well ask, What causes induce us to believe in the existence of body? but 'tis in vain to ask, Whether there be body or not?" I genuinely can't tell whether I'm reading the modern debate back into him. Is his "continu'd existence" the same persistence that endurantists and perdurantists fight about, or is he asking something else entirely — a psychological question about why we believe things persist rather than a metaphysical one about what it is for them to persist? And if it's the latter, does anything in the way he answers end up quietly siding with one camp? I want to get this right for the peice, so please be specific about exactly where the two questions come apart.
