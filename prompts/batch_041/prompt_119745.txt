A colleague and I keep returning to whether large language models have settled the debate over innateness and the poverty of the stimulus. Her position is that the argument was always an empirical wager about the child's data — that the input is too sparse and too noisy to determine grammar — and that since models trained on ordinary text corpora now acquire hierarchical syntax, the wager has been lost and the innate language faculty survives only as a leftover hypothesis. I suspect she is running several distinct claims together, but I can't state the conflation cleanly. What was the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument actually a claim about, and why does the existence of a learner that succeeds on a large corpus fail to bear on it in the way she assumes? I'd like the diagnosis to be specific: say something about how learnability results are always relative to a hypothesis space, about whether the absence of negative evidence and the subset problem are the same point or different ones, and about what turns on the difference between a training corpus and the input a child actually receives from an interlocutor. Then tell me what would change your mind — which finding or argument would show that the nativist position has genuinely been undermined rather than merely restated — and where your reasoning here is most likely to fail.
