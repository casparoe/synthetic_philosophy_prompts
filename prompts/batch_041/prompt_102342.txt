I'm in a reading group on vagueness, and we've spent the better part of a semester on the standoff between epistemicism and supervaluationism, and I've come to think the standoff itself is more interesting than either view. The epistemicist line, in Williamson's version, is that a vague predicate like 'tall' or 'heap' has a perfectly sharp extension, that bivalence holds, and that our inability to locate the cutoff is an epistemic limitation rather than a semantic gap; the supervaluationist program, developed by Fine and defended more recently by Keefe, is that a sentence is true only if true on every admissible way of sharpening the predicate, false only if false on every such sharpening, and neither otherwise, so that bivalence fails while classical logic is preserved at the level of validity. Each side has its stock objection and its stock reply: the epistemicist is asked how the cutoff could be sharp yet wholly unknowable, and answers with margin-for-error considerations; the supervaluationist is asked how the admissible precisifications themselves avoid a sharp boundary, and how, given that every admissible precisification assigns some sharp cutoff or other, the view can avoid making it supertrue that the predicate has a sharp boundary when denying exactly that was the point. We keep re-deriving the objections and re-deriving the replies, and nobody in the room has moved.

To try to break the deadlock I've been constructing a test case of my own rather than recycling the usual sorites examples, and I'd like you to work through it before diagnosing anything. Suppose a community coins a predicate — call it 'glorp' — with an explicit public rule: an object is glorp just in case its mass exceeds a threshold that a future authoritative measurement will fix, a threshold no current speaker knows and that no feature of present usage determines. Here there is, by stipulation, a single sharp boundary, universal ignorance of it, and no tolerance intuition in play at all. I want to know what each side is forced to say about this case, because it looks like a sharp-but-unknown cutoff that is nonetheless not a case of vagueness, and if that's right it pressures the epistemicist to say considerably more than 'the boundary is simply unknowable.' Then I want the diagnosis I actually came for. My working hypothesis, which I'd like assessed rather than assumed, is that the dispute is not really about the data — both sides accommodate the same intuitions about borderline cases by different machinery — but about what a semantic theory is for: whether its job is to assign sharp truth conditions to sentences, which pulls toward epistemicism, or to regiment the intuitive notion of there being no fact of the matter, which pulls toward supervaluationism, with higher-order vagueness and the sharp-boundary objection functioning less as refutations than as symptoms of that underlying difference in explanatory ambition. A competing hypothesis I want weighed is that this is closer to a verbal dispute about the word 'true' and its cognates, or alternatively that it is two camps answering different questions under one heading — one about the semantics of predicates, one about the metaphysics of indeterminacy — and I'd like you to say which hypothesis, if either, the actual commitments of the two views support.

Given whichever diagnosis you settle on, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves the standoff, a formal result that rules one side out, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected here and why. Separate clearly what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus — if your diagnosis depends on a particular interpretation of Williamson, Fine, or Keefe that others would reject, flag it as such. Please write in American English.
