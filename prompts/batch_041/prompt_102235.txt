Two philosophers who have argued past each other for years have agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and you are to draft the joint statement they could both sign. One of them holds the Self-Sampling Assumption; the other holds the Self-Indication Assumption. Work from the following rough characterisations, which the collaborators should sharpen rather than merely repeat. The Self-Sampling Assumption: you should reason as if you were a random sample drawn from the observers in your reference class, so that learning only that a hypothesis entails more observers like you gives you no reason to raise its probability. The Self-Indication Assumption: your existence is itself evidence, so that, other things equal, a hypothesis entailing more observers of the relevant kind is proportionally more probable. Neither position is to be caricatured, and every statement of a view must be one that the side holding it would accept as its own wording.

The document should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can sign, covering what each assumption is an assumption about, how each is meant to be applied to self-locating uncertainty, what the Doomsday argument requires in order to run, and what the presumptuous philosopher is supposed to demonstrate. These claims must be substantive: neither so weak as to be vacuous nor so strong that one side would privately dissent.

2. A table of the disagreements that remain, each entry phrased so that the side it describes would accept the phrasing, and each stated at the level of principle rather than of example.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one of them would move at least one side.

4. For each crux, a case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction, recorded in advance. One of these tests must be the following, which the collaborators have agreed to treat as decisive if they can agree on its resolution.

Calibration Room. An experimenter flips a fair coin. If it lands one way, she creates a single copy of you and places it in a room whose wall is painted, by an independent fair coin, either with a triangle or with a square. If it lands the other way, she creates a very large number N of copies of you, one per room, and each room independently receives a triangle or a square by fair coin. You wake in a room and see a triangle. Both sides agree that the triangle itself does not discriminate between the two procedures; the question is what credence you should now have that the many-copies procedure was used, and what that credence reveals about the two assumptions. Then consider a variant in which, under the many-copies procedure, only a randomly chosen one-in-a-thousand of the rooms is ever opened and its occupant awoken, while under the single-copy procedure the one room is always opened: state whether each side's credence changes, and whether the change arises from the same mechanism under both.

At least one further test must be constructed by the collaboration itself rather than borrowed from the familiar literature.

5. A verdict on whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal, for instance a disagreement about how the word probability should be used for self-locating belief, and, if so, precisely which part, and whether anything substantive survives once the verbal question is stipulated away.

6. A short section on how the dispute fares under a finite universe of unknown total population, under an infinite or open-ended universe, and in the Sleeping Beauty case, and on whether it is at bottom a dispute about credence or about decision theory.

7. A closing note on what, if anything, both sides agree would genuinely move the debate, and what they agree would not.

Write in British English and in a formal register. Do not include citations, reading lists, or attributions; the document must stand on its own arguments.
