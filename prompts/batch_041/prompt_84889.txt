I'm in a reading group on collective action and we've spent alot of the last few weeks on commons and public goods, and we got into a dumb fight at the end of the last session that I can't stop thinking about. My friend Dana says the phrase "tragedy of the commons" is really a claim about victims rather than about incentives: if a commons gets wrecked and no person is worse off, she says, there's no tragedy, just change. I don't think that's right, but I don't have a case that settles it, so I want you to build me one to order.

Here's what the case has to do. Realistic and small — a village, a reef, a pasture, an irrigation ditch, whatever, ordinary people with ordinary motives, nothing sci-fi, no more than a paragraph of setup. In it, a group of users of some shared resource voluntarily agrees to hold back, and the agreement is a real success on its own terms: nobody cheats, there's no expensive policing, no government forcing anyone, no NGO paying anyone off, and the thing they set out to protect actually recovers. Every one of them ends up strictly better off than they were under open access. And yet when you add up the harvesting and the damage across the whole system, it comes out at least as large as it was before, and I'd like it larger. The engine has to be displacement: their restraint tightens supply, and people outside the agreement — acting rationally, not out of spite or ignorance — take up the slack somewhere else. There's a word I've seen for this in stuff about protected areas, leakage I think: you protect one forest and the chainsaws just move down the road. Don't just invoke the word; I want the actual mechanism with actual quantities, and I want to be able to see the counterfactual, meaning: without the villagers' agreement, the outsiders would have taken less.

Then walk me through it. (1) Lay out the insiders' and the outsiders' situations separately and convince me each is a stable resting point — nobody leaving money on the table, nobody exploited, no free-riding anywhere, since I want to be able to say the failure has nothing to do with shirking. (2) Give me the numbers: harvest under open access, harvest under the agreement, and the difference, with whatever you have to assume about prices or entry spelled out. (3) Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, then take it away — no other site available, no common market for the resource, no mobility of effort — and tell me what happens and whether it collapses back into an ordinary commons story. (4) The part I actually care about: try to build it so that no person ends up worse off. Villagers better off, outsiders better off, and if consumers are paying more, tell me honestly whether that's a real loss or a transfer that nets out. If you can't get to no-person-worse-off without something contrived, say so and tell me what the minimal contrivance costs the case. Because if you can get there, then the only thing that comes out worse is the resource itself, and I want to know what that does to the word tragedy — does it name a structure of incentives, or does it need an identifiable loser? If the loser is the fish, or the next generation, or nobody at all, say which, and whether that's enough to earn the label. (5) Finally, give me the strongest objection from someone who thinks the villagers' agreement is an unqualified good — I'd guess it's that I've quietly moved the accounting boundary and their gains are perfectly real — and tell me whether the case survives that or just changes shape.

If some of these can't all be met at once, tell me which one gives and why. American English please, and don't bother with citations or a reading list, I can't look anything up anyway.
