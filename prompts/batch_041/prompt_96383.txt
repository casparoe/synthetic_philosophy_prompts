Take any voting rule that picks a single winner out of a field of at least three candidates and that satisfies unanimity (if everyone ranks A above B, society does too). Then either the rule is dictatorial — one voter's ranking fixes the whole social ranking — or the rule is manipulable: at every profile of preferences, some voter has a report that gets her a strictly better winner than her honest one. The theorem assumes only ordinal preferences: voters rank the candidates, with no cardinal utilities and no intensities. So strategic voting is not a pathology of any one electoral system but a mathematical certainty, and the fact that voters in real elections vote strategically is a kind of empirical confirmation of the theorem. There are the familiar escape hatches, each of which changes the setting: restrict voters to a structured domain such as single-peaked preferences, where the median-voter rule is strategy-proof and plainly not dictatorial; let voters record intensities rather than rankings, as with approval or score ballots, since the theorem binds only rules that consume ordinal preferences; or introduce cardinal utility and transfers, in which case a Groves mechanism will elicit honest reports in dominant strategies.
