I'm putting together a seminar on language and the mind and I want to finish on a film reading, so I've settled on Arrival — the one where the linguist has to crack the aliens' written language and ends up living her whole life out of order. What I want is the film read against the nativist case in language acquisition, i.e. the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument: children supposedly get nowhere near enough data, and nowhere near enough negative evidence, to fix a grammar by general learning alone, so something has to be innate. My trouble is that the film seems to me to place two big empirical bets at once and I can't tell whether they're compatible. Bet one is that a single adult with a notebook, no shared culture, no cooperative native speakers correcting her and no usable negative evidence nonetheless acquires a radically alien language out of a thin, noisy sample. Read one way that's a triumphant demonstration that the stimulus isn't so poor after all; read the other way it's the most extreme poverty-of-stimulus scenario anyone's ever filmed, and her pulling it off shows how much machinery a learner must already be bringing. Bet two is that acquiring the language doesn't just hand her a new code but reconfigures her experience of time, so that past, present and future stop being ordered for her. That's linguistic relativity of a very strong kind, and I'd always assumed it sat badly with the Chomskyan picture, where the language faculty is its own encapsulated module and doesn't get to rewire general cognition. So the film looks like it wants to be maximally nativist and maximally anti-nativist in the same two hours, and I want to know whether that's genuinely incoherent or whether there's a principled way to hold both.

One of my colleagues reckons the whole poverty-of-the-stimulus arguement is really two claims glued together — one about how poor the child's data is, a seperate one about how rich the learner's innate endowment has to be — and that you can't get the second out of the first. If she's right, I'd like the reading organised around that seam: which of the two bets, if either, does the film actually speak to, and does it say anything at all about the second claim? (There's also a bit I half-remember where one ambiguous symbol gets read as weapon and nearly starts a war, which is exactly the sort of thing you'd look at if you were asking how a learner recovers from a misreading with no teacher to correct her — tell me if I've got that scene wrong, or if it isn't really in the film.)

And please tell me where the reading strains rather than smoothing it over. She's an adult learning a second language from nothing, not a child acquiring a first, and I suspect that disanalogy may be fatal to using the film as evidence in either direction — but I'd rather be told than assume it. What I'm really after is what the film shows that the argument alone can't: does dramatising the acquisition change how we should think about the poverty of the stimulus, or is it just a vivid picture of a thought experiment we already had? Don't reduce the film to an illustration of the theory and don't bend the theory to fit the film — if it gets something wrong, say so. Open with your bottom line in the first sentence (can the film coherently have both bets, and which one does it actually need?), then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating of the question.
