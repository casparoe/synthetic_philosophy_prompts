I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar on decision theory and ethics, and I'd like help adjudicating an argument that two classmates had over a case our professor put up. She framed it deliberately so that we couldn't escape into the familiar point about infinite payoffs, and the case is this: you must choose between (A) certainly saving one thousand lives, and (B) an action that, with probability one in a billion, saves ten trillion lives and otherwise saves no one. On expected value, B saves ten thousand lives in expectation (10^13 × 10^−9 = 10^4) against A's one thousand, so the expected-value maximizer takes B, even though B almost certainly saves nobody.

Mara and Devan went back and forth for most of the session. I'll reconstruct it as faithfully as I can.

Mara: The case isn't hard, and our discomfort with it is not evidence of anything. Expected value is the only rule that survives contact with uncertainty, and here it plainly says B — ten thousand lives in expectation against a thousand. Our resistance is scope insensitivity: we can't feel the difference between a thousand and ten trillion, so we substitute a feeling of wrongness for a calculation. Worse, any rule that refuses B is exploitable, because it prefers a small sure gain to a vastly larger expected one, and a sequence of such preferences can be turned into a guaranteed loss.

Devan: The exploitability runs the other way. If you take B-type gambles on principle, then you take all of them, since each is positive in expectation by construction. Accept a thousand of them and you are almost certain to end with nothing; the fanatic who always insists on the largest expected number is the one who reliably ends up ruined, while the person who takes the sure thing ends up with something every time. A rule that guarantees you end with nothing is the one with the problem.

Mara: That conflates expected value with probability of success. A stack of positive-expectation bets is still positive in expectation; that it usually pays nothing is just high variance, and "I dislike variance" isn't an argument until you say what is wrong with variance — particularly when your own preference for the sure thing is itself a risk attitude that can be exploited in turn. By the metric at issue, expected lives saved, your rule simply does worse, and you owe me an argument for your metric rather than an assumption of it.

Devan: I'll drop the ruin point, since you're right that it's about variance, and I'll concede that exploitability cuts both ways. Here is an objection that doesn't depend on variance. The probability in B isn't a probability; it's a placeholder for "we have no idea." No one has a calibrated one-in-a-billion estimate for saving ten trillion lives, because there is no frequency to calibrate it against — it's a guess about a speculative mechanism. Multiplying a guess by ten trillion manufactures ten digits of precision out of nothing. With real, calibrated probabilities I will maximize expected value all day; the fanaticism problem only bites when the number being multiplied is a fabrication.

Mara: Then you've changed the subject. If your objection is that the estimate is unreliable, you've objected to the estimate and not to expected value — and the standard complaint that fanaticism is a reductio of expected value collapses, because expected value was never the culprit. And the same skepticism has to run against A: the thousand lives are certain, but your confidence that B's mechanism doesn't exist is itself an estimate, and if we may discount inconvenient probabilities because we distrust where they came from, we may discount anything we dislike.

I'm not on either side and I genuinely can't tell who is winning. Please rate each of the five moves in turn, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating, and then say who has the better of the argument overall and what both of them are missing. Reason from the case itself; I don't need references. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and without restating the question.
