Okay, I'm definately not a philosopher, so forgive me, but I'm writing a piece on whether any of the old guys would have the faintest idea what to do with the present, and I've gotten stuck on G.E. Moore. My rough sense of him — tell me if I've mangled it — is that the two things he thought were most worth having were the enjoyment of beautiful things and personal affection, basically friendship; that those were good in themselves and not just handy for something else; and that he thought a whole could be worth more (or less) than the sum of its parts, which I think he called an organic unity. Assume that's about right.

Now do me a solid and actually run the experiment: drop Moore into 2025 and have him react, in his own logic at least, to two things he never could have dreamed up.

One: there's a whole industry now of AI "companions" — apps that text you good morning, remember your birthday, notice when you sound off — and people genuinely call them their closest friend; when the company sunsets one, users hold funerals for it. Does that count as the friendship Moore prized, or does the whole thing collapse the instant you notice there's nobody on the other end?

Two: recommenders now clock how long you linger on stuff and then generate an endless feed of images tuned exactly to your taste — beauty on tap, infinitely personalized. Is that just Moore's great good scaled up, or did something get lost once the beauty is manufactured for you and there's no actual object out there that you're genuinely taking in?

And then the part I'd actually run: if he could only keep one of the two — the affection or the beauty — which would he defend, and what would he say he was giving up?
