I'm prepping to teach Galileo's 1615 letter to the Grand Duchess Christina next week and there's one move in it I can't get straight in my own head, so I'd like you to walk me through it. Roughly what he argues (I'm paraphrasing from memory, don't take the wording as exact): scripture and nature both come from God and can't contradict each other; nature is inexorable and doesn't adjust itself to human comprehension, whereas scripture was written to accommodate the understanding of ordinary people; so where scripture touches on natural matters and seems to conflict with a demonstrated truth, we should read it non-literally, and the Holy Spirit's aim was to teach us how to go to heaven rather than how the heavens go. OK. My problem is what this principle actually entitles him to, and when.

Three things I'd like you to untangle for me. (1) Does the accommodation doctrine give you any way, in advance, of telling which scriptural passages are accommodated and which are meant literally — or does it only kick in after the physics is already settled? If it's the latter, isn't the whole argument idle in 1615, when the earth's motion hadn't been demonstrated, and circular when it had? (2) He wants to say scripture doesn't err in its meaning, only in its wording — but doesn't that quietly turn scripture into a book with nothing to say about nature, and would he actually accept that consequence? (3) What stops the principle from becoming a general licence to reinterpret any passage you happen to dislike, which is exactly what his critics were worried about? Also, how much work is the appeal to the Holy Spirit's intention doing — is it a claim about the scope of revelation or about the audience it was addressed to? Please explain the argument step by step and then tell me where you think it's weakest, and whether the weaknesses are Galileo's or just the position's.
