I've been in a reading group going through Michael Dummett's On Immigration and Refugees — apparently the only book he ever wrote on the subject, which is wild given that he was the Wykeham Professor of Logic at Oxford — and it's got me thinking that most of the online open-borders/closed-borders shouting match happens at a level way above the actual arguments. So here's what I want: write a dialogue between two people, both of whom are good at this and neither of whom is a strawman. First, a Dummett-flavored philosopher: someone who thinks states have positive duties to non-citizens and not just negative ones, who thinks a lot of restrictionist rhetoric is racist at bottom even when it's dressed up in economics or culture talk, and who thinks the alleged right to exclude is doing far less moral work than its defenders pretend. Second, a liberal nationalist in the David Miller vein who thinks national culture is a real public good, that people have a legitimate interest in cultural continuity, and that a state can restrict immigration for reasons that have nothing to do with race and aren't just disguised prejudice. Let them go at it.

The part I actually care about, though, is a twist: Dummett spent his whole career arguing (as I understand it) that truth for certain classes of statements can't outrun our capacity to recognize it — the anti-realism thing, truth as knowable/assertible rather than evidence-transcendent. Have one of them press the other on whether any of that carries over here. Is "immigration is changing our culture too fast" the kind of claim that could be true without anyone being in a position to tell? Does the restrictionist need a fairly realist conception of national culture — as something with a determinate character that can be eroded whether or not anyone can see it happening — to get the argument off the ground at all? And if the Dummettian says no, push back: doesn't that just prove too much, since it would also make a bunch of perfectly ordinary social-science claims unassertable? Don't let this turn into an abstract philosophy-of-language seminar though. Keep it tethered to concrete stuff: refugees versus economic migrants, the difference between "we can't take everyone" and "we don't want these people," family reunification, and the weird fact that a state which claims it's totally overwhelmed by asylum claims seems perfectly capable of processing visa applications for rich people.

Wrap it up with the two of them landing on what would actually settle their disagreement — what empirical finding or conceptual concession would move one of them — and make it a real exchange, with interruptions, concessions, and direct replies to each other's actual points, not two monologues stapled together. I've read way too much rationalist blog archive and I can smell a fake dialogue from a mile away, so if one of them is just there to lose, I'll be annoyed. Begin with the dialogue itself — no preface, no framing, no commentary on what you're doing.
