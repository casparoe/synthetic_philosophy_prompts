Write a joke about Émilie du Châtelet's account of happiness. The account, roughly, is this: the desire to be happy drives everything we do, and happiness is more within our control than we like to admit, but it has conditions — you must be free of prejudices, healthy, and possessed of real passions that you put to work for your own contentment, and as you age you should trade the violent passions for gentler tastes. The condition I care about is the last one: she insists we stay susceptible to illusions and preserve them carefully, on the grounds that the person determined to see every object exactly as it is has swapped happiness for accuracy, and that a life with no illusions in it is likely to be calm and miserable. I want the joke to turn on that, not on the mere fact that an eighteenth-century marquise had opinions about joy — so the comic engine should be something like a person who takes up the preservation of illusions as a project, or a doctrine that tells you to shed your prejudices in one breath and guard your illusions in the next. Someone who knows her position should be able to see that the humour comes from the position itself, and it should land without footnotes. Do not put words in her mouth or invent quotations from her, and do not lean on the details of her life. Then, in a few sentences of plain prose, say which parts of her position the joke leans on and where the humour actually lies. Give me all of it as continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables, just the way you would write it in a letter.
