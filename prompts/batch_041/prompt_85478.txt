My friend is a moral philosopher who thinks the only interesting question about an action is what moved the person who did it, and I want to call her verdict on a new case before she gives it. Her record so far — every one of these is a case where the action itself is the right one to perform:

- a shopkeeper who hands back the correct change purely because shortchanging customers is bad for business: no credit
- a man who jumps in and drags a drowning kid out because he simply can't stand to watch it, and never once thinks about what he owes anybody: credit
- a woman who gives away a big slice of her salary because she likes the feeling of being a donor: no credit
- someone who visits a boring sick relative, not because she wants to, purely because she thinks promises have to be kept, and who would still go if it cost her dearly: credit
- the Huckleberry Finn shape — a kid raised to think that helping an escaped slave is a sin, who helps anyway because he can't bear the man's terror, and who believes he's damning himself by doing it: credit
- a woman whose parents set out from her toddlerhood to make her kind, who is now genuinely kind and wouldn't want to be any other way: credit
- someone who helps only when there are people around to see it: no credit

New case: a woman came out of a botched surgery with a chip in her head that floods her with compassion whenever anyone nearby looks distressed, and she acts on it every time. She never chose any of this and it still feels like something happening to her rather than something she does, though she's gotten used to it by now. What's my friend going to say about her?

Lead with that verdict in your first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble, and don't restate my cases back at me. If two of the rules land on the same verdict for the chip case, say which two and give me the case that pries them apart.
