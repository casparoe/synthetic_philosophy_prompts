A friend and I spent most of a long weekend arguing about whether history can explain anything the way physics does, and we parted without settling whether we were even disagreeing about the same thing. I hold the covering-law view: to explain an event is to show that it was to be expected, given the circumstances and the relevant general laws. She holds that historical explanation is narrative: that a historian explains by telling a story, and that the selection, ordering, and connection of events do explanatory work that no set of laws could replicate. Draft the joint statement we should have produced -- a document we could both put our names to, not a verdict for one side.

Give it a numbered section of agreed claims: about what an explanation is for, about what historians do when they explain, about whether they ever state laws, about what the covering-law reconstruction of a narrative would actually look like, and about what our dispute is not about. Then a disagreements table, with each remaining disagreement stated in wording its own side would accept, marked as empirical, conceptual, or merely verbal, and with a note on whether we mean the same thing by law and by explanation. Then the cruxes -- the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us -- and for each crux a case or argument we would both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

Three things must be faced rather than skirted. First, a stipulated case, since neither of us should rely on remembered history: a historian explains why a fishing settlement was abandoned, citing depleted soils, a shift in the fish stocks, an unusually bad winter, and the withdrawal of a distant patron's ships. Give both reconstructions of that explanation and say which parts of it are doing the explanatory work. Second, the objection that any valid argument becomes a covering-law explanation once one adds a conditional whose antecedent is the premises and whose consequent is the conclusion -- so either the model is trivially satisfiable, or the real question is what makes a generalization lawlike, which is a question about laws in general rather than about history. Third, explanations of things that did not happen: why a revolt did not occur, why a plague passed a region by. Say what each side owes here, and whether a narrative can explain an absence at all. If part of our argument turns out to be verbal, identify which part and say what remains of the disagreement once it is dissolved.
