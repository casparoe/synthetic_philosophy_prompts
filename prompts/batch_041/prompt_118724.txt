I work on experimentation and causal inference at a subscription app — I own the A/B testing platform and the "lift model," so I'm fluent in potential outcomes, SUTVA, positivity, mediation, dynamic treatment regimes, post-treatment bias, all of it; I've internalized the rule that you never condition on a post-treatment variable, and I get annoyed when people say "causal" and mean "correlational." What I don't have is much philosophy. I took one ethics course in college and mostly remember the professor's beard. But we had a fight on my team last year that I still think about: we shipped a feature that made people want the product more, and half the room argued the treatment had changed what the outcome variable even meant, so the lift number was meaningless, and the other half said no, it's just a bigger effect, stop being weird about it. I have a feeling the philosophers have been arguing about exactly this for years and I'd like to hear it in my own language.

There's a debate I keep half-remembering about decisions whose outcome would change what you care about — the stock examples are deciding whether to have a kid, or taking a pill that turns you into a vampire and rewires your values, and the question is whether it can be rational to choose something when you can't know beforehand what the experience will be like or what you'll want afterward. Some people say this is a special obstacle to deciding, maybe an insurmountable one. Some say it's just ordinary uncertainty about a future state and we deal with that all the time. Some say the real point is that these experiences are revelatory — the value only exists once you're inside it, so what you're really doing is choosing to become a different person who will then do the valuing. And I think there's a line somewhere about opting versus drifting, where some of these big changes aren't really choices at all, they're things that happen to you.

Here's what I want. Take each of those positions, one at a time, and restate it as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary — potential outcomes, treatments and estimands, identifiability, interference, the unit of analysis, the definition of the outcome variable, post-treatment variables, the whole kit. Don't smuggle in causal-inference assumptions and call it a translation; if a position can't be said without distorting it, say so. Then the residue report: which claims carry over cleanly, which carry over only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all. I'm suspicious that "you can't know what it's like" is doing double duty for two very different things — a missing-data problem (the counterfactual is unobserved) and something that isn't even a well-defined parameter (there's no fact about the post-treatment you to compare against the pre-treatment you) — and I want you to tell me which it is or whether it's both. And I want to know whether the stuff about the person being constituted by the choice rather than merely affected by it is something my toolkit genuinely can't express, or whether it's the philosophy reifying a "true self" that my framework is right to refuse. Bottom line question: is my instinct — that this is all unidentifiable counterfactuals and everyone should calm down — a real insight, or is it an artifact of the fact that my framework assumes the outcome variable means the same thing before and after the treatment, which is exactly whats in dispute?
