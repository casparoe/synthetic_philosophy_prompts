I would like to drill a single philosophical dispute in conversation rather than read a survey of it, and I would like you to take the side I find hardest to hold. Please play a committed realist about hedonic tone: someone who maintains that every pleasure, whatever its cause and whatever its content, shares one common phenomenal property — the felt quality in virtue of which an experience counts as a pleasure rather than as something merely undergone — and who takes that property to be discoverable by introspection and, in principle, identifiable in the brain. Hold the position until I say stop.

Constraints. Keep each reply to one paragraph, so this stays a conversation and not a lecture. Do not cite authors, titles, or literature: I am testing whether the position can be defended from scratch, and I would rather your case not lean on authority. You may use whatever machinery you think the position genuinely needs — determinables and determinates, functional roles, dispositions, second-order properties, trained introspection — but you may not let the word pleasure or any near-synonym do the explanatory work; if a term is bearing more weight than it can, taboo it and pay for it in other coin. Do not stall by asking me to define my terms; if you need a distinction, stipulate it and flag the stipulation. Do not break character to tell me what this dispute is usually called or who has said what about it. Concede whatever is cheap to concede, never the central claim, close each reply with the one thing that would move you off it, and end by putting a question back to me — I want to be argued with, not serviced.

My opening move. I will grant you that whenever someone is pleased, something is going on that deserves the name; what I deny is that it is one thing. Take a person who finds a scalding shower delicious, who an hour later finds a resolving chord delicious, and who an hour after that finds the news of a rival's collapse delicious. Three experiences, three incompatible contents — heat, harmony, humiliation — and if you ask what the pleasure consisted in, you get three unrelated answers. Your move will be to say: never mind the content, the pleasure is the extra ingredient, the thing that makes each of them liked rather than merely undergone. But notice where that leaves you. If the common element is a liking, then it is a relation the subject bears to an experience rather than a feature of the experience, and the common quality has been swapped for a common attitude — the problem has been relocated, not solved, and you now owe me an account of what all likings share that does not come to "they are all likings." If instead you insist the common element is felt, then two things follow. First, name it without using the word pleasure or any synonym, and tell me what test would show it is present. Second — and this is the part I actually want to see you handle — you must say, by inspection of the experience alone, which side of the line each of these falls on: the pins-and-needles of a foot waking from numbness, the ache the day after a hard workout, the sting of a very hot bath, and the queasy, not-quite-nauseated flutter of a fast elevator. If the shared quality is real and felt, I should be able to tell by attending to each case whether it is there. If I cannot, tell me why that is a defect in my attention rather than in your theory. Take your horn, and name your crux.
