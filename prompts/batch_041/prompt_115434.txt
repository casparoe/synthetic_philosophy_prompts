I'm a novelist — no philosophy training, just a lot of reading I've been doing for a book — and I've gotten stuck on something I can't unstick, so I'm hoping you can help me see the shape of it rather than just telling me who's right.

The book has a character who works on machine intelligence, so I've been reading about the Turing test. Here's what bugs me: the argument around it has been running for decades and it feels like nobody actually moves. Turing's line is basically — if you can't tell it apart from the transcript, on what grounds do you refuse to say it thinks. Then there's the whole other crowd: no, behaving as if you understand isn't understanding, the Chinese room, a simulation of a storm doesn't get you wet. And then the first crowd comes back with — fine, but behavior is all you've got with other people too, and anyway the test was never meant to be a definition of thinking in the first place. Everybody's cards seem to be on the table and the whole thing just sits there, year after year.

So what I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict. My hunch — and I'm not attached to it — is that the two sides aren't really answering the same question: one is asking what would justify us in *saying* a machine thinks, the other is asking what thinking actually is, and underneath that there's a hidden disagreement about whether a behavioral criterion is supposed to *settle* the matter once and for all or just give us evidence that could in principle get overturned by something else. Is that the real shape of it? Or is there a genuine disagreement doing the work — about the difference between a criterion and a mere symptom, or about whether 'intelligence' is a natural kind like water or a human-shaped category like 'game,' or about whether the test is even the right thing to keep arguing over?

I want you to actually engage what each side claims, not survey them, and tell me what kind of disagreement this is. Then be honest with me about what would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves the thing, some formal or empirical result, a change of question — or nothing, because the question is badly posed and we should just drop it. And please don't hand me "it's a verbal dispute" unless you can name which word is doing the work and say what would change if we used two words instead of one. Write it in American English, and keep it readable for somebody who isn't in the field — I have to be able to put this in my own mouth at a dinner party without sounding like a fraud.
