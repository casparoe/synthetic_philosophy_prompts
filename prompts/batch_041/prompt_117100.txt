I spent thirty-five years as an experimental scientist before I retired, and for most of that time I took it as given that to understand a domain is to find the general laws that govern it. Since retiring I have been reading moral philosophy, and I have been trying to give an honest hearing to a view that runs against that instinct. Call it particularism: the thesis that there are no true moral principles, because whether a given feature of a situation counts as a reason for or against an action, and how it counts, depends on the rest of the context. On this view no general claim to the effect that a given feature counts in favor of a given course of action is true across the board; the very feature that is a reason to act in one case may be no reason, or a reason to act the opposite way, in another. That an act brings someone pleasure, for instance, is ordinarily a point in its favor, but not when the pleasure is taken in another's suffering.

I drafted a short paper arguing that this position proves too much, and the argument I produced now strikes me as too smooth. I would like you to identify the step I should abandon and to tell me what abandoning it costs.

(1) A moral principle, in the sense at issue, is a general claim to the effect that some feature of a situation — that an act is a lie, that it causes pleasure — is a reason for or against acting in a certain way, in virtue of that feature.
(2) Particularism asserts that for every such feature there is some context in which it is not a reason, or is a reason the other way; no feature carries the same reason-giving force across all contexts.
(3) If no feature carries the same force across all contexts, then no general claim of the kind characterized in (1) is true.
(4) Hence, if particularism is true, no moral principle is true.
(5) But particularism is itself a general claim, and a claim about reasons: it asserts, of any feature whatever, that it is not uniformly a reason.
(6) Therefore particularism is itself a moral principle of the kind characterized in (1).
(7) By (4), if particularism is true then no moral principle is true; by (6), particularism is a moral principle; so if particularism is true, particularism is not true.
(8) Therefore particularism is false.

I am confident that something has gone wrong, because I do not believe that a philosophical position is refuted by the observation that it is a philosophical position. But I cannot find the error, and I would rather not save particularism by sleight of hand. I am attached to (2), which seems to me the honest statement of the view, and I would be reluctant to give it up. Tell me which premise or step to reject, why it looked acceptable in the first place, and what rejecting it costs — in particular, whether rejecting it commits me to the existence of moral principles in some sense the particularist ought to care about, or whether it leaves particularism standing but diminished.
