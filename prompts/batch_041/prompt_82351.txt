I'm finishing a seminar on the French Revolution, and the literature we've been through keeps dividing three ways: the Revolution as the playing-out of a class conflict, the Revolution as the career of an ideology or political discourse that outran the people who made it, and the Revolution as a run of contingent choices and accidents that might easily have gone otherwise. In section last week a classmate cut off an argument about the Terror by invoking the old saying that revolutions devour their children, as if the saying by itself decided the question in favour of the second of those views — as if the Terror were something the Revolution's own principles generated rather than something that happened to it. I don't believe the saying can carry that weight, but when I tried to say why, I found I couldn't separate the several things the sentence might be claiming, and I suspect the argument only needs one of them while the rhetoric trades on all of them at once.

Please lay the readings out for me as a menu rather than settling on one. Push them apart along the joints that matter: what exactly is devoured (the revolution's personnel, its programme, its beneficiaries, the promise it made to the future, its own founding claim to be a rupture with the past); whether revolutions is functioning as a bare plural with a universal reading, a statistical one, or a generic one that tolerates counterexamples; whether the sentence quietly presupposes that a revolution is an agent that can eat, and what survives once that presupposition is dropped; and what would count as a counterexample at all — a revolution that ends in a restoration or a military strongman rather than in a purge, or one that simply fades. Include the readings that are nearly tautological, the ones that are substantive and defensible, and the ones that are false or not even well formed; say which of them are genuinely distinct and which collapse into each other; and add any reading I've missed. I would also like, for each reading, an assessment of which of the three frameworks it presupposes — the thought being that a reading on which the devouring is internal to the revolutionary project favours the ideology view, one on which it is a settling of scores among the losers favours class, and one on which it is bad luck favours contingency — and where that mapping is too quick.

Give me the whole thing as a table I can bring to office hours, with these columns: Reading (a short label); The precise claim (the proposition stated so that it has determinate truth conditions — what it quantifies over, what devouring comes to); Claim type (causal, conceptual, moral, predictive, definitional, or ill formed); Verdict (platitude, substantive and defensible, contentious, false, or not even false, each with a one-clause reason); What would decide it (the kind of evidence or argument that bears on it, given what a historian of the period could actually have); Framework (which of the three views it presupposes, and whether the fit is tight or loose); and Supports the inevitability claim? (yes, no, or only with an extra premise, which you should name). Finish with a short paragraph on which reading the sentence is normally meant to carry when it is dropped into a conversation like that one, and whether any reading is both true and interesting.
