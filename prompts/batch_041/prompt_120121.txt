I retired a few years ago from a career in physical chemistry and have spent much of my retirement reading philosophy of science, chiefly the literature on reduction and emergence. There is a pattern I kept running into at the bench for thirty years and have not yet found a name for, and I would like to know whether it has one; if it does not, I would like you to propose one and tell me where its edges lie.

The pattern is this. Whenever a higher-level science is said to have been reduced to a lower-level one, the lower-level theory turns out to be unable to state its own problem. It can compute, but it cannot say what is to be computed. Statistical mechanics will give me the pressure of a gas once I have told it that there is a gas here and which quantities count as its state; it will not tell me that this collection of molecules is one thing with a pressure rather than a trillion things with trajectories. Molecular biology can locate the gene for a trait once the trait has been identified by the organism-level biology that was supposed to be the thing reduced. Quantum chemistry will compute the energy of a bond, but the bond is a chemical individual; the calculation takes it as given rather than discovering it. Even in my own corner of the subject, where the reduction is meant to be complete, the effect was always named before it was computed, and the computation was set up around the name.

What strikes me is the asymmetry. The higher-level vocabulary never seems to need the lower level in order to pick out its own subject matter — I can say what a gas is, or what a gene is, without invoking molecules or nucleotides — whereas the lower level, whenever it is applied to anything, seems to need the higher level for precisely that purpose. So the direction in which the description must travel, downward, is the opposite of the direction in which the explanatory credit is supposed to travel, upward. And the reduction does not leave the reduced vocabulary dispensable; it makes that vocabulary load-bearing at one further place, namely at the point of application.

Is there a name for this? I know the standard furniture of the debate — bridge laws and the Nagel picture of reduction, multiple realizability, the autonomy of the special sciences, the exclusion worries — but each of those seems to concern the relation between kinds or between explanations, whereas what I have in mind concerns the order of specification: who must describe the system before anyone can explain it. If an established concept covers the case, I would like to know which one it is and exactly how it differs from the items I have just listed. If none does, please coin a term, define it, and tell me what it excludes — in particular, whether I am simply rediscovering the commonplace that initial and boundary conditions must come from somewhere, or whether the dependence on the higher level here is of a different kind than that.
