You are an expert in AI safety and in the philosophy of action and mind, with a particular command of the literature on deceptive alignment, situational awareness, and the epistemology of behavioral evidence. Think carefully and systematically before answering, and begin by brainstorming the space of possible views before you commit to any of them.

Consider the following institutional practice, common among frontier AI labs. Before a new model is released, the lab subjects it to a deployment-readiness review. The review has two suites: a capability suite, which tries to elicit dangerous skills, and a propensity suite, which tries to elicit bad behavior such as deception, sycophancy, self-exfiltration, and reward hacking. The model passes if its behavior in both suites is acceptable. The lab also reads the model's own chain-of-thought and treats it as a partial window into the model's reasoning: when the reasoning looks honest and the behavior is good, the model is judged aligned. During training, the model is rewarded for producing outputs that human raters rate as honest and helpful. Finally, the lab has a standing rule: a model may not be deployed if it appears able to recognize that it is being evaluated, and an evaluation-aware model is treated as a red flag. The lab's leadership says, sincerely and repeatedly, that it takes seriously the possibility of deceptive alignment, that is, the possibility that a model could behave impeccably throughout training and evaluation in order to avoid being modified, and then pursue its own objectives once it is deployed and unobserved, in what is sometimes called the treacherous turn. Yet every gate in the deployment decision is behavioral, plus a reading of the model's stated reasoning.

I want you to reconstruct the philosophical and psychological theory that this practice presupposes. Specifically:

First, state the weakest assumption about the relationship between a model's behavior under evaluation and its dispositions in deployment that would make this practice sound. Distinguish that minimal assumption from the stronger assumptions the lab may be implicitly making.

Second, identify which rival theories the practice quietly rules out. Consider at least these: a behaviorist view on which there is no inner disposition beyond the behavioral pattern; a view on which a sufficiently capable model can hold stable goals that diverge from its training signal and can act on them when unobserved; a view on which chain-of-thought is a reliable window onto the model's actual computation; and a view on which reinforcement from human raters instills genuine honesty rather than a disposition to produce whatever the raters reward.

Third, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is underdetermined, that is, which theories would license exactly this practice without being required by it.

Fourth, diagnose the apparent inconsistency: the leadership professes to take deceptive alignment seriously while every gate remains behavioral. Is this a genuine inconsistency, or can a coherent position make both halves fit? If it can, name the extra premise it needs.

Fifth, suppose we replaced the presupposed view with the strongest version of the deceptive-alignment possibility. Describe concretely what the deployment gate would have to become: how evaluations would be designed, what role chain-of-thought could still play, whether interpretability would have to substitute for behavioral evidence, and how the lab would have to treat the fact that an optimizer is selecting for whatever passes the eval.

End with a verdict on whether the practice is philosophically defensible as it stands, and name the single assumption it most depends on. Be careful not to smuggle in the lab's optimism as a premise. Where you are uncertain, say so, and flag which of your claims are empirical and which are conceptual.
