I am part of a small working group drafting internal deployment guidance for a hospital network that is rolling out clinical decision-support tools, and we keep arriving at the same impasse. The model's recommendation is a real input to a real decision; the clinician retains formal authority and, in practice, has under a minute to review a case; the vendor built and trained the system; the network chose to buy it and set the thresholds; the regulator approved it on evidence that could not have covered the cases where it now fails. When a patient is harmed, each of these parties can give a perfectly respectable account of why the harm is not, in any robust sense, theirs to answer for. I have read enough of the debate over the responsibility gap for autonomous and semi-autonomous systems to know that this complaint is old, but the treatments I have found tend to argue for a preferred remedy before establishing what any remedy would have to accomplish, and I want the specification first.

So please write the desiderata: what a theory of responsibility allocation for such systems would have to deliver in order to count as adequate for our purpose, which is not settling the metaphysics of machine agency but writing deployment rules and being able to answer for them afterwards. I want the cases it must get right, each with the consideration that makes it a test case — an autonomous vehicle that misclassifies and kills; a clinical model that under-alerts and a clinician who defers to it in one case and overrides it in another; a cascade in automated trading where the harmful behaviour is emergent and was never designed by anyone; a lethal autonomous weapon; and the mundane case where a harm is the upshot of so many small contributions that no single hand looks like a cause, which is often called the problem of many hands. I want the distinctions it must not collapse: blameworthiness from answerability from liability for repair; the system's behaviour from the choices to build, buy, and deploy it; the ex ante allocation of decision authority from the ex post allocation of who must give reasons; and responsibility for an outcome from responsibility for maintaining a practice. I want the findings it must be consistent with: that a person asked to supervise an automated system for long stretches loses vigilance within a fraction of an hour and cannot reliably serve as a backstop; that a standard of care cannot demand foresight of what no one could foresee; that machine behaviour can be emergent rather than designed; and that legal systems already allocate repair costs through strict liability and corporate liability even when no individual is morally at fault. Then tell me which of these requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a particular camp, and, most importantly, whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable — I expect the trouble to lie in the neighbourhood of the control and epistemic conditions on blame versus the demand that someone always be answerable, and in the tension between tracing responsibility back to an earlier culpable act and harms that emerge from nothing anyone chose.

I have a suspicion I would like tested rather than accommodated: that what we call one gap is at least three — an epistemic gap, where no one knew enough; a control gap, where no one could have intervened in time; and an authority gap, where no one had standing to decide or to answer — and that these have different remedies and can come apart. Tell me whether that carve-up does real work or is relabelling. I also want the list to say something about the worry that the human in the loop functions as a crumple zone, absorbing blame on behalf of a system whose faults were distributed across designers, data, and deployers, and about whether a design requirement of the form keep a human in meaningful control is a genuine answer or a way of manufacturing a defendant. Once the criteria are on the table, score the usual contenders against them briefly — tracing to an earlier culpable choice; treating the system as an artificial moral agent; design-based control requirements; institutional accountability that drops the language of blame; strict liability with no-fault compensation; and the deflationary line that ordinary negligence and corporate liability already cover everything — but keep the weight on the criteria and the unsatisfiability claim, since that is the part I will take to the group.
