I work on localization at a mid-size software company -- about a dozen languages, and I sit between the translation vendors and the release schedule, so I see how it actually gets made. Two things about our setup have been bugging me and I suspect they're the same thing. First, the contract has to quality bars for post-editing: 'light' post-editing, where the editor is only supposed to fix whatever would stop a user from understanding the string, and 'full' post-editing, where the result is supposed to read as though a human translator had written it from scratch. Different rates, and a couple of vendors have tried to bill us by how little the editor touches the machine's output, so there's a live incentive to leave the machine's wording alone -- smaller edit distance, better margin for them. Second, when a support ticket arrives in a language nobody on the team reads, we run it through the engine and read the English, and nobody has ever once asked whether that English is the same as the original; it's obviously useful, and it's also obviously not something we'd ever do with the EULA, where every word is treated as operative and a human signs off. So in one part of the business machine output is a draft whose meaning is already in place, and in another it's a thing that can't be trusted with meaning at all.

What I want is the philosophical commitments this whole arrangement presupposes, taken seriously rather than waved at. If the post-editor's brief is 'fix the grammar, the terminology, the tone, don't touch the content,' then we're committed to content being something that survives a machine that has no idea what any of it says -- meaning separable from form, carried across intact, with 'light' a real standard rather than a euphemism for shipping it. Is that coherent, and is it the same commitment the 'full' bar makes, or do the two bars quietly disagree with each other? Same question for the round-trip check we run on UI strings -- translate out, translate back, and if it lands close to where it started we call it good. What is that test actually testing, and what does it assume about translation being invertible? And if that picture of meaning is wrong, tell me what in the workflow actually breaks, versus what survives fine on some other view -- I'd rather know which parts are load-bearing than get a verdict on the whole thing.

Push back on me here too. This started because of Hofstadter's writing about machine translation, the argument that it doesn't understand anything and that's why the output is shallow, and I honestly can't tell whether 'understand' is doing real work in that argument or is just a label we reserve for people. Does the shift from the older phrase-based systems to the neural ones change anything, or is the shallowness point orthogonal to whatever the engineering is doing that year? And tell me which parts of this have a settled answer among people who actually study meaning and translation and which are live fights -- if the field is split, say so and say roughly how, and dont hand me one side of a contested question dressed up as the consensus.
