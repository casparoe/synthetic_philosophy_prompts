I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm deep in a feature about the centipede game — the one where two players alternate passing a pot that grows each round and either of them can take at any point. I've been arguing about it for weeks with a friend who teaches formal epistemology, and she wrote out her case for why a rational player takes on the very first move as a numbered arguement on the back of a reciept. I keep staring at it. Here it is, cleaned up but otherwise hers: (1) In the game the players move in turn; at each node the player to move can take the share that's accumulated so far, or pass and hand the decision to the other player. (2) At the final node, the player to move does strictly better by taking than by passing, so she takes. (3) Both players know the structure of the game and that the other is rational. (4) So at the node before the last, the player to move can see that passing leads to step 2, which is worse for her than taking right now, so she takes. (5) The same reasoning applies at every earlier node, so by backward induction the whole thing unravels and the first player takes immediately. (6) Nothing in the argument depends on how long the game is or on the particular payoffs; a hundred nodes or a million, it comes out the same. (7) Therefore the uniquely rational thing to do is to take at move one. (8) Yet when people play this in labs they pass for a while, and the passers often walk away with more money than the immediate takers — and a theory of rationality that predictably leaves you worse off than a naive alternative seems like it's missing something. Assume I already know the textbook line about subgame-perfect equilibrium and the usual nod to common knowledge of rationality; I don't need that restated, I need to know whether this argument as written actually earns its conclusion.

So: as someone who knows both the game theory here and the philosophy of rational choice literature, tell me which numbered step to give up, and then price it honestly — what that step looked like it was doing, why it seemed innocent, and what else falls if I drop it. Does the diagnosis change if I swap in the versions I'm actually thinking about for the piece — one where the two players are the same person at two different times, so that the premise about both players being rational becomes the claim that I'm rational at every stage, and one where a player can burn some of her own payoff to credibly commit to passing for k rounds? And if the culprit turns out to be the common-knowledge assumption, does the argument still go through when there's even a tiny chance the other player is irrational, or does it only unravel under full common knowledge?
