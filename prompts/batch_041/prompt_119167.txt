I've arrived at a position about moral luck that I'd like you to press on rather than defend. It has two parts. First, responsibility is the attributability of an action to an agent's quality of will, and the quality of will is fixed at the moment of choice; outcomes and circumstances therefore cannot make an agent more or less responsible, and the Control Principle — the claim that one is morally assessable only for what lies within one's control — is not a constraint on responsibility itself but at most a norm governing our responses to it. Second, the outcome-sensitivity of our actual responses is not a mere irrationality to be apologized for: differential blame is defensible insofar as it amounts to updating on evidence about what the agent's choice revealed about her, and indefensible only where the outcome carries no such evidential value.

What I want is a map of this view's exposure. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and distinguish what a committed defender would concede from what she would dismiss as question-begging. Four pressure points interest me most.

The paired cases. Two agents make the same choice under the same epistemic conditions; in one world a child is killed, in the other nothing happens. By construction the outcome carries no information about the will. If our reactions still diverge, the evidential defense has nothing with which to justify the divergence — so is the view committed to a large-scale error theory about blame, and does that amount to collapsing into a rival rather than defending this one?

Luck that enters through the will rather than around it. Ignorance excuses, and ignorance is often itself a matter of unchosen circumstance; if the content of the will can be fixed by luck, does the first part of the position survive, or does the problem simply migrate from outcomes to character?

The first-person case. The agent who was blameless but whose choice caused grave harm seems rationally required to feel a distinctively self-directed form of regret that no one else need share. Is that a moral assessment of her, a non-moral residue, or something that breaks the two-level picture?

The institutional application. Suppose we adopt a bifurcated regime: criminal liability tracks culpability alone, so that a completed harm and a lucky near-miss are treated alike, while civil liability tracks harm caused regardless of fault. What does this solve, what does it merely relocate, and what would count against the claim that it is the correct institutional expression of the position above?

Finally, tell me whether the position predicts anything its rivals do not — something checkable in ordinary judgments, in legal doctrine, or in experimental work on blame — or whether its two levels have made it unfalsifiable, and whether that is a cost or a virtue.
