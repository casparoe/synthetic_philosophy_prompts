I teach a ninety-minute session in a two-week residential summer programme in philosophy. Fourteen participants, mixed levels, no set reading: about half live in the city where the programme is held, the other half are visiting from elsewhere, and I want to make use of that fact rather than treat it as incidental.

The idea I want them to feel is the difference between a local custom that is merely how things are done here and one that has hardened into something you owe people — and then, harder, the position of the visitor, who is subject to both their own customs and ours. I want them to arrive at three claims themselves, in roughly this order: that what separates the two kinds of custom is not the content of the custom but whether a person can decline it in a way that others can read; that the visitor's felt obligation to conform is produced mostly by their illegibility to the locals rather than by anything about the custom itself; and that the locals' sense of entitlement to the visitor's conformity is, on inspection, a claim about being able to predict and be understood, not a claim about harm. None of this should be said out loud before the debrief.

Build the activity on a customs sheet I will hand out for a fictional host city. Three customs on it. In cafés a service charge is already included, and leaving extra money is read either as a comment on the server's wages or as a bid for better treatment, so locals do not tip. At the tram stop there is no physical line, but boarding is strictly by order of arrival, tracked by eye contact, a half-step forward and a small nod — legible to locals, invisible to visitors, whose mistakes are read as rudeness rather than as ignorance. Crossing against the signal is unremarkable and locals do it mid-conversation, with one exception: nobody crosses against the signal when a small child is waiting at the curb. You may add at most one further custom of the same mundane kind if the activity needs it, and I would rather you did not add more than one.

Two things I want the activity to make participants do rather than discuss. First, for each custom on the sheet they should have to work out what a person would actually have to do to opt out visibly — what it would cost, who would notice, whether the opt-out would be read as an insult, an imposition, or mere difference — and then compare what happens when one person opts out with what happens when everyone does. Second, at some point a participant must choose in front of the others with something small but real at stake, so the choice is not hypothetical; points, turns, or standing in a queue will do, but no money.

Give me: the roles or rules; the materials, including how the customs sheet is introduced without lecturing; a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes; the stake scheme; and a phase late in the session in which the visitors have become the numerical majority and the group has to decide whether the locals still have any claim on them. Then the debrief, which should map particular moments from the run onto the three claims above rather than restating them in the abstract, and should tell me what the activity genuinely shows as opposed to what it merely dramatises. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — including what happens if the participants are too polite to break the customs, or if the visiting students simply defer to the locals — and how I should handle each case. Be explicit about which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce at all and that I will have to supply myself.
