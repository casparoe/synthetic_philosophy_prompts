I've been on the health desk for about a decade now, before that three years as an ER nurse, so I know the clinical pain vocabulary cold: 0-10 scales, the faces chart, nociception vs suffering, PRN orders, functional goals, charting what the patient reports next to what the patient actually does, the whole pain-as-the-fifth-vital-sign mess that we all got yelled at about later. What I never studdied is philosophy of mind, and that's the gap I need you to bridge.

Here's the thing I want to write about. There's this neurological condition where people will tell you, yes, that hurts, they can say where and how intense it is, but they don't pull away, they don't seem bothered, and in some of the case reports they laugh about it while it's happening. The sensation is intact, the awfulness is gone. Philosophers have used this to argue about what makes pain bad in the first place. One side says the badness is baked into the feeling itself, so if the awfulness is gone the pain must be gone too. The other side says the badness comes from a separate part, something like an alarm that tells you this is happening to you and you need to get out of it, and that part can go missing while the hurting stays.

What I want: restate that debate as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use at work, camp by camp, one at a time. The two-dimensions-of-pain stuff, the rating scales, triage, what gets charted and what never gets charted, the difference between a report and a behaviour. Then the residue report. What of the philosophers' debate comes through that translation clean, what has to be bent to fit, and what is simply unsayable in my vocabulary. Where it's unsayable, tell me honestly whether that's my vocabulary being too coarse for the question or the philosophers' question being vaguer than it sounds. And one last thing: in my vocabulary, is this condition even a distinct thing, or does it just look like a patient who's under-reporting? Skip the citations, I need the seams.
