I teach a ninety-minute epistemology seminar to twelve graduate students, and the session is on Aumann's agreement theorem. All of them can state the result — that agents who share a prior cannot agree to disagree once their credences are common knowledge — and I am fairly sure that for most of them it is a sentence they have memorized rather than a constraint they have felt. What I want from you is a session in which they run into the constraint themselves, while they are busy doing something else, before I name it.

I have the bones of a setup and I want you to build the session on it, though you should tell me if the bones are wrong. A fair coin will be flipped out of sight. Each student is privately shown a peek at the outcome, produced by a source that is right two times in three and that is independent across students; a student whose peek says heads therefore holds a credence of two-thirds that the coin is heads, and one whose peek says tails holds one-third. Each knows the accuracy of the source, and knows that the others know it. Nobody sees anyone else's peek. I then want to post even money and let them trade the heads side against the tails side, peer to peer, with a payoff scheme that uses no real money but gives a student with a two-thirds credence a genuine reason to want the bet and a student with a one-third credence a genuine reason to take the other side. My expectation is that most opposed pairs will trade, cheerfully and with conviction.

The floor should then fall out from under them. Once a pair reveals its two peeks, the posterior given both is one-half exactly — the two edges cancel — so the bet they were so pleased with was fair all along, and the willingness of a counterparty to take the other side was itself information they had been ignoring. I want that arithmetic to be done by the students, not announced by me. Then I want a second act: run the market again with the peeks public, and let them discover that nobody will trade. Then a third act in which they are invited to try to sustain a disagreement anyway, once everything is common knowledge, and to notice what they must assume about the other person — that he is not reasoning correctly, or that he holds different priors — in order to hold their ground.

One thing you must solve rather than paper over. Only an opposed pair cancels to one-half. Across the whole room the peeks will usually not balance, so if I reveal every peek at once the room's aggregate posterior will sit somewhere other than one-half, and the clean pairwise cancellation will be swallowed by a messier number. Decide how to stage the reveals — pair by pair and then the room, or some other order — so that the cancellation lands cleanly for the pair while the room still ends in agreement, and say why your ordering works.

Please give me the whole thing as a runnable session: the rules, the materials, how the peeks are physically delivered, the sequence and timing across the ninety minutes, the betting and payoff scheme, and how the reveal is staged. Then the debrief, mapping each beat of the session onto the parts of the theorem — the common prior, common knowledge, and the fact that it is the credences' being common knowledge that does the work — and tell me which part of the theorem the session genuinely makes them feel and which part it merely lets me assert.

Be honest about what will predictably go wrong the first time — the students who refuse to gamble, the pairs whose peeks happen to agree, the one who sees the cancellation coming and spoils it, the ones who cannot do the arithmetic — and how I should handle each. And tell me what the session cannot reproduce: I am especially worried that the setup builds the common prior in by construction, so they will feel the cancellation without ever feeling why the shared prior is load-bearing, and I want your advice on that gap.

Finally, give me one variant in which a participant has no peek at all and is willing to bet anyway for reasons that have nothing to do with information, and tell me what that variant teaches that the main session does not.

Write the whole answer as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you would in a long letter to a colleague.
