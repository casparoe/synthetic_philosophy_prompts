I've been reading rationalist blogs and hanging around LessWrong-adjacent forums for the better part of a decade, and for the last six months a small discussion group I'm in has been circling the same question without moving. I'd like you to diagnose the disagreement rather than settle it. The topic travels under a few names — acausal trade, multiverse-wide superrationality, evidential cooperation in large worlds — and the core claim, as I understand it, is this: if the world is large enough to contain other agents whose decisions are correlated with mine (because we run the same decision procedure, or because my choice is evidence about theirs in the way one copy's choice is evidence about its duplicate's), then I can cooperate with them despite having no causal contact with them — taking the cooperative move in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma against an unknown other player, or treating my choice of charity or career as evidence about what other value-aligned agents do. The proponents in the group say this falls out of taking evidential or functional decision theory seriously together with a sufficiently large universe, and that the evidential payoff swamps every ordinary causal consideration. The skeptics say the correlation is either stipulated into existence or is a fact about the world we have no way of learning, that the argument has to first win the cases where evidential decision theory looks absurd — Newcomb, the bet whose payoff is determined by the past state that caused you to make it, the button that would rid the world of psychopaths given that only a psychopath would press it — and that even if you grant acausal influence, we are so clueless about the sign and magnitude of the effects that it cannot guide anyone's action. There is also a running side-skirmish about whether the view requires abandoning a certain picture of the self as bounded, and whether that abandonment is a cost or a liberation.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the two sides are not really disagreeing about decision theory at all. It seems to me that the proponents are asking what a specified decision theory recommends under specified assumptions, while the skeptics are asking whether anyone could be justified in acting on it, and that those are different questions wearing one name. But I can also see a reading on which the real dispute is a hidden formal one — whether, once you actually write down a model of a large world with correlated agents, evidential or functional decision theory endorses cooperation or does something else entirely — with the rest being noise generated by conflicting intuitions about the puzzle cases. And a third reading, which I find least comfortable, is that the two sides simply start from incompatible bedrock intuitions about whether non-causal considerations can be reasons at all, in which case nothing either side says could ever tell.

What I want from you is the diagnosis: is this a verbal dispute, a substantive one, two questions under one heading, a factual or formal disagreement in disguise, or a clash of starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Ground it in what each side actually says, not in a general survey of decision theories, and be concrete about which specific move in the exchange is doing the load-bearing work. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis — a formal result, a distinction, a specification of the model, a change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected here and explain why. I don't need citations or a reading list; I want the structure of the impasse.
