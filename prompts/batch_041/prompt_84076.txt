Judy Benjamin is dropped somewhere in a region split into Red territory and Blue territory. Each territory is itself split into a Headquarters area and a Second Company area. Judy has no idea where she is and assigns equal probability to all four possibilities. She then gets a radio message saying that if she is in Red territory, the odds are three to one that she is in Headquarters. Assume the message is reliable. (a) What should her new probability of being in Red territory be? (b) Why doesn't Bayes' rule apply directly here? (c) What should she use instead, and what's the philosophical upshot?

The student wrote:

Judy's prior is uniform over the four cells — Red-Headquarters, Red-Second, Blue-Headquarters, Blue-Second — so each gets 1/4. The message tells her that the probability of Headquarters given Red is 3/4: if she's in Red, Headquarters is three times as likely as Second Company.

Bayes' rule doesn't apply directly, because she doesn't learn any proposition outright. Nothing is ruled out with certainty, so this is a case of updating on a conditional rather than on an observation.

The fix is to conditionalize on the propositional content of the message, which is the material conditional: she is not in Red, or she is in Headquarters. Conditionalizing on that eliminates the Red-Second-Company cell, and renormalizing the other three cells gives each 1/3. So her new probability of being in Red is 1/3.

This is basically van Fraassen's minimum-information solution: pick the posterior closest to the prior, and since the prior is uniform, keep the surviving possibilities as even as possible. So the upshot is that Bayesian updating can be stretched to cover conditional information, and the answer is 1/3.
