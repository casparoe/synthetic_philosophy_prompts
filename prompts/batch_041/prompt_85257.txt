I'm a doctoral student in political theory putting together a session for a graduate seminar on political liberalism and the place of religion in public justification. My students can all recite the stock formulas — that religious arguments are admissible provided adequate public reasons are eventually supplied, that religious citizens should not be asked to check their convictions at the door — but they cannot say which underlying commitments commit them to which position, and I suspect several of the labels they bandy about are doing no real work. Please build me a decision tree that carries a respondent from a handful of starting commitments to a named position on whether religious reasons may figure in the justification of coercive law, and if so how. I want the tree grown from the logical space rather than from a roster of names: give me the branch questions first, each phrased as a claim or a concrete case that a respondent can answer without already knowing which position the answer leads to, and then locate the actual positions within that space. Set it up for an audience of philosophy graduate students, and keep it to roughly eight to twelve branch nodes and six to nine leaves.

The distinctions I care most about — and I want the nodes to turn on these — are the following. First, whether the demand for public justification is a demand for consensus, on which a single reason must be shareable by all those bound, or only for convergence, on which each person may act from their own sufficient reason and no shared reason need exist. Second, whether the constraint falls on the content a law's justification can bear, on the conduct of the citizens and officials who argue for it, or on both at once. Third, whether the constraint binds ordinary citizens as strictly as it binds legislators and judges, or binds the latter more tightly. Fourth, where a religious reason is offered, whether the requirement is that it be accompanied by an independently accessible reason, replaced by one, or translated into one. A few views I specifically want to appear as leaves: a strict exclusion view that bars religious reasons from public justification altogether; a proviso view that admits them provided adequate public reasons are supplied in due course; a translation view; a convergence view that denies any shared reason is required; and an inclusionist view on which citizens may argue from their deepest convictions without a public supplement.

Where the branches generate combinations that no one actually defends, say so, and diagnose each: is the combination incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored? Where two leaves come out indistinguishable under your questions, flag them as such rather than letting a branch pretend to separate them. Name each leaf with the standard label for the position and add one sentence on the commitment that would sit most awkwardly for someone who lands there. Finally, be explicit about which of these distinctions are settled points of agreement among people who work on public reason and which are themselves live disagreements, so that I don't mistake a contested position for the consensus.
