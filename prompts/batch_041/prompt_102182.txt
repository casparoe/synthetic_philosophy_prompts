I teach a seminar on constitutional theory and I keep bumping into the same move from a colleague over in the law school, and honestly it's starting to bug me. Whenever someone presses him on why a constitution should be allowed to bind a later majority — why it isn't just the dead hand of the past, why entrenchment doesn't insult democracy — he shrugs and says: it's just a Ulysses contract. The people, or the founders, or whoever, know they'll be tempted later, so they tie themselves to the mast in advance; that's all a constitution is, and nobody thinks Ulysses was being oppressed by his earlier self. He deploys it to make the whole thing sound settled, almost boring, as if the only remaining puzzle is why anyone ever worried. I suspect the analogy is doing more work than he admits, and I'd like you to audit it.

Lay out the correspondence explicitly — who or what plays Ulysses, the crew, the mast, the ropes, the sirens, the wax, the original decision to be bound — and be upfront that the mapping gets slippery fast, since a polis isn't a single agent with one will and one memory. Then tell me where it actually breaks, and I mean the breaks that bear on the job he's making the analogy do. A few I'd want you to at least consider: an individual can in principle be released from his bonds or come to regret the whole arrangement and still be stuck with it, whereas the bound party here never consented and may not even be the same person who did the binding; there's the question of who gets to decide what counts as a siren versus just ordinary contested politics; and there's the suspicion that a constitution is less like one dramatic decision at the mast and more like a standing crew that keeps re-tying the ropes as it goes. Seperate the disanalogies that genuinely matter for his conclusion from the ones that are merely colorful. And then the real question: once all that is on the table, does his conclusion survive — is entrenchment still vindicated by the Ulysses story, or was the analogy quietly carrying the arguement the whole time? If there's a better analogy for what a constitution is actually doing, tell me what it is and why it beats his.
