I'm part of a small comparative-philosophy reading group — four early modernists and one historian of Islamic thought — and we have just spent three sessions on the seventeenth discussion of al-Ghazālī's Incoherence of the Philosophers, the one that denies the impossibility of a departure from the natural course of events and turns on what happens when cotton touches fire. Next week is our last meeting, and I have been asked to open it with a short poem and then say a few words about why I made it the way I made it. My first attempt was a sonnet, and it was wrong for the material: a sonnet turns, and this doctrine does not turn; it does not even advance. What I want is a poem whose form is the doctrine rather than a poem that mentions it, and it should stand on its own for the two members of the group who skimmed the reading.

The claim I need the poem to enact is narrower than people usually suppose. Al-Ghazālī is not saying that fire never burns cotton; he is saying that the burning is not necessitated by the fire, that observation ever shows us only one thing alongside another and never one thing by means of another, and that the agent of the blackening is God — directly, or through whatever intermediaries He pleases — while the fire itself, being inanimate, does nothing. So: six quatrains, twenty-four lines, in which the fourth line of every quatrain is the same refrain, repeated verbatim and never altered, not even in the final stanza; eight words or fewer, a complete sentence in the present tense, so that each repetition reads as a fresh act rather than as a description of a standing state. The first three lines of each quatrain must rhyme on a sound that appears nowhere else in the poem, so that the refrain's sound is the only sound that ever comes back. Every line must be end-stopped; nothing may spill across a line break, and no image may carry from one quatrain into the next, because in this poem nothing carries over. And there must be no connectives of any kind — no causal ones (because, since, therefore, so, thus, hence, causes, makes, produces, brings about, leads to, necessitates) and no adversative ones either (but, yet, however, though, except). The sequence of events is to be pure juxtaposition, so that a reader has to infer the pattern the way we do, by watching it recur.

Two further things. Somewhere in the poem there must be the case where the sequence fails — the body put into the flames and coming out unburned, with the fire still fire — and it must be given no formal sign whatsoever: same refrain, same freshness of rhyme, no wink at the level of the stanza. If that stanza gets a different grammar from the others, the poem has misunderstood the doctrine, since on this view the exception is not a stranger sort of event but the same sort of event, differently willed. And the refrain should be the only line permitted to name the giver, and it should not say by what means the giving is done, since al-Ghazālī leaves that open. Keep the diction plain and physical — no cause, effect, occasionalism, concomitance, necessity, custom, no Hume, no Malebranche — and give the poem a title; it will be read aloud, so it should run to about ninety seconds. Please give me the poem first, then a single section headed Note on the form, roughly two hundred words, explaining what each constraint is doing and identifying the stanza with the unburned body. No citations and no bibliography: I want everything the poem needs to be inside the poem.
