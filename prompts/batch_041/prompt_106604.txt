Last night a friend and I argued for the better part of three hours about whether Gödel's incompleteness theorems have anything to tell us about the limits of minds and machines, and we parted without either of us giving ground. His case runs roughly as follows. Take any consistent formal system strong enough to encode arithmetic: there will be a sentence it can neither prove nor refute, and yet a human mathematician can recognise that this sentence is true. It follows, he says, that no such system — and hence no machine whose reasoning can be formalised in one — can do what we do, and that there is an in-principle barrier to building a machine that could fully certify its own correctness, trust its own proofs, or safely rewrite its own code. He works on software verification, so his worry is concrete rather than mystical: he thinks no program will ever be able to verify its own safety, and that every self-improving system is therefore a leap of faith dressed up as engineering. My reply is that the argument moves far too quickly, but when I try to say exactly where it goes wrong, every objection I raise turns out to have been anticipated and folded into his premises, and we end up trading intuitions about what a machine, or a proof, really is.

I want a diagnosis of this disagreement rather than a verdict on who is right. Is it a substantive dispute, a verbal one that turns on the words machine or know, or are the two of us quietly answering different questions — he about what can be proved inside a formal system, I about what an engineered artefact can be made to do — under a single heading? Is some hidden empirical or formal claim doing the real work: that human mathematical reasoning is consistent, that it is effectively formalisable at all, that it is infallible about the relevant sentence, or that the theorem even applies to systems which are not recursively enumerable? Does the argument survive if the system in question is consistent but unsound, or if it reasons inductively rather than deductively? And is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, or merely that it is hard?

My own hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than assume, is that we are not disagreeing about mathematics at all but about what it is for a system to trust itself, and that the theorems are being asked to carry a conclusion they cannot reach without extra premises about the relation between provability and warranted belief. If that is right, say which premises are doing the work and whether they are plausible; if it is wrong, say where the real crux lies. Finally, tell me what would actually count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or whether none should be expected, and why.

Please answer in British English.
