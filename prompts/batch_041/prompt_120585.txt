You are a philosopher of moral psychology with a background in anthropology and the history of ethics, and you are scrupulous about the difference between a distinction that does explanatory work and one that merely relabels a folk contrast. I need a decision tree, not an essay: a taxonomy of the positions on two linked questions — whether guilt or shame is the better moral emotion, and whether a culture can choose which of the two it runs on. Work from the logical space rather than from a list of names: brainstorm the candidate positions first, check the combinations for coherence, then build the tree.

Two stipulations shape the instrument. First, treat the guilt/shame distinction as four potentially independent axes — the object of the emotion (a wrongful act versus the self as a whole), the audience (one's own judgment versus the gaze of others), the action-tendency (reparation and confession versus withdrawal and status-repair), and the source of the norm (an internalized standard versus others' expectations). No branch may presuppose that one of these is the essential one; the earliest branches should ask which axis the respondent treats as doing the real work, since that is where the positions genuinely diverge. Second, do not let the familiar contrast between shame cultures and guilt cultures serve as the tree's backbone; include it as one contested position inside the tree.

Phrase every branch node as a question answerable without already knowing which leaf it leads to, and prefer concrete cases to abstract theses: someone who lies on a CV and privately resolves never to do it again; someone publicly exposed for an old racist joke; a restorative-justice conference between a burglar and the person he robbed; a wrongdoer who feels nothing about the wrong but is distressed at being thought badly of. Use these or better ones.

Aim for about ten leaves, and make sure the tree reaches: guilt is better because it targets acts and prompts repair; shame is better because it binds the self to others while guilt is too easily discharged by restitution; the two do different jobs and neither is better; both are corrupting and the ideal person needs neither; the distinction is not real; a culture can choose its repertoire through institutions and ritual; a culture cannot choose it; and a culture could choose but would lose something irreplaceable in doing so. Give each leaf its position name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pairs of leaves the tree cannot separate and say why, and note which combinations of answers are unoccupied, diagnosing each as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

No citations or reading lists — I want the structure, not the scholarship. Keep it to roughly two pages of handout.
