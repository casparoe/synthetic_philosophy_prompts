I write a column on science and ideas, and I am at work on a piece about expert disagreement: the kind that persists between people who respect one another, read the same literature, and have nothing material riding on the answer. Before I file it, I want to know whether my own reactions to a set of cases add up to a principle, because at the moment they seem to pull in two directions at once.

I have been reading about Aumann's agreement theorem. As I understand it, two agents who start from the same prior probabilities and update by Bayes's rule cannot agree to disagree: if each agent's posterior probability for some event is common knowledge between them — each knows the other's number, knows that the other knows, and so on up the hierarchy — then the numbers must be identical. Disagreement that survives has to come from somewhere else: priors that were never shared, a failure of common knowledge, or a difference in how the two agents carve up what they know. I gather that in these models, if the two simply keep announcing their numbers to each other, they are bound to converge. I do not want a proof and I do not want a reading list; I want the principle, or the several rival principles, that my verdicts amount to.

Here are the cases, with my verdicts.

1. Two economists with the same training look at the same inflation data and forecast different numbers for next quarter. I think one of them has slipped up somewhere. The disagreement is a symptom; it is not itself a fact about the world.

2. Same two, except that the gap traces to how much weight each puts on a price relationship that held before 2020. Neither has slipped up, and I would not be surprised if they never converge.

3. A colleague I respect disagrees with me. I move a little; when I tell him I have moved, he moves a little; we end up somewhere in between. I think that is exactly the right behaviour, and I would distrust anyone who either refused to budge or collapsed at once to the other's view.

4. Two people who disagree about a policy have never heard of the hypothesis the other is entertaining. I do not think there is a disagreement here to adjudicate at all.

5. Two people agree on every probability and want opposite things done. I do not count the disagreement as a defect in either of them.

6. A first-year student who has read a single paper rejects the settled view of a whole field. I think the student should defer, not that the field should move.

7. Two people each read the other's stubbornness as evidence of bad faith rather than as evidence about the subject. Something has gone wrong, though I cannot say which of them is at fault.

Where I get stuck: case 1 treats disagreement as an error while case 2 treats it as a legitimate difference in starting points, and I cannot say why a prior should be exempt from the standard I apply to inference, given that a prior is something I reason with rather than something I merely bring. Case 6 makes it worse, because there I want the differing starting point to be a reason to defer rather than a reason to settle into permanent disagreement. And case 3 embarrasses case 2: if the two economists in case 2 have nothing to reproach each other with, why do I feel that the two of us in case 3 owe each other movement?

My working hunch is that a disagreement is legitimate when it comes from something the parties bring to the question and a defect when it comes from something they do with what they both have. I cannot make "bring" and "have" stay apart. Please test that hunch against the cases and repair it, or replace it.

What I need: the principle, or a small set of rival principles, stated precisely enough to apply; for each clause, the case that forces it; what it predicts for cases I have not listed — in particular, a case where the parties share both the prior and the data but one of them is simply the poorer reasoner, a case where the disagreement is about how much to trust the other's opinion, and a case where the two parties disagree about what counts as the same evidence; and an honest account of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, together with whether you would give up the verdict or complicate the clause.
