I recently encountered a short encyclopedia entry on John Taurek's "Should the Numbers Count?" and I would like to know how much of it I can rely on. The entry runs roughly as follows.

Taurek defends the striking thesis that numbers do not matter when we must decide whom to save. His central case is an agent holding a single dose of a drug that can rescue either his friend David or five strangers. Taurek argues that the agent is permitted to save David, because the five do not together have a claim any stronger than David's. His reason is that separate people's interests cannot be added up: there is no group that suffers the loss of five, only five individuals each of whom loses precisely what the one would have lost. Since the claims are equal and cannot be aggregated, Taurek concludes that it makes no difference whom the agent saves, and he proposes a coin toss as a fair way of settling the matter. The entry presents the position as the limiting case of the anti-aggregationist family of views, and notes that critics such as Parfit object that it makes the numbers altogether irrelevant, which clashes with the near-universal intuition that five deaths are worse than one.

I suspect this is a fair summary, and that Taurek really is committed to treating the choice as arbitrary — the coin toss seems to concede as much. But something about the entry feels off, and I do not know the literature well enough to say where. Please audit the summary claim by claim, distinguishing (i) what Taurek would endorse, (ii) what is a defensible but misleading simplification, and (iii) what he explicitly denies or would regard as a distortion of his argument. I am particularly interested in whether "the numbers do not matter," "it makes no difference whom the agent saves," and "the view makes the numbers altogether irrelevant" say the same thing or come apart; in whether the coin toss is even consistent with the indifference the entry attributes to him; in whether the friendship between the agent and David is doing work the entry ignores; and in whether the "limiting case of anti-aggregationism" framing is a fair placement or a category slip. Finally, tell me whether the entry's critical conclusion — that the view is refuted, or at least seriously embarrassed, by the intuition that five deaths are worse than one — survives once the misstatements are corrected, or whether it trades on them.
