Two colleagues of mine in an alignment research group had an argument last week that I have been unable to adjudicate. The setting is a safety evaluation: a model receives a task drawn from a deployment distribution D and produces an action; a harm function h assigns each action–task pair a value in [0,1], with 1 representing a catastrophe; and the model is evaluated on a finite sample from D. The question is what guarantee we should demand before deployment.

A argues: “A model is safe only if its worst-case harm over the deployment distribution is below a fixed threshold. Expected harm is insufficient, because a model can have low average harm and still be catastrophic on a rare task. Call the set of tasks on which harm stays below threshold the safety envelope; a safety case should require that the deployment distribution be contained in that envelope. If the distribution has a long tail, the tail is precisely what a safety case must bound.”

B replies: “That standard is either vacuous or unattainable. The support of D is a modeling choice, and every real deployment has a finite set of foreseeable tasks. A worst-case bound over an arbitrarily large set will be dominated by events we cannot rule out but also cannot meaningfully prepare for. What we actually care about is expected harm under the deployment distribution, perhaps supplemented by a tail-risk measure such as the average harm in the worst 0.1% of cases. A model that is safe on all but a negligible fraction of tasks is safe enough.”

A answers: “Negligible fractions are where adversaries live. If you optimize expected harm, you are explicitly training the model to be safe on the average and negligent on the tails. The only non-negotiable standard is a bound on the worst case over a set of plausible perturbations of D, not just D itself.”

B responds: “But then you need a metric for ‘plausible,’ and the worst case over that set is again a modeling choice. You have not escaped the need for a distribution; you have hidden it in the adversary’s choice. And a worst-case standard can be satisfied by a model that refuses to act at all, which is useless.”

A replies: “Refusal is a harm too; include it in h. And hiding the distribution in the adversary is fine, because it is an explicit assumption rather than a hidden one. The adversary’s distribution is the one that matters for safety.”

B answers: “Then you are not comparing worst-case with average-case; you are comparing two distributions. The real question is which distribution is the right one to evaluate under, and worst-case is simply the case where the adversary picks. That is a substantive empirical question, not a conceptual victory for worst-case.”

A replies: “It is a conceptual victory in one respect: a guarantee that holds for every distribution in a set of candidates is strictly stronger than a guarantee that holds for one distribution, and strength is what we want in a safety case.”

B replies: “Strength is not free. A stronger guarantee that no useful model can meet is worse than a weaker guarantee that can be met and audited. You are optimizing for the feeling of safety rather than for safety.”

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. I want a definite verdict on each of the six moves: which are clearly right, which are clearly mistaken, and which are partly right but overstated. Then say what, if anything, both A and B are missing—for example, whether a third standard reconciles their concerns, or whether the disagreement is really about the choice of a loss function rather than about worst-case versus average-case. Do not merely lay out the options; take a position and give your reasons. End with a single line \answer{...} containing a one-sentence overall verdict.
