I spent thirty-eight years as an industrial chemist, the last dozen of them running a failure-analysis lab, and I retired four years ago intending to read the philosophy I had been promising myself since graduate school. Along the way I was appointed as one of the seven public members of my state's Board of Pardons and Paroles — a post I assumed would be mostly clerical, and which has turned out to be the hardest thinking I have ever been paid to do. On Thursday the board votes on a single case, and the chair has asked anyone who wants to speak at length to circulate a written opinion beforehand, so that we do not simply re-enact last month's argument. I would like you to draft that opinion for me.

The case is this. A man of sixty-eight, serving a life term for a murder committed during a robbery when he was twenty-seven, is now forty-one years into the sentence and before us on a commuted eligibility date. The offense is as bad as that sounds, and the facts are not in dispute. Our statute directs the board to weigh two things and does not tell us how to rank them: the seriousness of the offense, and the likelihood that the person will commit further crimes if released. The actuarial instrument we are required to use places him in its lowest risk band. The warden supports release and has lined up work for him. The sister of the man he killed has attended every hearing for nearly forty years and will speak against release. Her argument, as I hear it, is not that he is dangerous; it is that releasing him now would mean the state never gave him what he took, and that a sentence shortened because the prisoner has changed is not a punishment but a discount. Two of my colleagues hold that the only real question is what he deserves, and that this was fixed when he was sentenced and cannot be revised by good conduct; two others hold that the word desert belongs to a metaphysics the state has no business enforcing, and that our only legitimate question is future danger. I am the seventh vote.

What has thrown me is the reading. I have spent the past year with the two philosophers who seem to be the poles of this dispute, Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso, and I cannot get the disagreement to settle into a decision. My rough grasp is that both reject the thought that he could have done otherwise in any sense requiring a break in the causal order. Dennett, the compatibilist, holds that blame and desert survive that concession, and that a penal system which gives them up will end up harsher and less humane rather than gentler. Caruso, the skeptic, holds that desert does not survive it, that retributive punishment is unjustified, and that the state's legitimate interest runs only to protecting people from those who are dangerous, on something like the model we use for contagious disease. I can follow each of them while I am reading him, and I lose the thread the instant I try to derive a vote from either. I am used to questions that have answers.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate, of roughly a thousand words, doing all of the following. First, give me a bottom line — release, deny, or defer to a date certain — with reasons of a kind my colleagues can argue with rather than merely contradict. Second, tell me plainly whether the Dennett–Caruso disagreement has any bearing on this vote at all. If you think it washes out, and that everything turns on which forward-looking considerations we weight, say so and defend it; if you think it does bear on the vote, say precisely where it bites — on what he deserves, on whether desert may be a reason for the board at all, or somewhere else. Third, deal with the petition he has filed: he declines to apologize, and when the examiner pressed him he said he does not regard himself as the author of the man who did it. Tell me whether that belongs to the question of desert, the question of risk, both, or neither. Fourth, deal with a colleague of mine, a retired sheriff, who says at every meeting that if the offender's choices were caused then so are ours, that the board is therefore off the hook, and that we may as well follow the spreadsheet; I cannot tell whether this is profound or a howler. Fifth, engage the sister's position in its strongest form rather than the form that is easiest to answer, since she will read the document.

I do not need citations; I want the argument. Write in American English, and write as a colleague reasoning in public rather than as an advocate, because at least two of the six people who receive this will vote the other way.
