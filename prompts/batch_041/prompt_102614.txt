I teach a 90-minute session in an upper-level philosophy of science seminar, 14 students, and I want them to run into the triviality objection to structural realism with their own hands before I name it. Most of them have only read the usual Worrall/Ladyman material and theyre a bit too comfortable with the idea that we should believe the structure of our best theories and stay agnostic about the nature of the unobservables. I want them to feel why someone would say: fine, believe the structure, but then youve believed almost nothing, because any old bunch of objects of the right size can be rigged up to satisfy any consistent structural description you like. Design the whole thing as a runnable activity with rules, materials, a timeline and a debrief. The idea has to land in what happens, not in what I say at the end.

My rough thinking, feel free to replace it if you can do better. Stage one, maybe 20 minutes: give the groups a phenomenon table (just a list of input-output pairs, photocopied, sealed in envelopes so they cant peek at each other) and two sealed mechanism cards that both reproduce the table but posit different unobservables. They have to say what they now believe about the unobservables. Most of them will say the structure of the table is the part worth keeping. Stage two, maybe 25 minutes: hand out blank structure cards listing only relations and cardinalities, plus a bag of assorted junk (coins, stickers, string, cups), and have them realize the structure on their objects and write one sentence on the nature of the entities. Then swap bags with another group, whose objects are totally different, and do it again. Both times it works. Stage three: put a number on the board, like the domain has five elements, and ask whether the structure can be realized on five pennies, five names, five moments, five numbers. Then ask what the strongest non-trivial claim about the world is that they can still make. That last question is the payoff and I want them arriving at it, not me asking it rhetorically.

Things I want spelled out: exact rules (including how to stop people smuggling intrinsic properties into the relation table by defining R as is redder than or whatever, which I expect will happen constantly), roles if you think a hostile entity-realist role in each group helps, a timeline, and a debrief that maps each moment onto the pessimistic meta-induction, the structural realist response, and then the objection usually called Newman's problem. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. And be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: my guess is it can make the triviality vivid but it cant reproduce the cardinality argument, the Ramsey-sentence framing, the distinction between epistemic and ontic structural realism, or any of the serious replies about natural properties, causation, or modality. I need to know exactly which pieces I have to supply myself, and roughly how, because I have maybe 25 minutes of debrief and no more.
