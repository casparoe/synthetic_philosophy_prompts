Ok so I need help with something thats been bugging me all term. I teach the population ethics unit in my intro course, and I've finally admitted to myself that I don't have a view, I have a pile of verdicts, and I'm fairly sure they can't all be true. I want you to find the principle behind them, or tell me which one has to go.

Scale first, so the numbers aren't doing hidden work: 0 is the line where a life is exactly worth living, 100 is about as good as a human life gets. Wherever I add people, the people already there are completely untouched — no crowding, no resource squeeze, no new inequality, nothing.

1. Ten billion at 80. Versus the same ten billion at 80 plus ten billion more at 70. Second is better. Obviously.

2. Ten billion at 80, versus that plus ten billion at 25. The 25s do hard labour, chronic mild pain, not much in the way of projects, but they're glad to be alive and would say so if you asked. I still think the second is better. I don't enjoy saying it, but I can't find the victim.

3. Ten billion at 80, versus that plus ten trillion at 5. The 5s are bored, mildly uncomfortable, no projects, not suffering, and again they'd say yes if asked. Here I'm pretty confident the first is better. Yes, yes, this is the neighbourhood of the repugnant conclusion, that isn't what's bugging me.

4. Ten billion at 5, versus twenty billion at 5, all of them new, nobody affected. I say the twenty billion. More lives that are equally worth living just seems better.

5. Ten trillion at 5 and nothing else, versus ten billion at 80 and nothing else. First is worse. Very confident.

6. One person at 4 — miserable, wishes they'd never been born — versus ten billion at 5. The ten billion, and it isn't close. If those are my only two options I take the ten billion every time.

7. This one may be a seperate issue, I can't tell. Ten billion all at 60, versus five billion at 100 and five billion at 20, same total either way. I'd take the first. I care about how bad the bottom is.

8. Also, the two populations don't overlap at all: policy A gives a billion people lives at 70, policy B gives a billion different people lives at 71. I say B is better even though nobody is better off. So I'm not a pure person-affecting guy.

Here's what's driving me up the wall. 3 and 4 are both about adding people at level 5. In 3 it makes things worse, in 4 it makes things better. The only difference is who they're joining. And the obvious patch — some cutoff level, lives above it are fine to add, lives below it aren't — I can't get to fit 6, and I wouldn't know how to pick the level anyway. Total welfare gets 3 wrong. Average welfare gets 1 and 2 wrong. I've tried a few other things and they all break somewhere.

What I want: the principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough that I can check them myself. Say what each one does with 1 through 8, and be honest about which verdicts it can't save — I'd rather know which one I have to drop than have it fudged. Then two cases I haven't thought about:

(a) The same ten billion people, except instead of eighty years at 80 they each get two hundred and fifty years at 75, same people, and the extra years are genuinely good ones. Better than the original? Does your principle treat extra good years the way it treats extra good people?

(b) Ten billion at 90 and twenty billion at 10, all at once, versus ten billion at 80. Which is better — and does the answer change if the twenty billion at 10 arrive one at a time instead of all at once?

And one more thing. Tell me whether the badness in 3 is about the new people, something wrong with those lives being added, or about the world they'd be added to. If it's the second, I want to know what that even means.
