I've been arguing with a friend about a proposed education-savings-account scheme, and I keep losing the thread of what it actually commits us to. Under the proposal, any family whose child attends a public school could withdraw and receive roughly eighty percent of the per-pupil amount as a restricted-use account. The money could go to private-school tuition, online courses, tutoring, or a neighborhood "microschool" — say, up to twelve children taught by a parent with no teaching credential and no state-approved curriculum. Families drawing on the funds would have to have the child sit one standardized test a year, and that test would be the only accountability mechanism. Meanwhile the same state requires its public schools to admit every child, teach a prescribed curriculum, employ certified teachers, and observe anti-discrimination rules. And in a few liberal democracies — Germany is the one usually cited — homeschooling is effectively prohibited outright, on the ground that the state's interest in raising citizens capable of participating in a shared civic life overrides the parents' educational preferences.

What I want to know is what the scheme presupposes. Treat it as an existing practice rather than a proposal to evaluate: what conception of the child's interest, of parental authority, and of the state's legitimate interest in education does it only make sense under? Does it require a view on which the state's interest in a child's education is fully discharged by a test score, so that everything else — formation, civic competence, sustained exposure to people unlike oneself — is either the parents' private business or not an interest of the state at all? Or is the scheme compatible with a much thicker account of the state's interest, with the test serving merely as a cheap proxy for something else that is really valued? Notice also the headcount line: twelve children in a kitchen is a family, thirteen is a school, and the regulatory difference between the two is enormous while the pedagogical difference may be nil. Is that boundary doing any philosophical work, or is it an administrative convenience that quietly presupposes a theory of the family as a sphere of authority whose limits are set by size rather than by function?

Finally, I'd like the reconstruction to be honest about what the practice strictly requires versus what it merely fits with: tell me which theories would license the scheme, which it rules out, and where it is simply underdetermined. And if the scheme does presuppose a view on which the family's claim over the child's education is prior to and stronger than the state's, tell me what would have to change about public schooling — compulsory attendance, the curriculum, the financing — if we replaced that view with one on which the state's interest is independent of the family's and attaches to the child directly.
