I am preparing a position statement on the epistemic credentials of democracy for a political theory seminar, and before I present it I would like an audit of whether my commitments are jointly tenable. My views, stated as I would state them, are the following.

(1) Majority rule is justified in part by the logic of the Condorcet jury theorem: where each voter is independently more likely than not to identify the better of two alternatives, the probability that the majority selects the better alternative rises with the size of the electorate and tends toward certainty as the electorate grows; correspondingly, where average competence falls below one half, the result reverses and larger bodies do worse.

(2) The principal value of democracy is epistemic. Democracy is worth having because it tends to track the truth about the common good, and not merely because it embodies the equal standing of citizens.

(3) Deliberation before voting improves the epistemic quality of the outcome. When citizens exchange reasons and evidence, they are more likely to be right, and this is the mechanism that makes the justification in (2) more than a happy accident.

(4) Voter ignorance is severe and pervasive. On most policy questions a large share of the electorate is not reliably better than chance.

(5) Real electorates are saturated with correlated error. Voters draw on shared partisan cues, media ecosystems, and social identities, so their errors are far from independent in the sense (1) requires.

(6) The epistemic case for democracy is nonetheless robust. It survives the ignorance in (4) and the dependence in (5), because what the aggregation result really requires is a satisfactory average competence and errors that cancel in the aggregate, not informed or independent individuals.

(7) A citizens' assembly of a few hundred randomly selected citizens, briefed by experts and deliberating over several days, will reach better-reasoned verdicts on a complex regulatory question than a national referendum on the same question.

I suspect that some of these pull against one another, but I cannot see where the fault lines run, and I am not antecedently committed to abandoning any of them. Please tell me which commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each genuine tension is. For each genuine tension, state what would have to be given up and what the least costly repair would be; order the genuine tensions by severity; and for the pairs that can be reconciled, say precisely what does the reconciling work rather than merely asserting that they are compatible.
