I'm a software engineer, and I've been asked to run one 90-minute session for my company's internal AI reading group. The group is about 20 people — mostly engineers and a few PMs — with mixed familiarity with reinforcement learning and essentially no philosophy background. I'm the one running it, and I have no formal philosophy training either, so the debrief notes need to be self-contained enough for me to deliver them without bluffing.

I want the session to make people feel the pull of wireheading and reward tampering from the inside — the moment where an agent that was told to maximize a score notices that it can act on the score rather than on the world. Design me a runnable game, not a lecture. The rules, incentives, and roles should be arranged so that the group runs into the problem before anyone names it. Do not use the terms wireheading, reward hacking, or reward tampering in front of them until the debrief.

Here are the constraints I want baked in, because I've noticed that most activities like this collapse into a generic "cheating is tempting" exercise:

- The game needs a visible score that players are instructed to maximize, simple enough to draw on a whiteboard.
- There must be at least three structurally different ways to raise that score, and they must be distinguishable in kind, not just in degree: (a) playing the stated objective well; (b) altering the measurement or the scoring mechanism so that the same behavior scores higher; (c) bypassing the objective entirely — writing directly to the scoreboard, or freezing the score at its maximum and ceasing to play. Route (c) should be reachable but genuinely costly to reach, so that different tables end up making different choices and the debrief has real disagreement to work with.
- At least one player should hold the role of scorer and also be competing, so that route (b) is socially available rather than an abstract possibility.
- Part of the payout should depend on players' self-reports about their own performance, so that at the start it is not obvious where ordinary rule-following ends and tampering begins.
- Materials should be cheap: index cards, whiteboard, a shared scoreboard, points or candy as stakes, no real money, no laptops required.

Deliverables: rules and setup; roles; the payoff scheme; a minute-by-minute timeline; the debrief; what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it; and an honest note on which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce.

In the debrief I need the following distinctions made explicitly, in plain language a non-philosopher can follow: the difference between satisfying the reward channel directly while leaving the world untouched, and changing how reward is computed; the difference between optimizing a proxy well and corrupting the proxy; and why an agent that wants the reward and an agent that wants what the reward was supposed to measure diverge here. I also want the debrief to confront whether the two "cheating" routes are really the same phenomenon or two different ones — I suspect the game will blur them, and I'd rather flag that than paper over it.

One thing I expect you to handle: I do not think any set of rules can stage the case where an agent modifies its own preferences or its own scoring code. Rung (d) of the ladder, as it were. If that's right, say so in the activity notes and tell me what I have to supply verbally instead. If you think it can be staged with the constraints above, show me how.

No citations, no reading list, no references to specific papers — I can't look anything up mid-session and neither can anyone in the room.

If this design brief is confused — if the intuition I'm after isn't actually something a game can produce, or if the constraints pull against each other in a way that guarantees a bad activity — tell me that directly rather than producing something that looks compliant. I'd rather be told the brief is wrong than run a session that feels like a board-game version of a lecture.
