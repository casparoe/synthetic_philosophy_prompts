I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been trying to get clear on an argument that keeps turning up in the corners of the internet I read. It concerns what its proponents call moral trade, and the whole case for it is carried by an analogy with ordinary commercial trade. I would like you to audit that analogy: to say what it actually licenses, and where it fails.

Here is the argument as I received it. Take two people who disagree about what matters. The first cares a great deal about animal suffering and almost nothing about space exploration; the second cares a great deal about space exploration and is indifferent to animals. Even so, they can strike a deal: the first makes a substantial donation to the space-exploration charity the second favours, and the second gives up eating meat. Afterwards each takes the world to be better than it was — by the first's lights because fewer animals suffer, by the second's because there will be more exploration. Neither was coerced, both understood the terms, and both would rather have the deal than not. The person who laid this out for me drew the following moral. This is simply trade, and the case for it is the case for ordinary trade. Trade is not a contest with a winner and a loser; it is a device by which parties who value different things can both gain, and it is for that reason presumptively legitimate and to be encouraged. There is nothing suspect about a farmer and a baker exchanging what each has for what the other wants, so there is nothing suspect about moral trade either.

I can see that the comparison is doing real work, and I suspect it is doing more work than it can bear. Please give me the following, in order.

First, the correspondence, stated explicitly — a table is fine. What plays the role of the goods traded, the prices, the medium of exchange, the parties' endowments, the gains from trade, the enforcement mechanism, and the fallback each party has if no deal is struck?

Second, where the mapping breaks. I expect the informative failures to be the non-obvious ones, so distinguish features of ordinary trade that have no counterpart here from features that are present but distorted.

Third, a sorting of those breaks by whether they matter to the conclusion. Note that the conclusion is not the modest claim that moral trade is sometimes worthwhile, but the stronger claim that it is no more objectionable than ordinary trade and that the usual objections to it are therefore confused. Which disanalogies damage that claim, and which are merely colourful?

Fourth, your verdict: does the conclusion survive in weakened form, and if so, what is the correct statement of it? If it does not survive, identify the step that fails and what would have to be added to repair it.

Fifth, if the trade analogy is the wrong one, say what a better analogy would be and what it captures that this one misses.

One request about how to answer. Please do not tell me that morality is not a market, or that some things are not for sale. I have heard both, and I do not yet see what either establishes. I want to know precisely what the trade analogy can and cannot carry.
