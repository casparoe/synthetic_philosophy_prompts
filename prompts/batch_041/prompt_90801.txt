Construct a thought experiment that separates the legal category of genocide from the moral category in the direction that is harder to motivate: a case that satisfies every element of the legal definition yet is, by the lights of a defensible moral conception of genocide, not a genocide at all. Constraints: it must be a single realistic scenario of no more than one paragraph, set in the last seventy years, with no invented states, tribunals, or statutes; the group in question must be a group that the legal definition protects, and the perpetrator's intent must be genuinely attributable on the evidence, not stipulated by fiat; the moral conception that yields the "not genocide" verdict must be one you state explicitly and defend as non-absurd. After the scenario, do all of the following. First, lay out the elements as the law actually operates them — the protected group, the listed acts, the special intent directed at the group "as such," and the requirement that the destruction be of the group in whole or in substantial part — and show, element by element, that the case satisfies each. Second, state your moral conception of genocide precisely enough that a reader could apply it, and show why the same facts fall outside it. Third, identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work of prying the two verdicts apart, and say what happens to the divergence if that feature is removed while everything else is held fixed. Fourth, give the strongest reply available to someone who insists that the moral conception you used is not really a conception of genocide but of something else (mass atrocity, crimes against humanity, cultural erasure, whatever), and say whether that reply succeeds.

Two further specifications. The moral conception must not simply be Lemkin's 1944 conception in its broad, cultural form — that direction (legally narrower, morally broader) is the easy one and I do not want it. You may use Lemkin, but only if you can construct a reading on which his conception is narrower than the legal definition in the relevant respect, and if you cannot, say so and switch to a different moral conception, naming whose it is or marking it as your own construction. Finally, do not resolve the case by appealing to prosecutorial discretion, evidentiary uncertainty, or the politics of who gets to apply the label; the divergence must survive the assumption that the facts are fully known and the label is applied in good faith. Where the case turns on a doctrinal or historical particular that a careful reader might dispute, flag it and say how much the argument depends on it. No citations or reading lists are needed; refer to positions and instruments by name only where the name is doing real work, and paraphrase rather than quote any legal or scholarly text.
