For about a year now a fortnightly discussion group I belong to — a retired nurse, a secondary-school teacher, a friend halfway through a psychology degree, and me — has been circling the so-called situationist attack on virtue ethics, and I have reached the point where I no longer believe we will settle it, not because either side is being stupid but because I suspect they are not, in the end, arguing about the same thing. As I understand the situationist side, it leans on a body of experiments in which ordinary people behave in ways that seem to reveal no stable character at all: seminarians on their way to deliver a talk on the Good Samaritan hurry past a slumped man because they have been told they are running late; people who have just found a coin in a payphone are likelier to help a stranger gather dropped papers than people who have not; and, more generally, how a person acts in one situation turns out to predict only weakly how they will act in the next. From this the situationists conclude that the robust, cross-situationally consistent traits that virtue ethics places at the centre of the moral life are largely a fiction, and that what actually drives conduct is the situation. The defenders of virtue ethics in our group reply that the experiments test something much narrower than the theory: that a virtue is a disposition which shows itself over a whole life and under conditions that allow it to operate; that a person may genuinely possess a virtue and still fail in a strange, contrived, once-only setting; and that being rushed is not a neutral background but itself a moral test. My psychology-degree friend keeps adding a distinction between "strong" and "weak" situations — the thought that a situation can be so constraining that it swamps whatever dispositions people have, so that the real question becomes what counts as a fair test of a trait at all. Everyone in the room knows these moves and the standard rejoinders to them, and yet nobody has budged in months.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict on who is right. My own hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than simply adopt, is that this is not one disagreement but at least two fused together: a genuine empirical dispute about how much of our behaviour is situation-driven, and a hidden disagreement about what a virtue is even supposed to be — whether it is a prediction about how people will in fact behave, or a standard we invoke when we praise, blame, and hold one another responsible, a standard that no frequency count could refute. I also suspect a third strand, that the two sides are quietly using different thresholds for how much consistency a trait needs before it counts as a trait, and that the choice of threshold may be a decision rather than a discovery. And I would like you to consider the possibility I find least comfortable: that the debate has stalled because the question "do people have virtues?" is ill-posed as it stands, so that some version of it could be answered while the version actually being argued over cannot.

Using what each side genuinely says rather than a survey of the literature, tell me what kind of disagreement this is, and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a sharper distinction, an experiment that could actually discriminate the two views, a decision to stop asking the question in one form and start asking it in another, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. Be committed: give me a definite diagnosis and defend it, rather than laying out the possibilities and leaving the choice to me. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
