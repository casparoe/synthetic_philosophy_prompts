I run a 90-minute session in an applied ethics seminar, 20 undergrads, and I want them to actually feel the inefficacy objection -- the thing where you think: my emissions, my steak, my one vote cant possibly make a difference, so why bother. I dont want to lecture first. Design a game where they run into the objection themselves, then run into the replies to it, then have to decide how much the replies actually buy.

My rough idea, improve it as you see fit: three rounds, tokens instead of money. Round one, everyone privately decides whether to pay a small cost into a pot that only pays out if nearly everyone pays in -- set the bar high enough that it predictably fails, so the contributors just lose their tokens and the room should come away feeling that contributing was dumb. Round two, same thing except nobody knows the exact bar in advance (it's drawn from a range after contributions close), so any single contribution has some real chance of being the one that tips it -- have them write down, before the result, their own chance of being pivotal, then compare that to what happened. Round three, make contributions public and sequential, and give each contribution a small direct benefit to everyone else whether or not the bar is met, so the act does something even when it isn't pivotal. Debrief: map each round onto a different reply to the inefficacy objection -- expected marginal impact under uncertainty, the ask-what-if-everyone-did-it reasoning, direct and social-multiplier effects, and being part of the group that did it as a moral rather than causal notion -- and say which of them you think holds up.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it, and which feature of the real problem the game can't reproduce at all and I'll have to supply myself.
