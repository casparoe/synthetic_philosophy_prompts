I am a palliative-care physician, and my father was recently diagnosed with early-onset Alzheimer's. I have been rereading Ayn Rand, whose ethics I admired in college, and I find myself unable to reconcile her condemnation of altruism with the care I am now giving him—and with the care I believe I owe him. I have constructed an argument that seems to show her ethics is false, but I suspect it proves too much. I would like to know which premise to reject and what rejecting it costs.

(1) Altruism is the doctrine that one's own interests ought to be sacrificed to the interests of others.
(2) When I cut back my practice to care for my father, I am subordinating my own immediate interests to his.
(3) Therefore, when I care for my father, I am acting altruistically.
(4) Rand condemns altruism as morally evil.
(5) Therefore, Rand's ethics condemns my care for my father.
(6) But my care for my father is morally required, or at least morally admirable.
(7) Therefore, Rand's ethics is false.

I know the standard reply: Rand's target is altruism as a moral code, not other-regarding action as such, and she distinguishes altruism from benevolence, insisting that love and friendship can be selfish. I also know she treats values as relational, so that another person's welfare can be a value to me. But that reply feels like a redescription of (2) rather than a refutation of it. I am not claiming that caring for him makes my life worse on balance. I am claiming that the act has the structure of putting his interests first, and that this is what Rand's anti-altruism is supposed to rule out. So I want a precise diagnosis: which premise or step is doing the illicit work, and if I keep Rand's central anti-altruism thesis, what does the repair cost me? If the argument is valid, I need to know which of (1), (4), or (6) is least costly to give up.
