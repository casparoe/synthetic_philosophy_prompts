I keep running into a pattern on the science beat and I've never found a good name for it, so I'm hoping you can either give me the term or invent one that sticks. Here it is. Somebody builds a model that everyone involved knows is a cartoon -- one representative household, a fluid with no viscosity, a population where every individual is identical -- and when you press them on it they say the same thing every time: the assumptions don't need to be realistic, only the predictions do. Fine, I've made my peace with that. What I notice is what happens to the number afterwards. The number leaves the paper and shows up in a press release, in a regulator's cost-benefit table, in a minister's speech. The caveat doesn't leave with it. And not because anyone is covering anything up -- the person who built the model will give you the caveat straight away if you call them. It's that the caveat only means something to someone who already knows how the model is assembled. "It assumes a single representative agent" isnt a sentence you can staple to a figure for a general reader; it just reads as noise. So the qualification is real, freely offered, and permanently stuck at the source. A philosopher friend told me this is just external validity, and I dont think that's it, because external validity is about whether results hold somewhere else, and this happens even when everyone agrees the results hold exactly where they're meant to. So: is there a name for the thing about the caveat rather than the model? If there isn't, give me one, and then tell me where its edges are -- how it differs from ordinary uncertainty, how it differs from plain spin, and whether the same thing happens when what travels isn't a number but a way of talking, when suddenly everyone in a field is saying "discount rate" or "phase transition" and nobody remembers whose model that came out of.
