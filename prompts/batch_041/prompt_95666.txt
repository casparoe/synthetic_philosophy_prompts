Right, so I'm three years into a PhD that sits awkwardly between environmental history and earth system science, and I keep getting dragged into the same fight about the Anthropocene — not the is-it-real fight, the when-does-it-start-and-who-gets-to-say fight. And I've noticed everyone in that fight argues from one case. Someone says 1950 because of the bomb spike; someone says 1610 because of the population collapse in the Americas and the reforestation that followed; someone says the 1780s because of coal and the steam engine; someone says it goes back to farming and if you push it that far why bother; and then a stratigrapher says none of those work because the thing has to be findable in rock, at a single point, worldwide. I want the spec first, before I have to referee any more of these arguements.

So write me the desiderata that any theory of the Anthropocene as a period has to meet. By theory of the period I mean a criterion, or a package of criteria, that says what makes something an epoch at all and pins down where ours begins. I want the requirements list — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the facts about how the discipline actually defines these units that it can't contradict — with the consideration behind each requirement. For each one, say whether it's non-negotiable or whether it's really a preference of a particular camp (the stratigraphers, the historians, the people who just want a usable word for what's happening to them).

Two things I especially want you to take seriously, because I think that's where the weight is. First, the convention that a unit in the rock column is defined from its base upward, by a single point, so that everything above that point belongs to the unit no matter what it looks like. That seems to mean the boundary can't be defined by the human story it's supposed to capture — only by a signal — and so the epoch-of-humans idea might be self-undermining, or at least it means the human content can't be doing the defining work. Tell me if that's right and what it costs. Second, the boundary has to be more or less simultaneous everywhere if it's going to be one point at all, but the actual human causes spread unevenly: industrialisation reaches different places at different times, the post-1492 collapse is not one moment, and even a fallout layer is only as synchronous as the atmosphere makes it. So several of the candidate starts look diachronous by construction. Is that fatal for them, or can a theory live with a boundary zone instead of a boundary line?

Then the part I actually care about, which is who decides. I want the desiderata for the decision as well as for the period: what has to be true of a body for its ruling to settle the question; whether the settling is a discovery, where there's a fact of the matter and the right procedure finds it, or a convention, where the picking is itself what makes it so; and whether a theory of the period can be adequate without ever being ratified by anyone — i.e. whether an informal Anthropocene that no committee ever adopts could satisfy every requirement on your list, or whether formal adoption is itself one of the requirements.

Flag any pair of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied. My money is on the synchroneity one and the must-reflect-the-real-causal-onset one being in tension, and probably also usable-as-a-period-for-historians-and-the-public against has-to-be-defined-by-a-rock-signal-nobody-can-see-without-a-mass-spectrometer. And tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidate starts, because I suspect the whole argument is really one constraint doing all the work.

Skip the bibliography, I've read it. A numbered list is fine, and put the case behind each requirement right next to it rather than in a separate section.
