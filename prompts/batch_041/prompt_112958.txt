I want you to write an essay on a puzzle about markets that I think is under-discussed, and I want you to actually land somewhere rather than surveying. Setup, spelled out so we're not just trading slogans: a market price is supposed to be an epistemic achievement -- the thing that aggregates dispersed information, so that the price of an asset is a decent estimate of its value given everything anyone knows. Now add two claims that each seem true. (1) If I have a reason to buy, its because I think the asset is underpriced, i.e. that I know something the current price doesn't already reflect. (2) The current price already reflects what everyone knows. Together those eat the reason to trade: the only trades left are people who are wrong, or people trading for non-epistemic reasons -- they need cash, they're hedging, they just like the action. So the controversy: does the efficient-price picture of markets undermine itself, implying that any trade is noise or error, and hence that the 'information in prices' is extracted by trades we've just declared irrational? Or is the tension a harmless artifact of stacking idealizations that never co-occur (common priors, no liquidity needs, equally informed traders), so nothing follows for real markets? Yes, I know there's a formal literature of no-trade results; don't recite it at me, give me the argument and a verdict. One thing I want folded in: what happens to the epistemic value of prices if the no-trade reasoning itself becomes common knowledge -- if everyone believes prices are efficient and so stops trading, is there still an informative price left to believe in? And tell me how confident you are in your conclusion, and single out the step you're least sure of.
