I work as a caseworker at a state injury-compensation agency — the kind of scheme where you get covered for an accident regardless of who was at fault. The reason this is on my mind is that my younger brother got hurt in a bad crash two years ago and the payout he ended up with felt completely arbitrary to both of us; now I'm on the other side of the desk making those calls, and I'm supposed to be the one who can explain them.

We get alot of these files, maybe three hundred a year, and the decisions are big: whether we cover lost earnings for someone who's now out of work, whether we top up treatment costs, whether we cover a condition that showed up years after the injury. There's a legal framework, but it's vague in exactly the places that matter, and my manager keeps saying to just use judgement. I don't want to just use judgement. I want something I can actually run and defend to the review board.

The idea I keep coming back to is the luck egalitarian one: inequalities are fair when they come from choices you can be held responsible for, and unfair when they come from brute luck, the bad stuff that just happens to you. Then the split between option luck and brute luck is supposed to do the work. Fine. But on my actual caseload the two are never cleanly seperate. Someone rides a motorbike without a helmet: the choice to ride that way is on them, but the fact that the crash left them with a specific brain injury rather than a broken wrist is luck. Someone smokes for thirty years and gets the disease most smokers never get. Someone takes a risk because every other option open to them was worse.

So please turn this into a decision procedure my team can actually run on a file. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is evaluative rather than factual. The hard part I need you to actually solve, not wave at, is how the procedure decomposes a loss into its chosen and unchosen parts, or what it does instead when that decomposition can't be done. Two more constraints. I know there's a standard objection that this whole way of thinking abandons people who make bad choices, and I don't want a cruel procedure — but I also don't want it to quietly collapse into cover everything, because then the distinction isn't doing any work. And assume the person running it has no philosophy background, about twenty minutes per file, and that anything above a small threshold goes to a manager who will ask where the number came from.

Then run it on three cases: the unhelmeted motorcyclist, someone whose injury came from a fight they started, and someone hurt while doing something that was only risky because they had no safe alternative — say, taking a night job driving because it was the only work they could get. At least one of these should come out in a way I won't like. Don't soften it.

Finally, tell me where the procedure is most likely to misfire, and what would change your mind about the whole approach — which argument or consideration would push you toward drawing the line some other way. I'd rather have it honest about its weak joints than look tidy. Hard limit: 900 words, no more.
