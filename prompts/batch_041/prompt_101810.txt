I have been sitting with an argument for a couple of weeks and cannot decide whether to keep it or throw it out, so I am handing it to you in the state I found it. It came out of a reading group on distributive justice, where one of us reconstructed it from a lecture he half-remembered and then could not say which step he actually believed. Here it is, as cleanly as I can state it. (1) You act as you do because of the sort of person you are — your dispositions, capacities, and commitments. (2) A person deserves credit or blame for an action only if the action is genuinely attributable to that person, rather than something that merely happens through them. (3) An action is genuinely attributable to a person only if the relevant parts of their character are genuinely theirs — that is, only if they are, in the relevant respects, responsible for being the sort of person they are. (4) To be responsible for being the sort of person you are, you would have to have chosen or formed your own character. (5) But any such choosing or forming would itself have to issue from a character you already had, from dispositions you did not choose, so the demand for a self-made character regenerates at every stage and is never satisfied. (6) Therefore no one is ever genuinely responsible for their character, and by (2) and (3) no one ever deserves credit or blame for anything. (7) Therefore the successful no more deserve their advantages than the unsuccessful deserve their disadvantages, and meritocracy is a sham.

What unsettles me is not (7) by itself — I am perfectly happy to say the wealthy did not earn their luck — but how far (7) seems to travel once I accept it. If no one deserves credit or blame for anything, then the same reasoning that strips the rich of their claim also strips the poor of any claim grounded in desert; it dissolves the wrongdoer's desert of punishment and the benefactor's desert of gratitude; and, worst of all, it seems to take away the standing of the very critic who wanted to use it, since on this view no one deserves to be praised for seeing through the meritocratic illusion either. A friend insists the regress in (4)–(5) is just an artefact of how the demand is phrased, but when I ask him to name the artefact he waves his hands. I would rather keep (1) and (2) than keep (6), but I am not sure that is a real option, and I suspect that whichever premise I drop to save desert I will end up saving a conception of desert that the original argument was right to distrust.

So: tell me which step to reject, and be specific about why that step looked so plausible in the first place. If you think the argument is valid and the real question is only which premise is least costly to give up, say so and price the alternatives against each other — what each rejection buys, what it forfeits, and in particular whether any available repair lets me keep a notion of desert sturdy enough for the egalitarian use while resisting the meritocratic one. If instead you think there is a genuine flaw — an equivocation, a shift in what "responsible" is being asked to mean, a scope slide between being responsible for one's character and being responsible for one's acts — name the exact move and show me how the premises can look innocent while the inference is not. I am less interested in a verdict than in a diagnosis with a price tag attached.
