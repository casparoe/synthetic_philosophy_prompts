I am a novelist, and I am writing a book set in a benefits office in a town that has seen better days. I had expected to find that my characters' moral reflexes were sharper than my own; they have turned out to be mine, and I cannot say what they add up to.

Here is what I find I think. A claimant does undeclared cash work at a car wash, and I think he should lose money for it, though I would not want to be the officer who takes it. A claimant turns down a job because childcare and the bus fare would swallow the wage, and I think nothing should happen to her. A claimant turns down a job at a payday lender, says so to the officer's face, and again I think nothing should happen to her. A claimant's partner moves in and she does not report it, so she goes on receiving the higher rate paid to people who live alone; she regards her household as nobody's business, and I find I cannot bring myself to call that fraud. A claimant who is genuinely too ill to work lays it on thick at her assessment, and I think that is fraud, or near enough. A family with a great deal of money claims the payment that goes to every family with children, and I have no objection whatever. A woman who fails to mention that she has moved house, netting herself four pounds a week, ought in my view to be prosecuted.

My own best attempt at a principle: the vice is not taking money you do not need, nor refusing work; it is dealing with the office as an adversary, managing what it knows. But to save my verdict on the concealed partner I have to add that only an active misstatement counts, which looks like special pleading, and the upshot is that the woman who embellishes her symptoms and the woman who quietly keeps four pounds come out worse than the woman who hides a partner. I cannot tell whether I believe that, and I suspect a simpler principle would simply overturn one of my verdicts.

What principle, or small set of principles, fits these verdicts best? What does it imply for two cases I cannot settle — a claimant who reports every hour she works truthfully but has arranged her hours so as to stay just under the threshold, and a claimant who is overpaid through the office's own error, notices at once, and says nothing? Tell me which of my verdicts you would have to overturn, what would change your mind about the principle you settle on, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
