I'm a fourth-year PhD student in political theory, slogging through a chapter on transgenerational responsibility, and I keep hitting the same wall. My supervisor thinks the whole idea that a nation can be *guilty* for something done generations ago is a category error with moralistic paint on it -- guilt is personal, you can't inherit it, and the people living in the country now didn't do the thing. My reading group thinks that's just methodological individualism refusing to look at how states actually work, and that if a state can act, plan, sign treaties and keep them over centuries, it's the kind of thing that can bear guilt too. Both sides win the exchange every time and neither of them convinces me, and I've finally worked out why: nobody has told me what the target is. We're arguing about answers to a question no one has specified. So I want the specification first.

Give me the desiderata. What does any adequate theory of collective and transgenerational guilt have to deliver -- the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the constraints it can't contradict -- with the consideration behind each one, and a mark on which are non-negotiable and which are just local prejudices of one camp. On cases: the obvious hard ones (a state that committed a crime no living member took part in; the dead wrongdoers, who are the only ones who could be answerable in the ordinary sense; the descendants of victims and the descendants of perpetrators; a country whose population has turned over almost entirely through immigration since the wrong; the case where an apology has been made and the case where it hasn't), plus my own toy case, which is a country where an official apology and a reparations scheme have run for twenty years, 40% of citizens arrived after the fact, and the victims' descendants are now a diaspora with no citizenship there. On distinctions: guilt vs. responsibility vs. liability vs. shame vs. mere stigma; the distributive reading, where each member is guilty in proportion, vs. the non-distributive one, where the group is guilty and the guilt doesn't divide; guilt for what one did vs. for what one failed to prevent vs. for what one merely benefits from; and the living vs. the dead vs. the not-yet-born, who I suspect get quietly conflated in half the literature. On constraints: guilt needs an act, an agent and culpability; ought-implies-can; and whatever you do with Parfit's non-identity problem, the theory can't be allowed to imply that the descendants of a wrong have no complaint becuase they wouldn't have existed otherwise. Tell me where Jaspers' four-way split -- criminal, political, moral, metaphysical -- sits on the resulting list: which of the four the theory should count as genuine guilt and which as something else wearing the word.

What I actually want, though, is the tension. I strongly suspect the list is jointly unsatisfiable and I want you to say where. The obvious candidate is the triple of (1) collective guilt has to be real guilt -- culpability and desert, the whole thing, not just forward-looking responsibility; (2) only agents with intentional states can be culpable; (3) present members of the nation don't have those states in any relevant sense. That looks like a proof that one of the three has to go, and I want to know which you'd drop, why, and what's left standing afterwards -- is the result still recognisably guilt or have you quietly converted it into something else? A table is fine if it helps. And take a side: don't hand me a menu of options and let me choose, give me your view. If your honest answer is "this should be called responsibility, not guilt," say that, and say why it isn't just a verbal dodge that leaves the moral work untouched.
