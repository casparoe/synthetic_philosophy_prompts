Next month I'm giving a paper at a reading group and I want to walk in actually ready for the Q&A. Here's the line I'm pushing. Moral foundations theory gets sold as descriptive moral psychology — here's what fires in whose head — and I think that's the wrong way to read it. Read the foundations instead as a repertoire of justifications people reach for, and the real empirical claim becomes about which justifications a group treats as load-bearing, not about mental modules. Fine. But here's the part I actually care about: once you read it that way, the psychology isn't normatively inert. The data give you a defeasible reason to think the so-called binding foundations (loyalty, authority, sanctity) are doing more coalitional work — they show up as post-hoc rationalization of in-group position in a way the individualizing ones (harm, fairness) don't — and if that's right, the whole "both sides just have different foundations, who's to say" line collapses, and the psychology quietly tilts toward weighting harm and fairness more heavily. That's my thesis and I know it's contestable, which is why I need the prep.

The room: about fifteen social/moral psychologists who run MFT-style surveys and treat the foundations as a measurement model, plus two normative ethicists — one Kantian, one rule-consequentialist — who are pretty sure none of this touches a normative question. Nobody there is a foundations-theory insider and nobody cares about my framing for its own sake.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet. I want the objections I'll actually get from *these* people, phrased the way they'd phrase them — the psych folks and the two ethicists will come at it in completely different vocabularies, so keep them seperate. Group the objections by who raises them, then order them by how much damage they do to the paper. For each one: a two-minute reply, plus a straight note on where that reply is soft or where I'm basically bluffing.
