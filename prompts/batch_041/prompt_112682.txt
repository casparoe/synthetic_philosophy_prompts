I am setting the final take-home exam for a graduate seminar on logical empiricism and its ethical residue, and I need one essay question on Schlick's treatment of the meaning of life. I want a question that cannot be answered by reciting the standard line that the Vienna Circle dismissed value questions as meaningless; Schlick's own essay on the topic is more interesting, and more internally strained, than that.

Please produce three things:

1. The essay question itself (roughly 120 words, answerable in about 60 minutes, worth 20 marks).
2. A model answer of about 700 words.
3. A marking scheme: five must-hit points, the marks each is worth (total 20), and a one-line note on the characteristic mistake that costs marks on each point.

The question should force the candidate to do at least the following work, and the marking scheme should reward precisely these moves:

(a) Reconstruct Schlick's diagnosis of the question. He treats the question of life's meaning as resting on a confusion: the assumption that a life, like a tool or a sign, must be a means to something beyond itself in order to be meaningful. On his view a meaningful life is not one standing in the right means-end relation to an external goal, and the question, posed that way, has no answer because it has no content.

(b) Connect that diagnosis to his non-cognitivism about value: value judgments do not describe facts and are not true or false; they express the speaker's desires or attitudes. There is accordingly no fact of the matter about the meaning of life for anyone to discover.

(c) Identify the tension. Having dissolved the question, Schlick goes on to give what reads like a substantive answer, locating the meaning of life in youth, in play, in activity that carries its own value rather than in future-directed striving for ends. If (b) is right, that answer cannot be a true claim; and if it is merely an expression of preference, it is unclear why it should bind anyone, or why the question warranted a whole essay rather than a shrug.

(d) Evaluate whether the tension can be resolved. Candidate answers might treat Schlick's positive view as a recommendation rather than a truth-claim, as a redescription of what people actually mean when they call a life meaningful, or as a straightforward inconsistency. The candidate should commit to one reading and defend it against at least one objection — for instance, that Schlick illegitimately privileges play over work, or that his dissolution proves too much, since it would equally dissolve the question of what makes a life go well, which he seems to want to answer.

(e) Say what would have to be true for Schlick's substantive answer to be defensible on his own terms, and what a cognitivist about meaning would say in reply.

Constraints for the model answer and the marking scheme: no verbatim quotation. I do not want candidates rewarded for recalling sentences, and I do not want you to put anything in quotation marks that you are reconstructing from memory. Attribute views in paraphrase. Do not lean on the essay's publication details or title; if you refer to the essay, refer to it descriptively. Do not assume the candidate has read anything beyond Schlick.

Finally, in a short closing note to me, state how confident you are in the philosophical claims you attribute to Schlick, and single out the one attribution you are least sure of.
