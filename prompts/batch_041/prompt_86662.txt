Theoretical computer scientists and philosophers of physics routinely put the Church-Turing thesis to two very different kinds of work. In one setting it appears as a definition or a stipulation: a function is called computable just in case some Turing machine computes it, so a proof that no Turing machine decides a given problem is reported, without qualification, as showing that the problem cannot be decided at all. In the other setting it functions as a substantive constraint on nature: when a proposed physical theory or cosmological model would allow a device to decide such a problem — or to outstrip any effective procedure — that consequence is often treated as a reason to doubt the theory, much as a perpetual-motion machine would be a reason to doubt a thermodynamic proposal. And yet when the thesis itself is discussed, it is standardly said to be neither provable nor refutable, since it relates an informal notion of an effective procedure to a formal one.

What view of the thesis's epistemic status does this practice presuppose? Does treating it as a refuter of physical theories commit the practice to a lawlike empirical claim about what physical systems can do, and is that commitment consistent with the stipulative reading that licenses the flat statement of undecidability results? Reconstruct the implicit account of how the thesis is known — by proof, by induction over independently motivated formalisms, by a transcendental argument about the possibility of communicating or following a rule at all, or by decision — and say which rival accounts the practice rules out and which it merely sits comfortably with. Then say what would have to change in each setting if the thesis were explicitly reclassified: if it were a definition, what could still count as evidence against a hypercomputational proposal, and what would undecidability results be results about? I care more about a precise reconstruction of the presuppositions than about a survey of who has held which position.
