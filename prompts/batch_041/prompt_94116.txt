I'm presenting a paper at a two-day workshop on freedom and its measurement next month, and what I need from you is a Q&A preparation sheet, not a defense of the paper. The paper argues that the familiar tripartite division of liberty into negative, positive, and republican is a taxonomic mistake: what gets called republican liberty is better understood as a corrective to how negative liberty has been operationalized than as a third species of liberty. My central move is a distinction between occurrent unfreedom — interference someone is actually suffering now — and dispositional unfreedom, the standing vulnerability to interference that no one happens to be exercising; the republican's distinctive contribution, I argue, is the claim that the second is the primary object of our interest in freedom, and that this claim can be granted without conceding that freedom is anything other than the absence of constraints on choice. I then add a second and more contentious claim: that arbitrariness in "arbitrary interference" should be read procedurally rather than substantively. On my account a power is arbitrary when the person subject to it has no effective means of contesting how it is exercised, not when the exercise fails to track her interests or the common good. The paper works this out through two applications, one to employer power over undocumented workers who cannot complain without risking deportation, and one to discretionary licensing decisions that are lawful, interest-tracking, and effectively unreviewable.

The audience is small and mixed, and each part of it will come at me differently. There are two neo-republicans who have spent their careers defending the irreducibility of non-domination and who will hear my procedural reading of arbitrariness as a quiet reversion to Hobbesian freedom-as-non-interference. There is a Hayekian and a classical liberal who think that any talk of standing vulnerability, however proceduralized, hands the state a license to rank citizens' interests for them and to call uncoerced arrangements unfree. There is a legal theorist who works on labour law and cares only about whether the account earns its keep in the undocumented-worker case, and who will press on whether my procedural test collapses into a demand for legal status rather than for freedom. And there is an intellectual historian who will observe that the labels "positive" and "negative" were coined for polemical purposes and were never meant to carve the historical material at its joints, so that my taxonomy-bashing may be beating a straw man.

Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience — phrased in their vocabularies rather than mine — ordered by how much damage each does to the paper rather than by how likely it is. For each, a reply I can deliver in two minutes, a note on where that reply is soft, and a clear statement of what I should concede up front because defending it is not worth the time. The format is unforgiving: fifteen minutes to present and twenty-five for discussion, so I cannot spend five minutes on any single question. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me which one of these objections should be read as a signal that the paper's framing, rather than its argument, has to change.
