I write about philosophy and the sciences for a monthly ideas magazine, and for the past few years I have also taught a final-year undergraduate option on the metaphysics of meaning at a university near my desk. The module ends with a unit on the unity of the proposition, and every year the same thing happens: my students can reconstruct Bradley's regress from memory — the argument that if a relation needs a further relation to attach it to its terms, then the same demand simply re-arises for the attaching relation, and so on without end, so that nothing is ever truly related — and yet the moment I ask them what the regress is an argument about, or what would even count as a satisfying answer to it, they fall silent. Recitation has crowded out thought. I am now setting this year's examination paper and I would like one essay question that makes the difference visible, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. Could you draft that for me?

Some parameters. The paper is sat by final-year undergraduates; they will have 45 minutes for this question and no texts with them, and they are expected to write something in the region of 700 to 800 words. I want the question to be unanswerable by a summary of the regress, so please build it around a crux rather than a topic. The crux I have in mind is the choice between the two ways out that the regress appears to leave open, and what each of them costs. One way out denies that the tie between a relation and its terms is itself a relation — the line Davidson took, that predication is not a relation at all — and I want the question to press the students on whether that is a solution or a change of subject, since it risks making the unity of the proposition a brute fact rather than an explained one. The other way out is the one Russell once took, treating judgement not as a two-place relation between a mind and a single proposition but as a multiple relation between the mind and the several objects judged about, so that no single unified proposition is required; the difficulty, an objection normally credited to the young Wittgenstein, is that this seems unable to distinguish my judging that A loves B from my judging that B loves A, and I would like the question to make the students say why that asymmetry objection bites, and whether it is fatal or merely awkward. Beyond that, the question should require them to say what premise actually generates the regress in the first place, and to say what a satisfying explanation of propositional unity would look like. Please keep the question as free of names and labels as you can — I would rather the students reason their way in and identify the positions themselves — and do not ask for citations, reading lists or references of any kind, since nothing may be looked up.

Finally, the marking scheme. Please give it as five must-hit points, with the marks attached to each and a total of 20, so that a colleague can mark against it without having to interpret me. Alongside those, list four or five specific mistakes that should cost marks — I am thinking of the things that look like insight but are not, such as treating the regress as merely an infinite regress to be shrugged at, or conflating it with the Third Man argument, or announcing that the unity of a proposition is simply primitive as though that settled anything. Add a line or two on what separates a first-class answer from a merely competent one. Everything — the question, the model answer, the scheme and the notes — should be in British English: recognise, analyse, generalise and so on. The model answer should run to roughly 700 to 800 words, written as a strong candidate would write it under time pressure, and it must not depend on knowing who said what.
