ok so I'm a third-year in phil of bio, working on causal modelling in the human sciences, and I keep bumping into a popular-science framing of heritability that I want to be able to take apart properly. The version I've got is from some behavioural genetics explainer blog post I can no longer find (classic), and it goes roughly like this:

'Heritability is the proportion of a trait that is caused by genes. When twin studies put the heritability of something like IQ at around 0.7, that means roughly 70% of your intelligence is genetic and the other 30% comes from upbringing and environment. A heritability of 0 means the trait is entirely environmental; a heritability of 1 means it's entirely genetic. And because heritability measures how genetic a trait is, it's a fairly stable biological property of the trait itself, which is why estimates for something like height come out similar across countries and decades. It also means that when a trait is highly heritable, differences between groups - between nations, between social classes - are mostly genetic too. And it tells us that interventions like better schools or better nutrition can only do so much, since the trait is mostly in the genes. The one thing heritability can't tell us anything about is individuals: it's a population-level statistic.'

How much of that survives scrutiny? I'd like a sentance-by-sentence audit: what a quantitative geneticist would nod along with, what they'd wince at and call a defensible simplification, and what's just wrong. Say explicitly what a heritability estimate is a statistic *of* - what has to vary for it to come out high or low, what it's blind to, and whether it can be a fixed property of a trait at all. The last line seems right to me and the rest seems dodgy, but I can't say were the reasoning goes wrong, and I have a suspicion that the last line doesn't actually rescue the earlier claims - tell me if that's right.
