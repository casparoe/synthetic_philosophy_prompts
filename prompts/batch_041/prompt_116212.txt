I am teaching a graduate seminar on the history of the philosophy of mind, and next week we reach Avicenna's flying man. The surrounding metaphysics I can handle; what I have never been able to state to my own satisfaction is the single inferential step at the center of the passage, and I would like your help in getting it right. Here is the passage in a standard English rendering:

> One of us must suppose that he was just created at a stroke, fully developed and perfectly formed but with his vision shrouded from perceiving all external objects – created floating in the air or in the space, not buffeted by any perceptible current of the air that supports him, his limbs separated and kept out of contact with one another, so that they do not feel each other. Then let the subject consider whether he would affirm the existence of his self. There is no doubt that he would affirm his own existence, although not affirming the reality of any of his limbs or inner organs, his bowels, or heart or brain or any external thing. Indeed he would affirm the existence of this self of his while not affirming that it had any length, breadth or depth. And if it were possible for him in such a state to imagine a hand or any other organ, he would not imagine it to be a part of himself or a condition of his existence.

The difficulty, as I see it, is this. The flying man reports that he affirms his own existence while affirming nothing about his body; the conclusion drawn is that the self — the soul — is something other than the body, indeed immaterial. As stated, the move looks like an application of the general principle that whatever can be conceived apart from something else is separable from it. Taken generally, that principle is far too strong: I can conceive of a quantity of water without conceiving of its chemical composition, and the water is not thereby separable from that composition. So either the passage relies on a narrower and more defensible premise, or it is not, despite appearances, an argument from conceivability at all.

I have a hypothesis that I would like you to test against the wording of the passage rather than against any commentary. My hypothesis is that the argument's real engine is the asymmetry between what is given and what is merely supposed. The flying man's self-affirmation is presented as a datum of awareness that cannot be withheld, whereas his body is precisely what fails to appear in that awareness; and the stipulations — that he is created "at a stroke," that his limbs are separated and out of contact, that no current of air is felt — are not idle scene-setting but devices for eliminating every possible sensory contribution, so that what remains is a bare self-awareness with no object. On this reading the argument is not the inference from being able to conceive oneself without a body to being able to exist without one, but rather an inference from the self's being given in a way that no body is to the self's not being a body — an argument from the mode in which the self is presented, not from the separability of its conception.

I would be grateful for the following, in order.

1. Whether the text supports that reading, and which phrases in it carry the weight. If you think another reading fits the passage better, say which, and why.
2. If the conceivability reading is right after all, what the missing premise must be, and whether Avicenna can supply it without begging the question — the worry being that he stipulates the very scenario in which the flying man has no body, so the possibility he needs appears to be assumed rather than established.
3. What the argument, on the best reading, actually establishes: the soul's immateriality, its mere distinctness from the body, or only a thesis about how the self is known. Is Avicenna entitled to the strongest of these, or does a self-awareness that is merely given tell us only how the self appears and not what it could exist as?
4. The strongest objection a critic could press against the step, and the most economical reply available to Avicenna from the passage itself.

Please keep the discussion anchored in the wording of the passage. I am not asking for a survey of interpretations; one reading carefully defended would serve me better than several gestured at.
