I am a doctoral candidate in archival studies, and I would like help with a sentence that keeps being used to end arguments in my department: 'History is written by the victors'. I last met it in a seminar on community archives and territorial claims, where a fellow student used it to wave away a question about whether a nineteenth-century colonial survey — the only written record of how a disputed territory was parcelled out, and the document on which a present-day claim depends — could still be treated as evidence about what actually happened on the ground. Her suggestion was that because the survey was made by the winning side, there is nothing in it to be learned about the dispossessed community's prior occupancy, and that the claim must therefore rest on oral testimony alone. I am not satisfied, but I cannot say precisely where the argument goes wrong.

What I want from you is a disambiguation. Set out the distinct theses that this sentence could be standing for, ranging from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical, and for each say what kind of claim it is, whether it is true or false or merely a platitude, and whether it would actually support the use to which it was put in that seminar. I suspect the sentence is doing several jobs at once, and that the job it was doing in that room is the least defensible of them.

Three distinctions from my reading group may help you keep the readings apart; none of them is drawn from any published source, so treat them as the working apparatus of the exercise. First, we distinguish three kinds of silence: documentary silence, where no record of a person, event, or transaction was ever produced; custodial silence, where a record was produced but was not kept, deposited, or preserved; and descriptive silence, where a record is held but is not catalogued, described, or otherwise findable. Second, we distinguish a source's provenance — who made it, to what end, under what constraints — from its reliability, on the assumption that a document may be impeccably reliable about one matter and systematically misleading about another. Third, we distinguish the victor as the author of a record from the victor as the custodian of a record, since the two need not be the same party, or even the same generation.
