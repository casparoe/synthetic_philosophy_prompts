I run the grants program at a small family foundation — we make one-off hardship grants, capped around $8k, to individuals in our county. Think: a guy whose van died and now can't get to dialysis, a kid who got into a training program but can't cover the fees, a woman whose basement flooded and has no savings. We have a fixed pot each quarter and always more applicants than money, and the board is sick of relitigating the same fight every meeting: do we give to the largest raw need, to whoever will get the most out of what we hand them, or do we try to get everyone to some kind of floor? Our director keeps saying we should only be compensating people for the hand they were dealt and not for their own choices — but in practice every file is some mix of both. The man who skipped insurance and then got hit by a truck. The woman whose 'expensive taste' turns out to be that she needs special food or she's sick for a week. And honestly we've never agreed on what we're even trying to equalize.

Please write me a procedure the board can actually adopt: the questions we ask, in what order, what counts as a good-enough answer to each before we move on, where it stops with a clean no, and where it has to come back to the board because it's a value call and not a fact. Constraints: it has to be runnable by a volunteer with zero philosophy background in about fifteen minutes per file; anything over $2k needs a board sign-off; and the board insists we be able to give each applicant a reason they could in principle accept, not just a ranking. Ground it in whatever the real debate about equality — welfare, resources, capabilities, luck — actually settles, but keep only the parts we can operationalize, and be upfront about which distinctions you're dropping to make it usable. Then run it on three files: (a) the flooded-basement woman, clear bad luck, high need, $6k; (b) a young man who quit a steady job to tour with a band and now wants $3k for a certification that would likely make him employable — mostly his own call, but the payoff looks big; (c) a family with a disabled kid who need ongoing equipment where each dollar buys visibly less improvement than it would elsewhere. At least one of those should come out as a no that I won't be happy about, and I want you to say which and why the procedure forces it. Finish by telling me where this thing will misfire on real files, how confident you are in the whole design, and which single step you're least sure about.
