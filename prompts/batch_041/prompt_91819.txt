A guy in my just-war reading group handed us this last week and I still can't find the leak. He thinks it shows nobody can ever fight in a war; he's fine with that, I'm not. Here it is, more or less as he gave it:

(1) Killing someone in war is permissible only if they're morally liable to be killed.
(2) Your morally liable to be killed only if you've forfeited your right to life through your own culpable wrongdoing.
(3) Now take the conscript on the unjust side. He was grabbed off the street, he'd be shot for refusing, he's from a minority his own regime doesn't trust, and he has no clue the war is unjust — he's just trying to keep his village standing. Nothing he's done is culpable.
(4) So he hasn't forfeited anything, so he isn't liable to be killed.
(5) The just side's soldiers kill him anyway.
(6) So the just side's soldiers kill a man who isn't liable to be killed.
(7) Killing a man who isn't liable to be killed is murder.
(8) So even the soldiers on the just side are murderers.
(9) And if even they're murderers, then no war is ever permissible — not even one to stop a genocide.
(10) Thats obviously false, so one of (1)–(7) has to give.

I'd sooner cut off a finger than drop (10). So which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost me? And be straight with me about which parts of this are settled among people who actually work on it and which are genuinely contested — don't hand me one school's line as if it were the consensus.
