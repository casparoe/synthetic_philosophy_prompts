Here's the question as it was given to them:

"In cases of pain asymbolia, patients apparently still feel pain but are not bothered by it. Explain how such cases bear on the question of what makes pain bad — in particular on whether the badness of pain is intrinsic to the sensation itself or is instead a matter of the subject's attitude toward it. In your answer, be clear about what asymbolia does and doesn't show."

And here's the student's essay:

"Pain asymbolia is a striking dissociation. Patients report that the stimulus hurts — they can localize it and describe its intensity — but they don't find it unpleasant, and some will even smile or joke while being tested. The natural reading is that asymbolia shows pain's badness is not built into the sensory experience as such. What is missing is the affective-motivational side: the sensation is intact, the aversiveness is gone. So the badness of pain must come from somewhere downstream of the raw sensation, i.e. from the subject's aversion or dislike, rather than from the sensory quality itself. That supports what we might call an attitude-based account of pain's badness, and it tells against the idea that pain is intrinsically bad just in virtue of being pain.

Two things seem to me to strengthen the case. First, asymbolia is basically the mirror image of congenital insensitivity to pain: in both conditions the patient is spared the suffering of pain, and in both the lesson is that pain experience and pain's badness can come apart. The difference is just that the asymbolic patient still has the sensation while the CIP patient doesn't. Since both lack the badness, the badness can't be the sensation. Second, patients typically don't show the normal protective behavior — they'll let you hold a noxious stimulus on them without pulling away — which shows that the aversion really is separable from the experience, and not just something they're verbally reporting.

Now, one might object that the lesion just disconnects the pain signal from the brain's emotional machinery, so the 'pain' these patients feel isn't really pain at all. But the patients insist it hurts, and we should take reports seriously, so I think this objection fails. The cleaner conclusion is that pain as such is not bad; it's only bad when the subject minds it. The clinical cases are just a vivid demonstration of a point that also shows up in the philosophy, where several authors have argued that the badness of pain depends on the subject's desires or evaluations rather than the sensation."
