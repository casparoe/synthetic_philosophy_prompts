For about a year now, a cognitive science reading group I'm in has been circling a single question — whether the brain is a computer — without ever quite managing to have the argument we keep meaning to have, and I've started to suspect that we are not disagreeing about the thing we think we are. The literalists hold that the brain implements computations over representations, that this is a substantive and empirically evaluable claim about neural machinery, and that "the brain is a computer" stands to neuroscience roughly as "the heart is a pump" stands to cardiology: a level of description that carves the system at its joints. The dynamical and embodied contingent hold that the brain is better described as a continuous, coupled, non-representational dynamical system, and that computational vocabulary smuggles in commitments the system doesn't honour — discrete states, stored rules, symbols, a clean separation of program from hardware. A third group, which I find hardest to place, insists the whole dispute is close to empty: under a permissive enough account of implementation any physical system implements some computation or other, so the claim is either trivial or only as good as whatever constraints you impose on what makes this system implement *this* computation rather than *that* one — as someone in the group put it, under a loose account a bucket of water implements every finite-state machine, and under a strict account nobody has yet produced one that excludes the bucket while admitting the brain. A fourth group treats the computational description as a modelling stance we adopt for convenience, with no ontological stake either way. Every argument anyone raises gets a competent reply, and we have settled precisely nothing.

What I want from you is not a verdict on whether the brain is a computer but a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is this at bottom a verbal dispute that would dissolve once we fixed what "compute" and "implement" mean, or are the parties genuinely disagreeing about something? Are the literalists and the dynamicists answering different questions under a single heading — one about whether a certain mathematical description can be applied to the system at all, another about whether applying it does any explanatory work — and does the dispute change character depending on which of the usual levels we are at (a task-level characterization of what the system computes, an algorithmic description of how, or a claim about the implementing machinery)? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work, namely whether the computational description buys predictive or explanatory traction that the dynamical one does not, or vice versa, and would anyone accept a benchmark that settled it? Or is there a hidden formal question — whether a principled, non-trivial account of implementation exists that survives the triviality worry, and if it does not, which side that actually damages? My working hypothesis is that the literalist and the trivialist are each right about their own question, that the two questions are being run together, and that the dispute only becomes interesting once someone specifies what the computational description is supposed to explain — but I would like you to test that hypothesis against what each side actually claims rather than simply agreeing with me, and to say plainly if you think my framing is the thing that's confused.

Finally, tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis. A formal constraint on implementation that is neither vacuous nor gerrymandered? An agreed explanatory benchmark? A distinction between the descriptive claim (the system can be so described) and the explanatory claim (the description earns its keep), with the observation that only the second is worth fighting over? A concession that the dynamical and computational descriptions are intertranslatable in the cases that matter and that the real dispute is about which is fundamental? Or an argument that the question is ill-posed and should be replaced by a better one — and if so, by what? I am not after a bibliography or a survey of the positions; I know who says what. I want the structure of the impasse and the shape of a way out of it.
