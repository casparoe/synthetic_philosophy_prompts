I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind that has gradually become a seminar on artificial intelligence, and I find myself unable to give a straight answer to what ought to be a straightforward question. The seminar keeps returning to whether large language models are agents, simulators, or something else, and the exchange has the character of a dispute that cannot be settled rather than one that is slowly being resolved. I want a diagnosis rather than a survey, and I want it to engage the positions as they are actually held rather than as they are caricatured.

The positions, as I reconstruct them. First, a behavioral or capacity view: agency is a matter of what a system can do — it represents ends, selects means, adjusts in light of feedback, and can be deflected from its ends by interventions that leave everything else intact. On this view the question is empirical, the evidence is behavioral, and the answer is that these systems are agents to a degree that increases with capability. Second, an internal-economy view: behavioral success is insufficient, because agency requires ends the system itself sustains — ends that generate reasons across contexts, that constrain what it will do when the prompt changes, and that it does not merely re-derive from whatever description it happens to have been handed. On this view a language model is a conditional continuation device whose apparent goals are supplied by the interlocutor; the appearance of agency is real at the level of the interaction and absent at the level of the system. Third, a simulator view: the model does not have ends, it models beings that have ends; attributions of agency belong to the simulated character rather than to the substrate, so asking whether the model is an agent is the mistake one would make in asking whether a novel had committed a murder. Fourth, a deflationary view, held by two of my students with more conviction than either would like to admit, that the whole dispute concerns a term of art whose criteria we are free to stipulate, so that the answer depends on which of the above questions one has chosen to ask.

What makes the impasse interesting is that arguments are not in short supply. When the first camp offers behavioral evidence, the second replies that it is evidence of the wrong kind. When the second camp asks for the internal structure, the first replies that this is a demand for a hidden essence no behavioral science could deliver. When the third camp offers its redescription, the other two treat it alternately as a rival thesis and as a verbal maneuver. The same pattern reappears whenever the seminar considers older cases — corporate agency, the thermostat, the nation that declares war, the animal that deceives — and those who disagree about language models disagree about those too, which suggests the disagreement is not really about language models.

One further wrinkle I have not seen addressed cleanly, and it is the reason I am asking. Everyone agrees that these systems are exquisitely sensitive to context: a single sentence of persona instruction can transform what the system appears to want. One side reads this as evidence of simulation, since the ends are supplied from outside; the other reads it as no evidence at all, on the grounds that any agent's goals are context-indexed — we want different things at a funeral and a football match, and no one concludes that our goals are therefore not ours. Consider, then, two systems: one whose apparent ends persist across contexts only because each context explicitly states them, and one whose ends persist without any such statement and constrain behavior in contexts that never mention them. If that distinction is the crux, the dispute is not conceptual at all but a difficult empirical question about what these systems represent — one that neither camp presently has the tools to answer. But I may be wrong, and I would rather be told so than have the point accommodated.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the parties are answering different questions under a single heading: a metaphysical question about what kind of thing the system is, an explanatory question about which level of description earns its keep in a theory of its behavior, and a normative question about when it is appropriate to hold something responsible. If that is right, the intractability is unsurprising. I want to know whether it is right, or whether a genuine substantive disagreement survives once the questions are separated.

1. The Positions, Restated (~200 words): correct anything I have mischaracterized, and name any position I have omitted that matters to the diagnosis.
2. The Kind of Disagreement This Is (~500 words): say whether the dispute is verbal, substantive, evidential, or a conflation of several questions, and defend the diagnosis against the most obvious objection to it.
3. Why It Has Stalled (~300 words): identify what each side is doing that keeps the exchange from converging, including whether any party is smuggling in a criterion it will not state.
4. What Would Count as Progress (~300 words): say what would move the dispute — a distinction, a formal result, an experiment, an abandonment of the question — and whether any of these is available now.
5. Against My Hypothesis (~200 words): the strongest case that my own diagnosis is wrong.
