I am a novelist at work on a book about a city planner, and I have reached a point where the moral center of the novel depends on a dispute I cannot resolve. At a dinner party last month, two acquaintances of mine—one a policy analyst, the other a civil-liberties lawyer—fell into an argument about a proposal very like the one my protagonist is drafting. I took notes afterward, and I would like you to adjudicate their exchange move by move.

The proposal, as they described it, is a Corridor Charter. It would lock in a density plan along the city's transit corridors for thirty years. It can be amended only by a two-thirds vote of the council followed by a one-year waiting period. Repeal is harder. A repeal proposal must first be approved by a Review Panel of twenty-five citizens chosen by lot from among those who voted against the charter in the original referendum. If the panel rejects the repeal, no repeal proposal may be brought again for ten years. The panel may consider only evidence that was already in the public record at the time of the original referendum. Any amendment to the panel clause itself must also be approved by the panel. Finally, a review every five years is conducted by a board appointed by the charter's original drafters; the default recommendation is continuation.

The argument went roughly as follows.

The policy analyst said: This is a Ulysses contract. The city knows it will be tempted to downzone whenever the political winds change, so it binds itself. That is autonomy-enhancing, not autonomy-subverting. The Review Panel is a clever device: it gives a voice to the losers, preventing a transient majority from undoing a hard-won compromise. It is like a supermajority requirement, only more targeted. And the self-referential clause is simply a safeguard against the panel being abolished by the very majority it is meant to check.

The civil-liberties lawyer replied: It is not a Ulysses contract. A Ulysses contract requires a single agent who is the same person over time. A city is not a person; future citizens are not the same agent. You cannot bind people who did not consent. The Review Panel is not a voice for the losers; it is a permanent veto for a random sample of them. The self-referential clause makes it self-perpetuating. And the review board is appointed by the drafters, so the review is a rubber stamp. This is entrenchment, not precommitment.

The policy analyst answered: The city is a corporate agent with continuous institutions. The current generation has the right to bind itself; future citizens inherit the benefits. The panel is not permanent: its members are chosen by lot, so it is not a fixed faction. It is a deliberative filter, not a veto. And entrenchment is sometimes justified to protect minority rights against majority tyranny. The charter protects the poor from displacement. That is a precommitment to justice.

The lawyer said: Then call it what it is: a constitutional court with a lottery. And the problem remains: the future self is not the same as the present self. If the city changes its mind, that is not weakness of will; it is a change of preference. You cannot call every change of preference a failure of autonomy. That is paternalism. Also, the panel may consider only evidence from the original referendum, so it cannot respond to new evidence. That is not deliberation; that is freezing the past.

The analyst replied: The whole point is to make repeal costly. If new evidence is compelling, the panel can still vote to repeal; it merely has to overcome the default. And the default is not permanent; it is ten years. That is a reasonable cooling-off period. Besides, the poor are the ones who benefit from stability; they cannot afford to wait for the next political cycle.

The lawyer answered: A ten-year cooling-off period after a single rejection by a random panel is not a cooling-off period; it is a veto. And the panel's composition is not a representative sample; it is a sample of losers. That is not a deliberative filter; it is a structural bias. If you want to protect the poor, you can do it with ordinary legislation and judicial review, which are accountable.

Please adjudicate. Rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. I do not want a summary of the debate or a general essay on precommitment; I want a point-by-point verdict on the arguments as they were actually made. Which claims are sound, which are confused, and where does each speaker concede more than they realize? Is the Corridor Charter best understood as a Ulysses contract, a constitutional precommitment, or something else? What are both speakers missing? And, if the disagreement turns on a distinction, say exactly what the distinction is and whether it survives the details of the proposal. I am not asking for sources; I want the argument assessed on its merits.
