I have become convinced of a version of Leibniz's optimalism, and I want an honest map of its exposure. Put as plainly as I can: there is a determinate fact about the comparative overall value of possible worlds; among all the possible worlds there is at least one that is best; and the actual world is among the best. I am not especially drawn to the theological machinery, and I do not need it for the question I am asking — what I care about is the axiological core, that the phrase best possible world names something real and that actuality and optimality coincide.

What I want is what would count against it. Sort the defeaters by how decisive they are, and separate those that would merely embarrass the view from those that would kill it. I am after both internal and external objections, and I want to know which of them are live rather than merely conceivable.

On the internal side, the view seems to carry commitments about the structure of the value ordering over worlds, and I want to know which of them it can shed. Does it require a total ordering, or could the ordering be merely partial — with pairs of worlds where neither is better, worse, nor equal? Does it require a maximum, and does it require the maximum to be unique? If there could be several equally best worlds, what follows for the claim that one of them is actual, and does the answer change if the tie is only apparent? And can the view survive the thought that for any world there is a better one, in the way that for any number there is a larger one, so that the set of worlds has no best member at all?

On the external side, the comparison of worlds populated by different people, and by different numbers of people, seems to presuppose a way of weighing lives against lives that we do not have and that some think cannot exist; and the very idea of a well-defined totality of possible worlds may be a formal artifact rather than a domain with a real structure. I would like to know which of these pressures the view can absorb and which it cannot, and whether it stands or falls with the principle of sufficient reason — that is, whether the objections that look fatal are really objections to optimalism or objections to that principle. Tell me also what the view commits me to that its rivals do not: what I should expect of the world, or of our axiology, if it is true, that I would not expect otherwise.

For each defeater, give me the best reply a committed optimalist can make, so that I can see what survives the reply, and tell me which of the objections a defender would concede as a genuine cost and which they would reject as resting on a misunderstanding. Finally, I want a straight answer to the insulation question: has optimalism been qualified into something no evidence could touch — by treating best as relative to a standard we cannot independently access, or by restricting possible to what is feasible given how creatures would in fact choose — and if so, is that a defect in the view or an unavoidable feature of any view about the value of worlds taken as a whole? The consideration I would personally find hardest to answer is the one about the ordering: if the value of worlds can fail to have a best member, or can be defined only partially, I do not see how the position keeps its content, and I would like to be told whether that is a real problem or only a failure of my imagination.
