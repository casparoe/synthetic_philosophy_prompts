Write a dialogue between two cognitive scientists on the language of thought. One holds a broadly Fodorian view: thought has a combinatorial syntax and compositional semantics, and the systematicity of thought — that a thinker capable of the content of "the cup is left of the bowl" is thereby capable of "the bowl is left of the cup" — is best explained by a medium whose structure mirrors that of language. The other holds that thought is a high-dimensional vector space in which concepts are points or regions and complex contents are assembled by algebraic operations on vectors, so that compositionality is a property of the geometry rather than of any symbolic code.

To keep the exchange concrete, have them argue over one shared case: a network trained only on statements about pairs of familiar objects in left-of relations generalizes correctly to novel pairs and to the converse relation, with no identifiable symbol-manipulation step. Each should state what their own view predicts about this case and what it can and cannot explain, and each should press the other on two questions: whether systematicity is a constraint on the architecture or merely an artifact of the training distribution, and whether a vector-space representation can distinguish representing a relation from merely encoding a correlation among co-occurring features.

Neither should be a strawman. Each should grant the strongest objection to their own position and say how it survives, and each should engage the other's actual claims rather than talking past them.
