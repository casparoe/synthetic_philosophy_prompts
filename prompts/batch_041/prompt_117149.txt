I am trying to pin down a number on a proposition: a short-term visitor to a foreign city has a moral obligation that can be outweighed, and not merely a prudential or courtesy reason, to comply with local everyday conventions—tipping at the expected rate, queueing in the local fashion, not jaywalking where locals treat it as an offence—when compliance is cheap and the convention is neither unjust nor gravely harmful. I have been distinguishing two views: that such conventions are co-ordination devices whose only moral force comes from the disruption caused by non-compliance, and that they are local claims on a visitor's conduct that generate an obligation of respect independently of disruption. My own credence is about 0.55; a colleague's credence is about 0.25. Please give me your credence, decomposed into the sub-questions it depends on: whether everyday conventions generate moral obligations at all or only co-ordination-based reasons; whether the visitor's transience, lack of consent, or status as a guest weakens or cancels any such obligation; whether the convention's content (co-ordination versus symbolic respect) matters; and whether the visitor's home conventions have any claim in the local setting. Give a rough probability for each, say how they combine, and identify which sub-question your number is most sensitive to and what evidence or argument would move it most. Answer in British English.
