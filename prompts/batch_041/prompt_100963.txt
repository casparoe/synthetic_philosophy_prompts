A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about whether Boomers, Millennials, and Gen Z name real social kinds or just convenient marketing labels. I found myself defending the view that generations are real kinds: that being formed in a particular historical window leaves durable, causally efficacious dispositions that distinguish cohorts in ways not reducible to age or period. What would count against that? I don't mean the familiar complaint that the boundaries are arbitrary; I mean the evidence and arguments that should actually move someone who holds the view. In particular, how should a generational realist handle the fact that age, period, and cohort effects are so hard to disentangle, and what should they make of findings where within-cohort variation swamps between-cohort variation? Suppose the apparent cohort differences vanish once you control for age, period, region, and education, or that the generation label predicts attitudes only because it proxies for stage of life. Would that refute the view or merely challenge a particular operationalization? Which of these would a committed defender concede as genuine defeaters, which would they dismiss as merely awkward, and is there a version of generational realism that risks nothing at all—and if so, is that a problem? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward.
