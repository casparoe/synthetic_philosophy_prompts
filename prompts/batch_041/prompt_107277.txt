Ok so some context first: I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background at all, but on my team I've somehow become the person who reads about this stuff alot, and next Thursday I have to present to our whole trust & safety org (~15 people) a memo where I argue we should kill the "show people the other side" feature we've been building for a year. My thesis in one line: the real problem on our platform isn't filter bubbles, it's something I've started calling the distrust ratchet.

Here's the distinction I'm building on. An "epistemic bubble" is when you just never happen to run into opposing views, so you don't even know what you're missing. An "echo chamber" is different -- you've been taught to actively distrust anyone outside, so a correction from outside makes you dig in rather than doubt. I think most of our users are in the second state, not the first. The ratchet part is my own addition: once someone's been corrected in a way that felt like a humiliation or a gotcha, every later correction lands harder, so extra exposure to the other side makes them worse, not better. If that's right then our whole roadmap is backwards -- we're optimizing for how much of the other side people see when we should be optimizing for who the message comes from.

The audience, roughly: (a) mostly ML and infra engineers, people who think in metrics and get visibly annoyed at anything that smells unfalsifiable or like folk psychology; (b) three policy/trust-&-safety folks who want to know what we ship Monday and are bored of "it's complicated"; (c) one person with a social-psych background who's actually read this literature and will know exactly which parts I'm mangling; (d) my manager, who has to defend the budget.

Prep me for the Q&A.
