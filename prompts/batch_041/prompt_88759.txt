Analyze the replication crisis in social psychology through the lens of Good's theorem on the value of information. Good's theorem says, roughly, that a Bayesian agent with a fixed prior and a utility function over states and actions should expect free information to leave her at least as well off in expected utility; the theorem concerns expected value ex ante, not the realized value of any particular piece of evidence, which may of course be bad news. The replication crisis is the wave of large-scale attempts to reproduce published findings in social psychology, many of which failed, together with the ensuing debates over pre-registration, adversarial collaboration, and the publication of null results. Using the theorem, say what it implies about how individual researchers and the field as a whole should have treated replication attempts and negative results, and then say where the mapping breaks: does the theorem apply to a scientific community rather than a single agent, does it survive when information is costly or when researchers' utility depends on their beliefs and careers, and what does the episode show about the theorem's scope or its limits? Be specific about who the agent is, what counts as information, and what the relevant utility is. Answer in American English.
