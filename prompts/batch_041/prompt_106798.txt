I've more or less bought the cluster-concept line on life: 'alive' isn't a natural kind, it's a family-resemblance term, and borderline cases — viruses, prions, a mule, the mitochondria in your own cells, a self-replicating crystal if we ever dug one up — simply have no determinate answer, and pressing 'but is it really alive?' is close to a category mistake. What counts against that? I want the defeaters ranked from decisive to merely awkward, and I want the ones a cluster theorist would themselves concede, not just the ones a critic would lob over the wall. In particular: does the view survive the lineage-level criterion a lot of astrobiologists lean on — life as a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution — where the thing that's alive is the lineage and not the individual, so a sterile mule and a single virion come out on the wrong side? If that reframing does draw a principled line, is the indeterminacy claim dead, or just relocated to a smaller set of cases? And be honest about whether 'no fact of the matter' has been quietly qualified into unfalsifiablity — if nothing could count against it, is that a bug or is it the whole point of the view? I'd be most moved by a case where I'd have to actually give the position up rather than just stick another caveat on it.
