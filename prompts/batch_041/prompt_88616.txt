I am a retired plant physiologist — thirty-odd years spent studying drought stress in cereal crops — who has, since retiring, been working through moral and political philosophy at a pace that embarrasses no one but myself. I mention the earlier career because it has left me with a stubborn intuition that famines are, at bottom, agro-ecological events: the rains fail, a blight or a pest arrives, the soil gives out, and people starve. So when a popular history of famine I recently picked up told me that this picture is naive, that famines are political crimes rather than natural disasters, and that this was established by Amartya Sen's work on Bengal, I found myself half persuaded and half suspicious. Before I start repeating the claim at dinner parties, I would like a careful audit of it.

Please take the role of a philosopher of the social sciences with a firm command of the historical and theoretical literature on famine, and reason through this step by step. Begin by reconstructing, in your own words, the strongest version of the "famine as political crime" thesis, so that we are sure we are grading the right target and not a caricature. Then go through the following summary, which is how the popular book put it, sentence by sentence, telling me what a careful defender of Sen would cheerfully accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong: "Sen showed that famines are never caused by a lack of food. What really happens is that the food is there but people cannot afford it — the market sends it to whoever holds the money, so a famine is just a distribution failure. That is why the Bengal famine of 1943 killed millions even though the harvest was normal, and it is why famines only occur under dictatorships: no democracy has ever had one, because a free press forces governments to act. The practical upshot is that famine relief is never really about food; it is about income, and the right response is to treat famine deaths as mass murder."

I would also like you to test the thesis against two other cases I know only vaguely — the Irish famine of the 1840s and the Great Leap Forward — and tell me whether one mechanism explains all three, or whether the political-crime claim quietly needs different machinery for each (colonial laissez-faire and the Poor Law in one, state requisition and export in another). Finally, tell me what to make of a hybrid view I have sketched for myself: that the natural trigger is real and not a fiction, but that whether a bad harvest becomes a famine at all is gated by politics — by who holds entitlements, how prices and wages move, and whether relief is permitted. Is that a genuine third position, or have I simply restated the entitlement view while keeping a sentimental attachment to rainfall? Give me a clear verdict on how fair the book's summary is, what I should say instead, and where the strongest remaining disagreement lies. Think carefully, and do not smooth over the parts where the cases pull in different directions.
