I advise a foundation that has committed to funding roughly ten researchers for four years on conceptual work — philosophy, history of ideas, and theory, not empirical aesthetics — on authenticity and the value of originals. They have asked for an agenda rather than a survey or a set of readings, and they will read the reasoning more closely than the list. Tell me which open questions in this area most deserve that effort, rank them, and defend the ranking: for each, why it is upstream of the questions below it, how far it can be settled by argument alone (without museum access, visitor studies, or market data), and what would move it up or down the order. Where two questions are entangled, say which one you would sacrifice if only one could be funded.

Assume the following as fixed background, since I want the agenda to concern what remains genuinely open rather than disputes that reproduction technology has already resolved. Reproductions of paintings, sculptures, and manuscripts can now be made that are indistinguishable from originals to most viewers under most conditions, so the empirical question of discriminability is largely closed while the philosophical question of what follows from it is not. Provenance is increasingly established by digital records that are fragmentary, contested, and sometimes deliberately falsified, so what counts as the original is often a matter of institutional testimony rather than any inspectable feature of the object. Museums routinely display facsimiles while originals travel, are repatriated, or sit in storage for conservation, and viewing a facsimile is, for most visitors, reported to be the same experience as viewing the original. Generative systems can produce new works in the style of a named artist, and can be trained on corpora that are themselves contaminated with forgeries, so the causal route from an artist's hand to an object in front of you can be broken twice over.

One candidate decomposition, which I offer for you to accept, reject, or replace: distinguish token authenticity (this is the particular object the artist made), type authenticity (this is a correct instance of the work), and chain authenticity (the causal and epistemic route from artist to viewer is unbroken). Is that the right cut, or does it collapse two things together or leave something out?

Finally, say where the foundational question, once answered, would actually bite: which live disputes — restitution claims, forgery law and its mental-state requirements, insurance and valuation practice, the ethics of AI-generated works in a living artist's style, the conservation decision to restore or to leave a damaged original alone — would come out differently depending on how it is settled, and which of those disputes are, despite appearances, indifferent to it.
