I'd like you to take on the role of a philosopher of probability — someone who works on the metaphysics of objective chance and on the epistemology of credence — and write me an essay that settles a controversy, rather than merely surveying it. I'm a graduate student spending the term in a reading group on chance and credence, and the essay is meant to resolve something that has divided the group. Please think carefully before you begin, and start by brainstorming the main positions and the strongest argument for each, so that the final essay is a considered verdict rather than a first reaction.

The starting point is the familiar deference principle, usually credited to David Lewis, according to which a rational agent's credence in a proposition should equal its objective chance, at least when the agent's total evidence is admissible in the relevant sense and does not itself settle how the chance event turns out. Almost everyone in the group accepts this in the ordinary case. The dispute is about what the principle requires once the chance is itself something we are uncertain about — that is, once our evidence leaves several chance hypotheses open and we assign them probabilities of their own. One member of the group proposes a revision she calls the Deference-to-Chance-under-Uncertainty principle, which she claims is stronger than anything the original principle yields and which she thinks we must adopt to avoid a class of counterexamples; the rest of us suspect her revision is either derivable from the original principle or ad hoc. To make the disagreement concrete, consider a two-stage device: a machine first selects one of several biased coins by an objective chance process whose parameters we know, and then flips the selected coin. We are told the first-stage distribution but never learn which coin was selected. The question is whether we should defer to the second-stage chance (the bias of the coin that was in fact selected) or to the mixture of the two stages, and whether it matters that the first stage in some sense undermines our access to the second.

Write an essay that adjudicates this. Weigh the main arguments on both sides, give the concrete case the attention it deserves, and reach a definite conclusion: does the original deference principle need amending, and if so, exactly what does the amendment say and why is it not ad hoc? If you conclude it does not need amending, explain precisely why the alleged counterexamples fail. Along the way, say how much your verdict depends on whether one is a Humean or a non-Humean about chance, and whether the principle should be read as a substantive constraint on rationality or as something closer to a definition of what chance is. Don't end on a non-committal note, and don't hedge by declaring the question merely terminological unless you can show that the apparent disagreement really does dissolve into a verbal one.
