Exam question (20 marks). A rubber band is stretched taut at t1 and slack at t2. Explain why this unremarkable fact is supposed to create a difficulty for the view that a material object is wholly present at every moment of its existence, and state precisely what the difficulty is. Then set out (a) the perdurantist's response, and (b) one response available to the endurantist that does not invoke temporal parts. Finally, assess the following suggestion: the perdurantist's response is the temporal analogue of the counterpart-theoretic treatment of de re modal predication, so that the two stand or fall together. Does the analogy illuminate the dispute, and does it show that perdurantism is committed to counterpart theory?

Student's answer.

The difficulty is that shape looks like an intrinsic property. A rubber band is taut or slack in itself, not merely in relation to other things, and if it is taut at t1 and slack at t2 then, on the view that it is wholly present at both times, one and the same thing is both taut and slack. That is a contradiction unless we say that it is taut at t1 and slack at t2; but saying this appears to turn its shape into a relation between the band and a time, which is exactly what intrinsicality was supposed to exclude. The endurantist must therefore either give up the intrinsicality of shape or give up the claim that the band is wholly present at both times.

The perdurantist takes the second option. The band is a four-dimensional whole composed of temporal parts; it is the t1-part that is taut and the t2-part that is slack. Each part has its shape simpliciter, so no single part is both taut and slack, and shape stays intrinsic, since a part's shape does not depend on anything outside that part. The price is that the whole is never taut or slack in its own right; only the parts are. Perdurantists accept this and reply that this is what change consists in — different parts having different properties.

The endurantist takes the first option in a modified form, holding that the band has its shape at a time, where having-at is taken as primitive. The band itself persists, whole, through the change. This is coherent, but it leaves it unclear in what sense the band's tautness is a feature of the band rather than of the pair consisting of the band and the time.

Perdurantism also sits comfortably with the B-theory of time. If all times are equally real, and objects are spread out along the temporal dimension as they are along the spatial ones, then perdurantism is the natural view, and it denies that anything literally passes from present to past. Endurantism, by contrast, is at home with the idea that only the present exists, since an object cannot be wholly present at times that do not exist.

There is a further commitment worth flagging. The perdurantist's move is structurally the same as the counterpart theorist's: just as the counterpart theorist says that the actual band is not literally taut in another world but has a counterpart there that is, the perdurantist says that the band is not literally taut but has a part that is. Since the two moves are the same, perdurantism carries a commitment to counterpart theory about de re modality, and its treatment of change stands or falls with its treatment of possibility.

One last point. Because the endurantist has the band wholly present at each time, she cannot allow an object to gain or lose parts: a band that loses a fragment, or a person who loses a limb, would not be the same object afterwards. This tells further against endurantism.
