I'm a fourth-year PhD student in the philosophy of economics, and the organizer of next month's workshop on welfare measurement has asked each of us to circulate a one-page statement of our position in advance so that the commentators have something to aim at. I'd rather discover now than in the session which parts of mine I can't actually hold together. I work on the measurement side of this, so the list below isn't casual: I would defend each item on its own.

(1) To prefer A to B just is to be disposed to choose A over B when both are available; a preference that is in principle disconnected from any possible choice is a manner of speaking rather than a state we could measure. (2) Welfare is the satisfaction of preferences, and the business of welfare economics is to rank policies by how well they satisfy the preferences people have. (3) Choices are pervasively shaped by frames, defaults and inattention, so an analysis that takes every choice at face value will be wrong in predictable directions; where the evidence is good we should evaluate policies against the ordering the agent would have under full attention and adequate deliberation, which I've been calling her residual preference ordering. (4) Transitivity and completeness are requirements of practical rationality, not just convenient idealisations for representation theorems; an agent whose choices cycle is making a mistake, not merely exhibiting an exotic preference structure. (5) The preference-satisfaction view gives no warrant for interpersonal comparisons of welfare, so the distributional questions that dominate political argument fall outside the competence of welfare economics. (6) When someone reports contentment with a life an outsider would call deprived, we should take the report at face value; calling it an artefact of adaptation and substituting our own judgment of what she really prefers is paternalism wearing the costume of measurement. (7) None of this unsettles my ordinary practice, which includes treating choice experiments and survey responses as evidence about preferences and using von Neumann-Morgenstern utility functions to talk about risk.

What I want is an audit, not a defence. Which of these are genuinely in tension with which, which pairs only look like they are, and for each real tension, what would have to give? Please order the tensions by how much the repair would cost me rather than listing them as equally bad, say which single revision would clear up the most, and be explicit about how confident you are in each of your claims — in particular, flag the one you're least sure about, including if the claim you're least sure of is that some pair of my items is compatible.
