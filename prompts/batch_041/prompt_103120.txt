ok so I've been trying to actually pin down what's going on with Goodhart's law and I keep feeling like half the "refinements" people offer are just the original claim wearing a hat. so I made up a list of candidate amendments/generalizations — some are mine, some I half-remember from somewhere — and I want you to tier them, separately for each item, don't merge them. use these tiers: S = a genuinely distinct, load-bearing principle I'd actually build on; A = useful restatement that sharpens something real; B = true but shallow; C = basically the same as something above it, or unfalsifiable; F = probably just wrong. one sentence of why per item, and flag any that are secretly the same claim as each other.

the list:

1. the classic one: any metric that gets used to make decisions stops measuring what it measured when it was only used to describe.

2. the damage scales with the tightness of the feedback loop, not with how bad a proxy the metric is — a metric nobody acts on can't be gamed no matter how crudely it tracks the goal.

3. when you optimize hard against a proxy you're selecting for the region of input space where proxy and goal come apart, so the failure isn't noise, it's a systematic drift you're actively steering into.

4. regressional version: even with zero gaming, picking the top of a noisy proxy selects for the cases where the noise was lucky, so the extreme of the metric is biased away from the goal.

5. adversarial version: any public metric becomes a coordination point, so gaming is a Schelling equilibrium and gets worse the more agents share the metric.

6. the mirror image: a metric that's ignored tends to get *more* accurate over time, because people who care about the goal self-select into fixing it and nobody's gaming it.

7. Goodhart is just the multi-agent case of overfitting, nothing special.

8. the law is really about the gap between what you can measure and what you care about, and that gap is irreducible — any fix just relocates the gaming to the next level.

9. if you make the metric hard to game, you've mostly just made it expensive to improve, so you trade gaming for stagnation.

then two follow-ups: if I had to give a fraction for how much of the real-world spec-gaming problem is captured by the S-tier alone, what number would you put on it (just 0 to 1) and what's the single most important thing the S-tier leaves out? and — is there a version of the law that's actually about the *optimizer's internal model* of the metric rather than the metric itself, because I have a nagging feeling that's the real crux and niether of the above quite says it. if so, write that version and say where it'd land in the tiers.
