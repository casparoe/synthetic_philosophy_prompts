I sit on the exhibitions committee at a natural history museum, and we have to vote on the script for a new permanent gallery about the shift from foraging to farming. Design freeze is the 14th, so I need to circulate a memo before then and I'm definately torn. The curator has built the whole gallery around the idea that taking up agriculture was a catastrophy for human beings: the central panel would say outright that farming made individual lives shorter, sicker, and more unequal, and the spine of the exhibit is a before-and-after comparison, forager skeletons taller and better nourished, farmer skeletons with cavities and signs of anemia, and then the first really big houses and the first really rich graves. She's not wrong that the evidence runs that way in a lot of places, and the gallery is beautiful. Our largest trustee donor, whose family money is in agribusiness, thinks it's nostalgia dressed up as science and that we'd be telling school groups civilization was a mistake; he's threatening to pull the education wing. I don't want to split the difference and produce mush.

What I need from you is the memo: a clear recommendation, approve the script, approve it with specific changes I can hand the curator, or send it back for rework, plus the reasoning, plus an honest account of the strongest thing the trustee has going for him. Don't just restate the evidence, the committee has read the dossier. I want the argument about what we're actually claiming.

Two things I'd like you to take a position on. First, whether the interesting question is even about how well off the average person was, since farming also multiplied how many people there were. A board member said at the last meeting that this is just the repugnant conclusion with stone tools, nobody had a good reply, and I suspect he's onto something. Second, how much the labels should hedge. Our label standards require us to distinguish what's well established from what's actively disputed, and I keep noticing that the whole idea of a single agricultural revolution is itself pretty shaky, since in some places people settled down, stored food, and got hierarchical well before anyone planted a seed, and plenty of foragers were sedentary and status-conscious. And is it even fair to compare one way of life against another, or does that smuggle in a standard neither group would have accepted? If the answer is that we can't compare, say so, but then tell me what the panel is supposed to say instead, because we do have to put something on the wall.

American English please, and keep it short enough that I can read it out loud at the meeting without losing the room.
