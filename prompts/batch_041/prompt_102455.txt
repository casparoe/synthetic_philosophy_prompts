For a graduate seminar on the ethics of lying, I am assembling a handout that maps the logical space of responses to the murderer-at-the-door case, and I want the map before I want the exegesis. Please write a taxonomy of the positions, laid out as a tree whose every branch node is labeled with the question that separates it from its siblings — a question a student could answer without already knowing which position the answer commits them to — and whose leaves are the positions themselves, each tagged with a name if it has one and with "unnamed" if it does not.

Build the tree from the space of possible answers first and only then locate the positions in it; do not organize it as a list of interpreters. The questions I most want the branches to turn on are these: whether the prohibition on lying is owed to the person addressed, so that his conduct can forfeit his claim to the truth, or whether it is owed to the speaker's own dignity (or to humanity in his own person), so that no one's conduct can release him from it; whether the prohibition attaches to asserting what one believes false or to producing a false belief by any means, which is the difference between lying and mere deception; whether the duty is to give the murderer a true answer, to avoid asserting a falsehood, or merely to refrain from assisting him; whether the case is a genuine conflict of duties to be resolved by weight, or a case in which the appearance of conflict dissolves once the duties are correctly described; whether consequences may enter into the specification of the duty (not whether they may override it, but whether they help determine what the duty is in the first place); and whether the wrong, if there is one, is a wrong to this hearer in particular or a wrong to the practice of assertion itself, and so to no one in particular.

The tree has to sort a fixed set of cases, and I want each case to appear at a branch point where the positions genuinely come apart: the plain case; the true-but-misleading deflection; the flat refusal to answer; the murderer who is a magistrate entitled by law to demand an answer; the lie told to save the speaker rather than a third party; the courtesy lie told where no one is endangered; the case in which the person hidden has consented in advance to being lied about, so that the question arises whether a duty that belongs to the speaker alone can be waived by anyone at all; and the case in which the danger at stake is the murderer's own, so that the truth would harm the person asking.

Where a combination of answers leaves a cell of the space unoccupied, mark it and say in a sentence whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because no one has bothered to occupy it. Where two positions land on the same leaf because the questions cannot separate them, flag the pair and say which further distinction would be required. Keep the instrument to at most seven branch questions and at most eight leaves, and keep each leaf to a one-line statement of the position plus one sentence naming its most awkward commitment. No citations or reading list are needed; I want the map, not the sources.
