I help run a volunteer tool library and repair workshop out of a church basement in my neighborhood — around forty volunteers, a bank account with about nine thousand dollars in it, a cash box for the day's donations, a key to the building, and a workshop with table saws and a grinder in it. Once a month the steering group meets and I am the one who has to bring the names: who gets a key, who is allowed to take the cash box home overnight, who trains new volunteers on the saws, who gets asked to take a break for a while. I have no training for any of this; I got the job because I kept showing up. Each decision takes me maybe twenty minutes and then a month of living with it, since most of these people are my neighbors and two of them are people I would call at two in the morning. Anything involving a key or the cash box has to be agreed by me, the treasurer, and one other steering member, so whatever I use has to be something I can say out loud to the three of us at a kitchen table and have it sound like a reason rather than a feeling.

I keep arguing about this with a friend who is finishing a doctorate in philosophy. He says I think I am judging what kind of person someone is — trustworthy, careful, generous — when what I am actually doing is guessing at what they will do next, and that the guessing is worse than I think, because what people do depends enormously on the situation they are in. He tells me about experiments in which people who would describe themselves as decent walk past someone who needs help because they are late for something, and about people who stay silent about an obvious error because everyone else in the room is silent, and he says there is an old argument between philosophers who think a person's character is the thing to look at — he calls them virtue ethicists — and psychologists who say that what we call character is mostly a story we tell afterwards about people we have already decided we like. He is probably simplifying; if he is, I want to know how. The part I keep chewing on is not the debunking but what he said after it: that the useful move is to stop asking whether someone is a good person and start asking in which situations someone is good and which situations make them worse. I would like you to turn all of this into a procedure I can actually run, in order, in about twenty minutes, without any training, on a name in front of me: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a good enough answer to each; which steps end the process with a no; and which steps are not factual questions at all but calls about what kind of place we want this to be, which I should be handing to the steering group rather than deciding myself. It has to be honest about the psychology rather than pretending a checklist can read a soul — if the honest answer is that most of the time the procedure should tell me to change the situation rather than judge the person, say so, and tell me what that looks like in a tool library. Three cases it has to handle: the volunteer everyone adores, who is warm and generous with everyone and has missed roughly a third of the shifts they signed up for; the volunteer nobody likes, who is short with people and has been complained about twice, and who has never missed a shift and once walked a lost wallet back across town; and my oldest friend in the group, who has asked for a key and who I would hand one to tomorrow without thinking. At least one of those should come out against what I would have decided on my own, and I want you to tell me plainly which one and why.

Please organize it as: a section headed 'The decision I am really making' (about 150 words, saying in plain terms what the procedure is going to output); a section headed 'What character can and cannot tell me' (about 250 words, on what the virtue ethicists are actually committed to and what survives the psychologists' complaint); a section headed 'The procedure, step by step' (about 700 words, numbered, each step giving the question, what counts as a sufficient answer, what a no means, and whether it is a fact call or a values call, followed by a short paragraph on which distinctions from the theory you dropped to make this usable and what dropping them costs me); a section headed 'Where the procedure stops and a person decides' (about 200 words, saying who that person should be); three worked sections headed 'Worked example: the one everyone loves who keeps not showing up', 'Worked example: the one nobody likes with a spotless record', and 'Worked example: my oldest friend' (about 250 words each); and a closing section headed 'What this procedure cannot do' (about 250 words, including how I will know when it has misfired, and one paragraph on the volunteer whose life has just fallen apart — out of work, sleeping on a friend's couch, asking for a key — because that is the case where I am most likely to punish a situation and call it a person).
