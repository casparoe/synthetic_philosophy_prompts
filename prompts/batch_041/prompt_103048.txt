Ok so I'm giving the opening remarks at a two-day workshop I organized, on why some countries are rich and others aren't -- the institutions crowd, the geography crowd, the culture crowd, a couple of statisticians who think the whole literature is a dumpster fire, and two funders in the front row who don't know the difference between Acemoglu and a cappuccino. I have about ten minutes and then we start the first panel, so no pressure. Write the remarks.

Here's what I want to build the whole thing around, and I think it's a genuinely useful distinction that people haven't quite laid out this way: we keep answering three different questions with the same one sentence, and they come apart badly. First, which factor accounts for the most of the variation in income across countries as they stand right now? Second, if you could actually change one factor in one country, which would bend its trajectory? Third, which of these claims could we ever establish as causal at all, given that nobody randomized anything, the "treatment" was applied two centuries ago, and every candidate cause is also downstream of the other two? My hunch is most of the shouting is people answering Q1 and thinking they've answered Q3. I want the remarks to make that separation vivid with at least one concrete-feeling example (invent it if you have to, just make it the kind of thing a historian in row 3 will nod at rather than wince at), and I want the endogeneity problem to land as "this is why it's hard," not as "therefore nothing can be known" -- I'm a skeptic but I'm not a nihilist, and I refuse to open a workshop by telling everyone the next two days are pointless.

It has to work for the whole room. The historians should feel I'm not pretending a regression with a lagged instrument settles the Renaissance. The statisticians should not catch me saying "explains" or "causes" or "the effect of" in any way they'd make a face about. And the funders should leave the room at the coffee break still believing there's something worth funding -- so give me one honest thing I can say to them when they corner me, that isn't "give up."

Dont cite any books, papers, or specific authors and don't quote anyone, I will absolutely get one wrong from the podium and it will be humiliating. Keep the whole thing under 700 words. Put the one sentence I most want them to walk out with in the first two sentences, then spend the rest of the remarks earning it. And please keep it sounding like a person talking, not a keynote -- I'm going to read this out loud to a room that includes my department chair.
