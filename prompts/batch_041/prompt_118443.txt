Question: Mill's liberalism is often defended against the charge of imperial complicity by pointing out that the harm principle is expressly restricted in its scope. Assess that defence. Your answer should (i) state the scope restriction and the rationale Mill gives for it, (ii) explain how his account of how a free state ought to govern a dependency bears on that rationale, and (iii) say whether the restriction renders his position consistent or whether it leaves a tension, and what the tension would be.

Student's answer:

Mill's harm principle holds that the only purpose for which power may be rightfully exercised over a member of a civilized community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others, and that his own good is not a sufficient warrant. The principle is therefore not a universal principle of political morality but one that applies at a certain stage. The rationale is that the principle presupposes agents who can direct their own lives and learn from their own mistakes; where that presupposition fails, paternalistic rule may be justified, and Mill says despotism over those he calls barbarous can be legitimate provided the end is their improvement and the means actually achieve it. So the restriction is not a cynical exception tacked on to save the doctrine; it follows from what the doctrine assumes about the governed.

The same exception explains Mill's position on India. In his essay on non-intervention he argues that the ordinary rules of international morality cannot obtain between a civilized nation and a barbarous one, because reciprocity is impossible: barbarous peoples have no rights as a nation except a right to such treatment as will fit them for becoming one. That is the licence for British rule. It also means that, strictly speaking, there are no moral constraints on how a dependency is treated, since the constraints that would normally bind a ruler are precisely the ones suspended. Mill therefore cannot be charged with inconsistency: he was not applying liberal principles to India and failing; he was declining to apply them, on grounds he states openly.

His account of how a free state should govern a dependency confirms this. He argues that the only real test of a people's fitness for popular institutions is whether they are willing to brave labour and danger for their liberation, and he concludes that liberty bestowed by other hands will be neither real nor permanent. Since the Indian population showed no such willingness, Mill took them to be naturally incapable of self-government, which is why he opposed transplanting representative institutions to India and favoured rule by a trained official body instead. The test is a capacity test, and the capacity was absent.

One detail worth noting is that Mill welcomed the transfer of Indian administration from the Company to the Crown, on the ground that a responsible ministry would be more accountable to Parliament than a chartered body had been.

The upshot is that the scope restriction does the justificatory work, and it is principled rather than ad hoc. The residual tension is that Mill presents the harm principle as a principle of utility, yet the restriction is not itself derived from utility; it is a historical claim about stages of development that he never independently establishes. If that premise is rejected, the defence collapses, but that is a cost of the premise, not an inconsistency in the doctrine.
