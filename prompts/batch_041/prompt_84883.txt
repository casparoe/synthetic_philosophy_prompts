I want to know whether any principle underlies my verdicts about payoffs offered for attitudes. By "attitude" I mean belief, emotion, and intention alike; by "payoff" I mean anything that makes having an attitude useful or advantageous rather than correct.

Here are my verdicts.

- A stranger offers me a large sum to believe that the number of grains of sand on a particular beach is even. I have no reason at all to believe it, and would be irrational to be moved by the offer; the offer gives me a reason to want the belief, and to buy a pill that produced it, but not a reason to believe.
- The same stranger offers the sum for being afraid of a spider I know to be harmless. No reason to be afraid, though a reason to leave the room, and a reason to buy a pill that made me afraid.
- Offered the sum for being amused by a joke I find flat, I have no reason to be amused, though a reason to laugh convincingly.
- Offered the sum for intending tonight to drink a harmless but foul glass of mud tomorrow, with the money paid tonight and the intention revocable by morning, I think I do have a reason to form the intention, or at least that forming it would not be irrational. This verdict is the one I find hardest to reconcile with the first.
- A patient is offered a place in a trial only if he is hopeful about the outcome; the evidence is poor, but his doctor tells him that hope improves recovery. I think he has a reason to be hopeful, and not merely to act hopeful.
- A parent who knows the evidence is thin, I think, has a reason to trust her teenager, since being trusted tends to make a person trustworthy. But a spouse who tells me to admire her poems or she leaves gives me no reason to admire the poems, only a reason to fake it.

So payoffs look like reasons for hope, trust, and perhaps intention, and not for belief, fear, amusement, or admiration. My tentative principle is that a payoff is a reason for an attitude just when the attitude is partly constituted by a disposition to act, and never a reason when the attitude is merely a verdict on how things are. I suspect this is too crude: fear disposes me to act, and trust is as much a verdict as fear.

Tell me what principle, or small set of principles, captures these verdicts, which of my verdicts it forces me to give up, and what it implies for cases I have not considered. Please structure your answer as follows:

1. The pattern — no more than 100 words, saying what my verdicts have in common.
2. Candidate principles — two or three, each stated precisely, in about 400 words in total; for each, name the verdict it was built to save and the case that strains it.
3. What I would have to give up — about 150 words on which verdict the best principle cannot keep, and whether I should drop it.
4. Untested cases — three or four new cases of your own construction, with verdicts and a short argument for each, in about 300 words, at least one of which the principle decides counterintuitively.
5. What remains open — about 100 words.
