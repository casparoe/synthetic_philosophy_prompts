Question 3.

In this course we examined the boundary problem: the difficulty that democratic legitimacy is supposed to derive from the decisions of a demos, while the composition of the demos cannot itself be settled by a democratic decision without circularity, since any vote presupposes an electorate. Consider the following proposal.

The recursive self-enrollment proposal. A group of people occupies a territory and wishes to constitute a democratic polity without presupposing any prior boundary. A provisional committee fixes two things in advance: the territorial boundary of the intended polity, and a date D. The procedure then runs as follows.

Stage 0. Every person physically present within the territory on date D is invited to participate.

Stage 1. The participants vote on a rule specifying who is entitled to participate in later stages.

Stage 2. A new round of voting is held under the rule adopted at Stage 1. If the resulting electorate differs from the previous one, the new electorate votes again on the participation rule.

Iteration. The stages are repeated until the electorate is identical across two consecutive rounds. The stable electorate is declared the demos, and the participation rule in force at that point becomes the constitution's membership clause.

Its authors write: "No boundary is imposed from outside. The demos gives itself its own boundary, and the procedure therefore solves the boundary problem rather than begging it."

(a) State the boundary problem precisely, and explain why it is a problem for democratic theory rather than merely a practical difficulty about running elections. (6 points)

(b) Does the proposal solve the boundary problem, dissolve it, or merely relocate it? Defend your answer. (8 points)

(c) The proposal's Stage 0 rule admits everyone present in the territory on date D. Which of the following does that rule presuppose: the all-affected-interests principle (all whose interests are affected by a decision should have a say in making it) or the all-subjected-to-coercion principle (all who are subject to a polity's coercively enforced rules should have a say in making them)? Justify your choice. (6 points)

Student's answer.

(a) The boundary problem is that democratic theory tells us that decisions are legitimate when they express the will of the people, but it cannot tell us who the people are. Every democratic procedure — a vote, a referendum, a deliberative poll — needs a list of participants, and compiling the list is itself a decision that determines who is bound by the outcome. If the list is settled by a vote, the vote presupposes a list. If it is settled by something else — a monarch, a committee, a treaty, a historical accident — then something undemocratic is doing the most important work in the entire system. So the problem is not merely practical, a matter of registrars and rolls. It is that the very idea of self-rule seems to presuppose an answer to the question of who is doing the ruling, and any such answer will look question-begging from the standpoint of those it leaves out. This is why the difficulty is often described as a paradox rather than an inconvenience: a boundary is necessary for legitimacy and also a standing threat to it.

(b) I think the proposal solves the problem, and the iteration is what does it. The key move is that Stage 0 is not a boundary at all but an invitation: it does not say who belongs to the demos, it says who is invited to begin talking about belonging. Because every later round is authorized by the previous round's rule, the demos is self-constituting at every stage after the first, and the first stage makes no claim to constitute anything. When the electorate stabilizes we have a genuine fixed point: the set of participants that the participants themselves have chosen. At that point the boundary is not imposed from outside but is the output of the procedure, so it cannot be the procedure's presupposition. One might object that the committee fixed the territory and the date in advance, but these are administrative matters of the same kind as choosing a polling station or a meeting room, and nobody thinks a polling station is a demos. Moreover, at the fixed point no one is subject to rules they had no opportunity to shape, since the rule that governs membership is the rule the members themselves adopted. So the proposal does not merely relocate the problem; it dissolves it.

(c) The proposal presupposes the all-affected-interests principle. Presence in the territory on date D is a rough proxy for having one's interests at stake in what the new polity decides, and the iteration then allows the class of affected persons to settle its own extent: each round is a way of asking who else is affected and admitting them. The two principles — all affected interests, and all subjected to coercion — are really two formulations of the same expanding criterion, since to be coerced by a rule is to be affected by it, and to be affected by a rule in the way that matters here is to be subject to it. Stage 0 relies on the affectedness idea in its loosest form: anyone who is around to have interests at stake is invited. That is why the proposal can avoid taking a side in advance between citizens and non-citizens, residents and visitors; it lets affectedness sort them out over successive rounds.
