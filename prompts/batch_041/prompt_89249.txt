At a colloquium last term, two colleagues of mine fell into an argument over Aristotle's account of exchange, and I could not referee it. The first contends that because Aristotle treats money as existing only by convention, his account supplies no natural measure of exchange value; on his reading, the proportionality that is supposed to make an exchange just rests on an arbitrary token, and any modern attempt to reconstruct a theory of the just price from these texts merely dresses up convention as nature. The second replies that this conflates the token with the measure: for Aristotle the true measure is need, which is natural, and money is only a conventional stand-in — a pledge standing in for a need that may not be present at the moment of exchange — so the conventionality of coin no more undermines the naturalness of the standard than the conventionality of units undermines the reality of length. The first presses further: need is variable and subjective, and Aristotle himself concedes that such matters admit of no exact measure, so a standard that can only be approximated is no standard at all, and the going rate is doing the real work. He adds a circularity charge — if money is the measure of all things and money is itself by convention, then the measure of everything has a value fixed by convention. The second answers that the concession of inexactness is exactly what we should expect from a realist who takes exchange to track a natural but rough commensurability, and that Aristotle's comparison of exchange to a reciprocal proportion among distinct crafts is meant to show that the parties are commensurable through their needs, not through any coin; the circularity charge, she says, mistakes representation for grounding. In a final round, the first observes that Aristotle also condemns retail trade as unnatural, and asks how exchange can be natural in one place and unnatural in the other; the second says the natural/unnatural line tracks whether exchange serves household need or unlimited accumulation, not whether goods change hands.

Please adjudicate this dispute move by move, rating each of the moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating, and then tell me which of the two is closer to the truth overall and what both of them are missing. I want a committed verdict, not a survey of possible readings: take a definite position and defend it, marking clearly where your confidence is lower.
