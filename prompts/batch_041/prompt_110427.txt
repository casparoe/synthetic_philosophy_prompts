I'm a journalist on the ideas desk of a general-interest science magazine, at work on an essay about possibility, identity, and what it means to say that something might have gone otherwise. I came to this beat after four years as a backend and data engineer, and the vocabulary I actually think in is still the old one: relational schemas, primary and surrogate keys, foreign keys, junction tables, joins, replicas and shards, referential integrity, and above all entity resolution — the business of deciding when a row in one table and a row in another are "the same entity," and then forcing that decision to behave. What I want from you is a translation. Take the dispute between David Lewis's counterpart theory and the rival view on which one and the same individual literally exists in more than one possible world, and restate it as faithfully as you can in that vocabulary — both positions, one at a time, in the terms a working data engineer would use. I am not asking for a translation that flatters my vocabulary; where the database idiom carries assumptions the metaphysics does not share, say so explicitly rather than letting the idiom quietly settle the question for you.

Then give me the residue report. Claim by claim: what survives the trip intact and what the exact mapping is; what arrives only after being bent out of shape, and which joint you had to break to fit it; and what has no expression at all in my vocabulary — plus, in the other direction, anything my vocabulary insists on that the metaphysical debate has no room for. I would especially like you to press on four points. First, ordinary identity is an equivalence relation by definition, whereas the counterpart relation, as Lewis describes it, is neither transitive nor symmetric and can run many-to-one or one-to-many; is there any way for a schema to represent sameness that isn't an equivalence relation, or does every workable scheme — unique keys, deduplication, transitive closure over a graph of fuzzy matches — smuggle equivalence back in? Second, does the de re / de dicto distinction correspond to anything real in query terms, or does it collapse into the difference between a query over a whole relation and a query anchored to one particular row? Third, the familiar complaint that a counterpart of mine winning an election is no consolation to me — does that objection have any analogue in the database picture, or does it trade on a distinction between a record and the thing recorded that the picture cannot even state? Fourth, entity resolution works by matching attributes; is that closer to a qualitative account of what makes for a counterpart, or to the view that a thing's identity across worlds is primitive and simply not matchable from the outside?

Finally, do not hand me a menu of positions. Commit. Tell me which parts of the residue are a genuine defect of my framework — things databases simply cannot say — and which are a sign that the metaphysical dispute is less determinate than its practitioners suppose, and defend that verdict against the most obvious objection from the other side. If the honest conclusion is that the translation vindicates one of the two views, say which and why; if it dissolves the dispute, tell me precisely what is left over once the translation is finished, and why that remainder is or isn't worth caring about.
