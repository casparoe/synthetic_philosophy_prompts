I'm preparing an interview for a philosophy-of-AI podcast with a philosopher who has turned Daniel Dennett's late warning about AI-generated counterfeit people into a systematic position. Her view is that large language models are counterfeit persons in something close to Dennett's sense: they can be treated as intentional systems and display competence without comprehension, but their aboutness is derived from designers, training data, and users rather than original to them, and they lack consciousness and therefore any independent moral status. She also defends Dennett's proposed response: watermarking and mandatory disclosure, plus severe legal liability for creating or passing along counterfeit people. You are a philosopher of mind and AI ethics who knows Dennett's work in detail. Think carefully about the strongest internal tensions in this position, then draft eight to ten interview questions that press her where the view is most vulnerable rather than letting her restate it. Include at least one question each on (i) how the intentional stance and the original/derived intentionality distinction apply to LLMs, (ii) whether the counterfeit-money analogy can bear the normative weight once Dennett's deflationary account of the self as a center of narrative gravity is in view, (iii) whether the policy should target deception and impersonation rather than creation itself, and (iv) whether Dennett's illusionism about consciousness -- his view that consciousness is not a special inner glow but a matter of capacities and reports -- makes the counterfeit/original distinction unstable. For every question, add a follow-up that anticipates her most likely reply and makes the deflection harder. Keep the questions concrete and grounded in Dennett's actual arguments; do not ask for citations, reading lists, or bibliographies.
