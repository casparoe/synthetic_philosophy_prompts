I'm in a political epistemology reading group that has spent the term on proposals for supplementing or replacing elections with prediction markets, and one member has become an enthusiastic futarchist. He has a reconstructed argument that I find genuinely hard to fault in the middle, even though I cannot accept where it ends. Here it is, in the form he wrote it on the whiteboard:

(1) For any decision problem with a well-specified objective, a conditional prediction market — traders betting on how much of the objective would be realized under each option — yields a better estimate of which option best realizes the objective than any feasible alternative: deliberation, expert panels, votes, cost-benefit analysis.
(2) Where one procedure yields better estimates of which option best realizes a given objective than another procedure does, the decision ought to be made by the better procedure.
(3) Therefore, for any decision problem with a well-specified objective, the decision ought to be made by a conditional prediction market on that objective.
(4) A polity's choice of which objective to pursue is itself a decision problem with a well-specified objective: we can ask which candidate welfare metric, if adopted, would leave the members of the polity better off, and run a market on that.
(5) By (3), the metric should therefore also be chosen by a market.
(6) But the second-order objective used to score that market is either itself chosen by a market — in which case we repeat the exercise at the next level up — or it is not, in which case some objective was fixed by something other than a market.
(7) Furthermore, (3) applies to every decision with a specifiable objective, not only to public ones: whether to accept a job, whether to marry a particular person, whether to sell a kidney.
(8) So the principle either cannot be applied to itself, leaving the metric that all the markets score against to be settled by ordinary, non-market politics — precisely what futarchy promised to get past — or it applies everywhere, down to the most personal choices, which is absurd.

What I want is a diagnosis. Tell me which step I should give up, whether the trouble is a flaw in the reasoning or simply a premise that turns out to be expensive, why the step looked innocent enough to write down in the first place, and what giving it up costs me elsewhere — in particular, what I would then owe by way of an account of when a procedure's superior accuracy does and does not entitle it to decide. I would like to keep (1) if I possibly can, since I do think markets aggregate information well in the right conditions, but I am willing to lose (2) or (3) if that is the price. I have been told by two different people that the argument commits a scope error and that it is valid but simply has a conclusion I dislike, and I cannot tell which of them is right, so please do not assume the answer is obvious.

Format the response as follows: a section titled "The step I should give up" (about 150 words), naming the step and stating in one sentence whether the argument is flawed or merely costly; a section titled "Why it looked innocent" (about 200 words), explaining the intuitive pull of the step and the reading of the earlier steps that makes it seem to follow; a section titled "What giving it up costs" (about 250 words), tracing the consequences for the rest of the argument and for the practical proposal; a section titled "The other candidates, priced" (about 300 words), giving a short table with one row per remaining step — the step, what rejecting it costs, and what it leaves standing — followed by two or three sentences on which of these you would take if you had to; and a section titled "Bottom line" (about 100 words), stating the verdict plainly.
