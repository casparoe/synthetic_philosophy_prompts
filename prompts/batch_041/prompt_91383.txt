I've spent the better part of a year trying to understand why the question of whether the brain is a computer has survived half a century of philosophy and cognitive science without either side conceding anything, and what I want from you is a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than another survey of the positions. Both sides accept essentially the same neuroscience; they part company on nearly everything else; and I cannot tell whether that is because the disagreement is deeper than it looks or because it is not, at bottom, a disagreement about anything at all.

Concretely, this is how the impasse presents itself to me. The computationalist holds that cognition consists in the manipulation of representations by processes that can be specified formally, and that this is the only framework on offer that explains how a physical system could be sensitive to what its internal states mean rather than merely to their physics; she points to the record of computational modelling -- systems that predict behaviour, decode stimulus identity from neural activity, and so on -- as evidence that the framework is on the right track. Her critic does not deny any of that data, and presses one of two objections. The first is that the claim is trivially true: there is a familiar argument, associated with Putnam, that any ordinary open physical system with enough states realizes every finite-state automaton, so a rock computes as much as a brain does, and the computational description of the brain is no more explanatory than the automaton description of the rock. The computationalist replies by narrowing the relevant class of automata to those whose states are built up combinatorially from components, and by requiring that the implementing system's causal transitions track the automaton's transitions non-arbitrarily -- and I genuinely cannot tell whether that is a real fix or a relabelling of the arbitrariness. The second objection runs the other way: the brain is a continuous dynamical system whose behaviour is not decomposed into discrete state transitions at all, so there is no fact of the matter about which computation it performs, and any decomposition is imposed by the theorist's interests rather than found in the system. To this the computationalist replies that medium-independence is the whole point: the computational level is supposed to abstract away from the physics.

My own hypothesis -- and I would like you to tell me whether it is right, and if not, what the real structure of the dispute is -- is that the phrase 'the brain is a computer' is doing double duty, and that the impasse is largely an artifact of that. On one reading it is a claim about explanation: that computational descriptions are the right level at which to explain cognition, a methodological claim assessable by whether computational theories predict, unify, and control. On the other reading it is a claim about ontology: that the brain literally implements some particular computation, a claim that is either trivially true on a permissive account of implementation or, on a strict account, underdetermined by any possible evidence, since any finite record of a system's behaviour is compatible with indefinitely many programs. If that is right, the camps are not contradicting one another so much as answering different questions under one name. Here is a test case I keep returning to and cannot resolve on my own: consider a system whose state transitions follow a program's transitions only because we fixed the state-to-state mapping after observing the behaviour, and a system whose transitions follow the program's because its physics forces them to; does any principled account of implementation distinguish these two without appealing to our interests, and if it cannot, does that make the computationalist's claim observer-relative, or does it merely show that the permissive account of implementation was the wrong one to start from?

So: diagnose the disagreement. Is it verbal, a clash of explanatory projects, a hidden formal question about the relation of implementation, or a genuine empirical disagreement about the brain's causal structure that current evidence underdetermines? Are the two sides perhaps counting different things as data -- successes at the level of the model against failures at the level of the whole system? Is a substantive question buried inside that could in principle be answered, or is the question ill-posed as asked? Engage what each side actually says rather than the cartoon versions, and be willing to conclude that one side is simply confused if that is where the reasons lead. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis -- a distinction, a formal result about implementation, an experiment, or an argument that no progress should be expected here and why -- and say honestly which of the candidate diagnoses you would bet on and what would change your mind.
