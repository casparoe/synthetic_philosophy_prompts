The assignment question was:

"Isaiah Berlin distinguishes negative liberty from positive liberty. The republican tradition associated with Philip Pettit and Quentin Skinner distinguishes freedom as non-interference from freedom as non-domination. (a) State both distinctions as precisely as you can. (b) Is republican liberty a third concept of liberty, a variant of negative liberty, or a variant of positive liberty? Defend your answer. (c) Some critics argue that the republican account of domination cannot make sense of subjection that has no dominator — subjection to an impersonal market, to an entrenched bureaucratic routine, to a social norm that no one is in a position to alter. Explain the objection and say how a republican should respond to it."

The student wrote:

"(a) Berlin's two concepts answer two different questions. Negative liberty answers the question of how much I am left alone to do what I want: it is the absence of interference by other people. Positive liberty answers the question of who is in charge of me: it is being my own master, obeying a law that I have given myself. Berlin held that these are not merely different but rival ideals. He worried that positive liberty, by splitting the self into a higher and a lower part, invites the state to identify itself with the higher self and to coerce people in the name of their own real will, and that this is how the ideal of self-mastery turns into despotism.

(b) Republican liberty is a third concept, but the reason is easy to miss. Pettit's non-domination differs from non-interference not in kind but in degree. A person is dominated when they live under someone else's capacity to interfere, and non-domination therefore requires that interference be improbable, not merely absent. The difference between the liberal and the republican, on this reading, is that the republican wants a stronger guarantee — a higher probability that interference will not occur. Berlin, who treated the state as the chief threat to liberty, would presumably have classified this as negative liberty with a safety margin; the republican's innovation is the margin, not the concept. Skinner's neo-Roman version, which traces the ideal back to the Roman law of the free man, makes the same point: the free man is the one who is not in another's power.

(c) The objection is that some subjection has no author. If a market or a bureaucratic routine dominates me, there is no individual who is doing the dominating, so there is no one whose arbitrary interference I am exposed to. I think the objection succeeds against Pettit's formulation but not against the ideal itself, since a republican can say that the office-holder or the firm is the dominator even when no particular person is. And the kindly-master case shows why the objection has force: since the slave of a kindly master is unfree even though he is never actually interfered with, no one who is subject to a law can be free, because law always holds out the threat of interference behind it."
