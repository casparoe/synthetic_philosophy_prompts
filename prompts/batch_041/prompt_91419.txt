On the night of 27 January 1986, on the eve of the launch of Space Shuttle Challenger, the engineers at Morton Thiokol argued against flying. The forecast was for temperatures far below those of any previous launch; the engineers had documented a correlation between low temperatures and erosion of the O-rings sealing the booster field joints, and the coldest launch on record had produced the worst erosion they had seen; the engineers recommended against launching at any temperature below 53 °F, the lowest at which a joint had flown. Thiokol's senior management called a recess, conferred among themselves, and reversed the recommendation; the NASA managers who had pressed for the reversal accepted it. Challenger lifted off at 11:39 the next morning into air in the mid-thirties Fahrenheit; the aft field joint of the right booster failed shortly after ignition, and the vehicle broke apart 73 seconds into flight, killing all seven aboard.

I want to use this episode as a test case for Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge — the account on which a true belief counts as knowledge only if it tracks the truth, being both sensitive (were the proposition false, the believer would not believe it) and adherent (in the nearby worlds in which it is true, the believer would still believe it), with the counterfactuals evaluated relative to the believer's method — together with its notorious consequence that knowledge is not closed under known entailment.

Work through four things.

First, map the case before theorizing. Identify the relevant believers — the Thiokol engineers, Thiokol's senior management, the NASA managers who overrode the recommendation — and state precisely what each believed, distinguishing propositions that have truth values (that the field joints would fail at the forecast temperature; that the launch was safe, or its risk acceptable; that the flight would end in the loss of the vehicle and crew) from practical and normative judgments, such as that the launch should not proceed, which the tracking apparatus cannot evaluate at all. Note where the engineers' belief was a judgment of risk rather than a flat prediction, and what difference that makes.

Second, run the tracking test on each belief, for sensitivity and for adherence, and say whether the verdicts are credible. Did the engineers know, on this account, that the joints would fail? Did the managers know the launch was safe? Consider whether management's position is structurally Nozick's grandmother — the case of the woman who believes her grandson is healthy because she sees him, but would have believed it just the same had the family hidden his illness from her — and whether the engineers satisfy the method-relative version of sensitivity once the method is specified at the right level of generality, since the verdict can be flipped by describing that method more narrowly or more broadly.

Third, the closure question. If the engineers knew the joints would fail, and knew that a joint failure of that kind would destroy the vehicle, tracking theory must say whether they knew the vehicle would be destroyed — and since closure fails on this view, it may have to say they did not. Work out the verdict, judge whether it is defensible here or whether the case is a reductio, and then say what, if anything, the theory can register about the fact that the difference that mattered was not between knowing and not knowing but between knowing and being unable to make the knowledge move an institution.

Fourth, turn the analysis around: what does the episode show about the theory — confirmed, embarrassed, or simply silent? Then test an extension I want to try. Define institutional tracking as follows: an institution tracks a hazard when its decision procedures would not have issued the launch decision in the nearby worlds in which the hazard was absent, and would have issued the abort in the nearby worlds in which it was present. Is that a coherent generalization of the individual-level notion, a metaphor, or a category mistake? Can an institution fail to track a hazard that every individual within it tracks, or track one that no individual does? And say briefly whether a safety condition, or a reliabilist treatment of the barn-style cases, would give different verdicts about the managers than tracking does.

Be disciplined about the mapping, and be explicit where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak. No citations: I know the literature and will supply references myself, so refer to positions and arguments by name rather than by page.
