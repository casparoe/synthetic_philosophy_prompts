I'm on the patient advisory panel at our regional health authority -- I got drafted because my sister has a rare metabolic disorder and I kept showing up to meetings. I'm a nurse, not a philosopher, but I read too much. Last month they walked us through how the authority decides which drugs to fund. The analyst kept saying things like drug A is better than B, B is better than C, therefore A is better than C, and then ranked everything by cost per QALY, with a severity modifier and an end-of-life premium and a rule-of-rescue exception for ultra-rare diseases. I asked whether 'better than' is always transitive, and everyone looked at me like I'd asked whether arithmetic works. But I've been reading about spectrum arguments -- the kind where you go from a mild headache to quadriplegia in tiny steps, and each step seems no worse than the last, yet the endpoints are obviously not equally bad. If that's right, then betterness might be intransitive, and the whole ranking procedure might be built on a presupposition that doesn't hold.

What I want to know is what view of value this practice actually presupposes. Does it need betterness to be transitive, or does it only need a numerical scale that is transitive by construction? Does it presuppose that health states are commensurable and that comparisons are context-independent? Do the severity modifier, the end-of-life premium, and the rule-of-rescue exception presuppose a single global ordering, or are they patches that let the authority avoid the ordering when it gets embarrassing? And if transitivity fails, what exactly would have to change in a QALY-based funding rule? Could we keep the arithmetic but treat the numbers as a decision aid rather than a ranking, or would the whole thing collapse? I don't need a reading list or citations -- I need the implicit theory spelled out, the rival theories it rules out, and the pressure points identified.
