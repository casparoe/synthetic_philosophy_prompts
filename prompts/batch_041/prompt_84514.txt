I'm an undergraduate taking a decision theory course, and we've just finished the unit on the expected value of information. The headline result was Good's theorem: since a rational agent can always ignore additional information and act exactly as she would have without it, the expected value of free information is never negative. The proof struck me as airtight, and the theorem has a pleasing sweep to it — it seems to say that any reluctance to acquire information is a mistake. But the more I consider cases outside the textbook, the more I suspect I've misunderstood what the theorem is actually claiming, and I'd like you to diagnose my confusion rather than simply reassure me that the theorem is correct.

Here are three cases that seem to me to push against it. First, my roommate is a medical student and she tells me that patients sometimes decline predictive genetic testing because they would rather not know, and that some legal systems even protect a right not to know; she thinks this is perfectly rational. Second, a friend in an economics seminar told me that for an ambiguity-averse decision-maker the value of information can be negative, which sounds like a straight counterexample to Good. Third, I've noticed that I sometimes prefer not to look up the ending of a novel or the score of a match I've recorded, and it doesn't feel like a failure of rationality — it feels like knowing would itself make my situation worse. My question is whether Good's theorem really proves that information can never make a rational agent worse off, and if it doesn't, what exactly it does prove and what it is silent about. I'd like you to correct whatever is confused in my framing — I suspect I'm treating the claim that the expected value of information is non-negative as a claim about how things turn out for actual people, and also ignoring something about what the theorem assumes regarding an agent's preferences and regarding what information is. Then tell me which of my three cases the theorem genuinely rules out as irrational and which it simply does not address.
