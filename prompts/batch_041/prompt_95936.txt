Hey — I've been chewing on Scanlon's contractualism and the aggregation problem for a few months, and I've got a fix I want to run past someone before I sink another month into it. I'm not fishing for encouragement, I want to know if the thing actually works or if I've just relabeled a problem and called it progress.

Quick version of where I'm at. I take the core of the view to be that an act is wrong if it's ruled out by principles nobody could reasonably reject, and that when you check for rejectability you're supposed to look at each person's complaint on its own, one person at a time — no summing complaints across people. My memory of the text is fuzzy on this point, but I'm fairly sure Scanlon somewhere lets numbers tip the scale when the complaints on the two sides are otherwise a wash, and I've never understood how that's supposed to work without being aggregation with extra steps. So here's my revision. Call it the same-kind restriction: complaints can be combined, but only with complaints of the same kind, where the kind is fixed by the specific interest at stake. Across kinds you don't combine at all — you compare the single strongest complaint on each side and that's the entire comparison. So one death beats any number of sprained ankles (different kinds, and the death is the strongest single complaint on either side anyway), but a hundred deaths beat one death, because those are the same kind and get summed. The motivation is that it gets the numbers problem in rescue cases right without letting me do the transplant: I never get to trade one person's life against a big pile of lesser goods, because lesser goods of a different kind never enter the sum.

My worry is the kinds themselves. Individuate them coarsely and the whole thing collapses into ordinary aggregation — all losses are just welfare losses of one kind. Individuate them finely and every person's loss is sui generis, nobody's numbers ever count, and I'm back to a view I don't want. I don't have a principled account of kind-individuation, only intuitions, and I know that's the soft spot.

The case I can't handle: one person permanently loses their sense of smell, versus thirty thousand people each spending a month with severe nausea and vomiting. Different kinds, so no summing — and if anosmia is the more serious single loss, the one person wins and thirty thousand people just eat it. Something feels wrong about that, but I can't tell if it's the view or my gut.

What I want from you:

1. Is there a principled way to individuate kinds here, or is my restriction ad hoc — a line drawn to get the answers I already wanted?
2. Does this actually dodge the standard objections to the individualist restriction, or does it just relocate them?
3. If this is already a known position under some name, tell me the shape of that position and how mine differs from it. I'm not asking for a reading list — I just want to know whether I'm reinventing a wheel and what the strongest existing version of it looks like.
4. Where have I got Scanlon wrong? Correct me if the framing is off.
5. Does it stay contractualist, or does it quietly turn into something else?
6. What would count against it — the things that should make me drop it rather than patch it.

Be blunt if it's a dead end. And please seperate what's actually settled in this area from what's genuinely contested among people who know the topic — don't hand me a contested reading as if it were the consensus. American English, I'm pasting this into a doc.
