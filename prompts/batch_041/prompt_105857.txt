My flatmate and I just spent three hours arguing about voting and got absolutley nowhere, and now I want to turn the arguement into a class activity. The fight was this: I said voting for the lesser evil is just what having a vote means, and he said no, that to vote for the lesser evil you have to actually *want* the lesser evil to win, and wanting that is already a small corruption of yourself, and if you vote for it while hoping it loses you're lying to the ballot box. He also kept saying that the strategic voter is acting as a proxy for people who never consented to be represented that way, which I think is him being dramatic but maybe not wrong. We couldn't settle it because we kept imagining different scenarios.

Anyway — second-year ethics seminar, 18 students, 75 minutes. I want them to walk into the split themselves, no lecture up front, and I want the split to be three ways: voting as duty, voting as expression, voting as lesser-evil calculation. And I want the proxy/complicity thing and the "do you have to want it" thing to actually bite rather than be something I say at the end.

Rough plan, rip it up as you like:

Round 1 — they're the electorate. Three candidates with short policy profiles: one competent but callous, one openly dangerous, one genuinely decent but polling around 5%. Each student also gets a private stake card saying something personal that hangs on the outcome (a job, a visa, a sibling's healthcare) so the tradeoff isn't bloodless. Everyone fills in a secret true-preference card: rank the three, one sentence why.

Round 2 — secret plurality vote, I tally it. Then I reveal national polling: dead heat between callous and dangerous, decent one can't win. Vote again. Anyone who switches off their stated true preference has to stand up, justify it in one sentence, and take questions from the room.

Round 3, the bit I'm unsure about — I want "your vote was never going to be decisive" to land without it becoming a probability lecture. My idea: after the second vote I open a sealed envelope naming which single ballot was the pivotal one, and ask that person whether they still defend their vote. Might be too cute.

What I need: a runnable version — rules, materials, the stake cards, a 75-minute timeline, and how to stage the second vote so it actually bites. A debrief mapping what happened onto the three positions and onto the proxy/complicity objection and the "you have to want it" objection. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — I've run forced-choice stuff before and there's always one student who refuses to play, and I'd like to use that rather than fight it. And be straight with me about which part of the idea the game can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself.
