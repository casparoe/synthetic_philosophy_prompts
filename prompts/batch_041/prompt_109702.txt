I am a PhD student in philosophy writing a dissertation on the moral status of animals in the wild, and I am trying to determine what I actually believe by attending to my reactions to particular cases rather than to any theory I have absorbed. What follows are my verdicts, stated bluntly, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they jointly express. I care more about the argument than about the literature, so please reason from the cases themselves; I would rather have a principle that is honest about what it costs me than one engineered to save every verdict.

Untangling a bird from discarded fishing line seems to me not merely permitted but required -- the harm is human in origin and I can undo it. Rescuing an animal I find dying of starvation, with no human cause anywhere upstream, seems permitted but not required: good to do, no failure if I walk on. Distributing vaccine-laced bait to halt an epidemic in a wild population seems to me straightforwardly good. Putting out food each winter for a population that would otherwise starve seems permitted but somehow different from the vaccine case -- an interference I would rather not see normalized, though I cannot articulate why. Extirpating a parasite species that inflicts prolonged suffering on its hosts seems to me permitted, where the ecology allows it. Culling a predator species so that its prey are not eaten alive seems to me impermissible, even granting that it would reduce total suffering. Engineering a species to feel less pain seems permitted in the abstract and repellent in practice. Protecting animals from harms that other animals inflict on them seems to me mostly off-limits. Leaving a flourishing, suffering-drenched ecosystem entirely alone seems perfectly permitted.

The two verdicts I find hardest to hold together are the parasite and the predator: I would remove the parasite but not the predator, yet the justification I reach for in each case is the same -- the reduction of suffering -- and the ecological footprint is comparable. I suspect that when I call the parasite case different, I am covertly treating disease as an aberration and predation as a natural process, a distinction I cannot defend once I notice it.

Please respond under these headings, at roughly these lengths.

Summary of the pattern (about 150 words): state, as neutrally as you can, the principle my verdicts most nearly approximate, and name the single feature of a case on which most of them turn.

Candidate principles (about 500 words): set out two or three precise principles that each capture most of my verdicts; for each, say which of my verdicts chiefly motivates it, which verdicts it preserves, and which it must revise.

The principle I should adopt (about 300 words): choose among them, or repair one, stating it precisely enough to apply to cases I have not mentioned, and say whether it concerns what I may do, what I must do, or what it is good to do.

Its verdicts on further cases (about 300 words): apply it to at least three interventions I have not listed -- for instance a permanent feeding program for a wild population, a decision to let a species go extinct because its members suffer, and an intervention that reduces suffering in one species at the cost of increasing it in another -- and flag any that expose a weakness in the principle.

The verdict I must give up (about 200 words): tell me which of my original verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate, and whether abandoning it is a cost I should accept or evidence that the principle is wrong.
