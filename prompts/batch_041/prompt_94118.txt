I am designing a one-semester interdisciplinary seminar for six graduate students drawn from philosophy, neuroscience, and computer science. The topic is von Neumann's views on the brain, especially as they appear in his posthumously published The Computer and the Brain, which began as the Silliman lectures and was left unfinished. I want the seminar to do more than rehearse the familiar brain-versus-computer comparisons. Instead, I want to reconstruct a specific research program from themes in that work: the brain as a computing system; the mixed analog-digital character of nervous computation; the suggestion that the brain's higher-level coding may be statistical rather than notational; and the problem of how reliable computation can arise from unreliable components. Call this reconstructed program the hybrid-coding, reliability-constrained, statistical-language view, or simply the VN-hybrid thesis. I am not asking whether von Neumann was right in every detail. I am asking which open questions inside this reconstructed program most deserve a semester of serious attention, and why.

Please write a defended agenda, not a survey and not a reading list. Do not provide citations, bibliographies, or reading recommendations; a model without web access should be able to answer from reasoning about the ideas themselves. You may refer to von Neumann's book by title, but do not lean on specific page numbers, quotations, or bibliographic details.

Structure your response in the following sections and order:

Bottom line. 150 to 250 words. State the single open question you would put first, and say in plain terms why it is the most important one for this seminar. Do not hedge; give me your actual top pick.

The candidate questions. 400 to 600 words. Identify five to seven open questions that a serious group could pursue within the VN-hybrid thesis. For each, give a compact sketch of what the question is, why it is not already settled by the existing literature, and what a good paper on it might try to establish. At least two of these questions should concern the statistical-language idea specifically, and at least two should concern the reliability or mixed-coding ideas. Include one question that cuts against the VN-hybrid thesis rather than assuming it, for example by asking whether the statistical-language interpretation is a substantive hypothesis or only a metaphor.

The ranking. 500 to 700 words. Rank the candidate questions from most to least worth the seminar's time. For each, explain the ranking using four criteria: how many other questions depend on it, how tractable it is for a mixed group of philosophy, neuroscience, and computer science students in one semester, how neglected it is relative to its importance, and what the payoff would be if it were resolved. Be explicit about trade-offs. If a question is highly important but not tractable for this group, say so and rank it accordingly.

Why this order rather than another. 300 to 450 words. Defend the ranking against the most obvious alternative orderings. For example, explain why you did not put the reliability question first, or why you did not treat the statistical-language question as merely a historical curiosity. Address at least one objection that a neuroscientist in the room would raise and at least one that a philosopher would raise.

What would change the ranking. 200 to 300 words. Describe the kinds of arguments, conceptual clarifications, or empirical findings that would move a question up or down. Be concrete about what would count as progress on your top question and what would count as a reason to abandon it.

Write in American English. Keep the prose polished and formal, but do not pad it. I care more about the reasoning behind the ordering than about the list itself.
