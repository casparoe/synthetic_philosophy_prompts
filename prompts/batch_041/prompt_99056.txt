I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been asked to advise on how to spend a small internal research budget — roughly one researcher for one year — on conceptual work at the intersection of the no-free-lunch results and the problem of induction. The immediate context is a recurring disagreement on my team. One colleague holds that because no learner can outperform another when performance is averaged over all possible target functions, the only substantive thing we can do is encode domain knowledge as inductive bias. Another replies that this simply restates the problem of induction: no amount of domain knowledge can justify carrying past regularities into a future that may differ. I do not want a survey or a reading list. I want a defended agenda: which open questions here most deserve that year, in what order, and on what criteria.

Please make the ordering dependency-aware, and be explicit about which questions are upstream of which. I am especially interested in four candidates, though I would welcome a better one if you think I have misidentified the center of gravity: first, whether restricting the hypothesis class to natural or simple worlds is a substantive escape from the no-free-lunch conclusion or only a redescription of the inductive assumption; second, whether resource-bounded learning changes the conclusion in a way that matters for practice or merely relocates the idealization; third, whether there is a principled distinction between inductive assumptions a learner chooses and those forced on it by its architecture or training procedure; and fourth, whether it helps to separate bias as a prior over hypotheses from bias as a constraint on the computational path by which a hypothesis is reached. For each, explain why it is upstream of other work, how tractable it is for someone with engineering habits of mind but no philosophical training, and what would count as progress rather than another restatement of the difficulty. Then say what evidence or argument would move a question up or down the ranking, and which one you would drop first if only two could be pursued. Finally, address the objection that these questions are all equally hopeless and that the rational response is to abandon foundational worries and simply build systems that fail gracefully when the world shifts.
