Right, so — I'm a novelist, and my last book was about a town that runs itself by lottery, which was enormous fun to write and which I am now unable to escape. The council here has actually gone and done it. We've got a sixty-person citizens' assembly being sworn in on Saturday and they've asked me to give the opening remarks, so I need an actual speech, about a thousand words, something I can stand up and say out loud without it sounding like a novel with the good bits taken out.

Here's the room, because it matters. Sixty assembly members, drawn off the electoral roll at random, most of whom did not want to be there and at least three of whom I know for a fact are furious about it. The outgoing councillors in the front two rows, several of them twenty years in the job — one of them, Doreen, has been mayor twice, everyone loves her, including me, so nothing that lands as a slap at her. The local paper. And about fifteen activists who wanted a full lottocracy and think this version is a sellout, and who will be listening to me specifically for heresy.

The rules we've ended up with, and I need the speech to take these seriously rather than just wave at them:

1. Members serve eighteen months, chosen by lot, and can't stand for the council or any other elected office for six years afterwards. The activists call this the one-way door.
2. The assembly can't be dissolved, only rotated — a third at a time, every six months, so there's always a majority who've done it before.
3. Any decision it makes can be sent back once, and only once, by a second panel of thirty drawn fresh by lot six months later, whose members aren't told they've been drawn until the morning they sit.
4. Whatever the assembly decides, the council has to publish its reasons in full, and can override only on a two-thirds vote with its own reasons published alongside.

What I want the speech to actually do is sort out the thing I've never been able to sort out in my own head: what replaces the fear of the electorate? With elections the whole discipline is that you can be thrown out. With a lottery nobody can throw you out — you just time out, like milk. The town is quietly pretending that the rotation and the second panel and the one-way door do the same job as a ballot box, and I don't think they do the same job, I think they do a different job, and I'd like the speech to be honest about what that different job is and what we've given up to get it. Also have a proper go at the term-limits idea, because the activists keep saying the one-way door is term limits done properly, and I've got a nagging feeling it just hands the place to the people who never leave — the clerks, the officers, the consultants, the ones with no term to be limited — while the amateurs rotate through and out. Say something honest about that without it turning into a defence of Doreen's twenty years, because it isn't one.

Two more things it should touch. Consent: sixty people who didn't volunteer are about to make decisions that bind the rest of us, and I don't know how to call that legitimate without lying, so I'd rather it just looked straight at the problem. And the sample thing — because the assembly is a sample rather than a mandate, there's no seat to buy and no candidate to lobby, which everyone's very pleased about, except the second panel meets in secret and I'm not sure that's the same as being un-lobbyable.

Shape it so the sixty frightened people hear themselves being taken seriously rather than patronised, and so the activists hear the heresy as an argument instead of an insult. Keep the jargon light — nobody in that hall has read any political philosophy and they shouldn't feel talked down to. Write it in British English, no American spellings, and dont put any quotes in it from real people, I'd rather it just sounded like me. Oh — and dont end it with a call to action, I cant stand that.
