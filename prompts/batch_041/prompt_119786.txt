I teach a 90-minute session in an undergraduate seminar on collective choice — 12 students, mostly second- and third-years, comfortable with the idea of a preference ranking but not with any formal social choice machinery. We have reached the point in the term where I want them to feel why the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem is a theorem rather than a complaint about politicians. Please design the whole session: a runnable activity with materials, a script, timings, and a debrief, in which the students themselves keep trying to build a decision procedure that nobody can game, keep failing, and finally arrive at a procedure that cannot be gamed and that they do not like the look of. They should not hear the words "strategy-proof", "manipulable", "dictatorial", or either theorist's name until the debrief; before that, everything should be phrased as ordinary committee business. No real money and no computers — points on a scorecard are fine, and I would like you to design the stakes so that the points actually matter to them without any cash changing hands.

Here is what I have sketched, and where I need your help. The committee of 12 must choose one of four concrete options (four candidate sites for a departmental retreat, say, or four ways of spending a fixed sum — pick something and name the options). I hand out preference cards so that the profile is fixed and I know in advance what will happen; each card states a strict ranking and a point payoff for each of the four outcomes, students keep their cards private, and the cards are their preferences for the purposes of the game. We then run three rounds on the same profile, announcing the rule in force before each vote but never announcing that we are hunting for manipulations. Round one: plurality, with an alphabetical tie-break announced in advance. Round two: a scoring rule where each voter awards 3 points to a first choice and 1 to a second, the top two scorers advance, and a tie in the majority stage goes to whichever of the two scored higher in the first stage. Round three: a fixed amendment agenda — a pairwise majority vote between A and B, then the winner against C, then that winner against D. After each round, everyone privately fills in a card reading "would you have wanted to change your ballot?", and I collect them anonymously. Then comes the design phase: pairs draft a rule that always names a winner, could in principle name any of the four, and cannot be gamed; pairs then swap drafts and try to break each other's rule, with me as referee keeping score of which rules have fallen. What I need from you is the exact profile to hand out — 12 cards, four options — with at least one voter who has a profitable misreport in the plurality round and one in the scoring round, and if you can find it, one in the agenda round as well. I also want a profile containing a Condorcet cycle, so that the agenda order alone decides the winner, which I can use to show that the outcome turns on something nobody voted on. Show me each manipulation in full — who reports what, the outcome under sincere and under insincere reporting, and why the misreporter is strictly better off — so that I can check my own run against your answer. If a profile with the properties I want does not exist, say so plainly and give me the nearest thing; I would much rather adjust the activity than have you fudge it.

In the debrief I want to work through the three exits I already know about, and I want the activity to make each feel like a discovery rather than a rescue. First, restricting the preferences: hand out a second profile in which everyone's ranking is single-peaked along a left-right line, run the median of the stated positions with whatever convention for even numbers you tell me to announce, and have them try to game it and fail. Second, allowing chance: a rule that picks a voter uniformly at random and enacts that voter's top choice, which they should again try and fail to game, noticing that what they gave up was determinism rather than honesty. Third, the failed drafts still on the board, including the one that quietly excludes options from the ballot and the one that hands the decision to the chair — tell me how to make that last moment land as the punchline, so that they notice the only ungameable rule they managed to build gives the decision to one person, and give me the mapping from each retreat to the hypothesis it abandons. I would also like one sentence on the randomized analogue — that strategy-proof randomized rules on an unrestricted domain must be lotteries over dictatorships — flagged as something we are taking on trust rather than something the session demonstrated, and one sentence on how putting money into the mechanism changes the question entirely. Be honest in a separate note about what the activity cannot do: a committee testing finitely many rules can never establish that every rule fails, so the step from "everything we tried fell except dictatorship" to the theorem has to come from me at the board, and tell me the smallest honest board proof I can get through in ten minutes without losing them. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time: I am already worried about ties with 12 voters and four options, and about how to keep the tie-break from becoming its own manipulation lever; about the students who happen to have no profitable misreport and therefore conclude the whole exercise is a dud; about caucusing and vote-trading, which turns individual manipulation into something the theorem says nothing about; and about the two objections I expect from the sharp ones — "this only shows that people should vote sincerely" and "I didn't know how the others would vote, so I couldn't have gamed it anyway". That second objection seems to me to be pointing at something real about dominant strategies versus decision-making under uncertainty, and I would like the debrief to give it its due rather than brush it aside.
