I am a philosopher of science whose own work is on historiography — specifically on the ways historians smuggle the present into their accounts of the past — and I have been asked to give the opening remarks at a two-day workshop that my department is co-hosting with the computer science department. The subject is the history of automated reasoning: the long, tangled lineage of attempts to get machines to prove things, from nineteenth-century symbolic logic through the era of theorem-proving programs and expert systems down to contemporary automated and probabilistic reasoning. I have eight minutes.

The room is split roughly in half, and that split is the whole difficulty. One half consists of historians of science and technology, who will wince at any sentence that treats the past as a rehearsal for the present. The other half consists of working researchers — graduate students, postdocs, a few people from industry — who signed up precisely because they want orientation: where did this field come from, and does any of it bear on what they should be doing now. The program as the organizers have arranged it flatters the second half. The sessions are ordered so that each one appears to be reaching toward the next, and the closing panel is essentially named after the destination, which is us.

There is also a person in the room. One of the invited speakers is a man in his late eighties who spent the better part of three decades on a line of work that everyone now regards as a dead end — not discredited, exactly, just bypassed. Under the organizers' framing he is a picturesque detour, a warm-up act, a branch of the tree that withered. He knows this. He is coming anyway.

I want to open the workshop honestly about all of this. What I do not want is to deliver the standard sermon about progress narratives being pernicious, which everyone will nod along to and then spend two days doing exactly what I warned against, myself included.

Take the role of a philosopher of history who has written a good deal on anachronism and presentism, and who also knows how to write for the ear. Please write my opening remarks. Before drafting, work through the following and show me your thinking briefly at the top. First, list the specific rhetorical moves that would make these remarks Whiggish in a way a careful listener could actually catch — I have in mind things like casting someone as ahead of their time, calling a piece of work a precursor, telling the audience that an old controversy was really about a question we have now, or arranging a sequence of anecdotes so that it appears to have been heading somewhere — and add to that list whatever I have missed, including subtler temptations. Second, decide what each half of the room needs from eight minutes of opening remarks, and what I can give one half without cheating the other. Third, settle on an organizing principle for the two days that is not a trajectory — something that makes the sessions cohere without implying that they culminate in anything.

Then write the remarks, in my voice: a professor in her fifties, dry, unsentimental, not self-deprecating, capable of being funny once or twice. They should be speakable in eight minutes, so roughly eleven hundred words. The remarks must give the audience something to hold on to about why the history of this subject is worth two days of anyone's attention, including the working researchers, and must do so without offering lessons from history, without promising that the past explains the present, and without implying that the attendees are the fortunate beneficiaries of a long ascent. If the remarks touch on the older speaker at all, they should do so in a way he could listen to without being consoled or converted into a symbol.

A few constraints. Do not quote anyone, and do not cite books, articles, or dates; if you need a historical illustration, use only something you are confident is true, keep it to a sentence, and where you are not confident, describe the shape of a development rather than asserting a particular fact. Do not use the phrase Whig history in the remarks themselves — I would rather the audience feel the problem than be handed the label. And do not end on a note of uplift.
