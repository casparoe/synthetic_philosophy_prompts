I host a monthly film night for a small mixed group — a few engineers like me, two friends who did graduate work in philosophy, and a retired schoolteacher who just likes movies — and next month we're doing Arrival, the Villeneuve film from Ted Chiang's novella. I'm the one who introduces the film and leads the discussion, and I've spent a week trying to get one idea straight before I stand up in front of them. Dennett, in Consciousness Explained, describes a pair of dots flashed a few degrees apart in different colours, where people report seeing the dot travel across and change colour in flight, which means reporting a colour change that appears to happen before the second dot has even been shown. He says the explanations on offer come in two flavours: an Orwellian one, where the experience really does occur and the memory of it is quietly revised afterwards, and a Stalinesque one, where the conflict is resolved upstream and what the subject gets is a finished, already reconciled experience that was never revised because it was never anything else. His own verdict, as I understand it from secondary sources, is that we should stop asking which of the two it is, because both pictures assume there is a moment and a place where processing turns into experience, and no such moment or place exists. Please read Arrival through this lens. The film opens with what looks like a mother's memories of her daughter and later reveals them to be premonitions of a child not yet born, and Louise's whole arc turns on the claim that learning the heptapods' language rewires her relation to time. I want a reading that takes both the film and the philosophy seriously, not one that uses the film as an illustration.

Concretely: which scenes should I press on with the group, and what should I say about each? Is the viewer's experience of the opening montage an Orwellian case (I had an experience of remembering, and the film rewrote its meaning afterwards), a Stalinesque one (the film arranged from the first frame that I would never experience it as anything but ambiguous), or is the question as ill-posed for me as Dennett says the dot question is for the subject — and if it is ill-posed, does that make the film's reveal a trick on the audience rather than a claim about experience? Louise is the more interesting case, since she seems to have future events as experiences and to act on them, which raises the question of what it even means for her experiences to have an order. Where does the film resist the reading? I suspect the honest critic has to concede that a narrative reveal about a character's temporal cognition is no evidence at all about hundred-millisecond perceptual processing, and that the premise about language rewiring temporal perception rests on a contested empirical claim about linguistic relativity that the reading may be quietly borrowing. I also want to know what the film adds that the argument alone does not: my half-formed hypothesis is that it dramatizes the thought that the very notion of an experience having a time is imported from the story we tell about it, but I'd rather hear whether that is a real philosophical gain or just a restatement of Dennett in cinematic clothing.

Please separate throughout what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who actually know this material — the colour phi data, the multiple drafts interpretation of them, the status of the linguistic relativity premise, and the film's philosophical reputation — and tell me plainly where my reading is a stretch, which parts I should drop rather than defend in front of the group, and which of my claims above you are endorsing versus merely entertaining. Do not present a contested view as the consensus, and where you're unsure of something, say so rather than smoothing it over. End with four or five discussion questions pitched so that the schoolteacher and the philosophers can both get purchase on them.
