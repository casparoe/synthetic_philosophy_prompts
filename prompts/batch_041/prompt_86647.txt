I'm teaching an upper-level seminar on rational belief this fall and I've hit the point where I have to admit I don't have a clear spec for what I'm even asking a theory to do. So before I put anything on the syllabus: write me the desiderata. What does any adequate theory of rational *acceptance* have to deliver? Not credence, not decision theory -- acceptance, in the sense of "I'm taking this on board, using it as a premise, asserting it, acting on it." Adequate for what? For teaching, mainly -- I need to be able to tell a student in one sentence what they're allowed to assert and why. The two things that make it hard are the lottery and the preface, and I've started to suspect we lump them together when they're doing different work.

Things the list has to cover, at minimum:

- the standard 1000-ticket lottery: one winner, you know that, 99.9% on each "ticket i loses," and yet accepting all 1000 is plainly irrational.
- the preface: a long book, every claim individually well supported, and you know there's an error in there somewhere. Believing each claim looks fine; believing the conjunction looks like a mistake; the two intuitions dont sit together.
- I want a case in the list that seperates the two engines, because I can't tell which one is doing the work. Take 1000 claims that are each 99.9% likely on my evidence and that don't exclude each other at all -- no winner, nothing lottery-shaped, all of them could be true at once. Does the pressure still build? Whatever requirement answers that is the one I most want to see.
- the realistic version of that case: 1000 readings off the same miscalibrated instrument, so the errors are correlated and I can't just multiply. Half the framings I know seem to quietly assume independence, and I want a requirement about what the theory owes me when they aren't.
- something about the state/act distinction: is acceptance a doxastic state, a practical commitment, a speech act, or all three wearing a trenchcoat? A theory that can't place it will keep sliding between senses mid-argument.
- and it should tell me what happens when I drop a claim. Is believing 999 fine and 1000 not? If there's a cutoff, I want it to come from somewhere other than a stipulation.

Then two things flagged. First, which requirements are genuine and which are artifacts of one framework -- a thoroughgoing Bayesian will tell me closure under conjunction is optional, and I want to know whether they're cheating. Second, and this is the part I care about most: I suspect you can't have (a) a high-probability threshold for acceptance, (b) acceptance closed under conjunction, and (c) consistency of rational acceptance, all at once. If that's right, say so, give the argument, and tell me which camp gives up which.

Skip the citations, I know the literature well enough. Give me two tables.

Table 1, the requirements. Columns: requirement | what it demands, one line | the case or consideration that forces it | genuine constraint, negotiable, or one camp's preference | which position it rules out, one line max.

Table 2, the clashes. Columns: the requirements that clash | why they can't hold together, argument in a few lines | what any theory has to give up | who gives up what.

Three or four sentences above the tables on whether the lottery and the preface are one paradox or two, and one line under them naming the single requirement that excludes the most.
