I belong to a reading group that has now given four sessions to the question of German responsibility for the outbreak of the First World War, and we are no closer to agreement than when we began. The impasse has become more interesting to me than the verdict, and what I want from you is a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than another judgment on 1914.

The positions, as concretely as I can state them. On one side is the line running from Fritz Fischer's 1961 study of German war aims: the imperial leadership did not merely stumble into a European war but pursued expansionist aims, accepted the risk of a general war as the price of a diplomatic victory, and committed its aims to paper within weeks of the outbreak — the memorandum drawn up in Bethmann Hollweg's office in September 1914, probably by Kurt Riezler, in which France is to be reduced, Belgium made a vassal, and a German-led customs bloc assembled in Central Europe. On this reading the war-guilt clause of 1919 was essentially vindicated, and the older German consensus that the war was tragedy and fate, that no responsible statesman willed it, was a defensive fiction. Against this stands a set of objections we keep re-encountering: Gerhard Ritter's insistence that the war was experienced and conducted as a defensive one and that foreign policy had its own logic; Egmont Zechlin's rival reading of German policy as a calculated war-risk in a system where every power was gambling; and the chronological point that the aims documents date from after the fighting began, so they show what Germany hoped to extract from a victory rather than what made the war happen. Then there is the more recent narrative — Christopher Clark's sleepwalkers — in which no single power is to blame, all of the continental powers accepted the risk of a general war, and none actually sought it.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that we are not disagreeing about the archives; we have all read the same documents and stopped disputing what they say. I suspect four different questions have been fused into one. First, causation versus blame: whether the claim that Germany caused the war is a counterfactual claim about a difference-maker or a moral claim about culpability. Second, intent versus recklessness: whether accepting a substantial probability of a European war counts as willing it, and whether a leadership that wanted a local or diplomatic victory but misjudged the risk is exonerated or convicted. Third, the bearer of responsibility: individuals, a ruling elite, the state, the nation, or a structure — and if the better explanation is structural (the social-imperialist account, the primacy of domestic politics, the arms race), whether anyone is left to blame or whether responsibility becomes a manner of speaking. Fourth, the object: responsibility for the July crisis, for the escalation from a Balkan quarrel to a European war, for the violation of Belgian neutrality, for the conduct and duration of the war, and for the settlement are five different questions, and Fischer's evidence, being mostly about aims, may bear on some and not others.

Some things we tried, none of which moved anyone. A counterfactual substitution test: replace the German leadership in 1913 with the most pacific plausible alternative and ask whether the July crisis still ends in a general war; one member regards the answer as decisive, another as rigged, since the substitution holds Austria, Russia, the alliance system and the war plans fixed. A risk ledger: score each actor's choices at each decision node by how much they raised the probability of a general war relative to the alternatives then available; one member objects that this converts guilt into a quantity no one chose and makes the verdict hostage to contested counterfactual probabilities. And an argument about the treaty clause, which affirms the responsibility of Germany and her allies for causing all the loss and damage to which the Allied and Associated Governments and their nationals have been subjected as a consequence of the war imposed upon them by the aggression of Germany and her allies — one of us notes that the article nowhere uses the word guilt and is really a finding about liability for reparations, so that vindicating Fischer is a separate matter; another holds that the clause and the thesis are the same judgment and stand or fall together.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, and why has it stalled? Which of the four strands is doing the actual work, and is any pair of us answering different questions under one name? Is any part of it verbal — cause in the historian's sense against responsible in the moral sense, or Germany as state against Germany as shorthand for a dozen decision-makers? Is there a hidden empirical question underneath (whether the December 1912 meeting was a decision to prepare for war or an inconsequential airing, whether the war plans made a two-front war automatic once mobilization began), or is that a distraction from a disagreement that is not empirical at all? How much of the heat comes from what the verdict was for — the treaty's shadow, German self-understanding in the 1960s, the continuity between imperial and Nazi aims — rather than from the evidence? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a decision procedure, a change of question, or nothing, because the disagreement is ultimately about whether a nation can be blamed for anything and no amount of history can settle that? If the question as posed is ill-formed, tell me what a better-posed one would be, and what we would lose by asking it instead.
