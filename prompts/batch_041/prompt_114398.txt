I am a novelist, and for the past six years I have taught a seminar for third-year undergraduates called Constraints: Form, Rule, and the Making of a Work. Twenty students, mostly literature and social science, no formal training in logic or mathematics beyond school algebra, and two or three who will announce cheerfully that they are bad at puzzles. I have one ninety-minute session to spend, and one thing I want them to feel rather than be told: the difference between working cleverly and indefinitely inside a system of rules, and the act of stepping outside it — the move that goes by the acronym JOOTSing, jumping out of the system. I spent four years trying to repair the middle of my last novel by rearranging sentences, and the repair, when it came, was to change who was telling the story; I have never found a better way to explain that experience to a room of twenty-year-olds than by letting them have it themselves in a smaller and harder form.

The material I intend to use is the MIU-system, which appears in Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach and is small enough to state on a whiteboard in five minutes. Three symbols, M, I, U; one axiom, MI; four rules for rewriting strings:

1. A string ending in I may be extended by a U at the end. (MI becomes MIU.)
2. From Mx, where x is any string, you may form Mxx. (MI becomes MII.)
3. Anywhere in a string, III may be replaced by U. (MUIIIU becomes MUUU.)
4. Anywhere in a string, UU may be deleted. (MUUU becomes MU.)

The puzzle is to derive MU from MI using these rules alone, and it cannot be done. I want the impossibility to arrive as a wall rather than as a sentence from me: the students should spend real time and real ingenuity inside the system, fail, and then discover that the failure was structural. And I want the explanation, when it comes, to feel like a leap they made rather than a fact they were handed — ideally emerging from something they have made with their own hands rather than from my mouth.

Please act as an expert in mathematical logic who has also spent years designing classroom games for non-specialists — someone who understands the difference between an activity that teaches a result and one that lets people collide with it. Think carefully and at length before you design anything. In prose I will read, work through the following: what the participants must experience, and in what order, for the wall to feel like a wall and the leap like a leap; where the trap in the exercise actually lies; and what the minimum scaffolding is that keeps everyone in the game without giving the answer away. Then brainstorm three or four genuinely different activity structures — different mechanisms, not variations on one idea — say which you are choosing and why the others fail, and only then produce the design.

The design should give me:

- A minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, with slack built in and a note on which parts can be cut if the room moves slowly.
- Materials and setup, assuming only index cards, markers, tape, a whiteboard, and no computers or phones in use.
- The rules of the game or activity exactly as I would read them aloud, in the order I would read them, with a note on which of my instructions are load-bearing and which could be varied.
- Group sizes, roles, and what each group is doing when it is not obvious.
- A precise indication of where the moment of contact with the idea occurs, and what the participants are doing at that moment.
- A debrief of no more than fifteen minutes, keyed to specific moments — when group three did X, that was Y — that names the inside/outside distinction, the fact that a system can grind out its own theorems forever without ever telling you what is true of all of them, and the fact that the move which solves the puzzle is not itself one of the rules.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, in order of likelihood, and what to do in each case. I am especially worried about the group that finds the reason early and announces it; the group that produces a nearly-MU string and mistakes it for success; the student who has read the source and knows the answer; and the student who decides this is a mathematics test and quietly disengages. Design around these rather than lecturing at them.
- An honest account of what the activity cannot reproduce and what I must supply myself. My guess is that the main limit is that the participants will feel the difference between inside and outside without coming anywhere near the machinery that makes the distinction philosophically serious — the arithmetization, the self-reference, the fact that any jump can itself be formalized in a larger system, after which there is a new wall. If that is not the main limit, tell me what is.

Two further things. First, I would rather the session end without a tidy moral than with one; a room that leaves slightly unsettled is worth more to me than a room that leaves with a definition. Second, if there is a way to spend the last ten minutes on a bridge to writing — a small exercise in which the students must stop fixing a problem from inside its own terms and change the frame instead — I want it, but only if it does not dilute the formal half; say plainly whether it earns its time. If you think a different target string, or a modified version of the system, would make for a better session than the one I have stated, say so and argue for it; I am not attached to my version.

Do not quote from any book and do not assume anyone in the room has read the source; I need no citations and would have no way to check them. Wherever you rely on a piece of mathematics, work it out explicitly in the text — including the reason MU cannot be derived — and if you find you cannot reconstruct an argument, say so rather than asserting it. As long as the design needs and no longer: I would rather have four pages I can run than ten I cannot.
