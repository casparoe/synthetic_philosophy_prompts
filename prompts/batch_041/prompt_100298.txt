I am a teaching assistant for an upper-level cognitive science course, and I have just been handed the free-will unit's midterm to grade. The material has become personally charged for me this term: a close friend has been undergoing deep brain stimulation for a movement disorder, and watching a device trigger, suppress, and reshape her movements has made the question of who authors an action feel less like a seminar puzzle than a fact about someone I love. I would like to grade the following answer by its merits rather than by gut impression, so please give me a grade and the specific comments that justify it.

The exam question was:

"Libet's experiments are often summarized as showing that the brain decides before we are aware of deciding. (a) Reconstruct the paradigm and its central timing result. (b) Consider two hypothetical participants. The first shows a readiness potential, reports no urge to move, and moves anyway. The second reports a clear urge to move, shows no readiness potential, and moves. State what Libet's own interpretation predicts about each case, and what the rival reinterpretation that treats the readiness potential as an artifact of averaging spontaneous neural noise predicts about each. (c) Do the original results, on their own, establish that conscious intention does not cause action?"

The student's answer:

"Libet had participants watch a fast-moving clock and note where the dot was when they first felt the urge to move their wrist, while he recorded scalp potentials and muscle activity. He found a readiness potential, a slow negative shift, beginning about 550 ms before the movement, and the reported urge, the W judgment, about 200 ms before it. Brain activity therefore precedes the conscious urge by roughly 350 ms. Because the readiness potential is the brain's decision, the decision is made before the agent is aware of it, and the conscious intention is a symptom rather than a cause. This is why Libet concluded that free will is an illusion.

In a later version, participants were allowed to veto the movement at the last moment; they could cancel it about 100 to 200 ms before it would have occurred, and Libet took this to show that a free won't survives. So consciousness does not initiate but can still veto.

A rival account holds that the readiness potential is an artifact of averaging: spontaneous fluctuations in ongoing neural noise occasionally cross a threshold and trigger the movement, and averaging many trials lines them up into an apparent ramp. On this account there is no ramp that decides; there is noise that sometimes fires. This handles the first hypothetical participant well, since a readiness potential need not reflect any urge, so someone could show one and report no urge. The second participant is harder for this account: because it says the readiness potential is generated by the fluctuation, a clear urge with no readiness potential should be impossible, so this case counts against the artifact account and favors Libet.

Finally, the original results do not by themselves establish that conscious intention fails to cause action: a readiness potential that precedes the urge could be a preparatory correlate that the agent can still endorse or veto, and the veto results show that conscious processes can intervene."

If you think a defensible grader could reasonably land on a different grade, say so and explain what would move it up or down.
