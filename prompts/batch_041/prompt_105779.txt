I host a modest interview series on decision theory, and my next guest is a philosopher who defends risk-weighted expected utility as the correct account of rational choice under risk. Please draft eight to ten interview questions for me, together with a follow-up for each that is keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. The questions should press on the substance of the position rather than invite him to restate it, and they should not depend on his citing any literature; I want the exchange to stand on its own for listeners who already have the theory in view.

Please build the sequence around at least the following lines of pressure, roughly in this order. First, an opening that forces him to say what the risk function is doing that a probability weighting cannot: why is risk-weighted expected utility not simply rank-dependent utility under another name, and what turns on the interpretation rather than on the formalism? Second, a question that brings out the fact that his theory assigns each agent a single, fixed risk attitude across every prospect the agent faces, with a follow-up raising the objection that the same person is plausibly risk-averse about medical prospects and risk-seeking about career prospects; ask him whether that is a defect of the theory or a defect of the folk psychology. Third, a concrete case: a choice between a sure forty and an even chance of zero or a hundred, with utility linear in money. Ask what his risk function would have to look like in order to reverse the expected-utility ranking here, and follow up on whether the degree of concavity this requires is independently plausible or merely fitted to the Allais pattern. Fourth, the consequentialism worry: if the risk function is not just shorthand for preferences over a richer outcome space, does the view forfeit the standard arguments for dynamic consistency, and how does he handle a prospect that is assessed differently depending on when the choice is made? Fifth, the boundary between risk and ambiguity: what does the theory say about Ellsberg-style cases, and is its silence there a limitation or a deliberate scope restriction?

Close with a question asking what evidence, short of a Dutch book, would lead him to abandon the view.
