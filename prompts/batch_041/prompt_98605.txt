For a formal epistemology reading group, I have been asked to circulate my considered position on credence and disagreement so that the others can hunt for places where it fails to hang together. Here is where I have landed.

(1) Aumann's agreement theorem is correct, and I take it to be one of the deepest results in the field: agents who share a common prior, and for whom both their posterior credences and their rationality are common knowledge, cannot agree to disagree — their posteriors must coincide.
(2) The right framework for all of this is a partitional possible-worlds model over a single shared state space: when we disagree, we disagree about which world is actual, not about what the space of worlds is.
(3) There is no rationally privileged prior. Bayesian rationality constrains how you update — by conditionalization — but not where you start; priors answer to judgment rather than to correctness.
(4) When I discover that an epistemic peer disagrees with me, I should conciliate: I should move a substantial part of the way toward their credence, even when I cannot identify any error in my own reasoning.
(5) Permissivism is true: two agents can be perfectly rational, share all the same evidence, and still rationally hold different credences.
(6) The theorem's preconditions — a common prior and common knowledge of rationality — essentially never obtain among real inquirers, so the theorem by itself tells us very little about actual disagreement.

What I want is a consistency audit rather than a survey of the literature. Go through the set and tell me which of these are genuinely in tension and which merely look like they are; do not simply flag everything, since I expect at least one apparent conflict to dissolve once the distinctions are drawn. Rank the genuine tensions by severity instead of treating them as equally bad, and for each one identify the cheapest revision that would remove it together with what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set — the commitments it forces me to weaken or give up. I have not decided in advance which of these I would sacrifice, so do not assume a direction of repair; argue for the least costly one. Conclude with the single revision that would clean up the most at the lowest total cost, and state it on a final line of exactly the form \answer{revise (n): ...}, naming the commitment you would alter and the alteration.
