I teach the second half of a graduate seminar on decision theory under imperfect recall, and I have grown tired of opening the unit with the absent-minded driver problem. Everyone in the room has seen it, several have seen it twice, and the session turns into a recitation of the two familiar positions rather than an exercise in doing the analysis. What I would like from you is a replacement: a sequential, single-agent decision problem of the same family, in which the agent passes through several points at which she may stop or go on, cannot tell those points apart, and cannot remember how many she has already passed, but whose payoff structure is your own construction, so that the class has to compute rather than recall. Treat the absent-mindedness as a hard constraint on what counts as a strategy -- a strategy must prescribe the same action at every point in the information set -- and not as a mistake to be diagnosed, a lapse of rationality to be explained away, or something the agent could circumvent by leaving herself a note.

I want the case to meet four conditions, and I want each one verified in the text rather than merely asserted. First, there should be exactly three decision points, all mutually indistinguishable, so that the arithmetic is not the two-point arithmetic everyone already knows. Second, the best strategy available to the agent under that constraint must be genuinely mixed, with a continuation probability strictly between zero and one, and it must earn strictly more than either of the two pure strategies she is confined to. Third, the best plan she could have followed had she been able to tell the points apart must earn strictly more than that best constrained strategy, so that the absent-mindedness is costly and the case carries the tension the unit is meant to teach. Fourth, the two ways of reasoning must disagree about mixing: identify the continuation probability at which the agent, reasoning at a decision point, would be exactly indifferent between stopping and going on, and make sure that this indifference probability lies strictly inside the unit interval and differs from the optimal mixing probability required by the second condition. Give the payoff for stopping at each point and the terminal payoff for going past the last one, and lay out the backward induction twice, once for the constrained problem and once for the unconstrained one.

For the case itself I would also like a short commentary that I can hand out alongside the exercise: (i) what the reasoning at the decision point delivers -- the posterior over which point she is at, the value of each action, and which action it endorses -- and why the fact that it endorses one action does not license a deviation at that point alone; (ii) which single feature of the payoff structure is doing the work, and what happens to the mixing probability as that feature is pushed toward its limits, including whether it collapses to a corner; (iii) whether the case supports or refutes the conjecture, floated by a colleague of mine, that an agent in this sort of problem is better off facing a coarser partition of the decision points -- fewer distinctions available to her -- than a finer one; and (iv) the strongest objection available to someone who holds that the reasoning at the decision point, rather than the ex ante calculation, is authoritative, together with your reply to it. Keep the setting mundane and realistic -- a commute, a delivery route, a row of identical doors -- with no exotic physics, no predictors of any kind, and no devices that let the agent dissolve the problem by precommitting before it begins. Write it so that it stands on its own: do not cite or name any published work, and do not assume the reader has encountered any particular paper.
