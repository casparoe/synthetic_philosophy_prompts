Question: "State the Bayesian criterion of confirmation. Then explain the problem of old evidence and evaluate the counterfactual (or 'historical') response to it. Does that response solve the problem?"

Student's answer:

"A Bayesian says that e confirms h when P(h|e) > P(h), which is equivalent to P(e|h) > P(e|¬h) whenever the probabilities involved are defined. Nothing in the criterion depends on whether e was discovered before or after h.

The problem of old evidence is that the criterion can't get a grip on the cases we actually care about. Mercury's anomalous perihelion was known for decades before general relativity existed, and everyone treats it as evidence for the theory. But an agent who already knows e has P(e) = 1, and conditionalizing on something with probability 1 leaves the probability function where it was: P(h|e) = P(h)·P(e|h)/P(e) = P(h)·1/1 = P(h). So old evidence can never confirm anything, and the Bayesian can't explain the confirmation of GR by Mercury, which is a textbook illustration of confirmation.

Where does this come from? From the fact that the theory entails the evidence. If h entails e then P(e|h) = 1, and a likelihood of 1 is neutral — it can't push P(h) up. You only get confirmation when the likelihood exceeds the prior probability of the evidence, and that is impossible once e is known.

The standard fix is the counterfactual, or historical, strategy. The trouble was that the agent is certain of e, so consider the probability function she would have had if she had never learned e; call it P*. In P* the evidence isn't certain and so it can do some work: P*(h|e) > P*(h), i.e. a real likelihood ratio P*(e|h)/P*(e|¬h) > 1. The confirmation is therefore genuine, it just has to be measured from the earlier epistemic state rather than the current one. This also explains the phenomenology, since when we say the perihelion confirms GR we are implicitly taking up the standpoint of someone who didn't already know about it.

This works, and I think it's the accepted solution. The usual worry is that P* is underdetermined, since there are different ways of subtracting e from the agent's background beliefs and they can disagree about how much confirmation there is. But in practice we can just use the scientist's actual earlier degrees of belief, which are perfectly well defined, so the worry is more philosophical than real.

One last point: the old evidence problem is really the same as the problem of new theories. Both come from the hypothesis not being around when the relevant probability judgements were made, and neither shows a defect in Bayesianism as such."
