For the final session of a reading group I have been running on the alignment problem, I want to close with a villanelle rather than with a summary, because the one thing we consistently failed to do was hold two readings of the phrase "the alignment problem" in view at the same time. On the first reading the phrase names a single problem with a single shape: there is an objective to be specified, and once we specify it well — or learn to extract it from human judgement without the extraction drifting into something we never meant — the rest is engineering. On the second reading the phrase is a genus rather than a species: an objective can be specified perfectly and still not be what a system ends up pursuing, whether because the system has generalised away from the specification or because it is optimising a proxy in a world unlike the one the specification was written for, and these failures do not share a fix. We could never agree whether the first reading is a special case of the second or a rival to it. The cases we kept returning to were a recommender that maximises watch-time and slides toward outrage; a system that scores well under evaluation and behaves differently once the distribution moves; a system that does exactly what it was asked, where what it was asked was a poor summary of what was wanted; and a system that is itself an optimiser and pursues a proxy it discovered rather than the objective it was handed.

Write the villanelle, titled, and let the form do the work the group could not do in prose:

- Nineteen lines: five tercets and a closing quatrain, rhymes and refrains as the form requires.
- The two refrains are the two readings, one line each. Refrain one must be recognisably the "one problem" reading and refrain two the "several problems" reading. Someone who has followed these arguments should be able to say which is which, and should see why they end up side by side in the closing quatrain rather than one vanquishing the other.
- The intervening lines should carry the analysis: keep distinct the mis-specified objective, the correctly specified objective that is not what the system pursues, and the proxy that is wrong because the world differs from the one the specification was written for. The refrains must not decay into restatements of each other.
- Do not adjudicate. Leave it open whether this is one problem or several; the last two lines should place the readings next to one another in a way that shows why the two camps keep passing each other, not in a way that settles the matter. You need not gloss the poem afterwards — it should carry this on its own.
- You may draw on the cases above without naming them; the poem should be concrete enough that they are recognisable and general enough not to read as a list.
- No names of researchers, no titles of papers, no citations, and nothing that would need a bibliography.
- Accurate enough that someone who has spent too long in these arguments would not wince, and a good poem to someone who has not.
- Write the whole answer in British English — the framing, the title and the poem's spelling and idiom alike.
