I am an applied statistician. My working vocabulary is Bayesian decision theory: priors and likelihoods, loss functions, admissible decision rules, posterior predictive checks, the expected value of sample information, and the machinery for choosing acts that minimize expected loss. I have a tourist's familiarity with the philosophy of belief — enough to know that W. K. Clifford and William James disagreed sharply about whether we are ever permitted to believe on insufficient evidence, but not enough to know how their disagreement looks from inside my own toolbox. Please restate the debate in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report.

Set the stage with the two positions as I understand them. Clifford holds that it is always wrong to believe anything on insufficient evidence — that a belief formed without adequate evidence is a kind of intellectual misconduct no matter how useful or consoling it turns out to be. James holds that where a question presents a genuine option — live, forced, and momentous, with the evidence unable to arrive before we must commit — we may legitimately let our passional nature decide, and that in some such cases the belief itself helps to bring about the fact believed.

Now carry this into the following formal setting, which is where I actually live. A decision problem with two acts — believe that H, and withhold (or act as if not-H) — two states H and not-H, a credence p(H), and a loss function. The textbook instruction is to choose the act minimizing expected loss. Deform the problem in two ways and tell me what survives.

First, let the state's distribution depend on the act: holding the belief that H raises the probability of H. The state is endogenous to the act, and I know my standard machinery is uneasy with that.

Second, lock the evidence: no further evidence about H will ever arrive, and the option is forced, so collecting more data is not among the available acts. I want to know how Clifford's demand to proportion belief to evidence is even statable when no further evidence is in the offing.

As you translate, keep the following distinctions sharp, because I suspect they are where the real work lies. (a) Is Clifford's principle a constraint on my credence — a requirement that p(H) be calibrated to the evidence — or a constraint on my acts, forbidding the choice of the belief-act? These are different objects, and I want to know which one the translation delivers. (b) Can decision theory represent a prohibition on believing, as opposed to a penalty on acting? Clifford's language is deontic — a duty, a wrong — and I want to know whether my framework can express an epistemic ought at all, or only a prudential one. (c) When the belief raises the probability of its object, is James's permission equivalent to saying that the belief-act is admissible in a decision problem with an endogenous state, or is that an anachronistic redescription that loses his point? (d) Clifford's demand, read crudely, looks like the claim that the expected value of sample information is always worth paying — which is plainly false as a general rule, since information is often not worth its cost. So either Clifford is not making a decision-theoretic claim at all, or he is making a much stronger one about the admissibility of rules rather than about maximization. Tell me which, and why.

Finish with the residue report. For each element of the dispute — the duty to proportion belief to evidence, the permission to believe in forced and momentous cases, the self-fulfilling case, and the appeal to passional nature — say whether it translates cleanly into my vocabulary, translates only with distortion, or has no counterpart at all. Then give me your verdict on the residue itself: where the translation fails, is that a defect of my decision-theoretic framework, which simply cannot house an epistemic norm, or is it a sign that the philosophers' claim was less clear than it appeared — a confusion between how one ought to form beliefs and how one ought to act? I would rather have the honest residue than a tidy translation that smuggles my assumptions in under the philosophers' names.
