Two colleagues of mine, a hospital ethicist and a criminal lawyer, spent most of a dinner arguing over a case the ethicist had invented, and they have asked me to referee. The case: a woman will die within days unless she receives a drug that is inert as manufactured and becomes active only when an enzyme in her one remaining kidney converts it; the conversion consumes the very tissue that performs it, so the treatment cannot work unless the organ is destroyed. The ethicist said the doctor who administers it is buying the cure with the kidney's destruction, and is therefore using that destruction as her means — which is precisely what the doctrine of double effect forbids, since the doctrine permits causing harm as a side effect of a good act but never as the route to it. The lawyer said the doctor is not aiming at the kidney at all: she is aiming at the cure, and would give a great deal for a route that spared the organ, so the damage is a side effect however certain it may be. The ethicist replied that "aiming" then means whatever the doctor chooses to avow, and that the same test would acquit the terror bomber, who kills civilians to break an enemy's will and would also far rather win the war without the corpses; the doctrine, he said, must turn on the structure of the act rather than on the agent's regrets, and the structure he proposed was that the harm must be spent in producing the good rather than merely be a by-product of producing it — ribs broken by chest compressions are a by-product, whereas kidney tissue consumed in activating the drug is not. The lawyer answered that this criterion is either too strong or empty: too strong, because the fractures are unavoidable if the compressions are to circulate any blood, and a harm one cannot avoid is not thereby one's means; empty, because "spent in producing" collapses into "whatever the agent's plan treats as load-bearing," which is the intention test the ethicist began by mocking.

Adjudicate their dispute in exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two: for each of the four contested steps, say who has the better of it and why, and use the fifth to say what both of them are missing. No introduction or conclusion.
