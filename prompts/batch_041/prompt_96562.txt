I'm in my third year of a joint history and philosophy degree, and one of my modules — a course on historiography and the philosophy of history — has its second half built around the Fischer controversy: Fritz Fischer's claim that Germany bore the primary responsibility for the outbreak of the First World War, and the uproar it caused among historians who insisted that the war was the product of a system, of rival alliance blocs, of miscalculation and drift, rather than of any one nation's design. I can follow both the evidence and the counter-evidence reasonably well, but I've realised I don't have a clear grip on what kind of claim either side is making when it says that a nation was responsible for a war. Please explain the conceptual issue here as precisely as you can: what exactly is the difference between the causal question (what made the war happen) and the responsibility question (who is to blame for it), and why does answering the first not settle the second? A classmate in the seminar thinks the whole controversy is just moralising dressed up as history, and I couldn't say clearly why I thought that was too quick.

I'd like the explanation to work through the specific difficulties rather than gesture at them. First, the outbreak was massively overdetermined, so it looks as though no single decision — not the blank cheque to Vienna, not the mobilisation timetables, not the Austrian ultimatum — made the difference between war and no war; and if causal responsibility requires making a difference, it seems to follow that nobody was responsible, which is absurd. Second, the relevant decisions were dispersed across dozens of ministers, cabinets, general staffs and publics, so that the outcome has the structure people call a problem of many hands: each contribution is partial, no one intended the whole, and yet the whole happened. Third, there is the question of who could even be the bearer of responsibility — individual officials, a state, a nation, a political culture — given that we are told a state is not a person and yet we seem perfectly comfortable holding it responsible, and given that in this case a verdict was in fact issued, at Versailles, in a quasi-legal form, which makes the historiography look downstream of a political settlement rather than independent of it. I can't tell which of these are genuinely distinct problems, which are the same problem wearing different clothes, and how much of the disagreement is conceptual rather than simply about the politics of blame.

What I want is a clean anatomy rather than a survey: where the concept of responsibility is doing legitimate work, where it is being stretched past breaking point, whether the no-difference objection and the many-hands problem threaten causal and moral responsibility equally or only one of them, and whether there is a defensible way to say something like Germany bore a special responsibility for the war that is neither a disguised moral verdict on an entire people nor a merely causal claim about one state among several. If the honest answer is that the phrase equivocates between several different claims and that the controversy is partly a dispute about which of them historians are entitled to make, I'd rather hear that spelled out. I'm not after a reading list — I'd like the distinctions drawn sharply, with the cost of each position made explicit.
