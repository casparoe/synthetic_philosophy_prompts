I am a novelist, some way into a book whose central figure is a bookmaker, and I have spent the past fortnight trying to get the intellectual furniture of his trade right without making a fool of myself. Over lunch a friend of mine — a good philosopher, though the philosophy of probability is not his patch — gave me his potted account of the Dutch book argument, and I wrote it down almost as he said it, because it sounded to me like a proof. This is what he told me: "If your degrees of belief break the axioms of probability, then a bookmaker who knows your credences can offer you a series of bets, each of which looks fair or favourable to you, such that whatever happens in the world you end up out of pocket. No rational person would accept a set of bets that guarantees a loss, and the only credences that make you immune to such a set are the ones that obey the probability calculus; so the argument proves that rationality requires you to have probabilistic credences. The extension to updating is straightforward: an agent who fails to conditionalise can be fleeced in exactly the same way, so Bayesian updating is likewise a requirement of rationality. And the beauty of it is that you need to know nothing about the man's tastes — a sure loss of money is a sure loss, whatever the stakes. Since a Dutch book can be run against anyone incoherent and never against anyone coherent, the argument vindicates the subjectivist picture: any coherent set of degrees of belief is as good as any other, and the only thing rationality has to say about your credences is that they must not be incoherent." He then grew excited about my book and proposed a twist he was sure would give it its tragic spine: "if a punter's credences are coherent, no Dutch book can be run against him, so your bookmaker can only make a living off punters who are irrational. A man whose trade is coherence-proof would starve — that is your novel."

I should like you to act as an expert in the foundations of probability and in Bayesian epistemology, and to audit all of this for me, carefully and without flattery. Go clause by clause and tell me what a philosopher of probability would accept without demur, what he would want to qualify or hedge, and what is simply wrong; where my friend slides from a theorem about bets to a thesis about rationality, or from the synchronic to the diachronic, or from coherence to justification, say so plainly, tell me which of his conclusions survive the correction and which do not, and, wherever you think the argument itself can be defended against his gloss, show me how the defence would go. I am especially keen to know whether his extension to updating really is as straightforward as he believes, and whether the twist about my bookmaker is right — it is the part I most want to be true, which is presumably the reason I should distrust it. Where the matter is genuinely contested rather than settled, tell me so instead of manufacturing a verdict, and mark anything that turns on how one reads a technical term rather than on a matter of fact. Then, so that I can actually use this, give me a short list of the sentences I could safely put into my bookmaker's mouth and the ones that would get me laughed out of a philosopher's dinner party.

Before you begin, set out in a brief paragraph how you propose to organise the audit, then work through it in order; define your terms as you go, since I am not a specialist and I would rather be told what a sure-loss bet is than have it assumed. I do not want citations, references or a reading list of any kind — I cannot look anything up, and a bibliography I cannot check is of no use to me — so give me the substance in your own words. Please write throughout in British English.
