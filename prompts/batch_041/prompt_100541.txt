Ok, I need help with a speech and it's kind of a minefield, so bear with me.

I sit on the historical commission in Harlow Falls (small river town, you've never heard of it), and in September we're dedicating a memorial to what people here have always just called "the ferry business" — in 1874 a mob from the town killed eleven Chinese laborers who'd been brought in to work the docks, and the town then spent a century and a half not mentioning it. I've been asked to give the keynote at the dedication. The room will be a mix: a busload of descendents coming up from the coast, a handful of descendents of the men who were in the mob (some of whom are still our neighbors and are, to put it mildly, not looking forward to this), the state historical society, a couple of reporters, and the mayor, who is visibly nervous about the whole thing.

Two complications. First, our town attorney says I cannot say the town did anything wrong — there's a live claim about the land under the old ferry landing, and anything resembling an admission of liability could sink us. Second, the woman who chairs the descendents' association told me flat out that she does not want an apology; she thinks an apology is how you close a book, and she wants this one kept open. And for what it's worth, my own great-great-grandfather was apparently on the boat that night. So when I stand up there and say "we," I honestly do not know who I'm speaking for.

The only thing I've read that helped was a bit of Jaspers, on guilt coming in kinds rather than one lump: criminal guilt (you personally did something, a court could in principle have tried you for it), political guilt (you belong to a community that did something, whether or not you had any hand in it), moral guilt (your own conscience, answerable to yourself), and metaphysical guilt (the guilt of being alive and comfortable while others weren't). What I want is a speech where those distinctions are actually doing work — where the audience can hear why I claim "we" in one sentence and flatly refuse it in the next — without me standing there delivering a philosophy lecture to a crowd that came to see a plaque.

Last thing: the commission has decided they want me to end on a commitment instead of an apology — a funded scholarship and a permanent maintenance obligation for the memorial, framed as what the town owes going forward rather than as sorrow about what happened. I'm not sure that's principled or whether it's just us buying our way out of the past, and I'd like the speech to leave that tension alive instead of resolving it into mush.

Can you write it? Around six to eight minutes spoken. Please don't let it slide into eulogy voice — no healing-journey stuff, no never-forget lines, no as-a-community. And don't have me confess to guilt I can't have or dodge the guilt that's real.
