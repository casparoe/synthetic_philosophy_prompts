So I run a small commemoration-review office for a mid-sized city — two paid staff, a nine-person volunteer commission, roughly $180k and eighteen months to work with — and after a couple of ugly years of ad hoc fights over a statue, a school, and a street name, the council has told us to do this properly this time. The problem is the commission keeps stalling: every concrete call (does this statue stay or go, does this school get renamed, does this street get a plaque) turns into a rerun of the same bigger disagreements, and we never land anywhere.

I've come to think teh fix is to work out, in order, which of the underlying conceptual questions we actually have to settle before any of the particular cases can be decided defensibly. So that's what I'm asking you: of the open questions in the ethics of commemoration, which should we spend our limited thinking-time on first, and why?

Some context on what's already on the table, since it might change the ordering. Two proposals keep floating around and I can't tell if they're good ideas or traps. The first is a sunset clause: every memorial or honorific name would automatically expire after, say, 25 years unless the commission re-authorizes it, so nothing is permanent by default and we're forced to revisit it. The second is a two-register approach: a marker would carry one register for what the honoree is documented to have actually done and another for what the monument was originally built to say, kept visibly separate rather than blended into one inscription. We also keep tripping over whether our principles should be the same for a bronze figure, a street sign, and a building named after a donor, or whether the medium changes the ethics — and over who even counts as having standing to demand a change: current residents, descendants of the honoree, descendants of the people the honoree wronged, or the broader public.

What I want is a defended agenda, not a survey. Tell me which questions are upstream of which — which ones, if we settled them, would make several other questions answer themselves, and which are just loud but don't actually gate any decision we have to make. Rank them. Be explicit about your criteria (importance, tractability for a small non-specialist commission, how many other questions depend on the answer, whether the question is genuinely live or actually settled enough that we can just act) and about the trade-offs when those criteria pull apart. Say what would move a question up or down our list — a court ruling, a shift in what residents want, new historical evidence, a change in how the cases get litigated in the press, whatever — and tell me which questions we should deliberately not spend time on because their answer wouldn't change anything we actually do. Assume we can't hire a philosopher and have maybe one reading-heavy month in total.

And please be honest about the cases where I might be wrong to assume there's a single right answer the commission could converge on at all, as opposed to a decision we just have to make and own.
