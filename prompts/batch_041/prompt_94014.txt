I am a high school student in a philosophy club, and we have been discussing Leibniz's moral philosophy alongside contemporary debates about artificial intelligence. A scenario came up that I cannot settle on my own. A technology company proposes to create millions of artificial minds by running detailed simulations of human brains on computers. The simulations behave as if they feel pleasure and pain, and the company markets them as companions that can be happy or sad. Some people argue that we must treat these beings as moral patients with rights; others say they are only software and cannot truly suffer. The company itself makes a confusing claim: because each simulated mind is built from many interacting parts, it is not a simple substance, and therefore it cannot be a genuine mind, even though it is advertised as one that can suffer.

If Leibniz were somehow able to consider this proposal, what would his position be on three connected questions: whether such a being could be a mind at all, whether it could genuinely suffer or enjoy anything, and whether it would be morally permissible to create it for our purposes? I am not looking for a general summary of Leibniz. I want you to reason from his actual principles, especially his account of monads as simple substances that cannot be assembled from parts, his principle of sufficient reason, his perfectionism, and his idea of justice as the charity of the wise. Please make the extrapolation explicit at each step, and distinguish what Leibniz's principles clearly imply from what they leave underdetermined. I would rather you tell me that my scenario rests on a confusion, or that Leibniz's framework cannot yield a definite answer, than force a tidy verdict. If my framing assumes something Leibniz would reject, say so plainly and explain where the question goes wrong.
