I teach a graduate seminar on causation and moral responsibility, and a student has produced an argument that I can neither refute to my satisfaction nor bring myself to accept. I would like you to diagnose it for me. The argument runs as follows. (1) An agent’s omission is a cause of an outcome whenever the outcome counterfactually depends on that omission: had the agent done something she could have done, the outcome would not have occurred. (2) Last month I could have donated a few hundred dollars to an effective charity that distributes antimalarial bed nets. Assume that my donation would have been marginal, that no other donor would have replaced it, and that it would have saved the life of a particular child, Aisha, who in fact died of malaria. (3) Therefore my failure to donate is a cause of Aisha’s death. (4) If my failure to donate is a cause of Aisha’s death, then I killed Aisha. (5) If I killed Aisha, then I am morally on a par with someone who deliberately smothered a sleeping child. (6) I am not morally on a par with such a person. (7) Therefore my failure to donate is not a cause of Aisha’s death, and the counterfactual test in (1) must be rejected.

A colleague in the department mutters something about contrastive causation and the normativity of causal selection whenever I press him on this, but I cannot tell whether that is a diagnosis or a dodge. I would rather keep (1) and (6) than accept (5), but I want to know what I am paying. Which step do you reject? Explain why that step looked plausible, and set out what rejecting it costs, both for a theory of causation by omission and for the moral conclusion about my failure to donate.
