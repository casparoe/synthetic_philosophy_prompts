I write a weekly column on science and ideas, and each term I guest-teach one session in a university philosophy department's upper-level seminar on action and causation; the instructor has asked me to set the take-home final for the unit on causation by omission. I want a single essay question that a strong student cannot dispatch by reciting the standard line that an omission "counts as a cause when it passes the but-for test." Before you write anything, take the role of a philosopher of action and causation and think carefully: brainstorm three or four candidate cruxes for such a question, say to yourself which one is hardest to bluff through and why, then discard the others and build the question around the survivor. Deliver three things in order: the exam question itself; a model answer of roughly 600 words; and a marking scheme that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain.

Build the question on the following vignette, which I have been turning over and which you should treat as stipulated (do not argue about whether the counterfactuals hold — assume they do):

A regional rail operator runs a semi-automated braking system. Overnight, an engineer named Dana is assigned to review a software patch, leaves the review unfinished, and never flags it; the patch ships. A supervisor, aware that the review was incomplete, declines to halt the rollout, because halting it has no precedent and would require a signature he would rather not give. A signal controller on duty at the time of the incident notices an anomaly on her display and, out of a settled habit of not second-guessing the automation, does not call for a manual stop. A collision follows. For each of the three people, and for the automated system itself, it is stipulated that but for the relevant inaction (or, in the machine's case, the non-intervention) the collision would not have occurred.

The question must force students to do more than list these as co-causes. It should make them confront why a but-for criterion alone cannot license calling all of them "the cause" without equivocation; whether an omission — a non-event — can enter a causal relation at all, and what a defensible answer to that looks like; and how a contrastive treatment of causation (the idea that a cause is always a cause of an outcome as opposed to some specific alternative) changes which of the four candidates gets selected and why. Then introduce, and require students to evaluate, a distinction I want the question to supply rather than borrow: between an omission that is a one-off gap in an otherwise active course of conduct and an omission that is the maintained output of a standing policy or habit — and ask whether causal selection should treat those two differently, and what the presence of the automated system does to that distinction, given that a machine can be said to "fail to act" only by courtesy at best. The question should also require students to say explicitly what their chosen criterion does not settle — in particular, why fixing the cause does not by itself deliver a verdict about blame, and where the moral and legal questions re-enter.

For the marking scheme, give five must-hit points, assign each a mark out of a total of 20, and for each point add one line naming the characteristic way candidates get it wrong — for example, treating the norm-violation or expectation-relativity of causal selection as if it were itself a criterion of causation, or sliding from "this omission was selected as the cause" to "this agent is therefore culpable." Make clear in the scheme what distinguishes an A from a B: an A answer must state its criterion for selecting a cause, defend it against at least one rival, and show it doing work on the vignette rather than gesturing at it. Assume the students have read the standard literature on negative causation and causal selection but should not be rewarded for name-dropping; the marks are for arguments, not citations. Set the paper so a well-prepared candidate could write it in ninety minutes.
