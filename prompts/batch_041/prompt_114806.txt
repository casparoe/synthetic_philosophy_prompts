I'm a backend engineer at a company that sells a subscription with a monthly allowance (think mobile data or cloud credits), and I've somehow ended up as the de facto owner of our pricing rules, which in practice means I'm the person who has to say no when the pricing team proposes another "harmless" swap. Here's the shape of it: users can switch plans mid-month, convert unused allowance into credits, buy top-ups, roll credits into next month, and so on. Every rule, looked at on its own, is somthing a sane person would happily take -- you keep your unused allowance, you get a small discount, you can always change your mind later. But I've been reading about a thing philosophers apparently call a money pump, where someone with slightly loopy preferences, or just preferences that shift over time, can be walked around a loop of trades that are each individually fine and end up strictly worse off holding exactly the same thing they started with, and I'm now fairly convinced our rule set contains at least two of these. What I want from you is a procedure my team can actually run on a laptop before we ship a rule change. Act like a decision theorist who also has to ship code and sit through release meetings -- someone who knows the theory but also knows how dumb a procedure has to be before engineers stop using it. Before you write anything, brainstorm out loud about what the procedure has to be able to see: the moves we allow, what each move costs the user, what state it leaves them in, which moves are reversible and which aren't, and how the answer changes depending on whether we're dealing with one user whose preferences are stable but slightly incoherent versus a user whose preferences at month-end are not the preferences they had at the start of the month (this second one is what the reading seems to call the diachronic thing, and I genuinely do not know how to handle it). Then write the procedure: the steps in order, what I have to write down to take each step, what counts as passing, what counts as an automatic no, and -- this part I care about most -- the points where it has to stop and say 'this isnt a fact question anymore, a person has to decide what we're willing to sell,' because I suspect half our rules live there and I keep pretending they don't.

Practical constraints, since they'll shape the whole thing: we ship weekly, so it needs to run in under half an hour; the person running it will be a PM who has never read a philosophy paper and shouldn't need to; and our head of pricing signs off, so she'll want a one-line verdict plus the reason. Assume we can't survey users or elicit their real preferences, so the procedure has to work off the state graph and a small number of assumptions we can defend in a meeting -- be explicit about which assumptions it's making and what breaks if one turns out false. It also has to handle the case where the loop only closes for a user who doesn't notice the trap at step one, because I need to know whether that's still a pump or just a bad deal for the inattentive, and our lawyers will ask me exactly that. Please run it end to end on three of our actual rule bundles: (1) the 'downgrade mid-month to a cheaper plan but keep the full allowance, then pay a top-up fee when the allowance runs out early' pair, which pricing loves and which I'm pretty sure is a straight pump; (2) the rollover rule where credits bought in advance expire two weeks later, which only bites users who planned around a use we then made inconvenient -- I can't tell if that's a pump or a different disease entirely; and (3) one where I hope the answer is 'you're overthinking it, that one's fine.' At least one of those should come back with an answer I won't like, and I'd rather hear it now than in a support ticket. Finish by telling me what this procedure structurally cannot decide, and which of the philosophical disputes underneath it -- whether it even makes sense to treat January-me and March-me as the same chooser, whether being willing to pay to avoid a future option proves the option is bad, whether a stable-but-incoherent chooser is being exploited or just has weird tastes -- would actually flip the verdict on a real rule, because if none of them would, I'd like to stop worrying about them.
