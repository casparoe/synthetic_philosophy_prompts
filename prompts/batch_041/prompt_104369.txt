I write fair-play detective novels, and for some years I have kept a private list of what I believe about how a plot earns its solution. A philosopher friend has agreed to audit the list, so I am trying to set it down cleanly first. Here it is.

1. A reader's degrees of belief are probabilities, and the only respectable way for them to change is by conditionalizing on what she learns.
2. A fair-play mystery must be solvable from the clues that precede the reveal. My test is rereading: on a second pass, each planted detail should read as evidence that had been favoring the solution all along.
3. The reveal is a genuine epistemic event. When the detective explains, the reader's confidence in the solution rightly rises, and it is correct to say that the earlier clues confirmed it.
4. Nothing is learned at the reveal. Every clue was already on the page; the explanation contributes no new fact, only emphasis and arrangement.
5. Surprise is a matter of attention and salience, not of justification — a reader can be honestly startled by something she already had grounds to believe.
6. There is no fact of the matter about what a reader who had not yet reached the clues would have believed. Counterfactual credences about merely possible readers seem to me not well-defined.
7. In drafting, I decide who did it and then keep the details that point at that person, discarding the rest. That is how I make (2) true.

What I want is an audit, not encouragement. Tell me which of these actually pull against one another, rank the tensions by severity, and say for each what would have to give. Where two entries only appear to conflict, say why they do not really — I would rather be told that one of my worries is imaginary than have everything treated as equally fraught. Finally, tell me which single change would resolve the most while costing the least.
