I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on free will and cognitive science, and I'd like to turn a half-formed idea into a proposal for my department's ten-week summer research fellowship. The idea concerns the sphex wasp — the insect that hauls her prey to the burrow, goes inside to inspect, and, when the prey is nudged aside while she is away, repeats the whole routine instead of dragging it in — and the label sphexishness that gets attached to such behavior. Discussions of the case tend to treat sphexishness as a single thing, one failure of flexibility that we are invited to worry we might share, but I think at least two distinct failures are in play. A system can be locally rigid, unable to depart from a fixed routine once it has begun, without being globally rigid, unable to step back and revise the procedure or the goal itself; and the converse seems possible too, since a system might improvise endlessly inside a task it can never call into question. I want to argue that only global rigidity bears on the free-will worry, and then to ask what could count as evidence of global rigidity in another system, or in ourselves, when a loop is visible only from outside it. My tentative fix is to index rigidity to the information the system itself has, so that a routine is sphexish only when the system possesses, or could represent, the very fact that would require it to stop — which may leave the wasp off the hook and put us on it. Please write the proposal: state the question so that it has a determinate answer, explain why existing treatments leave it open rather than settling it, set out what I would actually do week by week (I imagine a conceptual taxonomy plus a small set of worked cases, not anything empirical), say what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing, and be honest about whether ten weeks is enough. I'll add citations myself, so no bibliography is needed. Where the topic is settled versus genuinely contested among people who work on it, say which is which, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus; also keep it in continuous prose, with no headings, bullets, bold, or tables.
