Over the last few years I've been drawn into several small disputes about who gets to decide things, and I've started to suspect they share a hidden structure. In one, a tenants' group was writing up demands and had to settle whether the building superintendent — who lives on site but works for the landlord — should vote on them. In another, a residents' association argued over whether the shop owners on the ground floor counted as members. In a third, a neighborhood planning campaign split over whether renters, who don't own property, should have a say. Each time we hit the same snag: we couldn't vote on the question in front of us until we knew who was allowed to vote, and nobody had the standing to settle that first question without already assuming the answer. What struck me is that people kept offering two different principles for drawing the line — one said anyone whose life would be affected by the outcome should count, the other said anyone who would be bound by the rule should — and these come apart exactly in the cases that mattered (children, people abroad, people not yet here). Does this snag have an established name, and is it really one problem or two? I'm less interested in the label than in knowing how it relates to the familiar worry that a majority can be unjust to a minority, and whether anyone has argued that the circle can be broken rather than merely lived with.
