The question the class was set was this: set out the structure of the scale argument for taking insect welfare seriously — the argument that insects' enormous numbers, combined with even a small probability that they are sentient, may make the expected amount of insect suffering far exceed that of vertebrates. Explain what the argument does and does not require, assess how much weight it should carry, and consider at least one objection.

Here is what my classmate handed in. The argument has two premises and a conclusion. The first premise is a fact about numbers: insects are vastly more numerous than vertebrates, on the order of 10^18 individuals alive at any time by some estimates, which is many orders of magnitude more than all vertebrates combined. The second premise is that we cannot rule out the possibility that insects are sentient; there is at least some probability, even if it is small, that they have valenced experience. The conclusion is that the expected amount of insect suffering — the probability that they are sentient multiplied by the amount of suffering there would be if they were — is very likely to exceed the expected suffering of vertebrates, because a very small probability can be offset by an enormous multiplier of numbers. What the argument does not require is certainty. That is what separates it from the straightforward claim that insects are sentient, which would need positive evidence. It requires only that the probability not be zero, or at least that we cannot rationally assign it zero. In this respect it has the same structure as Pascal's wager: a very small probability is paired with a very large payoff, so the expected value dominates and the choice is forced. The argument is therefore a decision-theoretic argument rather than an empirical one. The argument does have limits. First, there must be some threshold below which the probability is so small that the numbers no longer compensate; if the chance of insect sentience were less than, say, one in a million, the argument would collapse. Since we have no way of estimating the probability precisely, we cannot tell whether we are above or below that threshold, and this is a serious weakness. Second, the argument establishes only that insect suffering may be very large in expectation; it does not tell us how to reduce it, and it does not follow that any particular intervention, such as avoiding silk or honey, is worth doing, since we would also need evidence that the intervention reduces suffering rather than merely redistributing it. Third, the argument treats suffering across species as commensurable, which some philosophers deny. I conclude that the scale argument gives a strong reason to take the possibility of insect sentience seriously, but that it is not decisive, and that the uncertainty about the probability means it is better treated as a reason for caution than as a proof that insects matter more than vertebrates.
