ok so background: I've been lurking on LessWrong and various safety Discords for years, and I've somehow ended up as the note-taker on my lab's internal deployment-review group. It's not glamorous — six of us, a shared doc of past calls, and a standing meeting where a team walks in with a proposed safety measure and we either sign off on shipping behind it or send them back to think. I've been trying to reverse-engineer what rule we're actually running, because I don't think it's the one we say out loud. And next week there's a proposal I honestly can't predict, so I want to get my head straight first.

Here's our track record, roughly as I recorded it:

- We approved an internals monitor — an activation probe that fires when the model's internal state looks like it's trying to deceive us — even though it's noisy, has a fat false-positive tail, and costs us throughput. We treated it as a tripwire, not as proof of anything.
- We rejected a proposal where the model graded its own outputs and reported a calibrated confidence, and deployment gated on that confidence. The objection in the room was basically "you're asking the fox how the henhouse is doing."
- We approved, with conditions, a setup where a weaker trusted model reads the untrusted model's outputs and can also just run them in a sandbox. The reviewer never sees the big model's reasoning, only the artifact it produced.
- We rejected as a safety case the argument "we fine-tuned it to be honest, and it tells us it's aligned now."
- We approved a honeypot eval — give the model an obvious chance to sabotage and watch what it does — but explicitly only as evidence that informs a decision, never as a deployment gate. The minutes say something like: this tells us about this model, it doesn't bind the next one.
- We rejected "we ran dangerous-capability evals and it couldn't do the bad thing, so we don't need runtime controls."
- We approved resampling plus a trusted comparison model: run the untrusted model k times, flag the runs that disagree.
- One more data point that I think matters: we've never once rejected something for being expensive. We rejected things that didn't work; cost only ever changed the conditions we attached.

Now the proposal. The team wants to gate deployment on a monitor that reads the untrusted model's chain-of-thought. Concretely: the untrusted model is required to write out its reasoning in a separate reasoning channel, and a monitor model — fine-tuned from the same base model, not a different family — reads that channel and scores each step for "is this steering toward a backdoor or some other sabotage." Deployment gates on the monitor score, and a human reviews only the top 2% of scores. Their pitch is: (a) a schemer can't help but leak its plan in the trace, (b) the monitor is a separate model making the call, so this isn't self-grading, and (c) the trace is what makes the whole thing auditable. They also say they checked that the model's answers degrade when you strip the reasoning channel, so the trace is load-bearing and not just decorative. I pushed on the obfuscation angle and they said sure, in principle, but "you have to put the suspicion somewhere, and a monitor with a trace is strictly more information than a monitor without one."

So: what should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which of our precedents is the closest analog, and does it actually control this case or just look like it does? I'm pretty sure there's more than one rule that fits every call above and that they come apart here, so tell me what the candidate rules are, what each one predicts about the CoT monitor, which one you'd bet we're actually running, and why. And if our precedents genuinely underdetermine it, say what principle we'd be adopting with each choice — because I'd rather know what we're signing up for than win the vote. Last thing: name the further case that would actually tell the rules apart, ideally one I can put on the agenda before we decide, because I don't want to relitigate this every quarter.
