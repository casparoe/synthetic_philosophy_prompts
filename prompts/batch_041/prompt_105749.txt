I'm doing an MA in political theory and I keep having the same arguement with my flatmate, who's a PhD student working on free will and criminal justice — she's a hard incompatibilist in the Caruso mould, I'm broadly on the Dennett side (compatibilist, thinks the reactive attitudes earn their keep). We've stalled on a concrete case and I want to see how the thing actually runs when neither side is allowed to hand-wave, so I'd like you to write it out as a dialogue between the two of them.

Here's the set-up. A state legislature is debating a bill that splits sentencing into two tracks. Track A is a straight custodial sentence, framed in the statute as something the offender deserves. Track B is a shorter period of mandatory cognitive-behavioural and pharmacological treatment, framed purely as prevention, with no desert language anywhere in it, and the state's own actuaries reckon it cuts reoffending substantially. Offenders get to choose. The case driving the whole bill is a man with a long record who has formally asked the parole board to stop treating his history as a symptom to be managed and to put him on the desert track instead — he says the therapeutic framing is an insult and he'd rather do the harder time. Dennett-side says: honour the request, that's what treating him as an agent amounts to. Caruso-side says: his preference is itself the product of causes he never chose, and honouring it just launders retribution with a consent form.

Give them names, and let them work through the bill and the man. I want each of them pushed to the point where the stock moves run out: whether basic desert is doing any real work once you've conceded that responsibility comes in degrees and that we already discount sentences for upbringing and circumstance; whether the reactive-attitude defence of punishment survives once you notice the attitudes themselves are causally produced; whether a choice between two state-designed tracks is free in the sense that matters; and whether the coerciveness of the prevention track is better or worse than the stigma of the desert track. They can both agree early on that indeterminism is a red herring, so neither of them wins by invoking quantum randomness. Neither should be a strawman, and they should genuinely answer each other rather than trading monologues — if one of them scores a point, the other should have to absorb it and adjust. Somewhere in the region of 1200-1800 words, if that's a helpful guide.
