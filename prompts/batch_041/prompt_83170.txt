After a career as a field biologist — most of it spent on the breeding behaviour of wading birds — I have retired and, for the past three years, have been sitting in on a philosophy seminar for non-specialists, where I keep meeting a line out of my own old discipline being made to carry a great deal of weight. The line, as it is repeated in that room and in the popular science I read, is that only costly signals are honest; sometimes it is given its technical name, the handicap principle, and credited to Zahavi, with a later formal vindication from Grafen and a close cousin in the economists' account of education as a signal of ability. In the biology I knew, that was a claim about a particular class of evolutionary scenarios, defended with models and contested on points of assumption; in the seminar it functions as a general theorem about information, to the effect that anything sufficiently expensive to do cannot be faked, so that any costly display is a window onto some hidden quality — the peacock's tail, a religious fast, a university degree, a politician's public humiliation. I find myself half persuaded and half suspicious, and I cannot tell which of the two attitudes is warranted when the sentence is used to settle an argument about trustworthiness, human or animal.

What I would like is not a tutorial in the biology, which I can supply myself, but a patient sorting of the distinct precise theses that sentence might be expressing. I imagine they run from the almost empty — where 'costly' simply means whatever is required to make deception unprofitable, so that the slogan becomes true by construction — through the substantial, where the cost must be a real drain on the signaller's fitness, or must fall more heavily on low-quality signallers than on high-quality ones, or must be visible and impossible for the signaller to withhold, up to the strong readings that would actually license what I hear: that cost guarantees truthfulness, that any expensive behaviour is thereby a signal, or that the principle tells us what human institutions must be doing. Please lay these out in continuous prose, in order, naming each and giving a verdict on it — trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false — and then tell me which of them the people in that room must have in mind for their conclusions to follow, saying plainly if the reading they need is one of the false ones.

I should add that I would rather have an honest map of the argument's exposure than a tidy summary: tell me which consideration would most likely change your verdicts, what argument or evidence would move a reading from defensible to untenable, and where in your own reasoning you think the weak joint is. And please write it as one continuous piece of prose, the way one would write a long letter to a colleague — no headings, numbered points, bolded terms, or tables.
