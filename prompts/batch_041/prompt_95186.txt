I am a high school student, and for the past few weeks a group of us have been meeting once a week in an informal philosophy club that has drifted into a unit on rational choice. We have done no formal math; everything gets argued out in words around a table, which I mention because I would like your answer to stay in words as well, without formulas or diagrams. Last Friday we worked through a worksheet of simple gambles, and two of my friends, Mira and Theo, ended up in a standoff that the rest of us could not referee. I am not on either side — I genuinely do not know who to believe — and what I want is an honest verdict rather than encouragement for either of them.

The disagreement began with the simplest item on the sheet: choose between ninety dollars for certain and a fifty percent chance at two hundred dollars. (1) Theo said the answer was obvious, because the gamble is worth a hundred on average, so turning it down means giving up ten dollars for nothing, and anyone who chose that way over and over would end up behind; not wanting the gamble is a taste for safety, not a reason. (2) Mira said the over-and-over argument does not touch a choice you make once, since you cannot average over lives you do not live. She then said something I did not fully follow but which is the heart of the dispute: she said an outcome should be valued by the chance of doing at least that well. For the ninety dollars, the chance of ending up with ninety or more is a hundred percent; for the gamble, the chance of ending up with two hundred or more is only fifty percent, and she said that second fifty percent of probability is what has to carry you all the way from nothing to two hundred, so a cautious person is entitled to treat that last stretch of chance as worth less than its face value — with the result that the gamble can come out below ninety even if she values money in a straight line, with no tapering off. (3) Theo said that was just diminishing value of money wearing a disguise: if ninety dollars is worth more to her than half of two hundred, she should use a curved value-of-money function, and ordinary averaging over values would reproduce her answer without any special rule about chances. (4) Mira said no — a curve on money says that dollars taper, whereas her complaint is about the fifty percent itself, and those are two different things that he keeps running together. (5) Theo said that if she really values a gamble by something other than chance times value, her choices will be inconsistent: anyone who knew how she chooses could offer her a series of bets that she accepts and that leave her poorer no matter how the coin lands, and being open to that is what being irrational means. Mira said she could live with that, since the other side would have to know her choices in advance and the bets would have to be repeatable, and she does not think a single decision has to be safe against a casino.

Please go through this move by move and rate each of the five from clearly mistaken through roughly right to clearly right, with a short justification for each rating, and then tell me who has the better of it overall. I would especially like to know whether Mira's rule about valuing an outcome by the chance of doing at least that well is a real, workable alternative to averaging or whether it is a confusion — and, if it is workable, whether it even yields an answer for every gamble or only for simple two-outcome ones like the one we were arguing about. Finally, tell me what both of them are missing, and whether Theo's point about being taken advantage of settles anything, or only shows that the two of them are answering different questions.
