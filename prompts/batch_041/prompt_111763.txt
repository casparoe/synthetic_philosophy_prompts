OK so I've just got back from the pub after a three-hour arguement with my mate Dan about prioritarianism and I need someone to settle it. Take the role of a philosopher who specialises in distributive justice and actually knows this area properly — and please think carefully rather than reaching for the version everyone trots out. Start by brainstorming how you'd state each of the two positions as precisely as you can, in your own words, before you get to the adjudication.

Here's the fight. I say prioritarianism is a genuinely different animal from egalitarianism. Prioritarianism says a benefit is worth more, morally speaking, the worse off the person who receives it is. Full stop — it never says that a gap between people is bad in itself. Egalitarianism says inequality is bad in itself. The standard reason for preferring the first is the levelling-down objection: if inequality is bad in itself, then knocking the better-off down a peg, without helping anyone at all, makes the world better in one respect — which is obviously mad. Prioritarianism supposedly dodges this because it only cares about how well off people are, not about how they compare.

Dan reckons that's definately a con. His line is that the priority view only ever gets its grip on our intuitions by quietly comparing people. He says that if you take the weighting seriously as a function of a person's own absolute level — how their life is going, no reference to anyone else — then you get a different and much weaker view than the one prioritarians actually deploy when they argue about cases; and that the moment you let the weight depend on how far below other people someone is, you're in the same boat as the egalitarian, just with the deckchairs rearranged.

What I want from you:

1. Lay out both views properly and tell me exactly where they come apart. Give me a concrete case with, say, three people in which they deliver different verdicts, and be explicit about which feature of the case is doing the work.

2. State the levelling-down objection in its strongest form. Then say honestly whether the priority view escapes it — and if it does, whether it escapes it for a good reason or just by changing what it is that's being weighted.

3. Take Dan's point seriously. Is there a coherent variant of prioritarianism on which the weight attached to a person's benefit depends on how their level compares with other people's, rather than on their own absolute level? If there is, does that variant face a levelling-down-style absurdity of its own — and can you build the case that brings it out, bearing in mind that on such a view the weights themselves shift as the distribution shifts, which makes it fiddlier than it first looks? Then say whether the non-comparative variant avoids that problem.

4. Finally, adjudicate. Who's right, me or Dan? And set out the strongest reply available to whoever loses.

Small numbers please, to keep it tractable. And don't just reassure me that prioritarians have a standard answer to levelling down — I know they do. What I want to know is whether the standard answer survives Dan's version of the objection.
