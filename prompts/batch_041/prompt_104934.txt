For the past eight months I have sat on the bylaws committee of the housing cooperative where I live, and in that time I have watched a pattern recur that I have no name for. I would like to know whether it is a recognized phenomenon, and if it is not, I would like it named and bounded.

The pattern runs roughly like this. A standing rule becomes inconvenient. Someone moves to amend it. Someone else points out that the rule governing amendments is itself shielded: changeable only by a two-thirds vote, or only at the annual meeting, or not at all while a dispute is pending. Very well, someone proposes amending that rule first — but the shield was itself adopted under the earlier, unshielded procedure, the minutes from that year are lost, and no one can now say by what authority it binds us. Over the decades the committee has accumulated a sediment of such shields, each one adopted by whatever procedure was then in force. The result is a body that can change almost nothing, though at no point did anyone act improperly and at no point was anything logically impossible. The binding happened one defensible step at a time, and there is no founding moment to point to, because the same body is author and subject at every step.

A second, mirror-image case from the same committee: for a while we had a rule that any rule, including itself, could be amended by simple majority. The question then arose what made the current text the operative one rather than the one before it, and the only answer available seemed to be that we had all agreed to treat it so. That answer felt like cheating, but I could not say why.

I am not asking only for a label. I want to know what the phenomenon actually is. Is it best understood as a problem about self-reference, about authority, about time and commitment, or about levels — and where are its edges? How does it differ from ordinary entrenchment, and from the complaint that the dead bind the living? I have a vague sense that logicians and legal theorists have separately written about rules that govern their own revision, and I would like to know whether my committee's sediment is an instance of that, a special case of it, or something with no home yet. If nothing established fits, coin a term, define it, and tell me which nearby cases fall outside it.

I do not need a reading list or citations; I am trying to get the concept straight, not to build a bibliography.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in the identification you settle on, and single out the point you are least sure of. A hedged answer that marks its own weak spot is worth more to me here than a confident taxonomy.
