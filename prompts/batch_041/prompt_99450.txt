I'm a novelist and I've somehow ended up as the token non-academic on a panel about surrogacy and egg donation next month. My last book had a surrogacy plotline, that's the entire reason I'm there, and I'm definately the odd one out — the rest of the panel are bioethicists, a couple of family lawyers, and one person from a disability-studies department who I suspect is going to be my hardest question.

Here's the argument I want to make, roughly. Everyone fights about bans versus markets — most of Europe criminalises paid surrogacy, most of the US doesn't, though the American "market" is really a patchwork and a few states are nearly as restrictive as the countries across the Atlantic. I think that's the wrong axis. What actually determines how the arrangement goes for the people inside it is who holds the walk-away option. Under a ban the contract is void, so the intended parents have no exit and no enforcement at all — if the surrogate changes her mind they have nothing — while she keeps every exit right. Under an enforceable market contract it flips: the parents can refuse the child if the scan shows something they didn't bargain for, and she's the one who's stuck. Call that exit asymmetry. And there's a second thing I keep noticing: bans don't stop the practice, they strip it of the scaffolding — the legal advice, the insurance, the screening, the escrow — so the weaker party loses the protection along with the prohibition. Call that the enforceability gap. Meanwhile alot of the same countries that criminalise paid surrogacy still run egg donation programmes, and some of them pay donors a fixed, state-set sum, which makes me think the real objection was never to money for reproductive labour but to who ends up as the parent. Egg donation is a one-shot transaction that produces no parental claim and no ongoing relationship; surrogacy is nine months of entanglement plus a status question. If the two get the same moral treatment, something's being smuggled in.

What I want from you: take the role of a bioethicist and comparative family-law person who has read the whole literature and also knows how these panels actually go. Be my corner man. Before you write anything, brainstorm for a minute about what each faction in that room really cares about and what they'll hear when I say the things above. Then give me:

- the eight or so objections I'll actually get, ordered by how likely they are times how much damage they do
- for each, who's likely to say it and the way they'd phrase it — in their vocabulary, not mine
- a two-minute reply in my voice, plain language, I'm a novelist not a philosopher
- a note on where that reply is soft and what I should just concede
- the one question I'm praying nobody asks
- three concessions I should make in my opening thirty seconds so I'm not fighting on ground I don't need

Don't give me a reading list or citations — I can't check anything and it isn't that kind of panel anyway.
