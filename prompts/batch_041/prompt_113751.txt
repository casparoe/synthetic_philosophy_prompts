For the past several evenings a friend of mine — a historian at work on the outbreak of the First World War — and I have been circling what began as a narrow disagreement and has turned into something I now think is a genuine philosophical problem. His view is that once you have established that the assassination at Sarajevo, the alliance commitments, the naval rivalry, the mobilization timetables, and the domestic political pressures on each government were each independently close to sufficient for a general European war, the question of what caused the war has been answered and, in a sense, dissolved: there is no single cause left to find. My view is that this merely relocates the puzzle rather than solving it, since the interesting question is why we fasten on one or two of these conditions and call them the cause while relegating the rest to "background," and whether that selection reflects something about the world or only about our interests and explanatory purposes. We got nowhere, but it seems to me that the productive residue of the argument is a set of open questions about overdetermination and multi-causal explanation in history — and I would rather spend my time on the right one than on whichever one happens to be most fashionable.

I am helping design a semester-long seminar for six or seven graduate students in philosophy and history, so the resource to be allocated is real but small: roughly fourteen weeks and a room of people who can read counterfactual analyses of causation, causal modelling, and the pragmatics of explanation, but who will not have time to become specialists in all of it. What I want from you is a defended agenda — an ordering of the open questions in this area, with the reasoning made explicit rather than a survey of positions. Please treat at least the following as live candidates, and add anything I have missed: whether overdetermination is a genuine feature of historical causation or an artifact of representing events as discrete and our causal judgments as contrastive; whether there is any principled, non-arbitrary account of causal selection in singular historical explanation, or whether selection is irreducibly pragmatic and interest-relative; whether the difference between asking why a war broke out at all and why it broke out when it did is a difference in causal structure or only in the contrast class we happen to hold fixed; whether historians' counterfactual claims are legitimate causal claims or disguised fiction; whether the analytic notion of overdetermination has anything substantial in common with the Marxist tradition's use of the same word, where a social formation is said to be determined by several contradictions at once; and whether probabilistic or degree-of-causation accounts make the problem easier or merely restate it. For each question, tell me how far upstream it is — how many other questions in the area would be settled, or at least reshaped, by an answer — how tractable it is with the tools a seminar of this kind actually has, how neglected it is relative to its importance, and what argument or finding over the next few years would move it up or down the list. Then commit to the two or three you would build the seminar around, and tell me which of my candidates you would deprioritize and why. I am not asking for a reading list or for citations; I want the ranking and the case for it, including the trade-offs you are accepting when you put one question ahead of another.
