You are a philosopher of mind with expertise in the metaphysics of intentionality, the debate over original versus derived content, and the teleosemantic tradition. I want you to work through a problem carefully rather than give a survey. Before you answer, spend some time brainstorming: lay out for yourself what the original/derived distinction is supposed to mark, what work it does in Searle's hands and in Dennett's, and what the strongest existing replies to each are. Then give me a diagnosis.

Background, so we are on the same page. Searle distinguishes original (or intrinsic) intentionality, which he takes to belong to brains and minds, from derived intentionality, which he takes to belong to words, pictures, computers, and other artifacts, and which they have only because minds use or interpret them. Dennett's two-bitser is a vending machine that accepts a slug as a quarter. Dennett asks whether it literally has a false belief about the slug; his answer is that the intentional stance licenses belief-talk, but the content is not intrinsic to the machine. Searle reads the case as showing that the machine's intentionality is derived and observer-relative.

Now here is a twist I have not seen discussed, and I want you to take it seriously. Consider a second-order two-bitser. It is wired to the first one, and it is designed to output a signal whenever the first machine accepts a non-quarter. Call it the auditor. Its output is supposed to mean something like: the vendor is misrepresenting the coin. So the auditor is a machine whose job is to represent another machine's representational error. That structure, a representation of a representation, is precisely the structure of a great many of our own mental states, for example my belief that you falsely believe the meeting is on Tuesday.

Here is the argument that a friend and I worked out, and that now worries me because it seems to prove too much. I would like you to tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. Numbered:

(1) If any state has original intentionality, its content is fixed by facts about the state itself, its intrinsic properties together with the laws of nature, and not by anyone's interpretation or purposes.

(2) A state has derived intentionality only if its content is conferred on it by something that already has original intentionality. Conferral is itself an intentional act.

(3) The auditor's signaling-state has content only if some interpreter or designer confers it; on Searle's view, the same is true of the vendor's accepting-state.

(4) But the designer's conferring act is itself a contentful state, so its content must be fixed somehow.

(5) Either the designer's content is fixed by intrinsic physical facts, or it is fixed by functional and relational facts about the designer.

(6) If it is fixed by intrinsic physical facts, then the vendor's sensor-state also has intrinsic physical facts, and so by (1) it should have original intentionality, which contradicts Searle's reading of the two-bitser.

(7) If it is fixed by functional and relational facts, then the vendor's state also has functional and relational facts of the relevant kind, and so it too should have original intentionality, again contradicting Searle.

(8) So either the vendor and the auditor both have original intentionality, or neither does; and since the auditor's meta-representational structure matches our own, the same reasoning threatens to make our meta-representations derived as well.

(9) Therefore the original/derived distinction, understood as intrinsic versus conferred, either collapses into a distinction without a difference or turns into a piece of biological chauvinism.

I would most like to keep (1), because without it I do not see how to state the intuition that our thoughts are about things in a way that a thermostat's states are not. I am less attached to (2) and (4), and I have a nagging feeling that the trouble is somewhere in the middle steps, but I cannot say which. I do not want to give up (9), but if (9) is right then I do not know what the debate between Searle and Dennett is even about.

Tell me: which premise or step do you reject, why did it look plausible in the first place, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, if you reject (2) or (4), say what content-conferral without a conferrer would look like, whether in Dennett's own terms, in teleosemantic terms, or in some other framework, and whether that story can still distinguish the auditor from a genuine believer. If you reject (5), name the third option and say why it does not simply relocate the problem. If you think the argument is valid and one of the premises must go, tell me which one is least costly to surrender and what the resulting position looks like.
