I edit the puzzle section of a small monthly magazine, and each issue carries one formal puzzle: solvers are given a starting string and a fixed list of legal moves, and are asked to produce a target string, with the assurance that the target can be produced. Twice now I have published a puzzle that a clever reader broke in a way I never intended, and once I came close to publishing one that may have had no solution at all; each time my only tool was patience. Solvers write in every month with arguments that a puzzle is unsolvable, and some of those arguments are wrong in ways I cannot name. I have no training in logic beyond an undergraduate course, I can leave a brute-force search running overnight on my laptop but nothing larger, and whatever I decide has to appear in the next issue, over my name, as a short note that a determined solver can check line by line.

What I want is a decision procedure I can run on any claim of the form "this puzzle cannot be solved": the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure is entitled to stop with a verdict of solvable or unsolvable, and the points where it must hand the decision back to me because what is left is an editorial or value judgment rather than a mathematical one. I am especially interested in the difference between my search having found nothing and there being nothing to find, and in exactly what a computer search can and cannot certify. I suspect that the way out of some of these puzzles is to stop and think about the rules as a whole rather than to keep making moves, but I do not know how to turn that into a checkable argument, and I do not want a procedure that amounts to waiting for inspiration.

Run the procedure on three cases. First, a calibration case whose answer I already know: start from the string MI, where the legal moves are (1) add a U to the end of any string ending in I, (2) take a string of the form M followed by some block x and replace it with Mxx, (3) replace any occurrence of III with U, and (4) delete any occurrence of UU; the target is MU, and MU cannot be reached. I do not want the verdict restated; I want the procedure to yield the argument, written the way I would have to publish it. Second, a puzzle we are arguing about right now: start from AAB, with the moves (1) replace any AA with B, (2) replace any BB with A, (3) replace any single A with BB, (4) replace any single B with AA, and (5) delete any occurrence of AAA; the target is the single letter B. Our submissions editor has a short derivation that ends at the single letter A and is certain that one more move reaches B; I have a feeling that B is out of reach but cannot say why. Tell me which of us is right, what the argument is if there is one, and how much of that argument I can print without ruining the puzzle for solvers who have not solved it yet. Third, the case I actually lose sleep over: a team of three has spent three weeks on a puzzle from our March issue and has sent what looks to me like a serious argument that the puzzle as printed has no solution, and in the meantime I have discovered that the script that generated the answer key used a slightly different version of one of the printed moves. I would like to publish "unsolvable," apologize, and move on to next month. Tell me what I am in a position to publish, what the difference is between a verdict about the puzzle as printed and a verdict about the puzzle as generated, and which part of this I cannot settle from my desk at all.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do and where it will misfire: which steps only look like procedure but are really judgment, where a confident-sounding argument should be treated as a guess, and what I should write to solvers when the honest answer is that I do not know.

Write the procedure as a numbered sequence of steps, each with a note on what to do when the step cannot be completed, and answer in American English.
