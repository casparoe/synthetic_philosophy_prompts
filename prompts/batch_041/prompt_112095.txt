I am a member of a philosophy of probability reading group, and for the past few weeks two other members have been locked in an argument about how to update on conditional information. The puzzle that started it is this: a person is parachuted into a territory that is divided into two regions, Red and Blue, and also into two areas, Second Company and Headquarters, which cut across the Red/Blue division. She has no information about where she has landed, so she assigns equal probability to each of the four combinations: Red and Second Company, Red and Headquarters, Blue and Second Company, and Blue and Headquarters. She then receives a reliable piece of information: if she is in Red, then she is in Second Company. She does not learn whether she is in Red, nor whether she is in Second Company. The question is how she should revise her probabilities. My friend A says that her probability of being in Red should remain exactly one half. Her reasoning is that the information she received is entirely about the relation between being in Red and being in Second Company; it tells her nothing about which region she is in, except in the conditional sense that if she is in Red, she is in Second Company. Since she has no independent information about Second Company, the information should not shift her credence in Red at all. It should only adjust the conditional probability of Second Company given Red to one, and leave the marginal probability of Red where it was. She says this is what it means to update on a conditional rather than on a proposition: you set the conditional probability, not the unconditional one. My friend B says that her probability of being in Red should drop to one third. His reasoning is that the information rules out one of the four initially equally likely possibilities, namely Red and Headquarters. If you simply eliminate that possibility and renormalise the remaining three possibilities equally, you get one third for each, so Red's total probability becomes one third. He says that A's proposal, which keeps Red at one half, requires you to boost the probability of Red and Second Company from one quarter to one half, which is a much bigger change to the distribution as a whole, and there is no justification for favouring that particular adjustment over others. He appeals to a principle of minimal change: you should revise your beliefs as little as possible, and the minimal change is to spread the probability evenly over the remaining possibilities. He also notes that if you keep Red at one half, you must also decide what happens to the conditional probability of Second Company given Blue; A wants to keep that at one half, but B says that is an extra assumption that the information does not force.

A replies that B's minimal-change principle is not neutral: it already assumes that the way to handle the eliminated possibility is to redistribute its probability mass uniformly across the remaining possibilities, and that is a substantive choice. She says that if you instead think of the information as a constraint on conditional probabilities, then the constraint is simply that the probability of Second Company given Red is one. That constraint is compatible with many different posteriors, including one where Red stays at one half. She says that B is smuggling in a material-conditional reading of the information, treating 'if Red then Second Company' as equivalent to 'not Red or Second Company', and then conditionalising on that disjunction. But she says that is precisely the controversial step: conditional information is not the same as the information that a material conditional is true. She says that if she were told the material conditional as a fact about the world, then yes, she would conditionalise and get one third. But she was told something conditional, not something categorical. B responds that he does not see the difference. If she is told, reliably, that if she is in Red then she is in Second Company, then she now knows that the combination Red and Headquarters is not the case. That is a categorical fact about the world: the combination does not obtain. So she has eliminated a possibility. He says that A's insistence that the information is 'conditional' seems to be a way of avoiding the obvious implication that one of the four possibilities is ruled out. He says that if the information is reliable, then the possibility Red and Headquarters is impossible, full stop. And once you know that, you have to update on that fact, and the natural way to do so is to conditionalise on the disjunction of the remaining three possibilities, which gives one third each. He says that A's alternative, keeping Red at one half, would mean that the probability of Red and Second Company becomes one half, which is a much larger increase for that specific possibility than for the other two, and that seems arbitrary. A says that B is conflating two different things: the proposition that the material conditional is true, and the conditional proposition itself. She says that when she is told 'if Red then Second Company', she is not told that the material conditional is true in the sense of being told a proposition that can be true or false independently of where she is. She is told something about the structure of the territory: that the Red region is contained in the Second Company area. That is a fact about the world, yes, but it is a fact about the relation between the regions, not a fact about her location. She says that updating on that relational fact is not the same as updating on a proposition about her location. She says that the constraint is on the conditional probability, and the conditional probability constraint does not determine the marginal probability of Red. She says that if you want to determine the marginal, you need an additional principle, such as maximum entropy or minimal relative entropy, and that principle is not itself given by the information. She says that B is implicitly using maximum entropy, which yields one third, but that principle is controversial and not forced by the logic of conditional information. B says that A's position leads to a kind of immobility: if the constraint does not determine a unique posterior, then any posterior that satisfies it is rational, and that seems too permissive. He says that in ordinary reasoning, if you learn 'if Red then Second Company', you would indeed become less confident that you are in Red, because you have ruled out a way of being in Red. He says that A's intuition that Red should stay at one half is based on a mistaken picture where the information is only about the conditional link and therefore 'screens off' the antecedent. But he says that the information is evidence about the antecedent, because it eliminates a possibility in which the antecedent is true. He says that if you think of it in terms of possible worlds, the worlds where Red and Headquarters are now excluded, and since some of those worlds were Red worlds, the total measure of Red worlds goes down. He says that A's proposal to keep Red at one half requires you to add measure to the Red and Second Company worlds to compensate, which is not justified by the evidence. A says that B's possible-worlds argument assumes that the excluded worlds are removed and the remaining worlds are reweighted equally. But she says that the evidence does not tell her that the remaining worlds are equally likely. It only tells her that the excluded worlds are impossible. She says that the evidence is silent on how to redistribute the probability mass that was on the excluded worlds. She says that one could redistribute it entirely to the Blue worlds, or entirely to the Red and Second Company worlds, or in many other ways, and all would satisfy the conditional constraint. She says that the fact that B finds uniform redistribution natural is a psychological fact about him, not a logical consequence of the evidence. She says that if you want to argue for uniform redistribution, you need a principle like maximum entropy, and that principle is not self-evident. She says that she is not saying Red must stay at one half; she is saying that the information alone does not determine whether it stays at one half or drops to one third. She says that B is presenting a particular update rule as if it were the only rational one, when in fact it is one among several. B says that A's position is too weak. He says that if the information alone does not determine a unique posterior, then we need a principle to choose among the permissible posteriors, and the most defensible principle is to minimise the change in the overall distribution. He says that maximum entropy does exactly that: it finds the distribution closest to the prior that satisfies the constraint. He says that A's alternative, keeping Red at one half, is a larger change from the prior than the one-third solution, because it doubles the probability of Red and Second Company. He says that if you care about not making gratuitous changes, you should prefer the one-third solution. He says that A's insistence that the information is 'conditional' does not get her out of the need for a principle; it just leaves her with an underdetermined problem. A says that B's appeal to minimal change is question-begging, because the measure of change depends on how you represent the possibilities. She says that if you represent the possibilities as four cells, then the one-third solution is a bigger change in some cells and a smaller change in others. She says that if you represent them differently, the minimal-change solution might be different. She says that the whole point of the puzzle is that the constraint that the probability of Second Company given Red is one does not uniquely determine the posterior, and that different plausible updating rules give different answers. She says that she is not claiming to have the final answer; she is claiming that B is too confident that his answer is the only one.

I have been listening to this for weeks and I genuinely cannot tell who has the better of it. I would like you to adjudicate their exchange point by point. Where is A right, where is B right, and what, if anything, are both of them missing? In particular, I would like you to say whether the information 'if Red then Second Company' should change the probability of Red, and if so, why; whether the one-third answer is forced by the evidence or is the result of an additional principle; and whether A is correct that the information is conditional rather than categorical, or whether B is correct that it rules out a possibility and therefore is categorical. Please also say how confident you are in your verdict, and identify the point in your reasoning that you are least sure about. Please write your answer in British English.
