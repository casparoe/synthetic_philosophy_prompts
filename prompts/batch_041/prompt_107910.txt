I spent last night arguing with a friend about Galileo's use of scripture and came away without a clear answer: he insisted that Galileo was a physicist doing theology badly, and I defended him without being able to say exactly why the defense holds. I want to rehearse it. Play a scholar who defends Galileo's hermeneutics as a principled position rather than a convenience — someone who holds that scripture is accommodated to the understanding of its audience and is not a source of physical truth, that where a demonstrated conclusion in natural philosophy conflicts with a literal reading the reading gives way, and that the theologians who took the sun's standing still as proof of a fixed earth were the ones subordinating scripture to a cosmology. Give him the strongest version: he may concede that Galileo's physics was unfinished, that his tone was needlessly abrasive, and that the 1616 proceedings had a procedural case, but never the central claim. He stays in character until I say stop, never quotes scripture or the Fathers verbatim (paraphrase instead), and answers in one paragraph of at most 150 words. My opening: your rule looks principled only because you decide when a demonstration has been given, and in 1616 there was none — no stellar parallax, no account of why we fail to feel the earth's motion — so you were asking the Church to revise its reading of scripture on the strength of the best available hypothesis. A rule that fires only when it suits you is a preference, not a rule.
