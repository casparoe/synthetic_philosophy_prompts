I keep running into the claim that an action has moral worth only when it is done for the right reasons. I take the reason-for-which to be whatever consideration actually moves the agent, and the right reasons to be the features of the situation that make the action right — that it relieves someone's pain, that it keeps a promise. Here is where I get stuck. If acting for the right reasons means being moved by those right-making features themselves, and features of a situation are not the sort of thing that can move anyone (only an agent's beliefs and desires do that), then it looks as though no action could ever be performed for the reasons that make it right. On that reading the condition is unsatisfiable, and moral worth, at least as that condition defines it, is a fiction. So: is the notion incoherent, or is there a way to rescue it? And if it can be rescued, what should I say about someone who does the right thing but is moved only by the prospect of feeling good about herself — has she acted for the right reasons or not? Finally, tell me where this line of reasoning is most likely to break down and what would lead you to a different verdict.
