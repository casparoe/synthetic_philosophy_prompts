I'm putting together a lecture on sortition for a seminar on democratic theory, and I keep running into a case I dont quite know what to do with: the Irish Citizens' Assembly. Here's the recap I give my students, and you can take it as given rather than reconstruct it: in 2016 the Irish government set up a body of 99 citizens picked by lot (plus a chair, a judge), to consider a short list of questions the government handed them, the big one being the constitutional ban on abortion; it met over several weekends, heard testimony from invited experts, voted, and recommended a change to the constitution; the Oireachtas then went off and did its own thing, and the 2018 referendum passed with roughly two thirds of the vote. Before that there had been a 2012-14 Constitutional Convention that mixed two thirds ordinary citizens with one third sitting politicians, and that one fed into the 2015 marriage referendum. Since then the format has been copied everywhere, France's climate convention being the obvious one.

What I want from you is the case analysis, in both directions. Map the Irish thing onto the standard case *for* sortition -- the descriptive-representation argument, the immunity-to-electoral-incentives argument, the deliberative-quality argument, the epistemic-diversity argument -- and tell me which of these the assembly actually instantiates and which it merely appears to. Then tell me where the episode strains the theory. Two things I've been chewing on: first, if the government picks the question, sets the timetable, and can ignore the output, is that a lottocracy or just a very elaborate focus group with better PR? Second, I've been calling the moment the names come out of the drum the 'blind break' -- the point where influence is supposed to stop -- and I want to know whether the Irish case honors it, given that the membership, the expert list, and the press coverage were all being contested in advance. Then run it the other way: what does the case show about the lottocratic proposal itself -- a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case it never anticipated? My suspicion is that it shows sortition works best as a supplement to elections rather than a replacement, which would be bad news for the stronger lottocrat claims, but I'd like you to push on that rather than assume it.

Please be genuinely noncommittal where the evidence is thin instead of picking a side to sound decisive, and finish by telling me what would change your mind -- which fact about the Irish case, or which argument, would flip your verdict on what it shows for or against lottocracy, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
