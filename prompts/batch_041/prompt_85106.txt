Hey, so I've been lurking on LessWrong and the EA forums for years and I finally got around to actually reading Parfit's Reasons and Persons instead of just absorbing the repugnant conclusion second hand through blog posts. I get why its called repugnant, obviously, but I have to say I don't see why it's treated as this giant unsolved crisis. As far as I can tell the whole thing only goes through if your starting point is straight total utilitarianism — just add up everyone's welfare and maximize. But there's an obvious patch: use a critical level instead. You pick some threshold of wellbeing, call it X, and lives below X count as negative, or at least don't get counted as positive, so a gigantic population of lives barely above zero stops looking attractive. It seems like that kills the repugnant conclusion dead while letting you keep all the nice additive aggregation machinery, and I'm genuinely puzzled why population ethicists don't just... do that.

So what am I missing? I'd like a fairly technical walkthrough of what a critical level actually buys you and where it leaves you exposed — my suspicion is there's some second-order problem that only shows up once you start fiddling with the threshold, but I can't see the shape of it. And more broadly I want to know whether the repugnant conclusion is really as tied to totalism as I think, or whether it falls out of much weaker assumptions, because if it's the latter that completely changes how I should think about the whole "just pick a better axiology" move. Basically: is the fix I'm proposing actually a fix, or is it the kind of thing that looks clean until you notice it's smuggled the problem in somewhere else?
