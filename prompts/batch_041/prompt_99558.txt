My dad retired last month after thirty-two years in the foreign service — mostly trade and cultural attaché work, never ambassador, which he liked to say saved him from having to be diplomatic. I'm giving the toast at his dinner. The room is about sixty people: half are his old colleagues from the State Department and a couple of foreign embassys, people who have spent their lives reading the room in other countries, and half are family and neighbors who just know him as the guy who always brings back weird candy and tells long stories about taxi drivers. So it has to land for both, and it has to be a toast, not a lecture. Here's my problem: his whole career ran on national character. He tells stories that start with the Japanese and end with the Brazilians, and he means them affectionately. But I've been reading philosophy on the side and I can't decide whether that's a coherent thing to do or a polite form of prejudice, and I don't want to give a toast that either pretends the issue isn't there or turns his retirement into a seminar.

A friend of mine who studies cultural evolution gave me a distinction she uses, and I want the toast built around it. She says there's national character as a summary statistic and national character as a script. The first is just a compressed, lossy description of a distribution — useful for predicting what might happen at a meeting, revisable when it fails, and always about groups, never about the person in front of you. The second is a claim about what people are really like underneath, and it explains their behavior by an essence. Her point is that ordinary talk slides from the first to the second without noticing, and that most defenses of national character only defend the first while actually using the second. Write me a four-minute toast for the dinner that does the slide in reverse: start with a story that seems to confirm a national-character generalization about one of the countries he served in, then show what the generalization leaves out of the particular person in the story, and land on something he actually taught me about the difference between knowing a people and knowing a person. Keep it warm and funny — he'd hate anything solemn — and please don't use the word stereotype or the phrase not all, because those are the easy moves. It also can't just be a speech about the difference between groups and individuals; it should make me, the speaker, look like I'm the one who had to learn it, maybe even catch myself halfway into a sentence that would have betrayed the second sense. And no citations or reading list, obviously, this is a dinner.
