For some years I have defended a position I call lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, and a colleague has at last produced an argument against it that I cannot answer and whose conclusion I will not accept. Let me state the position, then the argument, and then ask you to adjudicate.

The position. Pains and pleasures lie on a single scale of felt intensity. Below a threshold T they are commensurable: every sub-threshold pain has a finite sub-threshold pleasure-equivalent, and sub-threshold pains and pleasures trade off at that rate. At or above T, suffering is lexically dominant: no quantity of pleasure, however vast, and no quantity of sub-threshold suffering, however vast, outweighs, or even equals, a single episode of supra-threshold suffering. I hold the view because I cannot believe that any heap of mild pleasures — any number of people enjoying ice cream — purchases the goodness of a world containing one child's torture.

The argument.

(1) If supra-threshold suffering is lexically dominant, then any world containing one such suffering is worse than any world containing none, whatever else the two worlds contain.
(2) If one world is worse than another, and both are available to an agent, then it is irrational for that agent to choose the worse.
(3) So a world with a single supra-threshold sufferer and otherwise maximal pleasure is worse than a world of unrelieved bleakness that contains no supra-threshold suffering and no pleasure at all.
(4) So, where the bleak world is the only alternative to the first, one is required to bring about the bleak world — to extinguish every pleasure in existence, or to prevent sentient life from arising at all, in order to avert one episode of supra-threshold suffering.
(5) Any view that requires us to annihilate the world's happiness, or the world, in order to avert a single episode of suffering is unacceptable.
(6) Therefore the position is false.

I want to keep the lexical threshold; I will not accept that suffering is always for sale at some finite price. I also will not accept (5). So either the argument contains a bad step or one of my commitments must go. Identify the step you reject and explain why it looked compelling; then price the repairs — what do I lose by abandoning (1), by abandoning (2), or by abandoning the lexical threshold itself — and tell me which repair is least costly for someone determined to keep both the anti-commodification intuition and the refusal in (5). Open with the step you reject. Maximum 700 words.
