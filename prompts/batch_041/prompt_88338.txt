So this is from a very long car ride with two friends of mine, Dan and Priya, who both did history and neither of whom can let anything go. The topic was how moral revolutions actually end — slavery, dueling, that whole family of cases — and whether it's arguement that does the work, or interests, or honor. I stayed out of it and honestly couldn't tell who was winning. Can you go through their exchange and adjudicate it move by move, not just declare a winner overall? Here's how it went.

Dan went first. He said look at abolition — the thing that actually moved was money and geopolitics. When slave-grown sugar ran into competition, when suppressing the trade paid off for the navy and the empire, when free labor started looking more productive, the moral verdict conveniently followed the ledger. He said the arguments against slavery had been floating around for a century or more and changed nobody's mind until the material conditions turned, so argument is downstream of interest every time.

Priya said the timing kills that. Abolition happened while slavery was still wildly profitable, and the same arguments had been aired for generations without moving anyone. What changed, she said, is that owning people stopped being something a gentleman could be proud of — it flipped from a mark of status to a mark of shame. That's an honor shift, and honor isn't interest, because people will happily lose money to keep their honor.

Dan said that just relabels the problem. Honor doesn't float free; whose honor counts tracks who has power. The rising industrial and evangelical middle classes had honor to spend and the planters didn't, so when the planters' honor stopped being socially load-bearing the norm flipped. Honor is the mechanism, he said, but interest picks the direction.

Priya came back with dueling. No ledger there, no empire, no trade route. Dueling died because it became ridiculous — the campaigners won by making it laughable, and ridicule is an argument, not an interest. So argument does independent work after all; it's the thing that re-codes honor.

Then Dan brought up something I hadn't thought about: the way a bunch of places somewhere in the 1800s stopped hanging people in front of a crowd and started doing it inside the prison walls. He said that one is pure self-image — no economic stake, no deterrence gain, the respectable classes just couldn't stand the crowd anymore, and the deterrence arguments were cooked up afterwards to cover the aesthetic turn. So it's honor again, and honor with nothing underneath it.

Priya said fine, but then the three are tangled and the whole argument is malformed, and she offered a thing she called the honorable exit — her claim being that a moral revolution only sticks when the people who did the old thing can still tell themselves they'd have done the new thing anyway. She said that's why abolition held and dueling never came back, and that it's neither pure interest nor pure argument, it's a fourth thing.

I kept feeling like they were answering different questions, but I couldn't say which ones. So: go through it move by move, rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and say why. Where exactly are they talking past each other, and is Priya's honorable exit a real distinction or just a redescription of one of the other three? And what are they both missing?
