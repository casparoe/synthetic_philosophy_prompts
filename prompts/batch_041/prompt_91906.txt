I'm a retired analytical chemist — thirty-odd years in industry labs, and since I hung it up I've been trying to patch the holes in an education that stopped dead at thermodynamics — and my friend Fran, who taught economic history at a state university for about as long, has dragged me into a running argument about whether the world is getting less violent. I've realized I don't much care about the headline answer anymore; what bugs me is that I can't tell what rule Fran is actually running, and every time I think I've got it she says something that doesn't fit. So here's her track record on the cases I've put to her, as close as I can reconstruct it. The long fall in European homicide rates since the Middle Ages: real, and she'll defend it with her last breath. The two world wars: not a counterexample, because you have to look at the whole century and per capita, and she gets visibly irritated when people wave them around as a gotcha. The Taiping Rebellion's tens of millions dead: she won't touch that number in either direction, says the counts are basically invented. The Bengal famine: violence, flat out, even though nobody shot anybody, because the people running the wartime economy knew exactly what the numbers were doing. Traffic fatalities, which kill more people every year than wars do: not violence, and she says calling them violence isn't a stretch, it's a category error. The big rise in reported rapes and assaults back in the seventies and eighties: not evidence of more violence, because reporting changed. And when I said a colonial massacre of a few hundred villagers was rounding error next to the world wars, she said magnitude has nothing to do with whether something is violence. One more thing, which might be the whole key: she insists that once you fix the definition, whether violence has declined is a plain factual question with a right answer — but that fixing the definition is something we choose for moral reasons, not something we discover.

Now the cases she hasn't ruled on, and I've thought alot about how to describe them so you don't need any background. First: a company sells a chemical for twenty years while sitting on its own internal studies showing it causes a horrible cancer, buries the studies, and keeps selling; by the end tens of thousands of people are dead. Nobody forced anyone to buy it. The buyers were mostly poor, the warnings were suppressed, and from the company's point of view the deaths were a side effect of a profitable product rather than the point of selling it. Second, and this one keeps them seperate in my head in a useful way: a city government has ignored its own building code for a decade, inspectors aren't sent, and when an earthquake comes the apartment blocks that shouldn't have been standing come down and kill forty thousand people. In the second case nobody did anything at all — no product, no profit, just a decade of decisions not to act. I honestly can't guess what Fran does with either one, and I suspect the answer turns on some distinction I haven't even noticed.

What I want, and please write it in American English, and don't bother with references, I'm not writing anything up and I wouldn't check them anyway: (1) your best guess at her verdict on each of the two new cases, with the reasoning; (2) the rule she's most likely running, plus at least one rival rule that fits her entire track record just as well — I half suspect she needs the harm to come from a state, but she won't confirm it and I'd like something better than my guess; (3) what each candidate rule predicts about the two new cases, and exactly where the rules come apart on them; (4) the further case, real or invented, that would actually settle which rule she's running, and why that case and not some other; and (5) whether counting either new case as violence does anything to her trend claim — these harms scale with industry and with population, so does the decline survive, and does it push her toward or away from the per-capita accounting she keeps insisting on? If her record genuinely doesn't determine the new cases, say so, and tell me what principle she'd be signing up for with each choice.
