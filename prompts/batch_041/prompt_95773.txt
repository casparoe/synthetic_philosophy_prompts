I'm a philosophy professor, mid-seminar on conditionals (material conditional, Adams, Lewis, the usual cast), and I'm burried in midterms. I want a second opinion on one answer before I commit to a grade. It was the last question on the exam, out of 25, and it ran like this:

"(a) A hospital monitor gets described in two ways:

(1) If the patient's potassium is above 6, the alarm will sound.
(2) If the patient's potassium had been above 6, the alarm would have sounded.

Explain the indicative/counterfactual distinction as it shows up between these two, and say whether the material conditional analysis of 'if' gets either of them right. (b) State Adams's thesis about indicative conditionals and explain what work it's supposed to do. (c) Counterfactuals have a familiar possible-worlds semantics. Can that semantics be extended to indicatives like (1)? Defend your answer."

The three parts were weighted about evenly. Here's what the student wrote:

"(a) The difference between (1) and (2) is in the attitude the speaker takes toward the antecedent. In (1) the antecedent is left open — the speaker doesn't know the potassium level and is saying what happens if it turns out high. In (2) the antecedent is assumed false: we consider a situation that didn't happen and are told what would have been the case. Roughly, (2) is subjunctive and (1) is indicative, though mood is only a rough guide.

The material conditional says 'if A then C' is true just when A is false or C is true. This is wrong for (1). If it were right, we could infer (1) from the mere fact that the potassium is not above 6, which is absurd (the paradox of material implication), and (1) would come out true whenever the alarm is disconnected and the potassium is normal. The Oswald example makes the same point: we accept 'if Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy, someone else did' but reject 'if Oswald hadn't shot Kennedy, someone else would have', even though on the material reading both are true as soon as the antecedent is false. So the material analysis fails for (1).

For (2) it fails even worse, and the natural repair is the strict conditional: 'if A then C' is true at a world when every accessible A-world is a C-world. This is basically the Lewis semantics for counterfactuals — we look at the worlds most similar to ours in which the antecedent holds and check the consequent there.

(b) Adams's thesis is that the probability of an indicative conditional equals the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent: P(if A, C) = P(C|A). The point is to explain how we accept and reject indicatives — we conditionalize. It also explains why the paradoxes are bad, since they assign high probability to conditionals no one would accept, and it explains the Oswald pair, because the counterfactual is rejected while in the nearest worlds where Oswald didn't shoot no one else does either. On Adams's view this gives the truth conditions of the indicative: the conditional is true to the degree that P(C|A) is high.

(c) I don't think it extends cleanly. Lewis wanted a unified account, but the triviality results show you can't keep both a conditional that behaves like a probability and the thesis that its probability is the conditional probability, unless the conditional is trivial. That's why Adams gave up on truth conditions for indicatives. So the two come apart: counterfactuals get possible-worlds truth conditions, indicatives get a probabilistic semantics."
