I am setting the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on Kant's ethics, and I need one essay question on the universalizability tests. The question must not be answerable by reciting the distinction between the Formula of Universal Law and the Formula of Humanity, nor by running the four stock examples from the Groundwork (the false promise, the suicide, the rusting talents, the refusal to help). The crux I want to test is the internal machinery of the contradiction-in-conception test: what kind of contradiction it is supposed to be, whether the verdict survives re-describing the maxim at a different level of generality, and whether the test does any work that the Formula of Humanity does not already do.

To keep the question concrete and self-contained, build it around the two maxims below, which I have written for the exam and which the students will not have seen:

Maxim A: "Whenever I can secure a modest advantage by breaking a rule that others generally keep, and I judge that my own breach will not by itself undermine the rule, I will break it."

Maxim B: "Whenever I can spare myself effort or discomfort by declining to develop any of my natural talents, I will decline."

The question should require the candidate to do four things. First, run each maxim through the contradiction-in-conception test and state which, if either, fails it, distinguishing as they go between a contradiction in conception (the maxim cannot even be thought as a universal law without collapsing) and a contradiction in will (the maxim can be thought as universal but cannot be willed as such without conflict with ends one necessarily has). Second, exhibit a re-description of Maxim A — broader or narrower — that flips the verdict, and say what, if anything, could license preferring one description of the maxim to the other. Third, say whether the failure of Maxim B, if it is a failure, is a failure of the same kind as any failure of Maxim A. Fourth, reach and defend a verdict on whether the universalizability test can be applied without already presupposing the Formula of Humanity.

Please supply, in this order: the exam question itself, phrased so that it is answerable in fifty minutes; a model answer of roughly seven hundred words that takes a definite line rather than surveying the options; and a marking scheme worth thirty marks, divided into named must-hit points, each with its point value and a one-line note on how candidates characteristically lose those marks. The model answer must paraphrase throughout — no quotations from Kant and no page or section references — and the marking scheme must include at least one point whose presence or absence is what separates an A from a B, stated as the specific move the A answer makes and the B answer substitutes for it. The four stock Groundwork examples may be mentioned in passing but must not carry any of the argument.
