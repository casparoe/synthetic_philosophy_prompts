I'm an MA student putting together an application for a small summer research grant -- eight to ten weeks, about two grand, no lab, just me, a library card and a laptop -- and I've got a half-formed idea I'd like you to turn into an actual proposal. It started as an argument in the pub after my philosophy of psychology seminar. Someone said the cross-cultural intuition studies -- the ones from the early 2000s that reported East Asian and Western participants answering Gettier-style and knowledge-attribution vignettes differently, plus all the later ones that either got the same result or couldn't reproduce it -- either refute the method of cases or don't. What nags at me is that both sides argue as if the survey measures the same thing in every population, namely the participants' concept of knowledge. But the vignettes are written in English and then translated, and the words being translated ('knows', 'intends', 'because') are exactly the words whose meaning is in dispute, so the translator has to settle what they mean before the study can discover anything. And I suspect alot of participants aren't answering the question at all; they're answering 'would this guy get blamed?' or 'what would the committee say?'. So the question I want to propose is: does this literature measure variation in concepts, or variation in how the probe gets read, and can those two even be told apart with the evidence we have? I think the existing positions leave it open because the universalist side treats the odd results as noise or underpowered samples while the variationist side treats them as evidence about concepts, and both assume the instrument is transparent. I also want the proposal to argue that the instrument question has to come first rather than being a boring methodological footnote -- and to say why, if you're a philosopher with eight weeks and no lab, this is still your question and not the psychologists'.

For the method, don't just tell me to 'run a better survey'. I'm thinking something like: distinguish two readings of an intuition report (the report as a datum about a concept vs. the report as an interpretation of an under-specified scenario, where the participant fills the gap with something about blame or institutional response), then work out what would discriminate between them and lay out the predictions. Off the top of my head: if the effect is a translation/reading artifact, within-country variation along the same lines should be about as big as between-country variation; shifting the wording of the probe should move answers more than swapping the participants; and testing bilinguals in each of their languages should manufacture the 'cultural' difference on its own. I'd like the proposal to spell out which of those is actually decisive, what the study design would need to be for each, and what result would embarrass my whole framing. Be honest about failure conditions -- including the possibility that the two readings can't be pried apart in principle, because every design that separates them quietly presupposes which one is right, or that 'variation in how the probe is read' just collapses back into conceptual variation and I've made no distinction at all. And give me a realistic scope for eight weeks: what I can actually deliver (a taxonomy plus a pre-registered design plus maybe a small bilingual pilot I can recruit on campus), and what I should explicitly promise not to do. Don't bother with citations or a literature review, I'll add those myself.

One more thing: at the end, add a short note on how confident you are in the empirical claims you've had to assume to make this work -- especially anything about what those studies found or what's been replicated -- and single out the one point in the proposal you'd bet on least.
