I teach a unit on probability and evidence, and I keep meeting a move — in student papers, and more disconcertingly in published work — for which I have no name. A quantity is unknown: the bias of a coin, the true effect of a treatment, the share of a population with some trait. The reasoner says that since we have no reason to think any value of it more likely than any other, all values are equally likely, and computes. When I point out that the very same ignorance, applied to a different but mathematically equivalent description of that same quantity — the chance of two heads in a row rather than the coin's bias, the effect on a ratio scale rather than an additive one — yields a different answer, the reasoner does not withdraw the move. He or she replies that the first description was the natural one and that the alternative smuggles in an assumption. What interests me is not the inconsistency but the asymmetry in how it is diagnosed: the description the reasoner happened to reach for first is treated as neutral, and its equally arbitrary alternatives as loaded. It is as though the absence of a reason to prefer a description were being taken as a reason to prefer the description already in hand.

Is there an established name for this? I know the general territory — the principle of indifference, and the old puzzles about a chord drawn at random in a circle — but those are ordinarily presented as puzzles about ignorance, whereas what I am after is the specific habit of taking one's own formulation for the unformulated case. If the nearest concepts do not capture it, I want to know precisely where each falls short; and if nothing fits, a coined term, with a definition and boundary cases — in particular, how it differs from a mere change of variables, and whether it is a special case of something more general or a distinct failure in its own right.
