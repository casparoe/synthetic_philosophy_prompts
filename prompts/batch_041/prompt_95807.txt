My reading group did quantum immortality last week and it went nowhere, so I'm outsourcing it. The idea, in case you haven't had it thrown at you: if the many-worlds picture is right, then every time there's a branch where you die there's also a branch where the gun jams or the scan comes back clean, and since the only branch you'll ever be around to notice is the one where you're still here, your own death is something you can never experience, even though everyone at your funeral watched it happen. Someone asked whether that means you should be perfectly happy to play Russian roulette with a million-to-one revolver, and we spun out from there.

What I actually want is a narrower question answered: what would William James have done with this? He's the one I keep coming back to, because he refused to let a question be closed off with a shrug, while also insisting that believing something has to cash out in how you live. Its been nagging at me since, so, three things, and I want a call on each:

1. Would quantum immortality count for James as a legitimate thing to believe, by the tests he set up for beliefs you can't prove, live/forced/momentous or whatever his three actually were, or does it flunk one of them?
2. Once you've got it, does it change anything about how a person should act around lethal risk, or does the pragmatic maxim just say the difference is nil, since the you who dies never finds out and the you who lives can't report back?
3. The one I actually care about: if the survivor is always in a branch where nobody he's lost is ever coming back, is that the survival James wanted, or a bait-and-switch on immortality?

Don't give me a tour of the possible readings. Pick a line on each and defend it, tell me where James would have been torn and which way he'd have landed anyway, and flag where you're extrapolating past anything he actually argued. If you think the question is confused in a way that makes the three parts unanswerable, commit to that instead and say why.
