I chair the comms standards panel at my state health dept. and we have a mess on our hands. Our bowel screening programme launches next month and the vender has built five versions of the invitation letter. The panel has to sign off Thursday, which means I have to circulate something in writing before then.

Our charter says the department informs and enables people and does not manipulate them. That's the whole rule, that's it, no definitions. The five versions:

- The plain letter. Facts, dates, how to book.
- The regret letter: asks you to picture yourself in five years getting the diagnosis you could have caught early. Every claim in it is true. It works by making people act so they don't have to feel that.
- The deaths letter: gives the number of people in your borough who died of it last year. Also true. The vendor wants a ten-year window rather than one year because the number comes out bigger, and the borough boundaries we'd use were picked to make it sound worst.
- The silence letter: says nothing about false positives or overdiagnosis, because the vendor tested it and mentioning them cuts uptake by about a third.
- Not a letter at all: we pre-book the appointment and you have to cancel it.

Two other things you need to deal with. The vendor already ran a 20,000-person pilot of versions 2 and 4 without ethics review, on the grounds that it was only comms, not research, and the panel has to decide whether that was alright. And one of my members keeps saying the only thing that matters is whether more people get screened, and that a rule which blocks effective letters is itself unethical because people die of this. I think he's wrong but I can't articulate why in a way that survives contact with him.

Please draft the decision memo I'll circulate. Open with the memo itself, no note to me, no restating my question, and make the first line the recommendation. Go version by version and say approve, reject, or approve with conditions, and why. Then give us a standing rule for future campaigns with criteria a junior staffer could actually apply at their desk — I don't want a literature review, I want something we can use, and it has to say whether telling people in the letter what technique we're using makes any difference. Take my colleague's objection seriously enough that he'll feel answered. End with what happens to the launch date.
