I teach a 90-minute upper-level seminar on the cognitive science of religion to about 14 undergraduates. I want a runnable in-class activity that makes them feel why religious beliefs and practices can be treated as natural phenomena—specifically, how costly, hard-to-fake displays and ordinary memory biases interact to make some beliefs and practices spread and stabilize. The activity should not announce the theory first: participants should play a transmission-and-commitment game in which they pass on narratives under time pressure, choose whether to adopt a model’s belief after seeing how much it costs that model to display it, and then discover which beliefs survived and why. Include roles, rules, materials, a scoring or payoff scheme that does not need real money, a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the naturalistic mechanisms—at minimum, credibility-enhancing displays (costly, hard-to-fake behaviors that make a model’s professed beliefs more credible) and minimally counterintuitive content (concepts that violate a small number of intuitive expectations and are thereby more memorable)—plus a frank account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply. Finally, take a definite position on whether this kind of activity risks making the naturalistic explanation seem like a debunking explanation, and say how you would handle that risk in the debrief.
