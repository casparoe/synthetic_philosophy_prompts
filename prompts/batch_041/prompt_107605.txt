I'm on the board of trustees at a small liberal arts college -- 150 years old, about 1,400 students, endowment you'd laugh at -- and I have to write a memo before our October 14 meeting, where we vote on a bylaw amendment. I'd like you to draft it for me.

Here's the situation. Our bylaws provide that no college real estate may be sold, leased, or mortgaged without a two-thirds vote of the whole board taken at two consecutive regular meetings, with at least 90 days between them. Nobody knows why this exists. Our oldest surviving copy of the bylaws is a photocopy of a photocopy from 1904, and the article that presumably explained the article is just gone. There's also a clause giving the Society of the Founders' Descendants the right to nominate one trustee; the Society hasn't met since 1971, and our lawyer won't say whether the double-vote rule is somehow load-bearing for that clause or whether the Society's right has lapsed through disuse (he used the word desuetude and then changed the subject).

Why it's live: we're swapping a parcel with the town for a parking lot we actually need, and the town's offer expires December 1. The swap has already sat in limbo for 14 months because of the two-meeting rule. The amendment on the table would replace it with a single two-thirds vote, taken after 30 days' public notice of the terms. Under our current procedure, the first of the two votes has to be taken in open session with the terms attached to the posted agenda, which is the only moment our land dealings become public before they're done; under the amendment, notice would be posted by the clerk and the vote itself could happen in closed session.

I need this to land with two undecided trustees. One is a commercial real estate lawyer who says that if nobody has found a reason in 120 years, that's because there isn't one, and that 'Chesterton's fence' is just loss aversion wearing a tweed jacket. The other is an alumna who says her grandmother told her the rule is the only reason we still own the meadow. Both keep quoting Burke at us -- 'A state without the means of some change is without the means of its conservation' -- and I think at least one of them is misusing it.

Also, a confession: I've always understood Chesterton to mean you shouldn't get rid of a fence unless you can prove it was pointless. I'm told that's wrong, but I don't know exactly how.

Give me a page or so that recommends a concrete course of action with a timetable that respects the December 1 deadline, and that gives the strongest case for whichever side you come out against a fair hearing rather than a strawman. Tell me what 'knowing the reason for the rule' would even amount to here -- a purpose somebody wrote down, or a function the rule happens to be performing -- and if you think we should keep the rule, say what would have to turn out to be true for you to change your mind, and how we could find that out in six weeks.
