I chair the board of a small housing cooperative — nine members, monthly meetings, nobody with any mediation training — and we keep deadlocking on the same kind of quarrel. Someone wants the roof replaced this year; someone else says we cannot afford it and that the leaks are being exaggerated. One member's composting is either a shared resource or a nuisance. A mural on the bike shed is either community art or a defacement. I have been reading about the Jain doctrine of many-sidedness on my own, and I think it is what we actually need, but every version I have found either collapses into everyone's opinion being equally valid or turns into a hedge-everything formality that settles nothing.

Please write me a procedure I can actually run at a meeting — a checklist or a flowchart in words, in the forty minutes we can spare per dispute — that stays faithful to the machinery rather than the slogan. I want it to use, and not water down, the difference between the analysis into partial standpoints, each of which gets hold of one side of a thing, and the analysis of assertion, in which a claim is put forward as holding only in some respect, where that respect belongs to the claim itself and is not a measure of the speaker's confidence, and the sevenfold scheme of qualified predications, if any of that can be made usable without turning the meeting into a logic seminar. What has to happen in order, and what counts as a sufficient answer at each step? Where does the procedure stop and say that this is really a factual dispute with a right answer? Where does it stop and say that the question is one of values and has to go to a vote or to a person? And where does it not help at all?

Then run it on two of the disputes above, the roof assessment and the composting complaint, and include a small set of test sentences the chair can put to a contested claim to check whether the qualified version is both true and worth saying. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire: a member acting in bad faith, a member who is simply wrong about a fact, a majority that uses many-sidedness to avoid hearing the one person it is inconveniencing. Tell me what to do with the objection that the procedure is itself just one more standpoint. And tell me where it will give an answer I will not like, including the possibility that it rules against the position I hold. Please write in American English, and do not send me to any books — I am not going to read them.
