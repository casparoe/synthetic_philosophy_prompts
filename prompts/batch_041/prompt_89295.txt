**Can "Avoid Side Effects" Be Defined Without a Full Specification of Value?**

It is common in discussions of safe agency to treat the avoidance of side effects as one of the few safety desiderata that does not require us to settle any contested questions about value. A side effect, on this view, is simply a change to the world that the agent was not asked to bring about, and the injunction to minimise such changes is supposed to be neutral between competing accounts of the good. I want to argue that this neutrality is illusory. To identify a side effect you must compare what happened with what would have happened otherwise, and the "otherwise" is not given by the world. It is chosen. Since the choice of counterfactual determines which changes are counted, and since different counterfactuals encode different views about what matters, "avoid side effects" cannot be made precise without a specification of value.

Consider three baselines one might adopt:

1. The null baseline: the world as it would have unfolded had the agent never been built.
2. The continuation baseline: the world as it would unfold if the agent existed but took no action at this step.
3. The principal's baseline: the world as it would unfold if the principal's own existing plans were carried out without the agent's intervention.

These are not equivalent, and the differences are not academic. Suppose the agent is asked to fetch a file and, in doing so, occupies a doorway for two seconds. Under (2) the two-second occupation is the entirety of the agent's footprint. Under (3), if the principal would otherwise have walked through that doorway at that moment and caught a train, the agent's footprint includes a missed train and everything downstream of it. Under (1) the footprint includes the file's having been fetched at all — the very thing we asked for. So the same physical event is a null effect, a trivial effect, or a catastrophe depending on a choice we have left implicit.

One might reply that this shows only that impact measures need a baseline, not that they need a specification of value — any baseline will do, provided it is fixed in advance. But fixing a baseline is itself a substantive decision, because it distributes weight between the agent's effects and the world's own momentum. Adopting (1) makes the agent responsible for everything it touches; adopting (2) makes it responsible for very little and quietly absolves it of consequences that flow from what it did do; adopting (3) privileges the principal's plans over everyone else's, which is a claim about whose projects matter.

The most promising defence of neutrality appeals to instrumental convergence: for almost any objective, an agent does better if it preserves the resources, options and general shape of the world, so a low-impact penalty is a good proxy for almost any value specification. But "almost any" is where the value specification hides. The penalty must be weighted, and the weights determine how much option-preservation is traded against the agent's assigned task; different values imply different exchange rates. Moreover, option-preservation is not itself value-free: it favours agents whose values are already well served by the status quo, since the status quo is the baseline from which options are counted.

I therefore propose a term for the move I have been describing: *baseline laundering* — the presentation of a value-laden choice of counterfactual as a neutral methodological default. The term is meant to name a specific error, not to impugn the motives of anyone who commits it.
