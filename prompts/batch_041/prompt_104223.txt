I am hosting a two-day workshop on the value of information, jointly convened by our philosophy and statistics departments, and I have to give the opening remarks — seven minutes, meant to set the tone rather than to welcome people. The audience is genuinely mixed. Perhaps a third of the room are decision theorists and statisticians who know the formal result cold; the rest are practitioners — a hospital risk officer, two people from a public-health agency, an actuary, a handful of graduate students from outside philosophy — who have never seen a proof of it and who have come because the topic is fashionable. I want remarks that land for both halves: rigorous enough that the specialists do not hear me fudging, concrete enough that everyone else follows.

The spine of the talk is the result usually called Good's theorem. Stated in the terms I intend to use: a Bayesian decision-maker who faces a fixed decision problem, and whose utility is defined over actions and states of the world, can never be made worse off in expectation by acquiring costless information, because she can always ignore what she learns and act exactly as she would have acted otherwise. I want the talk to make that argument vivid for the practitioners, and then to do the harder thing: to show where it stops holding, and to make clear that the "she can always ignore it" step is what renders the result almost automatic once the assumptions are fixed — which is also why the real work lies in the assumptions rather than in the theorem.

Please write the opening remarks, in the first person, as spoken, at roughly the length of a seven-minute address. Build them around a concrete case rather than around the formalism. I have been using an invented one and want you to use it: a hospital board that voted against deploying a predictive deterioration score for inpatients, on the stated ground that once it knew which patients were deteriorating it would be obliged to act, and it would rather not be obliged. I want the room to feel the pull of that position before I take it apart.

Three things I need the talk to get right, because this is where the result is most often mangled. First, the theorem is about expected value under assumptions, not about the actual value of knowing; if knowing makes one miserable, or if it changes what one is permitted or obliged to do, or if the mere fact of having sought it changes other people's behaviour, then the assumptions have moved and the theorem may simply not apply. Say what happens in each of these cases, and say plainly which is a clean failure of an assumption and which is a contested extension of it. Second, keep the claims exactly as strong as the result supports and no stronger; the specialists will be listening for an overstatement, and I would rather understate it. Third, spend the final minute turning to what the workshop is actually about — the cases in which the theorem's assumptions are the ones we care about, rather than the ones that make the theorem go.

One request about how you write it: be explicit, within the text of the talk itself, about which claims are settled among people who work on this and which are genuinely contested, and do not let a contested reading pass as the consensus. I would rather the practitioners leave knowing where the disagreement lies than leave with a tidy slogan.
