I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've somehow become the discussion leader for a small reading group that has spent the last few months on the philosophy of time. We started with a popular book on entropy and the arrow of time, then spent two sessions on why the microscopic laws look time-symmetric while almost everything else in the world does not, and next month we're taking up Kurt Vonnegut's Slaughterhouse-Five, which I last read in high school and remember mostly for the Dresden sections and the short refrain that trails every death in the book. I'd like you to give us a serious philosophical reading of the novel through the problem of time's direction — the thermodynamic arrow and entropy, the past hypothesis that explains why entropy was ever low in the first place, the psychological arrow of memory, and the asymmetry of causation — and I'd like it to be a reading of the book rather than a physics lecture with a novel bolted on. The obvious hook is the Tralfamadorian doctrine that all moments coexist and are equally real, which Billy's involuntary travels through his own life seem to illustrate: the Tralfamadorian books are read all at once rather than one page at a time, and the aliens refuse to mourn the dead because they insist every moment is still there. Is the novel endorsing a block universe, or borrowing one as a consolation and an evasion? If every moment is equally real, what becomes of the arrow — is it merely the statistical fact that entropy increases as you move away from a special low-entropy past, a fact about our perspective rather than about time itself, or does Vonnegut think the direction of time is simply an illusion? And I want you to press on something I can't sort out: Billy's experience is not symmetric. He remembers where he has been and not where he is going; his knowledge runs one way; and that one-wayness is precisely what an entropy-based account of the arrow is supposed to explain rather than assume. Does the novel, despite its metaphysics, quietly vindicate the asymmetry it seems to deny?

Then the honest-critic part, which is the part I actually care about: a block universe does not by itself entail fatalism, and the claim that time is a dimension like space is not the same claim as the claim that the arrow of time is unreal, so tell us where the novel trades on a confusion between the two and where it is smarter than that. Point us to the scenes we should argue about — the zoo on Tralfamadore with Montana Wildhack, Billy's capture and the slaughterhouse in Dresden, Edgar Derby's execution over a teapot, the narrator's own framing of the book and his return to Dresden, Billy's calm foreknowledge of the manner and moment of his own death — and say for each what it shows, what it muddles, and whether the philosophy is doing any real work or is just decoration around a war memoir.

Finish by telling us what the novel contributes to the philosophical debate that a paper on the arrow of time cannot: whether a timeless view of one's own life is a genuine consolation, a way of not grieving, or a metaphysical commitment that a person would actually have to be able to live with if they believed it — and whether Vonnegut, who lived through what Billy lived through, is entitled to the equanimity his aliens preach.
