I serve on the ethics review board of an international research consortium, and the same argument returns at every meeting. One of our senior members opposes treating a single standard of informed consent and benefit-sharing as binding on all partner sites, and he defends this with an analogy he has made his own: morality is like language. Every human community, he says, has a complete and fully functional system; a French speaker who called Japanese a false or broken language would be making a category mistake; no speech community's grammar is more correct than another's; and just as we would not impose our grammar on another community, we should not impose our moral grammar on theirs. He concludes that our standards have no claim to correctness outside our own community and that enforcing them is moral imperialism. When someone objects that some moral demands — against needless cruelty, say — look universal, he adds a second point from the psychology literature: the universals people cite were themselves extracted from studies of Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic populations, so the supposedly universal is just our own parochial grammar dressed up.

I want a serious audit of this, not a verdict on relativism in the abstract. Start with the correspondence: what plays the role of the grammar, the speakers, native-speaker competence, the untranslatable word, the loanword, and the child who acquires the language without instruction. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and separate the breaks that matter to his argument from those that do not. I am particularly interested in whether the linguistic parallel is being asked to settle something linguistics itself does not settle — the difference between surface variation and a shared underlying capacity — and whether the claim that no language is false has any counterpart at all for a system that issues demands rather than describes. Finally, say plainly which parts of his conclusion, the relativism and the hands-off policy, survive the audit, whether the WEIRD point helps him or cuts against him, and whether a better analogy is available.
