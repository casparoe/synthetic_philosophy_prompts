Two people on my team got into it after our retro and I've been turning it over ever since. Some background: we're still on a homegrown deploy tool that basically everyone agrees is worse than the off-the-shelf one, and the question of why we're stuck on it turned into a way bigger argument than the retro deserved.

Sam's take: it's path dependence. Someone picked it back in like 2014 for a reason nobody remembers, and then the returns to using it went up for everyone -- we're all trained on it, the CI hooks assume it, half our scripts assume it, new hires learn it because everyone else uses it. So a worse option got locked in, and its not because it was better, it's because we were there first and leaving got expensive. He says this is a general pattern, not a one-off: when returns increase like that you get more than one stable resting place, so which one you end up sitting in gets decided by history rather than by quality.

Priya's take: that's a just-so story you can tell about any outcome after the fact. "Worse" is being judged by what we need today, not by what anyone could have seen in 2014; the alternative would've had its own problems we're conveniently not counting; and if it was genuinely better it should've been able to win on the merits eventually, because people do switch when the gap is real and painful. When Sam said switching isn't cheap, she said fine, but then "increasing returns" is doing all the work, and he still owes her three separate things: that the losing option was better, that the gap was big enough to matter, and that nobody could have measured it at the time. Sam said you can't run the counterfactual -- there's no version of us that took the other branch sitting around for you to compare against -- and she said that's exactly the problem, a claim you can't test isn't an explanation, it's a story that fits.

It went a few more rounds. She said if the mechanism is real it should predict which kinds of things get stuck and which don't; he said it does, look at how often the technically worse format wins, keyboards, video formats, whatever; she said half of those stories fall apart when someone actually checks the history, and he said the fact that we can't check the one we care about is the whole point, not a flaw in the argument.

I honestly couldn't referee it and I don't think either of them was being dumb. So: can you rate the moves one at a time -- which specific claims are right, which are overstated, where each of them is arguing past the other, and what both of them are leaving out? I also have a suspision they're really talking about four different things and keep sliding between them: (a) could it have gone the other way, (b) would we be better off if it had, (c) how stuck are we now, and (d) whether "it could've gone otherwise" is even the same claim as "we got the worse one" -- those seem like different claims to me but they use them interchangeably. Are they actually independent? Does Sam's argument get him all of them or only the first? And Priya said something at the end about how even if we'd picked the other tool we might have ended up here anyway by some other route, which I didn't follow at all -- is that a real point or is she just muddying it? Also, a more basic version of the same question: is "it went this way because of history" an actual explanation, or just a fancy way of saying we don't know why it went this way?
