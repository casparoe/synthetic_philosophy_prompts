I teach a 90-minute session in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on distributive justice. About eighteen students, several of whom arrive with settled views on affirmative action and will defend them at volume. I want an activity that makes the group feel a difficulty in group-conscious selection that classroom debate usually passes over, and that they should run into through the rules rather than hear named.

The difficulty has three parts, and I want the mechanics of the activity to produce all three. First, a representation target can be satisfied at the point of selection while leaving untouched the structure that generated the candidate pool — the group should find that it can hit the target without the pool having changed, and should notice how cheap that felt. Second, moving the constraint upstream, to the pool or the qualification stage, forces a choice about whom to benefit at a stage where no one is a candidate yet, which in turn forces the drawing of a boundary around the beneficiary group and a decision about which of its members count — including whether a comparatively advantaged subset of the beneficiary group ought to be excluded. Third, when the eligible pool is thin, satisfying the target concentrates the benefit in a few hands rather than spreading it, and the group should discover that this outcome is consistent with the letter of the mandate.

I do not want a pro/con debate, a lecture on the history of any national policy, or a bibliography. Norway's boardroom quota, India's reserved seats, and US university admissions can sit in the background as familiar reference points, but the activity must not depend on the details, dates, or case law of any of them.

Design the activity. Specify: the rules and any materials handed to participants; the roles and how they are assigned; a timeline that fits the 90 minutes with a hard stop reserved for the debrief; a scoring or payoff scheme that produces genuine incentives without money; and the sequence of rounds or constraints that yields the three discoveries above without my naming them. Include a compressed variant that runs in 45 minutes if I lose half the session.

Then the debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto the selection-versus-pool distinction, the boundary problem, and the concentration effect; tell me which words I should avoid so that the participants supply the formulations themselves; and state what resisting each of the three discoveries would cost a participant.

Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, given this group and this topic, and how to handle each failure.

Finally, be candid about what the activity cannot reproduce. A game of this kind will flatten at least one feature of the real policy landscape — I suspect it will make the trade-offs too clean, or make the beneficiary group look homogeneous. Name the feature, and tell me what I must supply in the debrief that the activity itself will not deliver.
