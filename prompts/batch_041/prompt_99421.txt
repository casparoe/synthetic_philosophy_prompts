I'm putting together the final for a 400-level seminar on Chinese and comparative ethics, and I need one essay question on Mencius vs. Xunzi on human nature. The problem is that every version of this question I've seen can be answered by writing "Mencius says we're naturally good, Xunzi says we're naturally bad, and it comes down to whether you think cultivation brings out or beats down what's already there." I want something that a student who has actually read both texts can answer and a student who has only memorized the labels cannot. So please build the question around the harder issue: whether the two of them are even disagreeing about the same thing, given that they don't seem to be using xing to mean the same thing, and given that they agree that people need a lot of cultivation to become good. The question should force the student to take a side on whether the disagreement is verbal or real, and to say what would have to be true for their side to be wrong. A student should not be able to answer it by quoting the child-at-the-well passage at me, and the question itself shouldn't lean on any particular scholar's reading, since I don't want them cribbing a name instead of doing the argument (the exam is closed-book and they have no internet, so anything that requires citing secondary lit is out).

Deliverables, in this order:

1. The essay question itself, phrased so it's clear what the student has to do in 60 minutes. It should have at least two parts, and the second part should make them commit to a verdict rather than survey both sides.

2. A model answer, about 600 words, in the voice of a strong student. I want it to actually do the work: get the two senses of xing apart, say what each thinker means by "good" and "bad" and why the words aren't being applied to the same item, and then say whether any substantive disagreement survives once the ambiguity is cleared up. If it says something survives, it has to name what it is and why it isn't just a difference in definitions. If it says nothing survives, it has to say what Mencius and Xunzi would each have to give up for that to be true. No hedging both ways at the end -- pick.

3. A marking scheme: five must-hit points, 4 marks each, 20 total, and for each point a one-line note on how students typically blow it. I want the scheme to draw a sharp line between the B and the A: the B answer clears up the ambiguity in xing and stops there, the A answer clears it up and then says what the argument is actually about afterwards. Also include a short list of the specific moves that should cost marks, including the one where a student says "they're just talking past each other" and treats that as the end of the discussion instead of the start of it, and the one where a student quietly imports a modern nativism/empiricism debate and pretends Mencius and Xunzi are running it.

One more thing: the model answer has to be self-contained -- no citations, no "as X argues." Everything has to be argued from the positions themselves. And keep the whole thing readable; I don't want it to turn into a literature review of the xing debates.
