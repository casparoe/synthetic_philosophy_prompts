OK, I'm putting together a grad seminar unit on precommitment and I need you to build me a case to order, not find one. Take the role of someone who works on practical rationality and the philosophy of law — I want the reasoning of a careful philosopher here, not a survey of the literature. Before you write anything, brainstorm three or four candidate cases to yourself, throw out the ones that are just the Sirens story with new names, and build the best one out. Think it through properly before you commit.

Here's the spec:

- One agent, ordinary stakes. No addiction, no crime, no life-or-death. A career move, a savings decision, a relationship, that kind of thing. Something a reader could plausibly face in the next year.
- The agent deliberately puts a constraint on their own future choice, and the constraint has to be ex ante rational: by the agent's own lights, installing it really does make their life go better on balance. Not a mistake, not a moment of weakness.
- Here's the part I actually care about. I don't want the usual escape hatch where we say the later self is confused, or weak, or that the earlier self "really" knows what's good for them. I want the later self to have a stable, considered, well-informed preference that simply differs from the earlier one. So the case can't be rescued by appeal to deeper or truer preferences.
- And the twist: the constraint is held by somebody else. A partner, an employer, a board — someone who can enforce it, at the agent's own request, in a way the agent can't unilaterally undo. So it's the agent's own agency running through another person's power. Keep it realistic, and keep the irreversability limited — I don't want a fairy-tale magic device.

Once you've got it: walk me through what three accounts say about whether honoring the constraint is required of the later self. (a) the planning view, where the earlier self's intention has authority over the later one; (b) the view that treats a person as a succession of temporally distinct selves with no special authority in the earlier one; (c) an autonomy view where forcing someone to get what they want can wrong them even when it helps them. For each, give the verdict and the reason, and flag where the reason is thin.

Then tell me honestly which single feature of the case is doing the work — the irreversability, the other-imposition, or the stable preference change. Change that one feature and tell me which verdicts flip and which don't. Also, how would a defender of whichever account comes out worst go after the case? Where would they say I've rigged it?

Last thing: does the feature you identified also carry over to the constitutional version — entrenching a rule so that a future majority can't undo it — or does the analogy seperate, and why? No reading list, no citations, I just want the argument. Keep it tight.
