Consider the following lottery, a variant of the Luring Lottery that Douglas Hofstadter introduced in his June 1983 Scientific American column on superrationality and later reprinted in Metamagical Themas. The rules are these. There are M participants, where M is an integer drawn uniformly at random from 2 through 1000 inclusive and is not revealed to anyone. Each participant simultaneously sends in any nonnegative integer number of postcards; entries are free and there is no communication, no side payment, and no repeat play. Let T be the total number of postcards sent by all participants. One postcard is then drawn uniformly at random from the T postcards, and its sender receives a prize of 1,000,000 divided by T dollars. Each participant seeks to maximize their own expected dollar payoff, everyone knows the rules, and the rules are common knowledge.

I would like you to rate the following ten candidate policies for how a single participant should choose its number of postcards. By a policy I mean a complete rule that maps the participant's information — the rules above and nothing else — to a choice of a nonnegative integer, possibly by randomization.

1. The symmetric fixed point: choose the integer k that maximizes a participant's expected payoff conditional on every participant choosing k.
2. The lure: choose the integer k that maximizes expected payoff conditional on every other participant choosing 1.
3. Kantian universalizability: choose the k that you could will that every participant choose.
4. Maximin: choose the k that maximizes the worst-case payoff, taken over all possible values of M and all possible choices by the other participants.
5. The mixed symmetric policy equilibrium: choose a probability distribution over the nonnegative integers such that no participant can improve their expected payoff by unilaterally switching to a different distribution, given that every other participant uses the same distribution.
6. Utilitarian total-surplus maximization: choose the k that maximizes the sum of all participants' expected payoffs, assuming every participant chooses the same k.
7. Causal decision theory: choose the k that maximizes expected payoff given your best estimate of the total sent by the other participants, treating your own choice as evidentially independent of theirs.
8. Evidential decision theory: choose the k that maximizes expected payoff conditional on your choosing k, allowing for whatever correlation your choice bears to the choices of the others.
9. Refusal: send zero postcards.
10. Level-k iteration: begin from the assumption that the others send one postcard each, best-respond to that, best-respond again to the result, and iterate a fixed number of times.

Deliverable. Sort the ten policies into tiers (S, A, B, C, D, F) according to how defensible each is as the correct answer for a participant who accepts the superrational idea that identical reasoners facing identical problems should arrive at identical choices. Give each policy a score from 0 to 10 on that dimension and one sentence of justification. Then state explicitly: (i) which of the ten policies coincide in this variant and which diverge, with the coincidences demonstrated rather than asserted; (ii) which policy you would actually follow if you had to submit a number, what number or distribution over numbers it recommends, and its expected payoff expressed as a formula or a numerical approximation; and (iii) which single additional fact about the setup would most change the top of your ranking, and why. Treat a policy's being well-defined under the uncertainty about M as a virtue; a policy that cannot be applied without further stipulation should be marked as such and placed accordingly. You may give the recommended number as an exact expression, an approximation, or a range.
