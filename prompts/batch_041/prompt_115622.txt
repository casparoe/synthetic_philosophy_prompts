Four of us — friends since university, unequal incomes, one joint mortgage — are drafting the agreement that will govern the house we're buying together, and after three weekends of argument the disagreement has stopped being about money and become about bindingness. One of us wants the thing deliberately hard to undo: the profit-split formula fixed now and not revisitable for five years; the buyout price locked to today's valuation rather than to whatever the house is worth when someone leaves; any change to the core terms requiring unanimity rather than a majority; and no exit at all without six months' notice. Her case is that we are currently calm, clear-eyed and thinking in decades, whereas any future version of us will be acting under whatever pressure happens to be acute that month, and that the present selves who sign are entitled to tie the hands of the future selves who will be tempted. Two of us are uneasy. One wants a sunset clause so that every term lapses after seven years and has to be re-ratified by whoever is then living in the house; the other has pointed out that one of us expects children, and that a house governed by a five-year-old agreement will eventually bind people who were not at the table when it was signed and never agreed to anything.

What I'd like from you is not a summary of the literature but the most important implications of the philosophical work on precommitment — Ulysses contracts, constitutional entrenchment, and the arguments about whether one self, or one generation, can legitimately bind another — for the specific clauses we are fighting over. Assume we are all moderately well read in the popular treatments of this material and that none of us is a lawyer. I want to know which features of a self-binding arrangement actually do the binding (the enforcement mechanism, the amendment rule, the exit price, the time horizon, the number of people bound) and which are decorative; where the analogy between one person binding her future self and a group binding its future members breaks down badly enough to change a clause; whether a sunset is a genuine answer to the problem of the dead governing the living or merely a slower version of it; and what the strongest case is that what my friend wants is not precommitment at all but simply a way of winning an argument now, while the mood is on her side.

If you think some clause we haven't considered — an emergency brake, say, letting any two of us suspend the whole agreement for a month — would matter more than any of the ones we are actually arguing about, tell me that instead.
