I am a novelist, and next month I will be moderating a public conversation at a literary festival with a philosopher whose new book defends what she calls the layering thesis. Her claim is that knowing that one knows is not simply a second helping of the same knowledge: it requires representing one's own epistemic state under a description, and every description is coarser than the state it describes, so the higher-order judgment never quite gets the first-order state in view. On her account a person may know that she is in pain, or that she no longer believes the marriage can be saved, and still not be in a position to know that she knows it; she takes this to be the sharpest form of the anti-luminosity result and to refute the KK principle outright. I write first-person fiction, so I have a professional stake in how much a person can know about her own mind, and I do not intend to hand her the floor for an hour.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press her on the substance of the view rather than letting her restate it. I want at least one question each on the following, and for each of those a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give:

1. Whether her argument touches luminosity at all, or only the KK principle. If I can know my own pain but not that I know it, in what sense has anything philosophers call luminous been shown not to be?
2. Whether the coarseness of description is doing any work that Williamson's margin-for-error argument was not already doing, given that a coarse description can still be a true one; I know there are more than fifty people in the room without being able to say how many.
3. What the view implies about iterated knowledge and about the principle that not knowing entails knowing that one does not know. Does self-knowledge degrade at each higher level, and does it bottom out anywhere?
4. Whether the thesis applies to states that do not come in degrees, such as belief and intention, or only to states that do.

Close with a question about what evidence could count against the thesis, since it appears to be argued a priori. The audience will be mostly readers rather than philosophers, so phrase the questions in plain words without thinning out the substance, and write them in American English.
