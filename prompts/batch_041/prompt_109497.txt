I write an ideas column for a general-reader magazine, and I keep returning to the argument over why some countries are rich and others poor. The frustrating thing, when I interview people about it, is that the geography camp, the institutions camp and the culture camp seem to be answering slightly different questions, so each side's evidence glances off the others'. Instead of another essay, I want to publish a flowchart that lets a reader work out which position they actually hold, and I would like you to write it. Keep it in plain enough English for a smart reader who is not an economist.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names: first the questions on which the positions genuinely come apart, then the combinations of answers those questions allow, and only then the named positions located in that space. Every node must be phrased so that a reader can answer it without knowing where it leads — a concrete comparison or a claim about a case, never a question like 'do you think geography matters?'.

I want these cases on the tree: the two Koreas; the two Germanies; the fact that the places Europeans found most densely settled and urbanized around 1500 are on the whole poorer today than the places they found nearly empty; China after 1978; Botswana against the other mineral-rich states around it; the disease burden and the soils and rainfall of tropical Africa; and the endowments — coal, coastline, navigable rivers — of the countries that industrialized first.

These distinctions should do work somewhere in the tree, though you should decide which are branch nodes and which cut across the branches: whether a view means to explain why growth started where it did or why incomes diverged afterwards; whether geography is taken to act on prosperity directly or only through the institutions it shaped; whether culture is a cause of institutions or a consequence of them; whether a factor is offered as a trigger or as a background condition; and whether the view thinks the historical record can identify the cause at all — quasi-experiment, structural narrative, or the position that with one observation per country the question is unidentifiable, together with its further step, that the profession's consensus is therefore as much sociological as evidential. Please also find room for the view that the nation is the wrong unit of analysis, and for the view that the choice of cause tracks what a person wants done about poverty.

Each leaf should carry the name of the position (or 'unnamed' if nobody defends it) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, say so and diagnose whether the view is incoherent, unattractive or merely unexplored. Flag any two positions your tree cannot separate, and say why. Keep it to four levels of branching and eight to twelve leaves. No citations or reading list — I will add those myself.
