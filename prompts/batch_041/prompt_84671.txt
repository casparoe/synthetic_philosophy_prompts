I keep returning to Otto Neurath's picture-statistics project, and in particular to a tension in his own description of it. The system he directed at the social and economic museum in Vienna from the mid-1920s, later known as Isotype, ran on a few severe rules: quantity is shown by repeating an identical, fixed-size glyph rather than by enlarging one; perspective and ornament are dropped; a standardised figure stands for a fixed value within a given chart; and a specialist whose whole job was to convert statistical tables into pictures decided which figures would do the work. He promoted the result as an international picture language, and at the same time insisted that it was a helping language which would always sit alongside words, and that it could never become a fully developed language in its own right.

I think the second half of that pair of claims should be given up, and I want you to argue for giving it up. The position to defend: the same system, extended in ways that remain pictorial, can carry the representational load of a scientific language — negation, universal quantification, and claims about how one quantity tracks another — without falling back on an accompanying natural language. I take it as common ground that a notation is a cognitive instrument rather than a neutral wrapper, and that the interesting questions about any notation are what it makes easy and what it makes unsayable; so the construction is the argument, and it needs to be load-bearing.

Specify the notation. Say what a negated claim looks like, what a universally quantified claim looks like, and how the notation distinguishes a claim that no district saw a thing from a claim that some district did not. Then render a claim of roughly this shape: in every district where the levy rose, consumption fell; in no district where the levy fell did consumption fall; the size of the fall tracked the size of the rise; and one district broke the pattern, its levy having risen the previous year. The figures and their arrangement must be doing the representing, rather than a code standing in for words, and at least one inference in your example should be available by inspection rather than by derivation.

Then build the strongest objection you can and answer it. The obvious one is that the moment you fix conventions for negation and quantification, each with its own reading rule, you have a written notation with pictures pasted on, and Neurath was right after all. A sharper one, if you can make it work, is that pictorial systems are too dense to be notations at all: there is no finite stock of features that are unambiguously the same or different from one chart to the next, so every mark is potentially meaningful and nothing is ever quite repeated. Say what distinguishes your extension from a cipher, name the weakest link in your own construction, and say what would show that the whole approach fails. One device worked through properly is worth more to me than four sketched.
