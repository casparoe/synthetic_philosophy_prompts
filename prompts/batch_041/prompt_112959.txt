i work on the utilization-review side of a health insurer (i basically read the rules that decide whether something gets paid for) and i keep noticing that our "medical necessity" policy seems to run on two totally different ideas of what a disease even is, and i cant tell if thats a bug or if the whole thing is secretly coherent. heres what trips me up:

- We wont pay for a drug unless the chart shows the thing its treating is backed by something measurable in the body — a lab value, an imaging finding. No finding, no coverage, no matter how bad you feel.
- But we do pay for depression treatment, which has no lab test, and the diagnosis comes out of a checklist in the psychiatrists' manual — and that manual keeps redrawing its own line, it decided a few years back that you can diagnose depression two weeks after a death, which used to be treated as ordinary grief and not a disorder at all.
- Gender-affirming care is covered, but only with a diagnosis attached, and the two big international manuals dont even agree on whether that diagnosis is a mental disorder or a condition of sexual health. So the same treatment counts as treating a disease in one book and not in the other, and we just follow whichever one our medical director happens to prefer.
- We'll cover chest reconstruction for a trans man, but not for a guy who wants a healthy leg amputated because it doesnt feel like his. Both are people asking us to remove healthy tissue to fix how they feel about their body. Our rule just says no on the leg.
- We cover prediabetes and "metabolic syndrome" — risk states, defined by cutoffs some committee picked, nobody is actually sick — while blood pressure in the normal range gets nothing, and we wont pay for a vitamin D test on someone with no symptoms.
- Infertility is a disease and we treat it. Pregnancy is not a disease, but we'll pay to prevent it. And we wont pay for vision correction for mild nearsightedness because thats enhancement, but we will pay if your vision is bad enough — so somewhere in there is a number where a body stops being fine and starts being broken.

So: what theory of health and disease does this policy actually presuppose? Ive heard the words naturalism and normativism thrown around but I dont know enough to say whether we're one or the other or some hybrid. I want to know which of our rules strictly require a particular view of what disease is, which ones would sit fine under any of the going views, and which of the rules actually contradict each other. Also where the policy just doesnt say enough to pin it down either way. And if you swapped our implicit view for the purest version of either side, what would visibly change in what we cover?

Give it back as JSON with exactly these fields, and keep the answers tight (a sentence or two each, except the first and last):

- "implicit_theory": {"label": a short name for the view, "statement": 2-4 sentences spelling out the view of health/disease our rules commit us to}
- "rule_by_rule": array, one entry per bullet above, each {"rule": one-line restatement, "leans": "naturalist" | "normativist" | "mixed" | "doesnt tell us", "why": one or two sentences}
- "strict_presuppositions": list of views our rules genuinely cant be true without
- "mere_fit": list of views the rules fit but dont require
- "ruled_out": rival views the policy makes unavailable to us
- "conflicts": array of {"rules": [the two rules in tension], "severity": 1-5, "what has to give": ...}, ranked worst first, and if a pair only looks like a conflict but isnt, include it flagged "apparent only"
- "underdetermined": places the policy is silent and could be filled in either way
- "swap_test": {"if_pure_naturalism": what changes in coverage, "if_pure_normativism": what changes}
- "bottom_line": one paragraph in plain language, no jargon, that I could actually send to my boss

And if the whole framing is off — if the policy doesnt really presuppose any one theory and is just a pile of compromises — say so in bottom_line and adjust the other fields to match.
