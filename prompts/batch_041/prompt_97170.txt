Daniel Dennett's heterophenomenology asks the science of consciousness to proceed in two steps: first, compile what he calls the heterophenomenological world — the record of what a subject says and believes about their own experience — and second, explain why the subject comes to say and believe those things, while staying neutral, at the stage of data collection, about whether any of the reports are true. His own comparison is that the heterophenomenologist reads a subject the way a reader reads a novel: the utterances are treated as a fiction to be interpreted, not as a transparent window onto a mind, and the point of the exercise is to account for the story rather than to certify it. Critics — the first-person or autophenomenological camp, who insist that trained introspective report is the real datum, and the philosophers who grant that we may explain reports without ever explaining experience — reply that this purchase on objectivity is bought at the price of the very phenomenon under study.

Read Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day through this method and against it. Stevens, the butler, narrates his inner life as flatly as he narrates the itinerary of his motoring trip: he reports no grief at his father's death, no attachment to Miss Kenton, no shame about Lord Darlington, and the reader, performing something very like Dennett's two steps, assembles a portrait of a man whose operative commitments are the inverse of the ones he avows. I want a reading that takes the novel and the method equally seriously, and that is honest about where each one gives.

Among the things I'd like you to address: Does the novel vindicate the two-step procedure, in that the reader's inference to the best explanation yields a better account of Stevens than Stevens' own report does? Or does it expose a limit, given that the method's data are precisely the reports that the novel shows to be systematically false, and that the discrepancy between the heterophenomenological world and the world it is supposed to explain is here not a marginal error but the whole subject? Is the novel's form itself an argument — that a heterophenomenological world can be a faithful self-portrait of self-deception? Consider also the value at the novel's centre: if dignity, for Stevens, is constituted in and only in how things are said and done, then the report is not a veil over the mind but the thing itself, and the method's demotion of the report to fiction looks like a mistake about what there was to explain. Where does the reading resist the theory, and where does the theory catch something the novel's own self-understanding cannot?
