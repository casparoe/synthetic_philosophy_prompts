I've been asked to give the commencement address at the small school where I've volunteered for years, and I've promised myself that I won't stand up there and hand a hundred and twenty people a slogan. What I've been turning over for months is a puzzle about how we check our own instruments, and I'd like the speech built on it.

Here is the puzzle as I understand it. I want to know whether the fuel gauge in my car is reliable. I look at it, it reads full, and I believe the tank is full — and the only reason I believe that is the gauge. I then notice that the gauge read full and the tank was full, and I count that as a point in the gauge's favour. I do this over and over and end up announcing that the gauge is reliable, even though nobody ever took it out and compared it with anything else. Something has plainly gone wrong, but it's surprisingly hard to say what, because the belief I leaned on at every step came from the instrument I was trying to certify. The version that bothers me more is the ordinary one: I look at a table, it looks red to me, so I believe it is red, so I believe it is not a white table under a red light, so I believe my eyes are working — and there I have certified my eyesight using nothing but my eyesight. That is too cheap. But if I insist that I may never rely on my eyes, or my memory, or my judgment until something outside them has vouched for them, then I can never begin, and I end up saying I know nothing, which I don't believe either. I've read enough to know that people divide here — some hold that this circularity is unavoidable and harmless when it comes to our most basic ways of finding things out, others that the reasoning above is a distinct mistake that can be avoided — and I don't need the speech to settle it, because I don't think it is settled.

What I need is for the speech to stay honest inside that tension. Here is why it matters to me for a graduation. Nearly every commencement address I have sat through ends by telling the graduates to trust themselves: that they already know, somewhere deep down, what is right for them, that their own judgment is the compass to steer by. That is the fuel gauge checking itself. If the only thing I can offer them is 'trust your own instrument', I would rather say nothing at all. But the opposite is no better; I won't stand in front of a hundred and twenty people and tell them that nothing can be known. The speech has to land somewhere else — clear-eyed that you cannot certify your own judgment in advance, and warm enough to be worth listening to. I suspect the honest landing has something to do with the difference between confidence that has been tested from outside, where it could have gone the other way and cost something, and confidence that has never been touched by anything but itself; but if you find a better landing, take it. What I don't want is a slogan or a shrug.

Three things I actually want in it. The fuel gauge should be there as a story, plainly told, because the grandparents in the room will follow a car and not an argument. The seniors who are going on to study philosophy, and the two teachers who teach it, should be able to hear the shape of the problem without my naming a single philosopher or a single book — please don't name any, and don't quote anything, since I couldn't check it and it would only be showing off. And it should be clear that the problem isn't the grand one about whether we can know anything at all, but the narrower one about a process being asked to vouch for itself. It can be light in places; it just can't be solemn for six minutes straight.

The room is a school gym with folding chairs and a bad PA system: families, grandparents, a few faculty, and the graduates themselves, who are eighteen and tired. Aim for about six minutes read aloud. Give me the speech itself, as continuous prose I could read straight through — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and no notes to me about how to deliver it.
