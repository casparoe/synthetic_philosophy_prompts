I'm in a metaontology reading group and we keep hitting the same wall: any time someone says a dispute is "merely verbal" or "just about words," nobody can actually say what that's supposed to rule out, and then we spend the whole session having what feels suspiciously like a verbal dispute about verbal disputes. So before we read another paper on it I want the spec first. Write me the desiderata for a theory of verbal disputes — what any adequate account of when a disagreement is really a disagreement versus when the parties are just talking past each other has to deliver. I mean the real list, not the fluff: which cases it has to get right, which distinctions it absolutely cannot collapse, which facts about language and about how disputes actually go it has to square with, and for each requirement the case or consideration that motivates it. Flag which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really just prejudices of one camp, and tell me whether some subset of them is jointly unsatisfiable — honestly that's the part I care about most, because if the list can't be satisfied then the list itself is the result.

Some cases I want the theory to handle, in rough order of how much they bug me. (1) My flatmate and I had a two-hour fight about whether a burrito counts as a sandwich; neither of us moved, and afterwards neither of us could say what the fight was actually about. (2) The tomato thing — culinarily a vegetable, botanically a fruit, and people seem to think one of these settles it, which seems wrong. (3) Two physicists who disagree about whether some process did "work" because they're using opposite sign conventions for the same equations. (4) The whole "are there composite objects" debate, where I genuinely can't tell whether the parties disagree about the world or just about when the word "object" applies, and (5) two communities that have drifted into using the same word for slightly different things over decades, so that neither is confused but they keep miscommunicating. I also have a half-baked hypothesis I want you to stress-test rather than just adopt: that a dispute is verbal exactly when the parties would agree on every fact if you rephrased each side in the other's vocabulary — tell me if that criterion is too strong, too weak, or circular.

Concretely I want the distinctions it can't flatten (e.g. a dispute being verbal vs. being trivial vs. being unresolvable; resolving a dispute vs. dissolving it; ambiguity vs. vagueness vs. polysemy as the source of the trouble; and the difference between a dispute that's verbal because of the words and one that's verbal because of the concepts), the constraints from actual linguistics and from the sociology of how disputes behave (why do participants so reliably resist the "you're just talking past each other" diagnosis?), and then — briefly, I'll do the scorecard in group — how the main families of answers (the "no genuine disagreement" camp, the "different concepts" camp, and the "it's substantive after all, the verbal framing is a dodge" camp) fare against the list. And tell me which single requirement does the most work in knocking out candidates. One more thing: I'd like the theory to be usable, not just true — I want to be able to apply the criteria to a live dispute and get an answer I could actually defend, so if some requirement is only satisfiable by a theory too abstract to apply, say so and tell me what we'd have to give up.
