I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere for a long time — LessWrong in the era when the sequences were still coming out, the sprawling comment threads, the logic-and-computation Discords that spun off from all of it — and the one habit that culture drilled into me is that you don't understand an argument until it has worked on you. I've been drafted to run a single session of a small upper-level undergraduate seminar, and I want to build the session around that habit. I'd like you to act as an expert designer of active-learning sessions in mathematical logic and philosophy of mind — someone who has actually run games with a room full of students, knows the difference between an activity that lands and one that quietly turns into a lecture with props, and has seen enough of them fail to predict how.

The setting: the seminar is called Minds, Machines, and Mathematics. Fourteen students, seventy-five minutes, one meeting. About half have taken a formal logic course; the other half are philosophy students who have never seen a formal system and will start to panic if I write a turnstile on the board. No computers, one whiteboard, paper handouts, and a projector I'd rather not depend on.

What I want them to come away with: I want them to feel from the inside the pull of the anti-mechanist argument that runs from Gödel's incompleteness theorems to Lucas and Penrose — the sense that for any machine you build, we can stand outside it, recognize that the sentence it cannot prove is true, and thereby show that we are not that machine — and then I want the reply to land on them too, not as the next bullet in a list of objections but as something they did with their own hands. The session succeeds if the room splits: half defending the anti-mechanist step, half holding an argument for why it proves too much, and both sides arguing about something that actually happened in the room.

Constraints on the activity. No lecture up front: the names Gödel, Lucas, and Penrose, and the word consistency, should not be heard until the debrief. It has to work without Gödel numbering and without anyone knowing the incompleteness theorems; the phenomenon needs to be visible by inspection of a toy system they can hold. The philosophy students must be doing something real rather than watching the logic students. It has to fit in seventy-five minutes including the debrief. Small stakes are fine, money is not.

Here is the mechanic I've been circling. Treat it as a starting point you're free to improve on, but preserve its shape. The class is split into a Machine team and a single Observer. The Machine gets a rule sheet for a toy string-rewriting system — a couple of rules, a starting string, nothing more — chosen so that every derivable string shares an obvious property that the rules visibly preserve. The Observer must write down a sentence about the Machine's future output that the Machine can never produce, together with a reason. The reason will have the form of an invariant argument, which a human can give by inspection but which the Machine cannot express, because nothing in its rule sheet lets it generalize over its own derivations. The Machine then loses, and the class feels the force of the anti-mechanist move. Then the Upgrade: the Observer's invariant is added to the Machine's rule sheet as a new axiom, the Machine gets stronger and can now reach claims it previously could not. The class must find the new sentence the upgraded Machine cannot reach — and the discovery I want them to make in their own hands is that the new sentence always needs the soundness of the rule that was just added, and the Machine has no way to license its own new axiom from inside. Repeat one rung. In the last round, hand the Observer's role to the Machine team and give them a weaker system to observe; they win instantly, and the point of the round is that the move they just watched a human make is a perfectly general mathematical operation, available to anything with the right rule sheet. (If the ladder needs a name in the debrief, the historical anchor is the transfinite hierarchy of systems in which each one proves the consistency of its predecessor, which is Turing's ordinal logics — but name it only if you're confident of the attribution, and never in the handout. I'd rather you describe an idea than cite one you're unsure of; no reading lists, no citations anywhere in the deliverable, since I can't check them and the students shouldn't get them.)

What I want back. First, before the design itself, a short brainstorm: three candidate core mechanics for making this idea felt, with a sentence each on why you kept one and dropped the others. Then the full design: materials and a photocopiable rule sheet for the Machine and the Observer's briefing card, written out as text I can hand to students; the roles and how I assign them; a minute-by-minute timeline for the seventy-five minutes; the exact words I use to open the session and to introduce the Upgrade and the role reversal, given that I must not name the argument; the stakes; and the debrief, which should walk through what happened in the room and map each moment onto a piece of the argument, including the mechanist's reply, in the facilitator's own words.

Then two more sections, and please don't soften either. First, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how I should handle each failure — I'd expect the Observer's win to be overread as a proof, the Machine team to feel the game is rigged, the philosophy students to disengage during the derivation round, and someone to say that adding the axiom dissolves the problem entirely. Second, an honest account of what this activity cannot reproduce, and what I therefore have to supply from the front of the room. I want that section to be the most careful part of your answer, since the whole point of running it rather than lecturing it is that I know exactly where the felt experience stops being evidence. Think this through before you commit to a design, and check your own rule sheet against the Observer's briefing card before you hand it to me: if the invariant is not actually visible by inspection, or if the Machine can in fact derive the forbidden sentence, the session dies in the first ten minutes.
