I am a novelist, three chapters into a book about a provincial police battalion in the occupied east, and for the past year I have been sending chapters and questions to the historian I have retained as a consultant. He returns verdicts the way a magistrate does: briskly, in writing, and without explaining himself at length. I have collected enough of them that I believe I can hear a machine running underneath, and I want you to tell me what it will do with the scene I am writing now.

His rulings to date:

1. The reservists of Police Battalion 101: men in their forties, shopkeepers, clerks, and former policemen from a single city, with no prior commitment to the regime's project, offered the chance to stand aside at their first mass shooting, and not punished by anyone when a minority did. Situation. What did the work, he wrote, was the role, the unit, and the fact that the order arrived before any of these men had had time to become the sort of person who could refuse it.

2. The mobile killing squads that followed the army in the first year of the eastern war: educated men, lawyers and physicians, few of them zealots. Situation. The load-bearing fact was the sequence, a week of humiliation followed by a week of escorting followed by a week of ditches, so that no single step ever required a decision.

3. The deportation apparatus: railway clerks and administrators arranging timetables and quotas, never touching a body. Situation. No hatred was required, only the ordinary ethics of a job competently done.

4. A small-town pogrom in which the killers and the victims had been neighbours for generations and the occupier merely stood by and permitted it. Character. The old quarrel, the debts, the shops that would change hands: the motive was already in the street, and no institution had to supply one.

5. A case I invented for him: a battalion ordered to shoot, whose captain refused and whose men refused with him, and who were not punished. Character. What explained the refusal was the men's prior ties and the captain's authority, both of which predated the war.

Six months ago I would have said his rule was the Browning–Goldhagen axis and that he sits near one end of it. Now I am less certain, because the scene I am drafting looks to me as though it sits on the axis rather than at either end of it.

Here is what I have built into my invented town. A state police unit is raised locally: the men come from the town itself, from its guilds, its market, its churches. The orders come from the state, with quotas, timetables, and an ideology distributed in pamphlets. Their task is to clear and shoot the Jewish population of their own town, people they have bought from, argued with, and lived beside for twenty years. Refusal is possible and no one is shot for it, but a refusal means a transfer east and a family left short of rations. The killing is done face to face, in daylight, in a wood a mile from the market square. The town's history of the two communities is not a story of organized antagonism; it is neighbourly indifference punctuated by the ordinary frictions of a market town, a disputed well, an unpaid debt, a lawsuit over a shopfront.

What will he rule?

Give me the candidate rules that fit his five verdicts, at least two and preferably three, each with a different load-bearing variable; say what each one predicts for my scene; and tell me which you think he is actually running, and why. If you believe he will split, character for the handful who volunteer for the wood and situation for the many who go along, say so, and tell me what that split would commit him to. Then give me the case that would settle the matter, something whose verdict the rival rules cannot agree on; if you can build it out of my own material, by preference do. I care about the prediction, but I care more about the rule, because the rule decides whether my men's prior lives are the engine of the book or only its scenery.

End with one final line, exactly in this form and nothing after it:

\answer{VERDICT; RULE; DISCRIMINATOR}

where VERDICT is one of SITUATION, CHARACTER, or SPLIT; RULE names the load-bearing variable in no more than six words; and DISCRIMINATOR names the decisive case in no more than twelve words.
