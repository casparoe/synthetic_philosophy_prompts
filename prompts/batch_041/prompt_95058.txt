Act as a political philosopher who works on distributive justice but has also spent real time on how benefits are actually administered — someone who knows the normative literature and the boring operational detail, and can tell them apart.

I'm putting together a unit on welfare conditionality — work requirements, sanctions, behavioural contracts, all of it — and before we read anything I want a proper spec of what a theory in this area has to do. So give me the desiderata. Not a theory: the list of things any adequate theory has to deliver — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the facts about how these schemes actually behave that it can't contradict — and for each one, the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than just something I happen to like.

A few things I want baked into the list, because I think they're where the interesting stuff is:

- Desert has to be inferred from what an agency can actually observe. Nobody can see who is really able to work or really trying. So any theory that needs mind-reading, or a perfect assessment of capacity and effort, isnt a theory of policy, it's a theory of a world we don't live in. What does that constraint do to the rest of the list?

- There are three different kinds of error, not one: someone who could work and won't (the cheater), someone wrongly sanctioned (the mistake), and someone who qualifies but never claims — driven off by the paperwork, the waiting, the stigma. Most theories only talk about the first. I want a requirement covering what a theory owes the other two, and a view on whether those three can be weighted independently or whether they always trade off against each other.

- Sanctions work partly by deterrence, which means a scheme can be functioning exactly as intended when someone who would have claimed and taken a job simply never shows up. Is that a success or a cost? The theory has to be able to answer rather than dodge.

- The claimant who agrees with the rule. People often endorse the work ethic that condemns them and insist they shouldn't get something for nothing, even while the conditionality is grinding them down. A theory that can only model them as compliant or resentful is missing something real.

Then two things I most want from you:

1. Say which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just commitments of a particular camp — libertarian, social-democratic, republican, whatever. And if some subset of the requirements can't all be met at once, say so plainly and explain the trade-off. My own guess is that accuracy, cheap verification, and treating claimants as equals can't all be had simultaneously, but tell me if I'm wrong or if there's a better candidate for the jointly unsatisfiable set. A list whose requirements can't all be satisfied is a result, not a failure.

2. Which single requirement does the most work at ruling theories out? My candidate is the demand that a theory explain why conditionality attaches to means-tested benefits and not to other transfers — nobody conditions an inheritance on the heir's effort, or mortgage relief on the borrower's sobriety — but I'd like to know if something on the list excludes more.

Finish with a short scorecard: desert-based theories, reciprocity or contribution-based ones, unconditional basic income, and the cynical view that conditionality is just an administrative technology for sorting and deterring rather than a moral position at all.
