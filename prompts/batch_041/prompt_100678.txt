ok so i run a small reading group / seminar thing for the local EA and rationality crowd, we've been doing animal-welfare stuff for a few months, and I need to write the final problem set. One essay question, that's it. I want it on the insect scale argument — you know the move, someone says bugs are maybe barely sentient, your credence is tiny, but there are like 10^18 of them so the expected suffering dwarfs everything in vertebrate agriculture.

Problem is every model I ask about this just recites "lots of insects, small probability, expected value is huge, therefore prioritize insects." Thats not an exam question, thats a memorized answer. I want something that actually forces them to think, and that a model with no internet can still do from the armchair.

So here's what I want. You're an expert in applied ethics and decision theory, and I want you to slow down and think step by step before you write anything. First, in a few lines, brainstorm for yourself where the real crux is — whats actually load-bearing in the scale argument, and where a sharp student would push. Then write the exam question itself.

The question should force students to notice that the scale argument has the exact same shape as Pascal's mugging: tiny probability, astronomically huge payoff, multiply the two and it dominates everything else on the board. Then they have to say whether there's a principled disanalogy between "take insect welfare seriously because expected value" and "hand the mugger your wallet because expected value" — and if there isnt one, own the consequence. I want them to have to pick a side, not hedge into "more research is needed."

To keep it in the weeds and kill the regurgitation, bake a little toy case into the question. Say one student assigns a 0.1% credence that insects are sentient, another assigns 0.0001%, and both agree there are ~10^18 insects. Make them work out what the argument says in each case, and whether the disagreement even matters. Then — this is the part I actually care about — make them address what happens when the credence isnt a clean point estimate but a vague range spanning several orders of magnitude (which is honestly how anyone should feel about insect sentience), and whether the expected-value machinery even has a determinate output then. If it doesnt, what does that do to the force of the argument? That should be the A/B line, not whether they know the numbers.

No citations, no reading list, no "as so-and-so argues in such-and-such." A model with no web access should be able to do this purely from the armchair, so dont lean on any specific paper or study. If they want to gesture at the literature, fine, but the marks cant depend on it.

Deliverables: (1) the essay question, written so it could reasonably be answered in ~90 minutes; (2) a model answer of about 600 words that actually takes a hard line and defends it, rather than surveying; (3) a marking scheme out of 20 points built on five must-hit moves, with the points for each, and for each move a one-line note on how students typically blow it. The A vs B distinction should come down to whether they engage the disanalogy question and the vague-credence problem head-on.

American English please. And dont be shy about making it hard — I'd much rather the question be too hard than recitable.
