I recently had a cardiac arrest — several minutes without a heartbeat before resuscitation — and I have been left with an experience that I would once have dismissed as a hallucination but that now feels, from the inside, more like evidence of something. I am trained in philosophy, and in trying to make sense of this I have gone back to A. J. Ayer, whose verificationist treatment of claims about survival after death I once found decisive. What I want from you is a careful case analysis, running in both directions between his theory and his own experience.

Here are the facts, so you need not guess at history. Ayer held, in the logical positivist tradition, that a statement is literally meaningful only if it is either analytic or empirically verifiable — that there must be some conceivable experience that would count for or against it. He applied this to religious and metaphysical claims, including claims about life after death, and concluded that such claims, as typically made, are not false but meaningless. He was himself an atheist and a humanist, and he thought the evidence for survival was nil. Then, in 1988, a year before his death, he choked on a piece of smoked salmon, his heart stopped for about four minutes, and he later described an experience: a bright red light, and figures he took to be responsible for the universe, with whom he tried to communicate. He did not, on the basis of this, become a believer. He said it had slightly weakened his conviction that death is the end, but he continued to think it was probably a hallucination caused by oxygen deprivation.

What I want you to do:

First, map the episode onto Ayer's own theory. By his verificationist lights, what exactly would a claim about survival after death have to be like to be meaningful? Does his own near-death experience count as the sort of experience that could verify or falsify such a claim, or is it disqualified in advance — and if disqualified, by what criterion? Be precise about the difference between a claim's being meaningful and its being true, and about whether Ayer's view allows an experience to be evidence for something that he would also say is not a genuine proposition.

Second, run the analysis the other way. Does the episode expose a weakness in the verificationist treatment of afterlife claims? In particular, consider the possibility that Ayer's experience was evidence — evidence for him, in the first person — even if he could not translate it into a publicly checkable proposition. Does the verification principle have the resources to handle first-person experiential evidence that resists third-person verification? If not, is that a problem with the principle, or a problem with the demand that evidence be public?

Third, say where the case genuinely tests the theory and where it does not. Ayer's own reaction — treating the experience as probably a hallucination while conceding it weakened his conviction — is itself a datum. Is that reaction a rational response by his own standards, or does it reveal that the verification principle was doing less work than he thought?

I am not asking for a verdict on whether there is an afterlife. I am asking what this episode shows about the verificationist account of what it is even to say that there is one.
