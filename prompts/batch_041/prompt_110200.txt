I am seventeen and I run my high school's philosophy club: fourteen students, fifty minutes after school, one whiteboard, no computers, no budget. I want to run a session on the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, and I want us to feel the thing before anyone names it.

What I take the theorem to say — correct me in a sentence if I have it wrong, before you start designing — is that any voting rule that can produce at least three different outcomes, and that has no single voter whose ranking always decides the winner, must be manipulable: there is some situation in which a voter does better by handing in a dishonest ballot than an honest one. I cannot prove that, and I would not try to in front of the club.

The club genuinely has to choose one of four things for the last meeting of term, so our preferences are real; but I need to control who wants what, so I want the preferences dealt rather than natural. Each student gets a card bearing a ranking, with a small stake attached to the card: if the top item on your card wins you get something you actually want, and if the bottom item wins you owe something mildly unpleasant, such as giving the opening summary the following week. Nothing involving money. If you think dealt preferences drain the incentive out of the exercise, say so and give me a version that does not.

The sequence I am aiming for, in this order:

1. An honest round, using a rule we agree in advance is fair, in which at least one student can see that a dishonest ballot would have produced an outcome they prefer, with their real stake on the line.
2. A repair phase. The group is invited to fix the rule. Every repair they propose is played out on a fresh set of cards and broken, until they stop offering repairs.
3. The moment someone proposes the escape hatch of declaring one option unable to win, or of letting the rule return a tie for the group to argue out. I want the group to notice that these work by shrinking what the rule can do, and that the discarded option was somebody's top choice in that room.
4. The moment the only clean fix left is a dictator — one person's card decides — and the group actually runs the vote that way and sits with it.
5. The distinction between nobody could have gained by lying in the round we just ran and nobody can gain by lying whatever everyone's cards say. I think the cleanest way in is a poll: they should be able to find the manipulation only because I tell them how the others voted, and then I ask whether honest voting was safe for them before the poll existed.

Constraints: no lecture at the start, the theorem's name only in the last ten minutes, and the whole thing runnable by me rather than by a mathematician.

Deliver the rules, the materials, the four options, the payoff scheme, and a minute-by-minute plan for fifty minutes. Then the debrief, mapping each moment above onto the corresponding piece of the theorem, including the point that the hypothesis concerns how many outcomes the rule can actually produce rather than how many options appear on the ballot. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — including the student who refuses to vote strategically and the student who feels tricked and stops playing — and what to do about each. Finally, be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: my guess is that it can only show that the particular rules we tried are gameable, never that every rule is, and I want you to say so plainly and tell me what I have to supply myself in the last ten minutes. And if two voters with three options is the smallest case worth putting on the whiteboard afterwards, tell me whether that is right and how to present it in two minutes.
