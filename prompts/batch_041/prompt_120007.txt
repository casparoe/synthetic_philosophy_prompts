I teach a graduate seminar in metaphysics and am preparing the handout for the session on individuation, and I would rather it be a decision tree than a roster of positions with famous names attached. The case I want it organized around is the two-spheres arrangement: a region containing exactly two spheres alike in every intrinsic respect — size, shape, mass, composition, colour, texture — with nothing else present, so that no intrinsic feature of either distinguishes it from the other. A colleague and I have argued about this case for years and my own verdict moves with the weather: some days the arrangement seems plainly possible and the identity of indiscernibles simply false, other days the description seems not to describe anything at all, and I have never managed to say exactly what turns on the difference. I want the tree to make that visible, because I suspect the dispute is less about the case itself than about what the principle is quantifying over and whether differences of identity are further facts about the world, and I want the questions to bring those two issues to the surface rather than presuppose them.

So: a taxonomy built out of the logical space rather than out of a list of labels — first the questions that genuinely divide answers to the case, then the positions that the combinations of answers locate. Every node must be phrased so that a student can answer it without knowing which position the answer leads to: no node may mention haecceitism, supervenience, bare particulars, or any other label by name, and none may simply ask whether one accepts the identity of indiscernibles. Start the tree from a student who grants the description of the case as I have stated it. Give me exactly five numbered items, with no introduction and no conclusion, each item being one leaf of the tree.

Each item should give, in order: the branch questions that lead to that leaf, with the answers that route you there; the name of the position, or a statement that it has no established name, in which case supply a descriptive one; and the leaf's most awkward commitment, the thing its defender must swallow that its rivals will press hardest. The five leaves are to be these, though you may sharpen the statement of any of them: a reading on which the principle holds of necessity, so that the arrangement is not a genuine possibility; a reading on which the principle is a contingent truth about the actual world, which should say what would make it more than a lucky generalization and whether the indistinguishability of particles of the same kind is an instance or a red herring; a reading on which the spheres are distinct in virtue of something over and above their qualities; a reading on which the principle is trivial if properties whose specification mentions particular individuals are allowed to count and false if they are not, so that the case cannot be settled until the range of the principle is settled; and a reading on which the arrangement is possible but there is no further fact about which sphere is which. If your questions cannot separate two of the leaves, say so in both of the items concerned and name the obstacle; if a combination of answers your questions allow corresponds to no position anyone holds, note that in the item where the path ends and say whether the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Keep each item to two or three sentences.
