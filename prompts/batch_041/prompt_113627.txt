Bit of an odd request, so bear with me. I took early retirement from a palaeontology department four years ago — most of my working life went on bryozoan systematics, which in practice is a lifetime of arguing about where one species stops and the next starts, and I came out of it a fairly hardline anti-essentialist: I don't think species have essences, I think they're more or less useful slices cut out of a continuum by people who need names for things. Since retiring I've been reading philosophy in a completely undisciplined way — a book group that's doing philosophy of mind, a few podcasts, a couple of forums where I mostly lurk — so please take the questions at face value and don't assume I know the literature. The thing that's got me is a puzzle my grandson showed me. You're given a few letters of an alphabet in some invented style — drawn on a coarse grid, so there are no smooth curves anywhere, everything chunky — say the 'a', the 'g' and the 'q' in that style, and the task is to design the rest of the alphabet so that it all reads as one style, and every letter still reads as itself. I got alot more into it than I expected, and now I find I'm carrying around a set of opinions about letters that I suspect don't fit together. What I want is an audit rather than a lecture: which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, how bad each real clash is, and what the cheapest thing to give up in each case is. Rank them by severity instead of telling me they're all in tension, and if one single change would clean up more than one clash at once, tell me which.

Here's roughly where I've landed. (1) Whether a mark is an 'a' is a determinate matter of fact — there aren't marks that are sort of half an 'a' — and it doesn't depend on any other letters existing; a single 'a' scratched on a desert island is an 'a'. (2) What makes a mark an 'a' isn't any one feature, it's the way the parts hang together, and the parts themselves only count as what they are because of the job they do in the whole — widen the bowl enough and it stops being a bowl and becomes a counter, or the whole thing stops reading as a letter at all. (3) All the same, an 'a' is what it is partly by contrast with the other letters; its identity includes the fact that it can't be confused with an 'o' or a 'd' or an 'e', and a one-letter alphabet seems like a contradiction in terms to me. (4) Every 'a' has something in common with every other 'a' that no 'o' or 'e' has — there's a core — and I'll admit this is where my anti-essentialism abandons me, because when I look at the 'a' in a printed book, the little bowl with a hood over it, and the 'a' in a child's handwriting, a circle with a stick on the right, I can't for the life of me say what feature the two share. (5) Some 'a's are better 'a's than others, membership comes in degrees, and there's no sharp line between an 'a' and a squiggle. (6) Style belongs to the alphabet and not to the letter — strictly speaking no single letter is 'in' a style by itself, which is what makes the grid puzzle hard. Though I notice I can usually guess the typeface from a single letter, and I'm not sure whether that counts against (6). (7) When someone designs that missing 'a' in the puzzle, there's a fact of the matter about whether they got it right; it isn't just taste.

One more thing, and then I'll shut up. I've deliberately not told you which of these I'd sacrifice first, because I've noticed I defend hardest whatever I'm most attached to, and thats probably where the trouble is. So if the real clash is somewhere I haven't noticed rather than between the pairs I've lined up, say so, and tell me what the repair costs me — I'd like to know what I'm giving up, not just that I have to give it up.
