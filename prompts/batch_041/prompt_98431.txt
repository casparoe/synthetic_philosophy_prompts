I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of biology; before that I studied electrical engineering, so I'm reasonably fluent in information theory and coding theory — entropy and mutual information, source versus channel coding, redundancy and error correction, the distinction between a code as a mapping and a code as a redundancy scheme, decoders as inference procedures, lossy versus lossless compression.

The debate I keep running into — whether DNA carries information, whether the genetic code deserves to be called a code at all, whether transcription and translation are literally a matter of reading a message — is conducted in a vocabulary I don't share: representation, semantic content, aboutness, causal specificity, the arbitrariness of the mapping, and function-based theories of content.

Restate the main positions in that debate as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I do have, then give me the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly into coding-theoretic terms? Which translate only with distortion, and what precisely is lost in the distortion? Which have no counterpart at all — the claim that the codon-to-amino-acid mapping is arbitrary or conventional, the claim that the degeneracy of the code is or is not redundancy of the error-correcting kind, the claim that a gene represents a trait, the claim that there is a fact of the matter about what a sequence means? For each item of residue, tell me whether the gap is a defect of my framework, a sign that the original claim was less clear than it appeared, or evidence that the dispute is about something genuinely outside information theory. I want to know whether my suspicion — that most information talk in biology is either trivial or confused — is correct, or whether it is my vocabulary that is impoverished.
