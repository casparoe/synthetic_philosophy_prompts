I've spent years in the rationalist blogosphere, where Aumann's agreement theorem gets invoked as a cudgel — the result that two Bayesians with a common prior whose opinions are common knowledge cannot agree to disagree — usually by people who treat it as a theorem about the world rather than a theorem about a pile of idealizations. I want a short story that dramatizes those idealizations instead of explaining them. Setting: a small town that has for a generation settled its civil disputes through a Concordance Office, whose procedure is a literal implementation of the theorem — both parties are assigned the town's official prior, must state a posterior on the disputed question, and must keep exchanging and publicly updating until they converge; arbitrators are barred from ruling while disagreement persists. The protagonist is a junior arbitrator handed the case that breaks the procedure: two citizens, both scrupulously rational by the office's own lights, who have each seen the other's evidence and each other's stated probabilities and still will not converge, and whose refusal is beginning to look less like stubbornness than like a hole in the town's founding assumption. Write it so the resolution turns on the protagonist working out which idealization has actually failed — a prior that was never truly shared, a disagreement that only appears to be common knowledge, updating that is bounded or quietly self-undermining, or a disputed question that turns out not to name a well-defined event — and choosing among those diagnoses from what the two parties say and do, not from a lecture. No narrator explaining the theorem to the reader, no tidy ending where the math simply wins; I want the failure to feel like something a person could live through.
