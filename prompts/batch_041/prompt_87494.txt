Write a satirical internal memorandum, roughly 650 words, in the voice of a newly appointed Director of Literal Communication at a mid-sized firm, announcing a new policy that eliminates implicature from all internal correspondence. The humor must come from the Gricean machinery rather than from generic corporate-speak. The memo should, in character and without winking, conflate the cooperative principle with a compliance code; treat the maxims of quantity, quality, relation, and manner as enforceable rules rather than as presumptions a hearer is entitled to rely on; and betray no awareness of the difference between flouting a maxim, which generates a conversational implicature, and violating one, which merely misleads. Include worked examples of prohibited utterances — a scalar expression that hints at 'some but not all', the connective 'but', and a relevance-driven hint that gets its meaning from the question it answers — and propose remedies that, if actually applied, would render each utterance either false or pointless, with the director never noticing. The director should prove unable to write two consecutive sentences of the policy without leaning on implicature; cancelability should surface as a loophole, with employees advised that they may always append 'or not'; and a closing FAQ should field complaints from a baffled pragmatics consultant. Keep it deadpan and legible, so that a reader who knows the framework sees exactly which distinctions are being trampled, and a reader who does not still finds it funny. Do not state the moral.
