I'm preparing a decision tree for a graduate seminar on whether honoring sunk costs is ever rational. Build it so that it sorts respondents by what they believe rather than by the labels they'd pick: every internal node should be a question answerable without knowing where it leads, and the first node should not simply be "do you think the sunk cost effect is a bias?" — the tree has to earn that verdict.

Put concrete cases at the nodes: a half-finished doctorate, a government continuing a failing war, a firm defending a doomed product line, a film you keep watching because you already paid for the ticket, a relationship sustained by the years already in it.

The leaves must include, at least: the orthodox view that at the level of the individual act sunk costs are never a reason; the view that persistence can be rational because the investment has changed the world and not merely the ledger; the view that it can be rational for an agent whose preferences are coherent but non-standard; the view that the unit of assessment is the policy or plan rather than the act, so that a standing disposition to finish things can be rational even when no particular act of finishing is; the view that the effect is a bias that nonetheless tracks something real in the environment; and the view that your own past willingness to pay is evidence about what you value, so honoring the investment can be a form of self-knowledge. Keep the descriptive question (whether people in fact weigh sunk costs) apart from the normative one (whether they may), and sort only on the latter.

Flag any two leaves your tree cannot separate by any question, and say why. Note which combinations of answers go unoccupied and whether that is because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unnamed. Give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Six to eight leaves.
