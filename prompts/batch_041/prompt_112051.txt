I'm putting together a session for a small interdisciplinary seminar — three economists, two psychologists, and a philosopher who keeps insisting the whole debate is a confusion about what "rationality" is supposed to mean — and the topic is the backward-induction paradox as it shows up in the finitely repeated centipede game. What I want out of you is not a survey but a tier list. Here is the menu I'm working with, described by content rather than by who is supposed to have said it: (a) accounts on which the players' payoffs are noisy or the game's terminal node is uncertain, so that passing carries positive option value; (b) accounts that deny common knowledge of rationality, or of the co-player's payoffs; (c) reasoning-depth or level-k accounts, on which a player best-responds to a partner assumed to be shallower than they are; (d) fairness-and-reciprocity accounts, on which the relevant utility is defined over the pair's outcome pattern rather than over one's own take; (e) team-reasoning or we-mode accounts, on which the unit of agency is the pair rather than the individual; (f) accounts on which passing is a norm-following move sustained by expectations about what people like us do in this kind of situation. Two further candidates are mine, and I want them judged on the same footing as the rest rather than humoured: (g) the authorship account, on which what a player avoids is being the one who unilaterally ends the exchange, so the disvalue attaches to the act of closing rather than to the payoff difference it produces; and (h) the audience account, on which players pass because they anticipate that their choice will be reconstructed and interpreted by someone later, so the move is addressed to an interpreter rather than to the co-player.

Please sort these eight into tiers by how much I should trust each to explain actual play, and do it separately for two settings: the standard six-node centipede played for real money with no deception, and a variant I care about in which the pot is fixed rather than growing — taking yields the same fixed share at every node, so passing can never increase what the pair has to divide, only change who ends up with the larger part. For each of the eight in each setting, give me a single sentence of verdict; flag which items move tiers between the two settings and say what that movement shows; and then collapse everything into a strict ordering of the eight by how well they do across both settings combined, best first.

A table would be easiest to read — one row per candidate, with the two settings as columns and the reason in a third — and please finish with the combined ordering on the last line as \answer{...}, containing just the letters in order separated by " > ". I have no interest in attribution: describe positions by their content, and don't give me names, dates, or reading lists. I'm asking only about the reasoning and about where you would put your money.
