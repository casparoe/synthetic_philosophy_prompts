I teach the Nietzsche portion of a second-year course in the history of ethics, and I am grading this term's final while in the middle of a personal situation — a long-running resentment toward a former mentor that I have been trying, and largely failing, to think through — that has made the first essay of the Genealogy stop feeling like an abstract exercise. I mention this only because I know how easily one can read ressentiment into a student's prose, and I would prefer the assessment to be calibrated to the assignment rather than to my own frame of mind. Below are the examination question and one student's answer. I would like a grade and, more importantly, the specific comments that justify it.

The question was as follows:

In lecture we drew a distinction between two things a genealogy might be doing. The first we called causal debunking: tracing a belief to an origin which, once seen, undermines the belief's claim to be held for the reasons its holder would give. The second we called typological diagnosis: identifying the kind of life a valuation expresses and what it does to the strength or health of that life, without any claim that the valuation is thereby false. With that distinction in hand, answer all of the following.

(a) Reconstruct the first essay's account of the slave revolt in morality, and say precisely why Nietzsche treats ressentiment as creative rather than merely reactive.

(b) Explain how the second essay's account of the origin of guilt and the bad conscience stands to the first essay's slave revolt. Are these the same event, the same process under two names, or distinct developments — and in what order does Nietzsche place them?

(c) Say which of the two things distinguished above — causal debunking or typological diagnosis — Nietzsche is actually engaged in, and whether either would license the further inference that morality is not objectively binding.

(d) Identify one point at which the argument turns on a psychological claim rather than a historical one, and say what work that claim is doing.

The student's answer:

"(a) The slave revolt begins with the powerless. Because they cannot discharge their hostility outward, it turns inward and becomes ressentiment. It is creative because it does not merely react to the noble valuation; it posits a new one. The noble says 'good' first and only afterward invents 'bad'; the slave says 'evil' about the other first and only then, derivatively, 'good' about himself. His 'good' is therefore an afterimage of his 'evil,' and this is why Nietzsche regards the slave's valuation as second-hand and unhealthy rather than noble.

(b) The bad conscience of the second essay is the same process seen from the other side. When the slave's ressentiment cannot be expressed outwardly it is internalized, and this internalization produces the bad conscience and, along with it, guilt. Guilt has its deeper root in the creditor-debtor relation, where the debtor's failure to repay leaves him feeling he owes a debt; Nietzsche uses this to explain how guilt could later be moralized into the sense of sin. So the slave revolt and the emergence of the bad conscience are not two events but one — the slave revolt being the historical name for the internalization that the second essay describes psychologically. The sovereign individual, Nietzsche's positive figure, is the noble who has fully discharged his instincts outward.

(c) Nietzsche is doing typological diagnosis, not causal debunking. He is not arguing that because morality came from something low it must be false; that would be the genetic fallacy. He is asking what kind of life the ascetic ideal expresses and whether it strengthens or diminishes that life. But once we see that the moral concepts are the residue of a fiction the weak invented in order to survive, we can also see that the objectivity morality claims for itself is illusory: the values are not discovered but made.

(d) The psychological claim is that the instinct for freedom, when blocked from outward discharge, turns inward. This is a claim about the mind's hydraulics rather than a documentable event, and Nietzsche needs it because the historical record alone cannot show why the internalization should take this particular self-punishing form."

Please grade the answer on a standard A–F scale and then supply the point-by-point comments that warrant the grade. For each of (a) through (d), state what is correct, what is wrong or unsupported, and why it matters; flag any conflation, misattribution, or step that does not follow; and note anything that is defensible but thin rather than outright mistaken. I want the assessment keyed to what the question asked for, not to whether the answer is generally impressive.
