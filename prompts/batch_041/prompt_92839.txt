I have recently had to make a run of consequential decisions — one medical, one financial — in which my evidence was never something I could state as a proposition I was certain of, and the textbook Bayesian instruction to conditionalize on one's evidence quietly stopped being an instruction I could actually carry out. I would like a dialogue between two proponents of opposing responses to that predicament, written so that neither is a strawman and each answers the other's actual points.

The first speaker holds that the correct response is probability kinematics: when an experience leaves one uncertain, one should take one's new credences in the cells of the relevant partition and renormalize, never treating any proposition as certain. The second holds that this is a confusion — the rational agent is always certain of her evidence, because the evidence is not the contested proposition about the world but the fact that she had such-and-such an experience or received such-and-such a signal, and once the evidence is described at the right level one simply conditionalizes, leaving probability kinematics either idle or ill-defined.

Let them engage. Have the second speaker press the objection that probability kinematics is not commutative — that uncertain evidence acquired in one order and then another can yield different posteriors, which looks like a defect of the rule itself — and let the first speaker answer rather than deflect. Have the first speaker then press a case the second cannot redescribe as certain evidence: an episode in which the uncertainty is second-order, so that the agent can locate her new credence only within an interval rather than at a point, and neither speaker is permitted simply to declare the case incoherent. Close with each stating the single concession that would most damage his own position.
