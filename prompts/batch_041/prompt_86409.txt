A friend and I spent most of a long weekend arguing about performance legitimacy, and we ended somewhere more interesting than we began. He holds that prosperity and order are what actually justify a government: when a state reliably delivers security and rising living standards, the absence of competitive elections is at most an aesthetic complaint, and the cases everyone reaches for, Singapore and China, are the demonstration. I hold that this describes successful compliance and dresses it up as legitimacy, and that an arrangement whose authority cannot survive its subjects' free and informed choice is not legitimate at all, however well it performs. We kept talking past each other, so I want the document we should have written instead.

Draft the joint statement that two well-informed, good-faith advocates of those positions could both sign. Structure it as follows.

1. Numbered agreed claims. What both sides accept about what legitimacy is and is not; about what prosperity and order can and cannot by themselves establish; about the difference between a population's acceptance of a regime and that regime's justification to the population; and about which parts of the usual dispute turn out, on reflection, not to be in dispute at all.

2. A disagreements table. For each genuine disagreement: the claim at issue; each side's own formulation of its position, phrased so that side would accept the wording; and whether the disagreement is substantive or turns on a verbal choice.

3. Cruxes. Identify the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each, state what both sides agree would count as evidence or argument bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Treat the following candidates as things to evaluate rather than assume: whether a legitimacy claim must be indexed — legitimacy of what, for whom, relative to which counterfactual baseline; whether a performance-based justification is self-undermining over time; whether the performance side would accept the same reasoning if the performance were delivered by an unelected body in a liberal democracy; and what each side should say about a well-performing authoritarian state with no plausible transition and about a badly performing democracy.

Two constraints. Neither side may lean on contested statistics — where a crux is empirical, write down the observation that would settle it rather than a figure. Neither side may cite authorities; the statement should stand on arguments a reader can assess directly. And say plainly which parts of our original exchange were verbal.

End with a single final line in exactly this form: Deepest remaining crux: <one sentence>
