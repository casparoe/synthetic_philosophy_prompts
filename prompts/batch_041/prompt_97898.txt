Zhuangzi lived in the fourth century BCE; every question below arose within living memory. Using the Zhuangzi as your evidence base, predict what he would say about each, and keep a clear line throughout between what the text actually supports and what you are extrapolating.

A prediction of the form "he would be serene" is worthless, so the exercise is organized around a stipulation. When a person says they want not to die, they may want any of three separable things:

(i) thread-continuation: the biographical narrative that has been running since birth keeps running, with its memories, attachments, and unfinished projects intact;
(ii) seat-continuation: some locus of experience continues to undergo, whatever its relation to memory or personality;
(iii) wake: the causal and relational aftermath of the life continues to shape the world and the people in it.

A position may accept one of these and reject the others, and the three come apart in most real cases. For each item below, identify which of the three is actually at stake, give Zhuangzi's verdict, and state what in the text licenses it.

1. Brain death and organ procurement. Contemporary medicine treats irreversible cessation of brain function as death, partly because that criterion permits organs to be taken from a body that is still warm and perfused, and it distinguishes this from donation after circulatory death, where the heart has stopped. What would he make of the criterion itself, and of the practice that hangs on it?

2. Cryonics. Its defenders rest on an informational rather than a functional criterion: a person is not truly dead until the information constituting them is destroyed, and a preserved body is a patient awaiting better technology. Is this a view about thread-continuation, seat-continuation, or neither, and would he regard the whole project as a category error, a harmless ritual, or something close to his own view of death as one transformation among others?

3. Grief technology. A company trains a conversational model on a dead person's messages and voice; the survivors can talk with it; a year later the widow edits the persona file to make it kinder than her husband was. Is the edited model a falsification of the dead, a continuation of a relationship, or neither? Which of the three things above is the widow buying?

4. Assisted dying. Here the target is suffering rather than death, and the request is for a death that is chosen, timed, and witnessed. Does Zhuangzi have the conceptual resources to distinguish this from the deaths he treats as merely natural, or does the distinction between a natural death and an elected one dissolve on his view?

Procedural requirements:

- Do not reach any verdict by attributing to him a general indifference to death. Each verdict must turn on a specific episode or strand of the text: the well-known episodes of his singing and drumming after his wife's death, his conversation with a skull, his remark that his corpse might as well feed the birds above ground as the insects below, the butterfly dream, and his recurring treatment of death as one more transformation. Describe these in your own words; supply no chapter numbers, editions, or scholarship.
- State each verdict twice: first in his idiom, then in the vocabulary of the modern debate, and note what the translation into modern terms loses.
- At least one verdict must be one a contemporary reader would find disturbing or offensive. If all four come out humane and consoling, you have imported contemporary values rather than read the text.
- Where the text genuinely underdetermines the answer, say so explicitly and give a confidence value between 0 and 1.
- Close by naming the premise that participants in these debates generally share and that he would reject as a false alternative, and say whether his rejection amounts to a substantive position or merely a change of subject.
