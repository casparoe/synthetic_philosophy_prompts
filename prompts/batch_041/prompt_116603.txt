I write a science-and-ideas column, and I keep walking into a fight I cant adjudicate. Someone -- usually a physicist, sometimes a neuroscientist, once a guy who studies slime molds -- says a pattern is real. Real, not just a handy way of talking. Someone else says no, you drew those lines, it's a projection. And I nod, and realize I have no idea what would even count as settling it.

So before I interview one more person about this, help me build the spec. What would any theory of "there's a pattern here" have to get right to be worth anything? I want the requirements list, not another theory.

Cases it has to handle, at least these:
- a checkerboard drawn in a bitmap, where the checkerboard is obviously there in some sense and also obviously just pixels
- a glider in the Game of Life, which is made of cells and also sort of isn't
- temperature and pressure in a gas, which nobody doubts, next to the same sort of statistics you could pull out of a random string
- a barometer that "predicts" rain versus a storm that causes it
- a model that finds a pattern in data which evaporates on the next sample, versus one that holds up
- something like a gene or a species, where the biologists themselves argue about whether it's a real joint in nature or a bookkeeping device

Distinctions it better not collapse: pattern versus mere regularity, real versus useful fiction, and observer-relative versus subjective. Also compressible versus there being a fact of the matter about which description is the right one.

Things it can't contradict: information theory and compression (if a pattern is just a short description, and any string is short-describable by some program, that seems to break the whole thing), physics being closed under the physical stance, and the plain fact that the design and intentional stances predict stuff alarmingly well.

Which of these are non-negotiable and which are just the tastes of one camp (Dennett's crowd, the structural realists, whoever)? And is there a subset that can't all be had at once -- say "the pattern is objective" together with "which patterns exist depends on our interests and our compression capacities"? That's the part I most want. Then a paragraph on how the three-stances picture does against the list. No citations, I'm not writing a dissertation -- I just want to know when to stop rolling my eyes.
