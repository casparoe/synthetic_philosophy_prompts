I keep using the word grounds in a paper I'm writing — X obtains in virtue of Y, Y is more fundamental than X, that kind of thing — and I've realized I dont actually know what I'm signing up for. Before I lean on it any harder, give me the desiderata: what does any adequate theory of metaphysical grounding have to deliver? The cases it has to get right (the statue and the lump its made of, the wall being red because it's crimson, a disjunction being true because one disjunct is, the moral grounded in the natural, the many grounded in the one or the one in the many), the distinctions it can't collapse (grounding vs. plain supervenience or entailment, full vs. partial grounding, immediate vs. mediate, grounding a fact vs. grounding a thing, grounding vs. causation), and the formal demands people put on the relation (irreflexive, asymmetric, transitive, factive, hyperintensional — finer-grained than necessity — and either well-founded, so there's a fundamental bottom, or not).

Then the part I actually want: which of those demands can't all hold at once. My hunch is that once you let grounding run both ways — picture a case where a sentence means what it does because of its words, and the words mean what they do because of their role in sentences, so it looks like a two-way street — you have to drop either asymmetry or the idea that fundamentality bottoms out, and maybe the hyperintensional/explanatory character is what makes the tidy formal demands impossible to keep in the first place. So tell me which single requirement does the most damage, i.e. rules out the most of the going theories. And be explicit about which of these requirements people in the area actually agree on and which are just one camp's preference dressed up as a constraint. American English, please.
