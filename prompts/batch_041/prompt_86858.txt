I am advising the director of a bioethics institute who holds funding for a four-person, three-year research programme and must decide where its centre of gravity should lie. The subject is the therapy/enhancement distinction: not whether some particular line can be drawn, but which of the open questions about such lines are worth a programme's scarce years. Assume the director is indifferent to adding another round to the familiar arguments and will judge the programme by whether it changes how some institution — an insurer, a regulator, an admissions office, a sporting body, a health ministry — actually decides cases.

To force the analysis away from the well-worn binary, I would like the answer organised around the following scheme, which I have found more useful than the distinction itself. Any proposed line between treating and enhancing can be asked to do three jobs, and it can do them one at a time:

- *Distributive*: allocate scarce resources, excuse obligations, and identify who is worse off through no fault of their own.
- *Expressive*: mark what a society treats as a misfortune to be remedied, as against an advantage to be envied or a vice to be discouraged.
- *Gatekeeping*: tell an institution whether it is looking at a medical need, a technology, or a consumer preference, and therefore which evidence standard, risk tolerance, and regulatory regime apply.

A line can succeed at one of these jobs and fail at another, and the failure is usually silent: a rule that is defensible as an allocation may be indefensible as a public statement, and the reverse.

Given that scheme, tell me which open questions the programme should prioritise, and in what order. I am less interested in the list than in the reasoning: for each question, why it is upstream of the others, whether it is tractable for philosophers rather than for clinicians or economists, whose behaviour would change if it were settled, and what would move it up or down the ranking over the three years. Which of the three jobs is most under-served by the current debate, and which apparent controversies would turn out to be idle once the jobs are separated?

Then stress-test the ordering against two cases. First, a cheap, safe, universally available intervention that raises some measurable capacity far above the population norm, with no medical indication. Second, two recipients of the identical intervention, at the identical dose, with identical effects, one of whom carries a diagnosis and one of whom does not. Say which of the three jobs the label can still do in each case, and whether your ranking survives.

Fold in one further consideration: the baseline of normal functioning appears to migrate, as spectacles, orthodontia, fertility treatment, and stimulants for the diagnosed once did, so that a line drawn on the disease side today tends to become an enhancement line later. Treat that migration as a phenomenon to be explained rather than a fallacy to be exposed, and say what it implies about which of the three jobs a stable line can do at all.

I do not need a survey of the literature. I need a defended agenda, with the criteria stated, the trade-offs acknowledged, and an explicit account of what would change the ranking.
