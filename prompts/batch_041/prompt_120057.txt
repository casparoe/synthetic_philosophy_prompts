I just read a pop-history book on the Great Divergence and I definately want to know how much of its big-picture summary I should trust. Roughly, it goes: China was the world's leading economy for centuries, and as late as 1750 the most advanced parts of China were just as rich and sophisticated as England, so the real puzzle is why the Industrial Revolution happened in Britain and not China. The answer, according to what the book calls the "California School" and claims historians now basically agree on, is coal and colonies — England sat on cheap accessible coal and had the Americas as a market and a source of land-intensive goods, China had neither. Conclusion: the West's rise was an accident of geography and luck, nothing to do with science or institutions, and the old question about why China never developed modern science is really the same question. Two things bug me: the "historians now agree" bit, and calling it the same question as the science one. A friend also says the whole framing is off, because "why Britain and not China" takes countries as the units of comparison when the real story is regional — the Yangzi Delta versus England, not China versus Europe.

Can you go through it claim by claim and tell me what an economic historian would nod at, what's a real position but genuinely contested, and what's flat wrong — and then whether the question "why Britain and not China" is well posed at all once you've fixed the errors? Please be explicit about which bits are settled and which are live disputes among people who actually work on this, rather than handing me one school's line as if it were the consensus.
