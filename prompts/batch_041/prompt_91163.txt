Next month's podcast guest thinks Bradley's regress isn't a puzzle to be solved but a proof. Her line: for a and b to be related, or for a to be F, something has to tie them together, and then something has to tie the tie, and it never bottoms out — so there can't be any such thing as a structured proposition at all. Her positive view is that the things we call propositions are a fiction you get by ignoring the acts of judging: the act is what unifies, and the "proposition" is just an abstraction off it. She isn't a famous person and this is her own view, so don't go trying to pin it on anyone; I've told you enough above.

The problem is she has one deflection she uses on every objection, which is "that's the same regress, one level up." She turns every counterexample into an instance of her own argument. I need 8-10 questions that make that move harder to use each time, and for each question, the follow-up you'd ask when she gives the obvious answer.

Things I definately want covered:

- The order problem. If unity is just "these constituents belong together," why is the proposition that a loves b not the same one as the proposition that b loves a — same constituents, after all. Is arrangement a further ingredient, or is she individuating propositions some other way? Follow-up keyed to whichever she says.
- The relocation worry. She says the act of judging does the unifying. But an act of judging is itself a complex thing — a judger, some concepts, an object. What unifies *that*? Have the follow-up ready for the two likely answers ("the act's unity is primitive" / "the agent just is doing the judging").
- Overgeneralization. If the regress kills structured propositions, does it kill sets, molecules, and ordinary composite objects too? Press on what's supposed to be different about them; if she bites the bullet, ask what she still thinks exists.
- Mind-dependence and unentertained truths. If propositions are abstractions from acts, are there truths nobody has ever judged? Plus the circularity version: can you individuate acts of judging without already having the contents?
- Whether the regress gets going for monadic predication the way it does for relational predication, how she handles propositions whose constituents are themselves propositions, and what she makes of the trick where judging is a relation to several things at once rather than to one thing (Russell's move).

Also, there's a familiar line that the regress is benign — infinite but harmless, like the truth regress — and she needs it vicious for her argument to run at all. I want a question that forces her to say what makes a regress vicious rather than just endless, with a follow-up if she says "vicious just means it never stops."

She also likes to say "you're presupposing that unity has to be explained rather than displayed." Somewhere in the set, make her say what "displayed" is supposed to mean and how anyone could tell a unity that's displayed from one that's just missing.

And finish with one question that's hard for her to turn back into the regress — something about what could count as evidence against her view, or whether there's any way she'd give it up.
