My friend's a cognitive science grad student and we got into a long, slightly drunk argument last week about Hofstadter and Sander's big book on analogy being the core of cognition — the one where they say analogy isn't just a tool you pull out for clever comparisons but is what thinking *is*. Her line was that whenever you recognize anything as anything, you're making an analogy, even when you look at a chair and just see a chair, even when you read the letter A in some font you've never seen before. I kept pushing back: when I see a chair I'm not comparing it to anything, I just see it. There's no two things being lined up. And when I *do* make an analogy, like saying this election feels like 1968, that's a deliberate, conscious move — it feels completely different from just perceiving a chair. So it seems like either she's stretching "analogy" until it covers all of perception and thought, in which case the word isn't doing any work and the thesis is unfalsifiable, or she's making a real empirical claim that I don't see how you'd test. She said the Copycat model and the letter-font programs are supposed to show the mechanism, and that concepts aren't fixed definitions but fluid things built out of past experiences, and that there's no such thing as a purely literal categorization with zero analogy in it — which, honestly, sounds to me like the lit-crit line that everything is a metaphor, just with computer programs attached.

I want to figure out what I was actually disagreeing with her about, because I have a nagging feeling I was arguing against a version of the view she doesn't hold. So: is the claim that seeing a chair is an analogy just a redescription of the fact that we sort things into categories, or is there a substantive, testable thesis underneath? If the analogy in ordinary perception isn't a comparison between two already-formed representations, what *is* it supposed to be, and how is that different from just saying that perception is influenced by memory? And what would actually count as evidence against the whole picture — if every possible result, including fast automatic recognition and slow deliberate analogizing, gets counted as supporting it, then how is this different from a framework that just renames categorization as analogy and calls it a day? I'd also like to know where the line is between analogy and plain similarity or association, since she kept using them almost interchangeably and I couldn't tell if that was sloppiness on her part or a real commitment of the theory.
