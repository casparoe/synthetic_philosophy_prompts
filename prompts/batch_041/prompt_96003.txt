I have drafted the following short essay for a graduate seminar on media and selfhood, and I would like a critical reading of it before I circulate it to the group. I have written it deliberately without quotation, so that the argument has to carry the weight rather than the wording, and I would ask you not to answer with references or suggestions for further reading. What I need to know is what this essay, as it stands, can and cannot support.

The draft:

The received picture of Kierkegaard's campaign against the press is that it is nostalgia with a bad temper: a man who had been made ridiculous by a satirical paper generalising his grievance into a complaint about mass media, noise, and the crowd. On that reading his target is scale — too many readers, reading too fast, at too great a distance from the intimacy of one person talking to another.

I want to defend a different reading. His target is not scale but structure. What the press makes possible, in his account, is a body that is addressed without ever assembling and that therefore cannot answer. That is what the public is: not a very large crowd, but an entity with no location and no members, which no single individual is, and to which no single individual can reply. Two consequences follow, and they are the ones he cares about. The first is that responsibility evaporates: an assertion can circulate with no one standing behind it, because a medium can transmit without a speaker and be received without a hearer. The second is that the two parties to public speech stand to one another as one absence to another — which is why the corrective he keeps reaching for is not a better press but the single individual, the one unit that can be addressed and can answer, and why the language of silence keeps surfacing in his later writing.

Once this is in place, a distinction is worth drawing that his critics usually miss. Anonymity comes in two forms, and they have almost nothing in common except the name. Aggregate anonymity is the anonymity of the many: a crowd, a mob, a mass, in which nobody is individuated. It has a location, a mood, and a capacity to act, and it is frightening precisely because it can act. Structural anonymity requires no plurality at all. Even a single reader can be anonymous in this sense, because the medium addresses a position rather than a person, and the addresser is as absent as the addressee. Call this the anonymity of the medium. His claim that a crowd is untruth belongs to the first kind; his critique of the press belongs to the second. Conflating the two is what makes him look like a snob. Kept apart, his objection is not to many people reading; it is to a form of address that nobody occupies at either end.

The distinction earns its keep when it is applied forward. If the public is a phantom because it has no location, then a medium that locates its audience perfectly — one that addresses each reader separately, in private, with material that is public in form — ought to dissolve the phantom. This is more or less what recommendation feeds do, and what they produce is not the single individual. It is what I call the audience of one: a private addressee of public speech, alone with content it cannot answer, treated as a person while remaining anonymous as a reader. The phantom does not vanish when the public is broken into its members; it merely loses its name. The interesting question is therefore whether being addressed as an individual is sufficient to make one one. I take Kierkegaard's answer to be no, and I take that answer to be the part of the account worth keeping.

What I would like, roughly in this order of priority:

1. Accuracy of attribution. Which of the positions I attribute to Kierkegaard are not his, and which are borrowings from later critics wearing his name? I would rather be told that a claim is not in him at all than that it is unfashionable.

2. The distinction. Does the split between aggregate and structural anonymity do real work, or does structural anonymity collapse into aggregate anonymity with the plurality subtracted? If it collapses, tell me which sentence of mine gives it away.

3. The extension. Is the move from the public to the audience of one a legitimate application of his account, or does it require a theory of the medium that he simply does not have, in which case I am reading my conclusion back into him?

4. The remedy. Have I got his remedy right? I have made it a matter of inwardness and withdrawal; if that is a distortion, say what the essay would have to give up in order to correct it.

Finally, tell me which single revision would most improve the essay, and which paragraph is doing the least work. Please be concrete about which sentences to cut or rewrite rather than offering general encouragement.
