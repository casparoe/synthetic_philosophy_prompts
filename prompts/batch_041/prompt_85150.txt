I volunteer as curator for a small village heritage group in a county with no professional archaeological service of its own, and I need help with a decision that has split our committee.

In 1968 a local farmer dug a shallow, stone-lined pit under what is now the car park of the village hall. The finds survive as a box of objects, some still bagged and labelled, along with his diary and a sketch plan. The pit held about forty sherds that refit into a single large storage jar; a pottery specialist who examined them says the jar was already a century or two old when it went into the ground. According to the sketch, the dog's skull was placed upright on top of the broken jar, facing along the pit's long axis. Around the jar's rim were seven iron nails, all bent, plus a whetstone worn hollow on one face and a small copper-alloy pin. The fill had two layers separated by a thin band of clean sand. The diary also mentions a second, smaller pit a metre away, which he emptied without recording anything; those finds went into the same box, so we can no longer tell which objects came from which pit. Nothing comparable has been found elsewhere in the field, which has otherwise produced only ordinary household debris.

We are rebuilding our permanent display and the committee is deadlocked. One member is certain the pit was a shrine and the objects were offerings: careful placement, an animal head, deliberate breaking. Another is equally certain it was a rubbish pit, and that the offerings are just what happens when a household clears out an old storeroom. A third says nothing was deliberately deposited at all, and that the assemblage is what you get when objects roll into a hollow and are sorted by water and burrowing animals. The farmer's daughter, who gave us the collection, has told me she will withdraw it if we describe the pit as a rubbish dump; her father was proud of what he called his little temple.

Two questions, and the second is the one I actually care about. First, what should the label say? Please draft me roughly ninety words of display text and tell me which reading it should lead with. Second: what makes any of these readings better than the others? I have a feeling the committee is treating the rubbish pit as the safe, boring default and the shrine as the exciting, unprovable one, but I cannot see why a claim about what people threw away is any better evidenced than a claim about what they believed. One member told me we can only ever infer what people did with their hands, never what was in their heads; another said that anything we cannot explain we end up calling ritual, which is just a way of not explaining it. Both remarks feel half-right and I cannot get them to fit together. I would also like to know whether the objects themselves can ever settle a question like this, or whether we are really arguing about something else.

Please do not send me a reading list or references; I have no way to check them, and what I need is the reasoning. Take the role of an archaeologist with a philosopher's habits — someone who has thought hard about how we are entitled to infer belief and intention from mute objects. Before drafting anything, spend a few paragraphs laying out what each of the three readings is actually asserting and what evidence each would need in order to be true, and what each would lead us to expect that we do not find. Then tell me where the real disagreement lies, and whether the missing second pit changes anything. Finish with the label text and a short paragraph I can read aloud to the committee. Think carefully about the difference between evidence that is absent and evidence that would not have helped either way, and be explicit about which of your own claims are shaky.
