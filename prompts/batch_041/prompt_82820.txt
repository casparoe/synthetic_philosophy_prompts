You are a philosopher of cognitive science with a working knowledge of mechanistic interpretability, and you have spent a lot of time adjudicating disputes about when it is legitimate to say that a neural network represents something. Think carefully before you answer, and start by brainstorming the candidate decision rules that could underlie the pattern of verdicts below; then commit to a best guess rather than hedging. Everything you need is contained in this brief, so do not cite any literature, and write your answer in British English.

Background. A colleague of mine, whom I shall call Nadia, runs a mechanistic interpretability group. She is a realist about representation in networks: she thinks questions of the form, does this network represent X, have determinate answers. But she refuses to state a criterion. When pressed, she says that any criterion she writes down will be gamed by the next finding, and that the only way to see what she thinks is to watch her work. Here are five findings from her group, and the verdict she gave on each; I have simplified them, but not distorted them.

1. The Number Direction. A single direction in the residual stream of a small language model. A linear probe reads singular versus plural subject from it with 99 per cent held-out accuracy, and adding or subtracting the direction flips subsequent verb agreement. Nadia: the model represents number.

2. The Register Direction. A single direction that a linear probe reads as formal versus informal register with 95 per cent held-out accuracy. Every intervention the group tried — adding it, subtracting it, ablating it, projecting it out, and overwriting its subspace with a learned decoder — left the output distribution statistically indistinguishable from baseline, and the direction turns out to be reconstructible from tokeniser statistics alone. Nadia: not a representation, merely decodable information.

3. The Antecedent Code. A pair of mid-layer computations whose joint state carries the antecedent of a pronoun across long distances. No linear probe, no sparse-autoencoder feature, and no human-inspectable pattern recovers the antecedent from that state; the code appears high-dimensional and context-rotated. But interchange interventions, swapping the state between minimally different sentences, take the model's pronoun choice from 92 per cent correct to chance. Nadia: a representation, and one we do not yet know how to decode.

4. The Copy-Suppression Head. A head whose causal role is to cancel the signal that would otherwise cause the previous token to be repeated. Its content is most naturally specified as the token the model would otherwise have emitted, not as any fact about the input. Nadia: a representation, of the model's own counterfactual output.

5. The Wheel-Texture Feature. A feature in a vision model that is decodable, causally load-bearing for wheel classification, and whose content is fixed by what the downstream consumer does with it: it fires for wheel-shaped texture painted on a wall as readily as for a wheel. Nadia: a representation, of wheel-texture rather than of wheel, because the consumer decides the content.

The new finding. The Hedge Probe. In a mid-layer of a language model, the group found a direction H such that a linear probe reads whether the model is about to hedge rather than assert flatly with 97 per cent held-out accuracy, and the same probe, without refitting, predicts hedging across three domains and in a fine-tuned variant of the model. However, every intervention on H — patching it to either extreme, ablating it, projecting it out, and a nonlinear intervention that overwrites the whole eight-dimensional subspace around it — leaves the hedging rate statistically indistinguishable from baseline, with power to detect a five-point change. The hedging behaviour is controlled instead by a separate family of directions G, which are not predictable from the input and which no probe reads as hedging; interchanging G flips hedging from 8 per cent to 91 per cent. H sits downstream of G and faithfully reports the state that G sets, but nothing further downstream reads H.

What I need. Predict Nadia's verdict on the Hedge Probe. Set out the candidate rules that fit her five earlier verdicts — I expect more than one, and I want to know which of the five verdicts rules out which rule, and whether any of her verdicts are jointly inconsistent. Say what each surviving rule predicts about the Hedge Probe, give your best guess at her actual verdict with your reasons for preferring one rule over the others, and give a confidence figure. Then tell me which further experiment would best separate the rules that survive. We have one unpublished result in the drawer that may or may not do the job: a direction whose injection reliably flips behaviour, but only by inflating the residual-stream norm, carrying no content of its own. Say whether that experiment discriminates among the candidate rules, and if it does not, describe one that does.
