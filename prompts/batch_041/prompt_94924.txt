I'm a high school junior and I run my school's civics club, which meets once a week for fifty minutes with about eighteen regular members. I've been reading about what democratic theorists call the boundary problem: the people who are supposed to decide things democratically have to be defined somehow, but any vote on who belongs already presupposes an answer to who is entitled to vote on it, so the founding decision seems to sit outside democracy rather than inside it. I want my club to run into that circularity rather than hear me explain it, and I don't want to say the word "boundary" until the debrief.

Design a runnable activity for one fifty-minute session with those eighteen students. My rough idea, which you should feel free to overhaul, is a staged vote. In the first stage the club writes its own membership rules, and everyone has to state a general principle for admission out loud, not just push for a favorable line. In the second stage we hold a real vote on something with stakes for us, such as whether to hand our meeting slot to a club that doesn't exist yet. In the third stage a group of students shows up claiming membership under the rules we wrote, in a way that would flip the earlier result, and there is no authority above the club to appeal to. I'd like the incentives arranged so that each member's preferred membership rule quietly tracks how the applicants would vote, while the public principle they gave makes that preference embarrassing to defend.

Give me roles, rules, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the actual structure of the problem, including the difference between being affected by a decision and being subject to it, and why the excluded have no standing to complain without already assuming a people that includes them. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
