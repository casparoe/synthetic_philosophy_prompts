You are a philosopher of mind who has spent years working on theories of consciousness and who also writes fiction well enough to know that a philosophical idea survives in a story only when it is carried by people, objects, and paperwork rather than by dialogue that sounds like a seminar. Before you write anything, think the brief through and brainstorm on the page: list the distinctions you will need, sketch three or four candidate scenes, decide where the story's central ambiguity should land, and choose which character will be unable to say the thing the reader most needs to hear. Then write.

The concept I want the story built around is the higher-order family of theories of consciousness. Its core claim is that a mental state is conscious not in virtue of anything intrinsic to the state itself, but because a suitable higher-order representation of it exists — a representation whose content is that first-order state. The family divides in ways that matter for a plot. The higher-order element may be a thought-like judgement about one's own state (so that being conscious of a state is closer to believing something about oneself), or a perception-like inner sense (so that it can misrepresent the way perception misrepresents, and can be fooled by its own instrument). It may be an occurrent episode or a standing disposition to make the first-order state available for report, memory, and control. And the whole family faces a first-order rival: the view that a state is conscious in virtue of its own properties — how it is processed, how widely it is broadcast, how long it reverberates — in which case the higher-order representation is a report on consciousness rather than its maker.

Two consequences of the higher-order picture are the engines I want the story to run on. First, if the higher-order representation can be wrong, then a subject can be conscious of a state she does not have, and can carry a state she is never conscious of; the mismatch is not a breakdown of the theory but a possibility it builds in. Second, if what makes a state conscious is the presence of the right higher-order representation, then a creature whose first-order life is thin or alien could nonetheless have a rich conscious life, while a creature with vivid first-order states but no capacity for the relevant higher-order ones would have none at all.

Set the story in a near-future clinic that has turned one version of this picture into a medical product. The procedure — call it a second-order calibration — implants a small monitor that continuously generates higher-order representations of the patient's bodily and affective states; it is those representations, not the underlying states, that the patient can report, remember, and act on. The clinic's consent materials insist that the procedure "does not change what you feel, only what you know you feel." Tell the story from the point of view of someone with reason to doubt that sentence: a patient, the technician who runs the calibration sessions, or a family member being trained to interpret the readouts. At some point the mismatch must become concrete — a pain that is reported but never felt, or felt and never reported and visible only in posture and flinching; a stretch of blank calm in a body that is plainly in distress; a report that is true of someone else's state, or true of a state that has already passed. Do not let the story settle the philosophical question. End somewhere a reader can hold both readings at once: the implant as a window onto a consciousness that was already there, and the implant as the thing doing the making.

If it helps, you may run a second thread at the clinic involving a patient who cannot form the relevant higher-order representations at all — an infant, or someone in a minimally responsive state — so that the theory's cost becomes visible on the page.

Craft constraints. No lectures, no exposition dumps, no scene in which a scientist explains the theory to the protagonist. The staff should speak like clinicians and administrators, not philosophers. The story itself should avoid the vocabulary of the debate: "qualia," "phenomenal character," "higher-order representation," "metacognition," "zombie." Let the philosophy live in what characters can and cannot report, in what the paperwork promises, and in what a nurse notices and writes down. Roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words; give it a title.
