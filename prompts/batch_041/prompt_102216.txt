I am enrolled in a graduate seminar on historiography and the origins of the First World War, and we have spent several weeks on the Fischer controversy — the long-running fight over whether Germany and its leadership bore the main responsibility for the war's outbreak, and over what kind of evidence could possibly settle a claim of that shape. The more time I spend with it, the more I suspect that the historical dispute is really a conceptual dispute in costume: the participants disagree less about the documents than about what "responsibility" is even supposed to mean when the bearer of it is a state, an elite, or a nation rather than a person. I would like to pin my own view down numerically instead of retreating to "it's complicated."

Please give me your credence in the following proposition, stated as precisely as I can manage it: that the German imperial leadership bore primary responsibility for the outbreak of a general European war in the summer of 1914. I intend "primary responsibility" here as a stipulation, not as a term of art from the literature: the actor (a) whose decisions had the largest counterfactual impact on whether the war occurred, and (b) whose conduct, judged against the norms and the information available at the time, was the most culpable. Then decompose that number into the sub-questions it depends on, giving each a probability and a one-line justification: the probability that the war was contingent at all, meaning that some plausible divergence in the choices of a few decision-makers would have averted it, rather than its being overdetermined by structural factors such as the alliance system, the arms race, the cult of the offensive, or the instability of the Balkans; the probability that, conditional on contingency, the pivotal decisions were German or Austro-Hungarian rather than another power's; the probability that the pivotal decision was the one that made a local Balkan quarrel into a continental war rather than the one that lit the initial fuse; the probability that those decisions were culpable rather than a defensible response to genuine security dilemmas; and the probability that responsibility of this kind can legitimately attach to a state or a leadership group at all, rather than only to particular individuals. Add any term you think belongs and that I have missed, and feel free to split mine if they are doing too much work.

Show how the terms combine into your final figure, and then tell me which term the number is most sensitive to and what single piece of evidence or argument would move it the most. I will be arguing about this with a classmate who holds that the entire question of national war responsibility is a category mistake, that only individuals can be blameworthy and that attributing guilt to a state is either a metaphor or a dangerous fiction; so please also say where you think the real disagreement between her position and mine lives, and whether it is a disagreement about the history or about the concept. End with a line in the form \answer{0.XX} giving your single overall credence.
