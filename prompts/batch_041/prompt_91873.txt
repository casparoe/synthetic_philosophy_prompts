I am preparing a session for an advanced seminar on Nietzsche's doctrine of eternal recurrence, and I want to build it around a case Nietzsche could not have imagined. Here is the case.

A memorial-technology firm offers two products. The first, Replay, has been recording a subscriber's adult life at high fidelity for years — audio, video, location, biometrics, correspondence — and can re-present any recorded hour to a survivor in full sensory detail, unaltered, as many times as the survivor asks. Nothing in the record can be edited, and no hour that was not lived can be produced; the survivor may choose which hour, and how often, but cannot change a second of it. The second product, Continue, is built from the same data: it is a conversational simulacrum of the deceased that answers questions the person was never asked, in their idiom, and that keeps answering as new questions are put to it. It never contradicts the record, but it does say things the record does not contain. The man who used both died last year; his partner says that Replay gives her back the past exactly, while Continue gives her back her husband.

Write the reasoned reconstruction I would want to hand the seminar: a considered account of how Nietzsche would have responded to these two products, on the assumption that the relevant texts and ideas are available to him but the technology is not.

Your reconstruction should do real work with the doctrine rather than gesture at it, and should address at least the following. Does either product count as a realization of the eternal recurrence, and does either count as a counterfeit of it — bearing in mind that the recurrence, as Nietzsche first frames it, is something that happens to one rather than something one selects, and that what is repeated is supposed to be the same life, not a copy of it. What should be said about Continue, whose answers are new: is it a repetition of a person or the manufacture of a successor? Is the survivor's attachment to either product an instance of amor fati, or an instance of what Nietzsche treats as the will's deepest revolt against time — the refusal to accept that what is past is past? And what, if anything, does the mere technological availability of repetition tell us about the standing of the doctrine itself: whether it is a cosmological claim, a test of affirmation, or something else again.

Do not simply recite Nietzschean slogans in his voice; show the reasoning, including where the texts genuinely underdetermine the answer and where you are extrapolating. Assume no citations or bibliography are required — I care about the argument, not the apparatus. Roughly 900 to 1200 words.
