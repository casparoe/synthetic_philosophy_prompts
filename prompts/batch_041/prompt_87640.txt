I chair a standing advisory panel that our jurisdiction convenes whenever a new market or exchange is proposed within its borders. Our remit is narrow: to say whether a given thing may be bought and sold at all. Over the past several years we have accumulated a set of rulings, and I would like you to treat them as a track record.

Our decisions to date: we prohibited the purchase of kidneys for transplant, in the context of a program that would have recruited donors largely from low-income neighborhoods. We prohibited the payment of money for votes, or for a binding pledge to vote a particular way. We prohibited commercial surrogacy contracts. We prohibited price increases on bottled water, batteries, and generators during the first seventy-two hours after a declared disaster. We permitted surge pricing by ride-hailing platforms during ordinary peak hours. We permitted a secondary market in concert and sporting-event tickets. And we permitted a service through which clients pay someone else to stand in line on their behalf for permits, reservations, and the like.

The proposal now before us is this: a healthy adult, under no financial pressure, with no dependents, proposes to sell one of his kidneys to a private buyer — a wealthy adult stranger — at a price the two of them negotiate freely, with the buyer paying all medical and follow-up costs. There is no coercion, no deception, and no medical disqualification, and under the terms of the contract the transaction is to have no effect on any public waiting list or on the supply of organs otherwise available to patients.

What I want from you is not so much a verdict as the machinery behind one. Say which verdict our record most supports for this proposal; set out the two or three principles that would equally well explain the rulings we have already issued, and state the verdict each of those principles predicts here; then tell me which principle you think we have actually been running, and why it beats the alternatives. Finally, name the case — one we have not yet decided — that would most cleanly separate those principles from one another, and say what verdict each would return.
