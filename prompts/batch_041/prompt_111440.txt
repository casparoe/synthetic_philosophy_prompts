I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, working mainly on political philosophy but drifting steadily toward the philosophy of technology, and I sit in on a seminar on the history and politics of automation. Last week we were assigned a chapter from a recent trade book on the future of work, and the author's account of Marx has been nagging at me ever since, because it is fluent, confident, and I suspect quietly wrong in ways I have not been able to pin down. His summary runs more or less as follows. Marx was the first theorist to recognise that automation is the engine of history. In the *Grundrisse* notebooks he argued that once production becomes fully automated, living labour will cease to be the source of value and capitalism will collapse from the sheer success of its own productivity; Marx therefore welcomed the machine as an emancipatory force and regarded the Luddites as reactionaries. In *Capital* he catalogues the miseries machinery brings under capitalism — deskilling, longer hours, downward pressure on wages — but insists that this indicts the capitalist employment of machinery and not machinery as such, and he looks forward to the automatic factory eventually delivering socialism by rendering the worker superfluous to production. The author's conclusion is that Marx is a technological determinist: the growth of the productive forces drives history, socialism is its inevitable terminus, and class struggle is at best an epiphenomenon of the machine.

What I want is an audit of that paragraph rather than either a defence or a denunciation of Marx. Which claims would a historian of nineteenth-century political economy accept, which are common simplifications that a specialist would wince at, and which would Marx himself have rejected outright? I am especially interested in whether the determinism conclusion follows from anything in the summary that survives correction, and in whether the author has flattened distinctions Marx was at pains to keep — between machinery and its capitalist employment, between a speculative notebook passage and a published doctrine, or between the development of the productive forces and the political transition to socialism. I am inclined to think the determinism charge is unfair, but I cannot tell whether that is a judgement or merely a preference, so please do not defer to my hunch. I do not need citations, a reading list, or a bibliography — I can chase those down myself — and I would much rather have the substance of the disagreements than a list of sources.

Please lead with your bottom line in a single sentence, then work through the summary claim by claim, and close by saying what, if anything, the author gets importantly right. No preamble, and no restating of my question.
