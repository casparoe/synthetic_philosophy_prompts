I'm in a seminar on 19th century social philosphy and I have to lead discussion next week on the section of Kierkegaard's Two Ages (the one that gets translated as The Present Age) where he goes after the press and the public, and I'm stuck on one move he makes.

Here's my paraphrase of the bit I mean, since I'm working off my notes and don't have the book in front of me: he says that in the present age nobody is passionate enough to actually do anything, so instead everyone reflects and observes and talks about doing things; the press is what makes this possible, because it turns any event into a piece of news that everybody can have an opinion about without any of them being involved in it. Then he says the public isn't a group of people, not a crowd, not a nation, not a gathering — it's something he calls a phantom, something that is everything and nothing at the same time, and that because it's nobody in particular it can never be held to anything, never be ashamed, never be responsible, never be satisfied. The individual who belongs to it becomes nobody too — the public swallows the person. He also says the levelling the public does is not simply something to mourn, because it dissolves the false distinctions of an earlier age, and that the real task is for the single individual to come out the other side of it. That last bit is what I don't get.

So: what is he actually claiming the public *is*? Is it just a way of talking about a certain kind of audience that print creates, or does he think its a real thing that does things? And how does a phantom have the causal power he seems to hand it — how does something that is nobody level anything, or turn individuals into nobodies? Also, why is levelling supposed to be partly good, and how does that square with the fury of the rest of the section? I can't tell whether this is ordinary social criticism that happens to use weird metaphysical language, or whether the metaphysical language is doing real work that a normal sociological account couldn't do. Help me get clear on which, and on what he'd say to someone who told him the public is just an abstraction and there's no ghost to be angry at. If it helps, connect it to what he thinks a newspaper reader is doing when they read, as opposed to what a person who acts is doing.
