I am a retired biomedical scientist — thirty-five years in a research institute, most of it on infectious disease — and since retiring I have been reading philosophy with more appetite than method. Please take on the role of a philosopher of science whose specialism is social epistemology, and specifically the epistemology of conspiracy theories. Think carefully before you write, and begin by setting out in your own words, without assuming that I know the literature, the two broad positions that structure the debate, so that we are agreed on what is being tested; only then turn to the case.

The case is the Tuskegee study. The facts I take as given, so that nothing turns on your recall of them: from 1932 to 1972 the United States Public Health Service ran a study of the natural course of untreated syphilis in Macon County, Alabama; roughly six hundred poor African American men were enrolled, about four hundred of them with latent syphilis; they were told they were being treated for 'bad blood'; effective treatment existed from the outset and penicillin was standard by the mid-1940s, yet treatment was withheld; the study ended in 1972 only after a leak to the press, by which time something like a hundred men had died of syphilis or its complications and a number of wives and children had been infected; the disclosure led to the Belmont Report and to the modern apparatus of research ethics, and to a documented and durable distrust of medicine and of government among African Americans. In the same population, and in the decades since, surveys have recorded widespread belief that the government created HIV to destroy Black communities, or that contraception is a plot to limit Black births.

Now the theory. Call the first position generalist: the mark of a conspiracy theory lies not in the truth or falsity of its content but in its epistemic form — it posits a hidden hand, it reads the absence of evidence as evidence of concealment, it is unfalsifiable and self-sealing, and the person who holds it is credulous or paranoid rather than merely mistaken. Call the second position particularist: there is no epistemic kind here worth theorising about, only particular claims about particular conspirators, each to be assessed on the ordinary evidence, and since real conspiracies occur and are sometimes uncovered and officially admitted, suspicion of institutions is not in itself a failure of rationality.

I want the episode analysed through these positions, and the positions tested against the episode. Specifically: identify what in the case is a conspiracy and what is a conspiracy theory, and say whether that distinction does the work asked of it; say what each position implies about the downstream beliefs about HIV and contraception, and whether either can account for the fact that the distrust has a documented, non-imaginary cause; examine the inference from an admitted conspiracy to a further unproven one — is there a defensible evidential principle governing it, what would it look like, and what would it license here; and put yourself, so far as you can, in the position of someone in Macon County in the mid-1970s reasoning about the institutions in question without access to the documents, and say what belief was then warranted and on what grounds. Tell me which features of the episode each position explains and which it distorts or passes over in silence. I am especially interested in whether the generalist's central charge — that such theories are unfalsifiable and self-sealing — survives a case in which the concealment was real, and in whether the label 'conspiracy theory' does epistemic work at all, or is better understood as a way of marking who is speaking rather than what is true.

Conclude with a verdict you are prepared to defend: does the case confirm the theory, embarrass it, or fall outside what it was built to handle, and what further facts would settle the matter? Say plainly where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak. Keep the moral appraisal of the study separate from the epistemological analysis — I take its wrongness as settled and it is not what I am asking about.

Please write in British English, structure the answer under clear headings, and give me no references, citations or reading lists — I want the argument, not the bibliography.
