I've been kicking around an idea and I can't tell if its brilliant or dumb, so I want you to actually work it out for me. I teach a unit on divine foreknowledge and free will in a philosophy of religion course, and every year some student brings up Groundhog Day as a joke. This year I started thinking about it through Molinism and now I can't unsee it.

Quick version of the view, since it's the whole engine here: Molinists, following the 16th-century Jesuit Luis de Molina, say God has three kinds of knowledge. Natural knowledge is of everything necessarily true, including what's possible. Free knowledge is of what God himself has decided to do. In between is middle knowledge: knowledge of contingent counterfactuals of freedom, that is, facts about what any possible free creature would freely choose in any circumstances God might place them in. The strange part, and the whole point, is that these counterfactuals aren't up to God. He doesn't make them true; he finds them true, and then he picks which world to create knowing that only some worlds are feasible. So God's power over free creatures has a ceiling: he can arrange the circumstances, but he can't make a creature freely do anything, because if he made it happen it wouldn't be free. The big objection is the grounding one -- what makes those counterfactuals true in the first place, if not God's will and not the creature's actual choice, since the creature might never exist at all? You can set that aside, I mostly want the reading.

Now the film. Phil Connors relives the same Feb 2 over and over. He learns everyone's lines, learns what makes them tick, and tries to engineer the perfect day to get Rita to fall for him, and it fails no matter how perfectly he arranges things. In the end, once he's stopped trying to engineer her, she chooses him and the loop breaks. Meanwhile he learns the town well enough to be in the right place at the right second for whoever needs him, but the old man in the alley dies every single time and Phil can't stop it.

So: read the film through Molinism, and be honest in both directions. Don't use the movie as a costume for the theory, and don't bend the theory to make the movie look prophetic. Some specific things I'm chewing on. Is Phil acquiring something like middle knowledge, and does it matter that he gets it by trial and error from inside the loop, whereas God is supposed to have it before he decides anything and not by observation? Is that montage of failed seductions a picture of an infeasible world -- a counterfactual of freedom that's just false, so no arrangement of circumstances gets you the free yes you want? What do you make of the old man who can't be saved? And does the ending, where she loves him only once he stops trying to make her, say something real about the limits of power over free creatures, or is that just a self help movie about becoming a better person?
