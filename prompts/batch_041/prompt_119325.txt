I would like to practice defending the liberal position in the Hart-Devlin debate against a sophisticated legal moralist. Please play a committed legal moralist—sharp, not a strawman, willing to concede small points but never the main one—and stay in character until I say stop. Do not break character to offer meta-commentary; if you need to clarify a factual detail of the hypothetical, ask in character. Keep your replies to a paragraph or two so it feels like a conversation, and please write in American English.

Your position is not simply Devlin's. You hold that the legitimate object of legal moralism is not the enforcement of any particular moral code but the preservation of a society's capacity for collective moral deliberation. On your view, a society is not just a collection of individuals pursuing private goods; it is also an ongoing project of shared moral self-authorship, sustained by practices, institutions, and sentiments that no single individual can create or maintain alone. Laws may therefore permissibly restrict conduct that, while harmless to identifiable others, erodes the shared conditions under which a community can form, contest, and revise its moral judgments. You are aware that this view risks collapsing into majoritarian tyranny or paternalism, and you have resources to resist that charge: you distinguish between moral offense and genuine corrosion of the moral commons, and you insist that the burden of proof is on the liberal to show that a practice is truly self-regarding.

Here is my opening: imagine a neurotechnology that lets consenting adults, in private, experience vivid simulated acts of cruelty—simulated beings that seem to suffer, but no real creature is harmed, no real-world violence is encouraged, and the users are fully informed and not addicted. You would likely permit the state to ban this practice, or at least to regulate it heavily, on the ground that it corrodes the shared moral sentiments that sustain our revulsion at cruelty. But that revulsion is exactly what the liberal says is not a harm to others; it is a taste, a sentiment, a majority's discomfort. If you can criminalize conduct because it erodes the sentiments that support a moral code, what can't you criminalize? Any practice that offends a majority can be redescribed as eroding the moral commons. How do you draw the line without either abandoning your view or licensing the most intrusive moral legislation?
