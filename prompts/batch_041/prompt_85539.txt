I am trying to find a name for a pattern I keep meeting in discussions of the technical abolition of suffering, and I have not found one that fits, so I would settle for a careful definition instead.

The pattern is an asymmetry in when a certain kind of value claim is made. Suppose someone proposes a pharmacological or genetic intervention that would remove the capacity for grief, or the chronic dread of one's own death, or the aversive quality of physical pain, while leaving memory, attachment, and the ability to distinguish the harmful from the benign intact. The characteristic reply is that the state in question is valuable: that grief is the price of love, that the awareness of mortality is what gives projects their seriousness, that pain is what teaches care for one's body, that a life without sorrow would be shallow. Now put the mirror-image proposal: an intervention that would *increase* the quantity of grief, or deepen the dread of death, or raise the intensity of chronic pain, defended by the very same goods — more depth, more seriousness, more care. The same speakers do not make that argument, and most of them, when the mirror case is put to them, will say at once that they would not. The goods are never taken to justify manufacturing the state; they appear only when the state is under threat of removal. I notice the same structure outside this domain — for instance in the claim that the possibility of error is what makes inquiry worthwhile, offered only by people resisting a proposal to reduce error — but the suffering case is the one I care about.

Three features seem to me to distinguish this from a general conservative reluctance about change. First, the goods cited are usually ones the removal proposal could in principle leave untouched, and the reply that it would not leave them untouched is asserted rather than argued; the burden of explaining why grief's contribution to love could not be had without grief is never taken up. Second, the objection tends to strengthen as the removal becomes technically more credible: the speaker who is unmoved by ending malaria becomes eloquent about the meaning of mortality. Third, the asymmetry survives being pointed out — the speaker concedes the mirror case and keeps the original objection.

I know several of the neighbours. Sour grapes and adaptive preference cover the person who loses something and comes to disvalue it, but here the speaker stands to lose nothing, and the claim is that the state is valuable, not merely bearable. Status quo bias and the reversal test, as Bostrom and Ord set it out, are close, and the reversal test is exactly the instrument one would use here, but it is a heuristic for detecting a bias in judging where a parameter should sit, whereas this looks like a claim about the conditions under which certain goods are possible at all — a constitutive claim rather than a level claim. Work on post-traumatic growth is descriptive; the tradition that suffering ennobles is a position rather than a name for the move; and system justification and belief in a just world concern the defence of an existing arrangement, whereas this pattern can be run on arrangements that do not yet exist.

Is there an established name for this asymmetry — for a value predicated of a state only in the defensive direction and never in the constructive one? If so, I want to know which of the neighbours it is and precisely where it parts company with them. If not, give me a term and a definition that marks the edges: whether the state must be aversive, whether the asymmetry must be exhibited by a single speaker or may be spread across a tradition, whether the mirror-image case must be genuinely available to the speaker, and how the thing differs from the bare observation that many people believe suffering has value.
