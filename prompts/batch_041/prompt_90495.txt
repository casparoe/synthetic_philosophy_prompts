I am taking a seminar on democratic theory, and after last week's session I ended up in a long argument with a friend who has become an enthusiastic partisan of sortition. His central argument is an analogy he treats as decisive: a legislature chosen by lot would be like a jury. We already entrust twelve ordinary people, selected at random, with the power to decide whether a fellow citizen goes free or to prison; we do not insist that they be lawyers, and we do not require them to spend decades campaigning for the privilege. They hear evidence, deliberate, and return a verdict. If that is a rational way to decide a person's fate, he says, then it is a rational way to decide public policy, and the only reason we have career politicians is that we have never seriously tried the obvious alternative. He uses the analogy to license a strong conclusion: not merely that we should add citizens' assemblies to the existing system, but that sortition should replace election for the legislature, and that term limits are a half-measure which merely rotates the same professional class. He also asks, rhetorically, whether we would tolerate a permanent professional jury; if not, why do we tolerate career politicians?

I do not want to answer him with the usual slogans about expertise and accountability, so I would like you to audit the analogy properly. Give me an explicit mapping of what corresponds to what—juror and voter, defendant and citizen, charge and bill, evidence and expert testimony, judge and speaker, verdict and statute, appeal and election—and then a careful account of where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in the structural features: the judge's instructions on the law, the adversarial framing, the burden of proof, the unanimity requirement, the retrospective and binary character of a verdict, the fact that jurors are amateurs for a short tour rather than professionals for a career, and the fact that a jury decides a case rather than governs a polity. Which of these disanalogies actually matter for his conclusion and which are mere differences of scale? Does the jury analogy support sortition for a legislature, only for certain bodies or certain kinds of decisions, or does it collapse into a much weaker claim? Does it give any reason to prefer sortition over term limits, or does it only cut against professionalization in general? And if the analogy cannot carry the weight he puts on it, what would be a better analogy—citizens' assembly as a statistical sample, as a deliberative poll, as a jury with expert witnesses, or something else—or is the whole attempt to argue from an analogy to institutional design a mistake?
