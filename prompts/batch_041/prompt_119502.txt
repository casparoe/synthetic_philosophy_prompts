I am a second-year MA student in history, and I am preparing a seminar presentation on a question that has been quietly dividing our department: whether historians can, or should even try to, escape the evaluative standards of their own time. The immediate occasion for the fight is a disagreement about how to write about a nineteenth-century reformer whose humanitarian work I genuinely admire and whose pronouncements on race and empire I find repellent; one faction in the seminar wants him "read in his own context," the other insists that contextualising him is just a more respectable way of excusing him. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher of history and write a dialogue between two historians who take opposing sides on this, of roughly equal competence and intellectual seriousness, neither of them a strawman. Please begin by briefly brainstorming, in a short bulleted list, the strongest available case for each position, and only then write the dialogue itself. The dialogue should be genuinely dialectical: each speaker should engage the other's actual arguments, concede a real point at least once, and revise or sharpen a position under pressure rather than simply restating it. The speakers should be able to see that their disagreement may be substantive or may turn out to be partly verbal, and the dialogue should end either with a narrower, better-specified disagreement or with an explicitly articulated stalemate — not with a tidy resolution. Aim for roughly 1,200–1,800 words of dialogue, written mostly as speech with only minimal stage directions, and don't worry about citing any literature; I am after the argument, not the scholarship.

Please build the exchange around the following three distinctions, which the two speakers can treat as shared common ground while interpreting them in incompatible ways. First, a distinction between *evaluative presentism* — passing moral verdicts on past agents by present-day standards — and *agenda presentism* — the apparently unavoidable fact that the concerns, categories, and moral sensitivities of the historian's own moment determine which questions get asked, which sources get noticed, and which silences get read as significant. Second, what I have been calling the *self-exemption problem*: the professional norm against presentism was itself forged in a particular present, largely as a reaction against the progressive, triumphalist history that Herbert Butterfield attacked in his 1931 book *The Whig Interpretation of History*, so it is unclear how the anti-presentist can exempt her own governing norm from the very historicising critique she applies to everyone else's. Third, what I call the *counterfactual-contemporaneity standard*: rather than asking whether a past agent met our standards or his own, ask whether he acted in accordance with the most defensible standards actually available to him at the time — while acknowledging that determining what was "available" to him seems to require precisely the present-laden interpretive judgment the standard was meant to avoid. I would like at least one speaker to press the thought that the second and third points together threaten to collapse contextualism into a covert presentism, and the other to argue that the collapse is only apparent — and I would like the dialogue to make clear what, exactly, is at stake in that dispute, including whether the anti-presentist can consistently claim that escaping one's own standards is both impossible and nonetheless a regulative ideal worth pursuing.
