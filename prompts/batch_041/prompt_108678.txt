Bit of an odd request, and a long setup, sorry. I'm a retired chemist — thirty-five years on catalytic surfaces, never read a word of philosophy until I stopped working, and now I'm in a reading group with two ex-colleagues and we've spent most of this year on just war theory, mostly arguing. Anyway: the village I live in is putting up a small stone this autumn, at the spot in the fields here where a German bomber came down in 1940. All four of the crew died, the youngest was nineteen. It's a joint thing with the German town we're twinned with, so the pilot's nephew is coming over, and the British Legion branch will be there with their standards, and a woman who was nine years old that night and lost her parents, and the vicar, and a good many people who mainly want it to be nice. The parish council asked me to give the address — I think because I'm the only person in the village with books and no job — and I said yes before I worked out what I'd actually be saying.

I've got six minutes, so call it eight hundred words. I can't do the usual thing, the "they were all brave lads, both sides suffered, the real enemy was war" thing, because the two from my reading group will be in the third row and they'll know I'm dodging. What I can't get round is whether those four were doing anything wrong. There's a doctrine — I think it's called the moral equality of combatants — that says they weren't: a man fighting for his country, even for a bad cause, isn't a criminal, he has the same licence to kill as the man shooting at him, so the lad who shot them down over our field did nothing wrong and neither did they. And then there's the other line I keep running into, that this is a cop-out, that if it was plainly a war of aggression then men flying bombing missions in it were doing something seriously wrong, however decent they were personally — and that "he was nineteen" or "he was conscripted" doesn't change that, it only changes how much we blame him for it. I keep fliping between the two depending on who I imagine listening.

So: write the address. Six minutes, spoken register, something I could actually stand at a lectern and read; no footnotes, don't cite philosophers at them, and don't invent quotations from anyone real or lean on a poem — nobody there wants that. But do it in a committed way. First, in a few lines, tell me which of those two views you're writing from and why, and then make the speech actually say the hard thing out loud rather than gesturing at it. If you land on the equality side, the speech has to find something honest to say to the woman who was nine; if you land with the revisionists, it has to find something honest to say to the nephew, who has come a long way and is expecting to be allowed to grieve. And give me two or three lines at the end on where the speech leans on a contested reading of the doctrine, so I'm not caught flat-footed if someone asks me about it afterwards over the sandwiches.
