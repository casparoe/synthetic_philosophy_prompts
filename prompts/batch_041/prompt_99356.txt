A colleague in the department, whose views on mental illness are broadly Szaszian, has spent years sorting conditions into genuine illnesses and what he calls problems in living, and his rulings have been remarkably consistent. He counts as genuine illnesses the dementia of late syphilis and a personality change wrought by a brain tumour; he also counts as a genuine illness a child's sudden onset of involuntary tics and vocalisations, for which no psychosocial trigger can be found, which she cannot suppress, and which runs in her family. He counts as problems in living, and not illnesses, a soldier's hand-paralysis, with no nerve damage, that is present only when he believes himself watched and lifts when he thinks he is unobserved, and a woman's fixed conviction that she has no brain, a conviction that runs in her family, dates from a family crisis, and leaves her otherwise untroubled. The case now before him is a woman who, after her children drown while in her care, develops a profound and unremitting despondency, self-reproach, and inability to eat or sleep, which she and her community understand as grief; it is unaltered by reassurance, suggestion, attention, or ritual, takes the same form in every culture studied, and lifts only with time or with drugs. I had assumed he was applying a criterion of bodily lesion, but his verdict on the child's tics appears to rule that out. Tell me, then, what rule he is in fact running: set out the candidate rules that fit his record, say what each predicts about this case, commit to the one you think he is running and give your reasons, and name the further case that would tell the candidates apart. I want a definite position, not a survey of options, and please write in British English.
