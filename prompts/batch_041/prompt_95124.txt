The question was:

Question 3 (20 points). Dennett argues that a lot of philosophy and science of consciousness is still in the grip of the Cartesian theater, and he offers the multiple drafts model as the way out.

(a) (6) What is the Cartesian theater supposed to be, and why does Dennett think that someone can reject Cartesian dualism and still be committed to it? Illustrate with one example of the kind of theory he has in mind.

(b) (8) Dennett discusses the color phi phenomenon, in which subjects report seeing a single dot that moves and changes color halfway along its path, even though the second dot's color is not presented until the second flash. Explain how the Orwellian and the Stalinesque accounts of this case differ, and explain why Dennett thinks we should not expect any experiment to decide between them. Be explicit about what he thinks does and does not follow from that.

(c) (6) A critic objects: if there is no fact of the matter about when a content becomes conscious, then Dennett's view implies that we aren't really conscious, and that our reports about our own experience are worthless. Explain why Dennett would say this misreads him, and state the positive criterion he gives for a content's being conscious.

The student wrote (this is the whole answer):

(a) The Cartesian theater is the picture of consciousness as a show that plays in one place, at one time, for one audience. Everything that happens in the mind has to be assembled and presented somewhere, and the moment of presentation is the moment the content becomes conscious. Descartes' own version of this was the pineal gland, the point where the immaterial mind meets the body. Dennett's point is that materialists inherit the picture without noticing: they stop looking for a soul but keep asking where and when it all comes together for the subject, so the theater survives as an assumption about the architecture. The multiple drafts model is meant to replace it with many parallel, continuously revised streams of content fixation — no privileged stage, no single finish line, no inner audience. It's worth saying, though, that the theater is ultimately a dualist idea: the audience would have to be something over and above the physical processes, since no physical process could be the one that watches the show. So the critique really lands on Descartes, and a consistent physicalism — one that identifies conscious states with physical states — has already left the theater behind.

(b) In the color phi case the subject reports a dot that moves and changes color midway. The natural reading is that she must have seen the green before it was flashed, which is impossible if the second flash is what supplies the green. Dennett's two options are the Orwellian and the Stalinesque stories. On the Orwellian story, the subject really does have the experience of red first, and the brain revises the record afterward, so the memory of what was experienced gets rewritten to fit the moving dot. On the Stalinesque story, the revision happens before the content ever becomes conscious: the brain holds the materials, fixes up the content, and only then is anything presented, so nothing needs to be rewritten after the fact, because there was never a conscious red-then-green sequence to be remembered in the first place. Dennett's point is that these two stories make exactly the same predictions about everything we can observe, including what the subject says and what she later remembers, so no experiment could ever choose between them. Asking when she became conscious of the green presupposes that there is a specific moment when the content arrives on stage, which is the theater assumption again, so the question has no answer rather than an unknown answer. It follows that a subject's reports can't be treated as evidence about her experience at all: they're just more behavior to be explained, so using verbal report to test theories of consciousness is a mistake.

(c) Dennett isn't denying that we're conscious; he's denying that consciousness requires a single place and time where the content is displayed to an inner observer. What he offers instead is a criterion in terms of influence: a content is conscious to the extent that it achieves cerebral celebrity, winning the competition among the brain's contentful states and going on to have a lasting effect on memory, on subsequent behavior, and on the control of speech and report. There's no threshold a content crosses at a moment — being conscious is more like being famous than like being lit up by a spotlight. So the critic's objection mistakes the rejection of the theater for a rejection of consciousness, when Dennett's actual complaint is that the theater picture is what makes consciousness look inexplicable in the first place.
