I'm in a small reading group that meets fortnightly to argue about science and religion, and last week one of the members — a physicist by training, which is relevant only in that he is difficult to interrupt — set out an argument he claims shows that the fine-tuning argument proves too much. I copied it down more or less as follows.

(1) A datum D is evidence for hypothesis H against a rival H* exactly when P(D|H) exceeds P(D|H*), and the strength of the evidence is a function of the size of the ratio. (2) Under the chance hypothesis, the constants of physics take their actual values as the upshot of a process that can be treated as random over the range of values they might have had. (3) The values that permit complex chemistry, stable stars, and anything we would recognize as structure occupy a vanishingly small fraction of that range, so on the chance hypothesis the observed values are fantastically improbable, whereas a designer who wanted a universe with life in it could be expected to produce them with something close to certainty. (4) So the observed values are overwhelming evidence of design. (5) The same reasoning can be run for any outcome whatever, since under the chance hypothesis every outcome is fantastically improbable, and for every outcome there is a corresponding design hypothesis — a designer who wanted precisely that outcome — under which it is probable. So we obtain equally overwhelming evidence for a designer who wanted life, for a designer who wanted a dead universe of hydrogen and nothing else, for a designer who wanted this exact distribution of galaxies and no one to see it, and so on without end. (6) These conclusions cannot all be correct, and the friend says that none of them can be dismissed without dismissing the argument for life along with them. (7) Therefore the inference from (3) to (4) is unreliable and the fine-tuning argument fails. He is also impatient with the anthropic reply: he says that if we could not have observed anything but a life-permitting universe, then the life-permitting character of the constants is not surprising, which is not a rescue of the fine-tuning argument but a second way of killing it, and that in any case it cannot be right to explain away data by appealing to our own existence while continuing to use that same data as evidence.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict on the conclusion. Tell me which step I should give up, and be specific about why it looked so good before I gave it up — I am looking for the kind of answer a good referee would give, one that names the step and then prices the alternatives, rather than an answer that simply labels the argument with the name of a fallacy and stops. I would rather keep (1) than not, and I am not prepared to say that the values of the constants are evidentially inert; so if the step I must abandon is one of those two, I want an honest accounting of what it costs, and whether the same bill comes due for the ordinary fine-tuning argument even if the friend's reductio is sound. If you think the argument is invalid rather than valid-with-costly-premises, say where the invalidity lies and what turns on it, and say briefly whether the same objection, whatever it is, also tells against the multiverse as a rival to design — since that is the move another member makes whenever the conversation reaches this point, and I would like to know whether he is buying the same thing he is selling.
