Here's what she turned in: "The three sentences all have the same shape: the speaker says something about the weather and then says something about her own mind. None of them is false the way 'it's raining and it isn't raining' is false. Take (A). I could just be wrong about the weather, so 'it's raining but I don't believe it' can be perfectly true -- I'm out of the loop, that's all. What's wrong with it isn't in what's said but in the saying. If you assert that it's raining, you've expressed your belief that it's raining, so you can't turn around and say you don't have that belief. The two halves don't contradict each other, but one speaker can't put them both forward in the same breath. That's why the oddity evaporates as soon as you change the context: 'if it's raining but I don't believe it, my umbrella is a lucky accident' is fine, and so is 'it was raining but I didn't believe it', which shows the whole thing is tied to the present tense and the first person. (B) is a different animal -- that one is a flat contradiction, because if I believe it isn't raining then as far as I'm concerned it isn't raining, so I can't be asserting the opposite in the same breath. (C) is really just (A) again and gets the same explanation: knowledge implies belief, so someone who says 'it's raining but I don't know it's raining' is denying the very belief she expressed by saying the first half. The moral is that the oddity is entirely a fact about assertion and not about belief at all. A silent thinker could believe the whole conjunction without any problem; she just can't say it out loud."
