I am designing a seventy-five-minute session for a mixed reading group, half humanities graduate students and half engineers, on the four kinds of minds usually distinguished as Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures. They know a little about evolution and reinforcement learning, but they have never encountered this taxonomy, and they will arrive assuming it is a ladder of intelligence running from instinct to culture, with each later kind simply smarter than the one before. I do not want you to explain the four kinds to me; I want a teaching plan for how I should explain them. Give me the order in which the components must be introduced so that each next distinction feels forced rather than merely listed: what has to be in place before the difference between a Skinnerian and a Popperian creature can be understood, what has to be in place before the Gregorian idea makes sense, and where the whole sequence would break if I reversed two steps. Then choose the one carrying example that should run through the entire session, not four separate illustrations, and tell me what that example must contain and what it must lack at each stage. I am tempted by a foraging animal that learns by trial and error and then by a human using a calculator, but I suspect the example needs to be more carefully chosen; tell me whether either works or what would work better, and explain why a rat in a Skinner box followed by a scientist testing a hypothesis and then a person using a spreadsheet would be the wrong way to structure the examples.

I also need the misconception map. Say which misconceptions the group will predictably bring or form, for example that Darwinian means purely genetic and Skinnerian means purely learned, that Popperian requires language or consciousness, that Gregorian is just another name for tool use or culture, that the four kinds are mutually exclusive stages a single organism passes through, or that later kinds replace earlier ones. Tell me exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off. Also identify the misconception I am most likely to create myself by the way I phrase the transition from Skinnerian to Popperian, and give me the wording or move that avoids it. Finally, give me one check for understanding near the end that would show whether they have grasped the dependency structure rather than memorized four labels, ideally a case they have not seen, such as a slime mold navigating a maze, a chess engine evaluating moves, or a corporation adapting to a market, that they must classify and defend; tell me what a good and a bad answer would look like. What should I explicitly leave out at this level and say I am leaving out, so that the session stays honest about its simplifications? Assume I have a whiteboard and no slides, and do not give me readings, citations, or names of papers.
