I have read rationalist blogs and forums long enough to have a standing suspicion of any claim that a technical constraint is value-neutral, and I want to test that suspicion against something concrete rather than against a slogan. The thing I have in mind is the "risk-informed decision" regime that public agencies increasingly run — a child-welfare department, a pretrial services office, a housing authority, an admissions office — where a model scores each case and the agency then acts on the score. I will describe the bundle of policies as I understand it, because I think the philosophical interest is in the bundle rather than in any one piece.

The agency deploys a model that emits a probability, say of a re-arrest or a placement disruption, and intervenes above a threshold. Around that model it has adopted a set of rules that look, individually, like ordinary good practice. Protected attributes such as race and sex are excluded from the feature set. Features that are strongly correlated with those attributes are retained, on the ground that they improve predictive accuracy; the agency is aware that they function as proxies and retains them anyway. The vendor certifies that the score is calibrated — among people assigned probability s, roughly fraction s experience the event, and this holds within each racial group as well. The agency does not attempt to equalize error rates across groups, and when pressed says only that the score means the same thing for everyone, so the disparity in who gets flagged reflects the world rather than the tool. A human caseworker makes the final call and may override the model, which the agency describes as protecting individual judgment; in practice overrides are rare and track the score closely. And the agency reports a single accuracy figure, an AUC, and calls the tool validated.

Two things puzzle me. First, the agency insists it treats each person as an individual, yet its central defense of the tool is a group-level statistical property. Second, it insists it does not use race, while deliberately keeping variables it knows to be proxies. There is also, as I understand it, a mathematical result to the effect that when base rates differ across groups, you cannot simultaneously have calibration within groups and equal false-positive and false-negative rates; so the choice to privilege calibration looks like a substantive moral decision dressed as an accuracy requirement.

I would like you to reconstruct the philosophical commitments this practice presupposes. In particular: what conception of fairness does the bundle encode, and which rival conceptions does it quietly rule out? What view of the moral unit — individual or group — does it commit to, given that it invokes individual treatment and group statistics in the same breath? What does it presuppose about the meaning of a probability assigned to a person: is the score a description of a fact about them, an ascription of desert, or already a decision? What view of the relation between intent and effect does the proxy policy presuppose? And does the human-in-the-loop override presuppose a theory of responsibility that the rest of the practice undercuts?

Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely fits well with; I expect some parts to be underdetermined, and I would rather you say so than manufacture a single hidden theory. Then tell me what would have to change concretely if we replaced the presupposed view with equal-error-rate fairness, or with a strict disparate-impact standard, or with a bare "treat like cases alike" individualism. Note also that a model can satisfy a fairness constraint for each of several protected groups separately while failing it for their intersection, which may bear on what the bundle can claim.

Finally, tell me where your reconstruction is most likely to be wrong: which additional fact about the agency's stated rationale would change your answer, and which step of the inference from practice to presupposition is weakest.
