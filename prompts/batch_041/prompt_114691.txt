I'm a software engineer with no formal background in political science or philosophy, and I run a monthly lunchtime workshop for about twenty colleagues from engineering and operations. Each session is ninety minutes, in one conference room, with paper and a whiteboard and no computers. I want to build a session that lets people feel the trade-off between first-past-the-post and proportional representation instead of hearing me describe it, and I'd like you to design it for me.

What I want participants to come away having experienced, in their hands rather than in their heads:

- That the same set of preferences produces different outcomes under the two rules, and that neither outcome is simply the fair one.
- That the two rules fail in opposite directions. One destroys information at the moment of voting; the other moves the real decision to a bargaining table after the votes are counted.
- That proportional representation is a family of allocation methods rather than a single method, so that the question of who governs can turn on a rounding rule nobody in the room voted on.
- That a party's share of seats and its actual power to decide anything can come apart, because what matters is whether you are needed by some coalition that can reach a majority.

Design requirements:

1. Two rounds on the same fixed preference profile. Only the rule changes between rounds; the participants' preferences do not.
2. Round one should use single-member districts with a plurality winner. Round two should use multi-member seats with proportional allocation. Give me the district map and the ballots as concrete numbers I can hand out.
3. Arrange the preference profile so that in at least one district the plurality winner is not the option that would beat every rival in a head-to-head matchup, and so that two different proportional allocation methods applied to the same round-two ballots give different seat totals. I want the group to discover that the rounding rule mattered.
4. Include one party with a small seat share that is needed by every viable coalition, and one with a larger seat share that is in none of them.
5. Give the group a published poll before each round, so that the first-past-the-post round tempts people into abandoning the party they actually prefer and the proportional round does not. The point is to make the desertion visible to them afterwards.
6. End with a real decision. After round two, the elected legislature must actually settle something by forming a government, and the outcome must take effect before the session ends (I will put a small amount of the team's real budget on the table). Bargaining has to cost them something.
7. Assign participants fixed preferences and roles rather than letting them vote their own views, but tell me how to keep the assignment from feeling arbitrary, because their first accusation will be that I rigged the profile to make my point.
8. No framing talk before round one. I don't want to name the failure modes in advance.

Please give me the rules, the materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, the numbers, the roles, and a debrief that maps particular moments in the session onto the ideas above, including the parts where the two systems diverge. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it in the moment. Give me the worked seat counts and coalition arithmetic so I can verify before the session that the activity lands where I want it to. Finally, be honest about what this activity cannot reproduce — I suspect it cannot show how a rule shapes which parties even form over repeated elections, or capture the geographic side of representation, but tell me what else is missing and what I will have to say myself.
