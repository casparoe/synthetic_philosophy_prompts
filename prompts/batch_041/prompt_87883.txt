I'm a pricing analyst at a mid-sized insurer — my week largely consists of deciding which pool of past policies a new risk should be compared against — and I'm also in a small reading group that has been working through a popular book on risk and probability. For our next session, the member of the group with a statistics doctorate was asked to write a short introduction to the reference class problem, and this is what he circulated:

"Every probability claim about a single event is really a claim about a class of events, and any event belongs to indefinitely many classes — this toss is a toss of a fair coin, a toss by a bored philosopher, a toss on a Tuesday, a toss in a room with a broken clock. Since the frequency differs from class to class, and nothing in the world tells you which class is the right one, the probability of a single event is not merely unknown but undefined. Frequentists, who identify probability with long-run frequency, are stuck with this; Bayesians are not, because they replace frequency with degree of belief, and a degree of belief needs no reference class. The same problem, under the name of the prosecutor's fallacy, is what makes statistical evidence legally useless: whenever a conviction rests on a match probability or a base rate, the number depends on a class the prosecutor chose, so statistical evidence can never be enough to convict. The moral is that 'the probability of this event' is a phrase without a referent."

He's not a philosopher and knows it, but he's a careful reader of numbers, and the rest of the group took the paragraph at face value. Something about it strikes me as too tidy: if the problem really landed where he says it lands, I don't see how my day job is anything other than arbitrary, and I don't think a Bayesian colleague of mine would accept the consolation being offered to her.

What I want is an audit of the paragraph, claim by claim. I'm especially interested in whether the errors are load-bearing — whether the closing sentence survives once the questionable steps are removed — and in whether the legal conclusion is a consequence of the reference class problem at all, or a different problem wearing the same coat. Two members of the group think the paragraph is basically right and that I'm being fussy, so I'd like to be able to say precisely where I disagree instead of gesturing at a general unease. Please take a definite position rather than surveying the options: where the paragraph is defensible, say so plainly; where it is wrong, commit to a diagnosis; and where the issue is genuinely contested, tell me which side you would defend and why. Don't hand me a reading list or a set of names to look up — I want the reasoning itself, plus a short closing statement of what the paragraph would have to say in order to be fair.
