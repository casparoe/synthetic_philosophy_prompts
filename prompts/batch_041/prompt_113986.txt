I am drafting a two-page proposal for a one-year fellowship in the philosophy of science and would like help turning a half-formed suspicion into a proposal built around a precise, answerable question.

The suspicion concerns the reference class problem. The standard telling runs roughly like this: any event belongs to indefinitely many classes, each with its own frequency; nothing non-arbitrarily singles out the relevant class; therefore the frequency interpretation cannot assign a probability to a single case. My sense is that this telling misidentifies both the source and the scope of the difficulty. The source, I suspect, is not frequentism but a demand that every interpretation of probability shares — that a single event have a unique probability — and the familiar escapes (take the narrowest class, the most homogeneous class, the causally relevant class) do not so much solve the problem as relocate it. Narrowness and homogeneity need a measure to be well-defined, and relevance is either circular or covertly causal, in which case the trouble reappears one level up as the choice of causal model.

So the question I want the proposal to make sharp is this: is the reference class problem a defect of some particular interpretation of probability, or a structural consequence of asking for single-case probabilities at all? And if it is the latter, can a causal account of relevance explain why one class is privileged without reinstating the same underdetermination at the level of the causal model?

Please turn this into a proposal that does the following. State the question so that it has a determinate answer, rather than leaving it as a gesture at a family of worries. Explain why the existing positions — frequentism, subjectivism, propensity views, and the more recent causal-model and best-system treatments — leave the question open rather than settling it, and be specific about where each one stops. Set out the method: what I would actually do across the year, in phases, and why this conceptual work rather than, say, a formal decision-theoretic treatment. Say what the possible conclusions are, which considerations would decide among them, and what would count as the project failing or as a negative result still worth reporting. Give an honest assessment of whether the question is answerable in a year and what would have to be cut. Keep it legible to a reader who knows probability but not this literature.

Anchor the discussion in at least one concrete case — the chance that a particular atom decays within the next minute, or the probability that a particular patient responds to a treatment — and bring out the stakes downstream in actuarial practice, evidence-based medicine, and the treatment of the single case in law, so the reader can see why the question is worth a year. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I will add those myself.
