Im in a seminar on Hegel and we've just finished the war material in the Philosophy of Right, and for the essay I want to actually test it on a real war instead of just restating it. Roughly what I take the view to be: war isn't simply an evil, it has an ethical moment, because it yanks a state and its citizens out of the settled routine of civil life and makes them face the finitude of their particular ends, so that property and even life show up as things the state can demand as a sacrifice; a long peace would supposedly rot a people the way standing water goes foul, so war keeps a nation ethically healthy; and where states can't agree and no judge sits above them, war is how the dispute gets settled, particular states being finite and world history passing the last judgment on them. I've picked the First World War, since that's the one everyone throws at Hegel — not the diplomatic origins, I don't care who was to blame, but what the war actually did to the states and the people in them. Two threads I want kept apart: does it fit the ethical-health/sacrifice story, and does it fit the recognition-and-contingency story? A friend insists the second is the real Hegel and the first is a flourish we've all overread, and I half believe her. So tell me where each reading actually gets purchase on the war, where it just rubber-stamps catastrophe, what the war does to the theory — confirm it, embarrass it, or land it somewhere it was never built for — and what the account is simply silent about here (the inward, revolutionary side of that war, say) rather than stretched to cover. Gimme exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no conclusion.
