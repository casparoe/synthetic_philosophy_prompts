I've been going back and forth for months with a friend of mine — Rosa, she has a philosophy PhD and now does policy work, she's read all the value theory and I just lurk on the rationalist blogs — about wether "better than" is transitive, and at this point I'm less interested in the answer than in reverse-engineering the rule she's actually running. Three of her verdicts I'm confident about. The dial: a machine where each turn pays you ten grand and adds one more increment of a pain you can't feel. She says that at every step you're no worse off — she won't say better, she'll only ever say no worse off — and she says the person at the top of the dial is worse off than the person at the bottom, and that neither of those is a slip of the tongue. She also says the person who never touches the dial is being perfectly rational, and that the person turning it up isn't making a mistake at any particular turn. The population one: adding one more person with a life that's just barely worth living doesn't make the world worse; do that a hundred billion times over and she says the result is worse than the small happy world you started from; she says that's the same phenomenon as the dial, not a separate puzzle. The dust specks one: an astronomical number of people each getting one speck of dust in the eye, versus one person tortured for fifty years. She says neither world is better than the other, that the comparison simply has no answer at that scale, and that this isn't a dodge — our value judgments just run out. She does think "better than" is straightforwardly transitive whenever the two options differ in only one respect, and she calls that the boring case.

What I can't work out is which of two things she's actually committed to. Option one: the loop is in better-than itself, the relation genuinely isn't transitive, and that's a discovery about value. Option two: she's been careful the whole time and thinks only the weak relation loops — that no-worse-off isn't transitive, because at each step of the dial the two settings are neither better nor worse than each other (on a par, vague, whatever you want to call it) — which would leave strict betterness perfectly transitive and make her sound more radical than she is. I've tried to pin her down on whether a single turn is ever strictly better, and she just says the money is obviously worth more than a jolt you can't feel, which sounds like a yes, but she won't say the word.

So here's the new case I actually want a call on. Same dial, different currency: each turn pays you $10,000 and raises your chance of dying in the next year by one in a million. I've heard her say, about other cases, that ten grand for a one-in-a-million death risk is obviously worth taking, that the numbers aren't remotely close. I've also heard her say that someone who goes all the way to the top of a dial like that — by which point they're as close to certain to die this year as makes no difference, with a fortune banked — is obviously worse off than someone who never touched it. Both of those are her, in plain words, in the last year.

So: what rule is she running, what does she say about the new dial — does she call it a genuine loop in better-than, or does she say one of her own judgments has to give, and which one — and what further case would actually settle which of the two she is? I want your best guess with the reasoning behind it, not a menu of options. And tell me where you're most likely to be wrong: what would you have to hear from her to flip your verdict?
