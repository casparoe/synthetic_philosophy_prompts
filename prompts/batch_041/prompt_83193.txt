The case. For well over a century paleoanthropologists have disputed whether the Neanderthals — conventionally Homo neanderthalensis, though many writers prefer Homo sapiens neanderthalensis — were a species distinct from us. The salient facts are these. Neanderthals inhabited Europe and western and central Asia and were morphologically and genetically distinct from modern humans, with their lineage thought to have been diverging from ours for several hundred thousand years. Modern humans expanded out of Africa into Eurasia tens of thousands of years ago and overlapped with Neanderthals in some regions; Neanderthals then disappear from the fossil record around 40,000 years ago. Sequencing of the Neanderthal genome in 2010 established that the two groups interbred: people outside sub-Saharan Africa carry on the order of one to a few percent Neanderthal DNA, whereas the signal in sub-Saharan African populations is much weaker. On a strict reading of the biological species concept — species as groups of populations that interbreed, or could interbreed, and are reproductively isolated from other such groups — that interbreeding suggests the Neanderthals and modern humans were a single species; yet prevailing practice has been to treat them as two, and the two lineages remained distinguishable throughout their overlap.

The theory. Analyze the episode through the species problem — the cluster of difficulties about what species are and how they are to be delimited. Among the concepts to weigh are the biological, phylogenetic, ecological, and evolutionary species concepts, and morphological or phenetic criteria; among the positions to weigh are realism, nominalism, pluralism, and the thesis, associated with Ghiselin and Hull, that species are not kinds at all but particular historical individuals — spatiotemporally bounded lineages rather than classes of organisms.

Work in both directions.

First, from theory to case: for each concept, say what verdict it delivers on the Neanderthal question and why, which features of the case it captures, and which it misses or distorts. Be specific about where each one strains.

Then, from case to theory. The episode is often cited as a counterexample to the biological species concept; assess that claim. Is it a counterexample to the concept or only to a particular reading of it — and if the latter, which reading? Does sporadic interbreeding between two lineages that stayed distinct for hundreds of thousands of years tell against the concept, or is it what the concept's defenders would expect? Consider as well the temporal dimension: the biological species concept is applied to populations at a time-slice, whereas lineages change gradually through time, so that no non-arbitrary line may separate an ancestral species from its descendants. What, if anything, does the Neanderthal case show about that vertical difficulty?

Finally, test the following organizing device and say whether it earns its keep: separate the species problem into a semantic question (what the word "species" means), an ontological question (whether species are real, and what manner of thing they are), and an epistemic or methodological question (how species boundaries are to be discovered and drawn). Which of the three does the Neanderthal case actually press on? Then evaluate one specific attempt to dissolve the problem, which I state for you: the proposal, advanced by Kevin de Queiroz, that the competing concepts in fact agree on a single general notion — a species is a segment of a metapopulation lineage evolving separately from other such lineages — and that the apparent disagreement concerns only the operational criteria by which such segments are recognized. Does the Neanderthal case support that unification, or undercut it?

Be disciplined about the mapping — say what counts as evidence and what would count as a verdict — and be honest about the places where the species concepts fall silent rather than forcing them to speak. The analysis should run in both directions, the concepts illuminating the case and the case testing the concepts, and it should close by saying what the episode reveals about the species problem that is not already visible in the abstract.
