I'm a science journalist — I mostly cover mind, brain and the occasional philosophy story for a general-reader outlet — and I'm putting together an essay built around a parlor game I can't stop playing: what would Aristotle have made of functionalism? The setup is that functionalism, the idea that a mental state just is whatever plays a certain causal role in a system, plus its companion claim of multiple realizability — that the same state could be realized in silicon, or in an octopus, or in a Martian's entirely different biochemistry, because what matters is the organization and not the stuff — both showed up roughly two thousand years after he did. I've been chewing this over with a friend who actually knows the corpus, and we keep deadlocking, so I'd like you to work it out on paper for me. Be concrete, and stay inside his own commitments rather than just pinning him as a proto-functionalist or a proto-materialist.

Run two cases through it. First, a creature grown in a lab out of silicon and polymer: no cells, no DNA, but it navigates a room, remembers where the food is, flinches from a bright light. Would Aristotle say it sees? Would he say it has the same soul, or form, as a seeing animal, or only that it mimics one — and does your answer shift once we move from perceiving to thinking, given the way he treats the intellect as something separable and not obviously tied to the body? Second, keep that same functional profile but spread it across a whole city of tiny dumb relays, so nothing anywhere in the system is doing anything mind-like by itself; does he care about the substrate at all, or about the unity of the thing, or about whether it's alive, or about whether it came to be by nature rather than by craft? Somewhere he pictures tools that work on their own — shuttles weaving and lyres playing with no hand to guide them — as a way of imagining workshops that run without workers, and I want to know how far that remark actually commits him. Give me your best prediction, the reasoning that gets you there, the specific spots where the prediction is shaky, and what in his own framework would decide those shaky cases. And tell me where the anachronism is doing damage — i.e., where forcing a 20th-century distinction onto him makes us ask a question he didnt have the vocabulary to have.
