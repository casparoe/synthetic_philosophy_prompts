I have no background in philosophy, but I have read a great deal of popular writing about consciousness, and I have talked myself into a position that I suspect is not quite the standard one. I now think there is no such thing as the felt quality of experience — no inner feel, no what-it's-like — and that what we call consciousness is the brain's processing plus a mistake the brain makes about itself. So far so familiar; I gather this is called illusionism about phenomenal consciousness. But the version I actually hold is narrower than that, and it is the version I want you to attack.

I think the mistake is mostly a mistake of language rather than of perception or of belief. We have a word, experience, and that word comes with a grammatical slot for a subject and a grammatical slot for what it is like for that subject. Because the sentence always has somewhere to put an answer, we assume there is an answer — that there is a fact of the matter, an inner something, that the sentence is about. I think the illusion is the shadow cast by the grammar, and that if we stopped talking that way, the seeming would go with it.

Tell me what would count against that. I am not asking for a reading list — I have no way to look anything up, and I want the arguments themselves. Sort them by how much they should move me, from the ones that would sink the view to the ones that are merely awkward, and tell me which of them people who hold the more usual version of illusionism would themselves accept as real costs.

I am already uneasy about several things. If the mistake is only a habit of speech, why does it survive knowing about the habit? I can know perfectly well that what-it's-like is a grammatical slot and still feel as though there is something it is like for me to read this sentence — does that feeling refute me, or can the linguistic story absorb it? Second, if the illusion is produced by language, then creatures without the word, infants and animals, should not have it, and I am unsure that is true or even what it would mean to check. Third, and this is the objection I fear most: a habit of speech can produce a misleading sentence, but can it produce a seeming? If it cannot, then my view is not a claim about the mind at all but a claim about a word, which would make it a merely verbal thesis — a bad joke for a view that says the hard problem is merely verbal. And if it is only a claim about a word, what exactly is it supposed to be denying?

I would also like to know what my version predicts that the usual one does not, and whether the usual one has some way of handling the cases above that mine lacks. If the whole thing really does come down to how we use a word, say so plainly; I would rather be told that than be flattered.

Please write your answer in American English.
