ok so context: I'm a 3rd-year PhD in legal/political theory (my dept calls it "history of ideas", which I resent) and I've been circling Bentham's writings on sexual morality for way too long. What bugs me is that he gets to a very liberal place — consensual sex between adults isn't the law's business, the unnaturalness objection is just noise, the punishment does more harm than the act — by way of a method that looks like it should license the opposite. I want to write a parody to unstick myself. Could you write it for me?

Form: an official document out of a fictional Benthamite ministry. An inspector's field manual plus one filled-in inspection report, deadpan, ~800 words. Invent the office (Office of Felicific Audit, Bureau of Consensual Offences, whatever you like), give it letterhead, form numbers, the works. The inspectors go into bedrooms and score intimate conduct on the seven dimensions — intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, extent (I might be mangleing the order, doesn't matter). They have, God help them, actually built the hedonimeter that the old economists used to daydream about.

Requirements, because I've seen versions of this go wrong:

- No winking. Deadpan throughout; a reader should be able to tell what's being mocked without you nudging them.
- The jokes have to come from getting the method right, so someone who's actually read the Principles laughs instead of wincing.
- It has to land these without ever stating them: (1) the purity clause loops — an act is impure if it's followed by pain, and the big pain here is the shame that the law and the neighbours manufacture, so the bureau's own output becomes evidence for prohibition; (2) extent — the neighbours' displeasure at merely knowing about the act counts as a real quantity in the sum, so majority taste quietly becomes criminal law; (3) measurement — the manual has to tell the inspector how to put interpersonally comparable numbers on other people's pleasure; (4) the inspection principle, i.e. he designed a prison where the inmates can't tell whether they're being watched, and now the bureau needs its data.
- Consent has to show up as one coefficient among others, not as a side constraint. That's the part I actually care about.
- End in a way that leaves the reader with the live question: does the liberal conclusion come out of the calculus, or was Bentham just a liberal who happened to own a calculator?
- Don't quote Bentham at me, I'm not going to check citations. Invented procedural language is fine and welcome.
