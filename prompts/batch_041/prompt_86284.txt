I'm a chartered accountant — I do statutory audit for mid-size manufactures, so my whole working life is debits and credits, accruals, provisions, contingent liabilities, impairment, write-offs, consolidation, functional currency, the lot. I sat in on my partners comparative-religion course last term and we spent a week on karma, specifically the idea that karma is a theory of moral desert: that what you do comes back to you, that suffering is in some sense deserved because of what was done before, that the debt is yours and it gets carried forward. I found it weirdly intuative because it sounds like bookkeeping, and now I want to know how far that really goes. So put on the hat of someone who is both a philosopher of religion and actually competent in accounting, think carefully, and restate the desert reading of karma as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary — the general ledger, the trial balance, accruals and deferrals, provisions, bad-debt write-offs, consolidation, the accounting entity, closing the books, carrying balances forward, revaluation. Go one mapping at a time and don't cheat by quietly importing accounting's own assumptions (a stable reporting entity, a common unit of account, an external record, a closing date) and calling that a translation. Then give me the residue report: which bits of the doctrine survive intact, which survive only mangled, and which can't be said in accounting terms at all.

The part I most want you to sit with is the entity. Accounting needs an account holder — some legal person whose books these are, persisting from period to period, so that a balance can be carried forward and a debt can still be owed. My understanding is that a lot of the karma doctrines, at least the Buddhist ones, are pretty insistent that there's no such persisting owner, just a causal stream of moments. If that's right, then either the ledger has no owner, which I don't think accounting can even represent, or the doctrine smuggles in a self it claims not to have. Tell me which, and whether the failure is accounting's poverty or the doctrine's confusion. Also say something about merit transfer and dedication — I gather some traditions let you give your good karma away or share it — since that seems to blow up the per-entity account model entirely, and I want to know if it's just consolidation under another name or genuinely outside what a ledger can express.

Before you answer, brainstorm the candidate mappings first and tell me which ones you threw out and why, since I'd rather see the joinery than only the finished cabinet. And be upfront about your confidence: tell me where you're least sure you've got the doctrine right, and what would change your mind — which feature of the tradition, if it turned out to work the way I've described it, would make you say the accounting frame was the wrong translation altogether.
