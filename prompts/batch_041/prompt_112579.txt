Okay, this has been bugging me for a couple weeks and I need someone to settle it. Last month my brother got food poisoning because I didn't tell him the leftover curry had been sitting out all afternoon — I knew, I just didn't bother saying anything, and he ate it. He says I "caused" him to get sick. I told him that's not how it works: I didn't do anything, I just didn't say a word, and you can't cause something by doing nothing. Then he dug up some reading list from an ethics class he took that had causation by omission on it, and now he's walking around like he won the argument. I'm in an intro philosophy course myself and the professor keeps breezily saying things like "omissions can be causes" and then moving on, which drives me up the wall.

Here's why I don't buy it. It seems to me that whole idea is just moral blame wearing a lab coat. If not-doing counted as causing, then I'm "causing" starvation everytime I walk past a food bank without donating, and I'm "causing" my neighbour's burglary because I didn't stand outside his house all night — which is obviously insane, nobody would accept that. So the sensible reading has to be that "you caused it by not saying anything" is really just a fancy way of saying "you should have said something," a judgment about what I owe people, not a fact about what actually made him sick. And honestly the line between doing and not-doing seems completely obvious to me, so I don't see what the big philosophical fuss is about.

So which is it: can an omission ever genuinely be a cause of something, or is "causation by omission" just disguised blame-talk? And if it really is the second one — if the only real content is "you had a duty to speak up" — then why do philosophers treat this as a deep problem worth arguing about, instead of just a straightforward distinction between acting and failing to act?
