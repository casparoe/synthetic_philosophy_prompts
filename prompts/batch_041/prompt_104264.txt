Write a joke whose humour depends on the structure of hinge propositions as described in Wittgenstein's late notes collected as On Certainty. The joke should turn on two connected thoughts: first, that some propositions are held fast rather than known — they are the hinges on which inquiry turns, exempt from doubt not because they have been tested and passed, but because doubting them would leave us without the practice in which doubting makes sense at all; and second, that this status is not permanent, since what serves today as the settled bed of the river may later flow as water, and what flows as water may settle into bed. A good joke here would be one in which someone tries to doubt, or to demand a justification for, something that was never a candidate for justification in the first place — and where the attempt itself, rather than any answer to it, is the funny part.

Two constraints. First, the hinge the joke exploits should belong to a specific practice or form of life rather than to the well-worn examples of the external world or one's own hands: it might be a certainty internal to a trade, a profession, a family, or a bureaucratic procedure — something whose denial would strike those inside the practice not as false but as unintelligible, or as a sign that the denier has stepped out of the game. Second, invent the situation yourself and avoid any stock philosophical example that an informed reader will have met a dozen times; nothing in the joke should require the reader to have memorised section numbers, and no citation or bibliography is needed.

The result should be recognisably a joke, with a setup and a punchline rather than an essay, and it should amuse someone who has actually thought about hinge epistemology: the punchline must turn on the content of the idea, not merely on the fact that the characters are philosophers. Roughly 150–250 words.
