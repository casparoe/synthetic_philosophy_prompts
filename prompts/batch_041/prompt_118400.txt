I want to work out what I actually believe about everyday conventions — the ones that are neither laws nor obvious moral rules — and I would rather have a principle than a pile of verdicts. So I will set out my verdicts on a series of cases and ask you to find what, if anything, unifies them.

Tipping. In the United States, leaving nothing for a sit-down server is wrong, and not merely stingy: I take the server to be relying on the tip in a way that makes withholding it a kind of taking. In Japan, where I understand tipping is simply not done, a visitor who leaves money on the table has done nothing wrong — caused a small flurry of confusion, perhaps, but wronged no one. Yet a Japanese visitor to the United States who does not tip has done something wrong, and I do not accept "it is not done at home" as an excuse.

Escalators. Where I live the convention is that the left side belongs to people walking and the right to people standing. Standing on the left is a mild wrong; it obstructs people who are relying on the convention. A visitor who blocks the left side out of ignorance is, I think, still doing something wrong, though I blame them less for it.

Jaywalking. Crossing against the light on an empty street at two in the morning is fine; I feel no pull at all to call it wrong. Crossing the same way in front of a class of children being taught how to cross is wrong, even though it endangers no one and no one is relying on my compliance.

Conventions that have outlived their point. My club still expects members to rise when the secretary enters the room, a rule whose original purpose no one can now reconstruct. Failing to rise is not wrong, in my view; it is merely eccentric.

Conventions that protect something visible. In a village where water is rationed by custom, a visitor who draws more than the local allotment because there is no such custom at home has done something wrong. The point of the rule is plain to anyone who looks.

Conventions that are only about acknowledgment. Where one is expected to greet the shopkeeper on entering, a visitor who does not greet has, I think, done nothing wrong, whereas a resident who never greets anyone has committed a small wrong.

The case I keep going back and forth on. A visitor cheerfully asks shopkeepers how they are doing without waiting for an answer, which in that country reads as insincere. The locals are genuinely offended. I cannot decide whether this is wrong, since they were offended and the convention was discoverable, or not wrong, since no one was harmed and the offense is the offense of a broken expectation rather than of an injury.

I have a tentative principle, and I distrust it: a convention binds you exactly when it protects some discernible interest of a person present, or when compliance is plainly expected of you and costs you little. Please test it against the cases above. Say which verdicts it saves and which it forces me to give up; note that it seems to cut against my Japan verdict, since the local expectation there is that one will not tip, and not tipping costs the visitor nothing. Then tell me how it rules on the case I am undecided about, and on at least one further case of your own devising that pulls its two clauses apart. If a different principle, or a pair of principles dividing the labor, does better, I would prefer that — stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause and an honest account of the verdicts it cannot accommodate.
