You are a philosopher of science and metaphysician with a research specialism in the ontology of dispositions, causal powers, and laws. I am assembling the specification for a graduate reading group on dispositions, and what I need from you is a carefully reasoned specification rather than an essay. Work through the problem carefully before you write anything, and open with a compact brainstorming section: lay out the live families of view on what a disposition is — the conditional analyses and their various repairs, dispositional monism or pan-dispositionalism, categoricalist and Humean accounts that treat dispositional talk as a gloss on a non-dispositional base, essence- or function-based accounts, and any further options you take seriously — so that I can see the space you are designing the requirements for. Keep that survey brief; the desiderata are the deliverable.

Your main task is to set out the desiderata that any adequate theory of dispositions must satisfy. Treat the list as the object of construction in its own right, not as a preamble to a defence of one view. For each requirement, state it crisply, give the consideration or case that motivates it, and say what kind of requirement it is: a phenomenon the theory must explain, a case it must get right, a distinction it must not collapse, a result from the sciences it must be consistent with, or an explanatory demand internal to the project (for instance, that it account for the modal force of dispositional ascriptions, or for the possibility of unmanifested dispositions). Mark each entry as either non-negotiable — something a theory cannot fail and still be a theory of dispositions at all — or a preference of one camp that a rival may reasonably reject. Be explicit about why the non-negotiable ones are non-negotiable.

The cases and distinctions I expect the list to be answerable to include, at least: finkish cases, where the disposition is removed or reversed precisely by the arrival of its stimulus condition; masking and antidote cases, where the disposition survives but its manifestation is prevented; mimicking cases, where the manifestation occurs without the disposition or the disposition without the manifestation; multi-track dispositions and the relation between a disposition and its manifestations; iterated dispositions, such as a disposition to acquire a further disposition; dispositions whose stimulus condition is itself dispositional; and probabilistic or propensity-like dispositions. Add anything further you think the list must cover, and say what it costs to leave it out.

I also want you to test the emerging list against a case I have been worrying about, because I suspect it sits awkwardly between the standard categories. Imagine a pressure-relief valve disposed to open once the internal pressure passes a threshold; but the housing is made of a material that stiffens as the pressure rises, so that the greater the pressure, the less the valve can actually open. The mechanism that prevents the manifestation is triggered by the very same condition that is supposed to trigger the disposition. Does your list force a verdict on whether this device still has the disposition, or does it expose a gap in the categories of fink and mask? Say which single requirement does the work here, and whether that requirement is doing more work than it should.

Once the list is complete, address two things. First, tell me whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — whether some pair or triple of requirements, each independently plausible, cannot all be met by any single theory. That is the part I most want to see, and if the answer is that they can all be met, I want your argument for that, not an assertion. Second, give a short scorecard in which the families of view from your brainstorming are assessed against the list, with one line each on where each family fails or strains, and a note on which requirement excludes the most candidates.

Write in British English throughout. Use headings and, if it helps, a table for the scorecard, but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring. Do not supply references, reading lists, or citations; I want the reasoning and the requirements, not a bibliography.
