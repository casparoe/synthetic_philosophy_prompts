I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, mostly epistemology, and I'm TAing the upper-level "Knowledge and Skepticism" seminar this term. I've been handed the job of writing the final, and my supervisor wants one 90-minute essay question worth 20 marks on the tracking theory and the closure problem — but she's been very clear that it can't be a question students can answer by reciting the four tracking conditions and then saying "so he has to deny closure, e.g. brain in a vat." (The conditions, roughly: you know p iff p is true, you believe p, if p were false you wouldn't believe it — sensitivity — and if p were true you'd still believe it — adherence — with the whole thing relativised to the method you used to form the belief.) So in Wednesday's lecture she put a repair on the board that she says is her own invention and definitely not in the literature, and she told us not to send students hunting for a paper about it. Roughly: keep the conditions as they are, but when your belief in q is derived from a proposition you know, via an entailment you know (you believe p, you believe if p then q, so you believe q), run the sensitivity test on q while holding the entailment fixed — i.e. ask whether in the nearest worlds where q is false but "if p then q" still holds, you would still believe q. If you wouldn't, you know q. She claims this makes the premise's sensitivity transmit up the entailment, so closure comes back, and (this is the part I'm suspicious of) without handing the skeptic the win.

I also want the question to force them onto a case, because our section spent a week on lucky true beliefs and I don't want a purely abstract answer. The case we worked through: Priya runs a seismograph with a red lamp. The manual, accurately, says the lamp lights only when the sensor circuit closes, and the circuit closes only when the ground moves. She sees the lamp lit and concludes that the circuit closed. In fact it did close — there was a tremor at 4:12. But the lamp has a flaky relay, so in nearby worlds with no tremor the lamp lights anyway (the ventilation rattles it) while the circuit stays open. Intuitively she doesn't know the circuit closed, since she'd have believed it regardless. Build the question around assessing the repair, using that case, and make sure the question paper itself contains whatever of the above the students need, since it's closed book and they'll have no notes.

Can you write the exam question, a model answer of about 700 words written the way a strong student would write it, and a marking scheme of five must-hit points totalling 20 marks, with one line for each point on how candidates typically blow it — plus a sentence on what seperates an A from a B? A couple of things: don't build the scheme around name-dropping or citations, they dont get marks for that and I don't want them rewarded for guessing at page numbers; the marks should attach to moves. And I'd rather the question not be answerable by a recitation of the standard verdicts on the brain in a vat, so make the crux the repair itself — whether it does what she says it does, and whether it gets the Priya case right. Before you draft, please brainstorm the ways the repair could fail in both directions — cases it gets wrong, and things it claims to fix but doesn't — and then pick the ones a good student could actually reach in 90 minutes.
