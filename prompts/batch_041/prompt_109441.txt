I have no training in medicine or philosophy; what follows comes out of reading and a good deal of private brooding, so please give me the analysis itself rather than a sketch of the standard positions.

I should like an episode from the history of medicine read through the two criteria for death that are usually set against one another — the irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory function, and the irreversible cessation of all functions of the whole brain — and then read back the other way, so that I am told what the episode does to the criteria and not merely what the criteria do to the episode.

The episode is the coming of heart transplantation in the late 1960s. Before it, a person was dead when the heart and lungs had stopped. The operation, however, requires a donor whose heart is still beating, so the donor has to be declared dead by another route, and the criterion for that is generally traced to a committee at Harvard that sat in the same period. I do not want the history as such. I want the conceptual work done on it.

Begin with what each criterion actually says about the donors in those early operations. Take someone whose brain has irreversibly lost all function but whose heart still beats because a ventilator is breathing for them. On the whole-brain criterion they are already dead, so the surgeon who removes the heart does not kill them. On the circulatory criterion they are not dead, because their circulation has not stopped — and if you switch off the ventilator in order to satisfy that criterion, it is you who stopped it. I should like that tension spelled out rather than talked around, including the question of what the circulatory criterion is even measuring once a machine is doing the breathing, and where the rule that a donor must be dead before the organs are taken leaves the whole arrangement.

There is a distinction I keep returning to, and I should like you to test it rather than simply take it up. The word irreversible can mean that something cannot be reversed, or that it will not be. The circulatory criterion seems to need the second sense — a stopped heart is often restartable, and we call the person dead because a decision has been made not to restart it — while it presents itself in the first sense, as though it were reporting a plain fact about the body. The same suspicion attaches to the neurological criterion, whose tests are conclusive only once we have excluded the conditions that would reverse the loss. If that is right, then part of what we call death is a decision wearing the clothes of an observation. Say whether the charge is fair, and what follows for the debate if it is.

Press the criteria from the other side as well. Is the whole-brain criterion stable, or does it quietly become something else in practice? I understand that in some places the test is for the death of the brainstem rather than of the whole brain, and that on a third view it is the cortex that matters, which would make a patient in a persistent vegetative state dead, though both of the main criteria count such a patient alive. Tell me which of these is really one view under different names and which is a genuine third position, and what the episode of those early transplants implies for the choice among them.

Then the part I find hardest. If a criterion is settled upon at just the moment when a new technology needs a supply of organs, does that count against it? The criterion may be correct and the timing a coincidence; or the timing may be evidence that we settled on the criterion that suited us. I do not want a verdict produced by instinct. I want to know what would have to be true for the sceptical reading to be right, whether the same scepticism would tell against the circulatory criterion, and whether these are two theories of what death is or two tests for one underlying event — and if the latter, what that event is supposed to be.

Finish where the criteria fall silent, and then give me your own verdict on the debate as the episode leaves it: does the neurological criterion come out of this as a discovery about what death is, or as a convention we adopted because it was useful? Say what the criteria do not settle — whether death is an event or the end of a process with no sharp line in it; whether the question is biological at all, or whether what we are really fixing is when it becomes permissible to stop treatment, to grieve, and to remove organs. I would rather have an honest account of the limits than a tidy reconciliation.

Please write your answer in British English.
