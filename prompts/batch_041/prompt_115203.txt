For several months I have been sitting in a reading group that keeps circling what its members call the strategy-stealing assumption, and one participant has taken to settling disputes with a maxim he repeats in roughly this form: whatever a strategy can win, you can win by taking that strategy. He has used it twice in the past fortnight, for very different purposes. First, he used it to establish that the first player must win Hex, while cheerfully admitting that neither he nor anyone else in the room could describe the first player's strategy. Second, he used it to wave away a worry about competition over advanced AI: if some other lab's approach to a hard problem would work, he says, then that approach is available to anyone who cares to take it, so no lab can hold a durable advantage — the strategy can always be stolen.

I suspect the maxim is doing several incompatible jobs, and I want a menu rather than a lecture. Separate out the precise claims it could be making. Which sense of winning is in play: a win against every opponent, against every computable opponent, a win in the limit, a win in finite time? Which sense of stealing: a proof that a winning strategy exists for me because it exists for you; a computable procedure that converts your strategy into mine; an agent's capacity to reason about a better agent's policy and adopt it as its own? And which structural assumptions are load-bearing: that the game is symmetric, that it cannot be drawn, that an extra move is never a liability, that the strategy is finitely describable, that the stealer is logically omniscient about the strategy being stolen? For each reading, tell me whether it is trivial, contentious, outright false, or the closest true thing to what he actually said.

Then apply the menu. Which reading is needed for the standard argument that the first player wins Hex, and at exactly which step does that argument lean on the premise that an extra stone can never hurt? Take two modifications of the game. In the first, draws become possible: does the argument still deliver a win for the first player, or only the weaker conclusion that the second player cannot win? In the second, an extra stone can hurt — for instance, a stone played on a marked square obstructs its own owner rather than helping, so the extra stone cannot simply be ignored. Does the argument survive, and if it does not, does that show the conclusion is false or only that this proof method has gone silent? Chess endgames in which the player to move would rather pass are the familiar illustration that a move can be a liability; I would like to know how much of the argument's force rests on excluding that possibility.

Finally, say which reading his AI remark requires, whether that reading holds in that setting, and what the nearest true claim is. The questions I care about are whether a strategy's being available in principle to a rival is the same as its being adoptable by a rival, and whether a first mover's head start is an asset or a liability once the second mover can observe it.

Bring the whole thing back as a list I can hand round at the next session: one entry per reading, a one-line verdict for each, and a note on which entries he is quietly conflating.
