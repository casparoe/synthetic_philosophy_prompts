On Thursday our committee votes on whether to recommend that the permitted residue limit for a widely used agricultural fungicide be lowered fivefold. I chair the committee, and I have to circulate a memo beforehand. We are nine members; the evidence is frozen — the same eleven studies and the same meta-analysis, circulated six weeks ago — and no new data will arrive before the vote. Our two most senior members, a toxicologist and an epidemiologist, have read all of it and hold opposite credences, 0.85 and 0.15, that the present limit permits a real risk of harm at realistic exposure levels. Neither has produced an argument the other regards as new; each treats the other's reading as a mistake rather than as a difference in values. Under a rule we adopted last year, every member states a credence in the minutes before discussion begins and may revise it only by identifying a specific study or inferential step previously overlooked. Both have now stated their numbers twice. Neither has moved. A third member has twice invoked Aumann's agreement theorem to argue that the two cannot rationally disagree, and adds that a further result shows that members who keep announcing their posteriors to one another must converge, so the persistence of the gap is itself evidence that someone is violating a norm of rationality. The toxicologist answers that the theorem is a statement about idealized agents, and that real disagreement among competent people is itself evidence about how good the evidence is. Our charter requires the committee to speak with one voice: dissents are minuted but not published, and the regulator has twice overruled us when our recommendation arrived split.

Draft the memo I will circulate before the vote, written for eight other scientists, several of whom will be looking for a reason to vote no. It needs a clear bottom line on the substance (what we should recommend about the limit) and on procedure (whether to vote on Thursday at all, whether to record a split, whether to commission an independent re-analysis, and on what timetable), followed by reasoning a dissenting member could respond to rather than simply restate. In the reasoning, work through each of the following.

- What Aumann's theorem establishes and what it leaves open. In particular: whether what the theorem requires is more than everyone's having read the same numbers in the same circulated minutes; how much work the assumption of a shared prior is doing, and whether genuinely shared evidence is possible when two competent readers weight the same studies differently; and whether a result about probabilities has any bearing on a recommendation that also depends on how much risk each member is willing to accept.
- Why our announcement rule has not produced convergence, given the third member's claim about repeated public announcement, and what that implies about the rule itself.
- The strongest version of the view that each of the two should move toward the other, so that the bare persistence of disagreement is a reason for both to revise — and what the committee gives up if it acts on the majority view regardless.
- One paragraph the dissenting member could sign.

No citations, references, or reading lists; I want the argument, not the literature. Roughly 1,000 words.
