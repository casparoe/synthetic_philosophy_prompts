I'm in the last year of a philosophy degree, taking a seminar on historical explanation, and a friend from the law faculty has been needling me about whether it can ever make sense to blame a country for a war. She thinks the whole notion is a hangover from nineteenth-century nationalism. When I objected that the First World War is the paradigm case in which a nation really was blamed — and that the controversy Fritz Fischer ignited in the early 1960s, arguing largely from documents such as the September Programme of 1914 that Germany's leaders had deliberately sought a war of European hegemony, is one of the most consequential historical debates of the century — she wrote out the following on a napkin and told me to find the error. I have been staring at it for a week and am no longer sure there is one.

(1) To hold a nation responsible for a war is to say that the nation is answerable for the war — that it may be blamed for the war and owes something by way of acknowledgement or repair. (2) Only agents are answerable for anything. A nation is not an agent over and above the people who compose it; whatever a nation does, individual people do. (3) So if a nation is answerable for a war, some individual people must be answerable for the war itself, and not merely for their small parts in a long chain of events. (4) A person is answerable for an outcome only if the outcome was up to her: she understood what she was doing, and things could have gone otherwise had she chosen otherwise. (5) In every belligerent state in 1914 the decision for war was taken by a handful of men, each of whom could say with truth that had he alone acted differently the war would very probably have come anyway; and none of the millions who cheered it, paid for it, or fought in it had it in their power to prevent it. So no individual was answerable for the war. (6) Hence no nation was answerable for it. The clause in the peace treaty that made Germany accept responsibility for the loss and damage caused by the war was therefore not a moral verdict at all — it is nicknamed the war guilt clause, though it does not contain the word guilt — and the historians who maintain that a particular set of men aimed deliberately at a European war are trading on a confusion. (7) And nothing in the argument turned on 1914 in particular, so no nation has ever been answerable for any war.

Two friends offer opposite escapes. One says the argument is sound up to (3) but that groups can be agents in their own right; the other says the mistake is to look for responsibility in persons at all, when it belongs to institutions and offices. I am suspicious of both, partly because the same reasoning would apparently show that nobody is answerable for a pogrom, a lynching, or a century of industrial pollution — outcomes that many hands produced and no single hand controlled — and I am not willing to say that. I would sooner give up (7) than (2) or (4): I am a naturalist about groups, and I do not want to hold anyone answerable for what was not up to them. But I cannot see where the argument slips, or whether it slips at all. Tell me as definitely as you can, and without hedging, which step or inference I should reject — or, if you think none can be, which premise is least costly to surrender — then explain why the offending step looked so plausible, and price the alternatives: what would the position you favour have to say about the treaty clause, and about the historians' claim that a particular set of men aimed deliberately at a European war? I would rather have one defended answer with its costs named than a survey of the options. End with a single final line beginning "Verdict:" that names the step you reject (or "none") and, in a phrase, the repair you would adopt.
