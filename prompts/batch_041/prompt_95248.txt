I'm sitting on our hospital's research ethics committee and also TA-ing a grad seminar on rational choice, and the two keep colliding in my head. Our committee will only approve a randomized trial when there's genuine equipoise — when nobody can honestly say which arm is better — and then we let a random number generator decide who gets which treatment. Meanwhile in the seminar I keep telling students that the whole point of a rational agent is that it has reasons for what it does, and that a choice made for no reason at all is the donkey starving between two identical bales of hay. But that seems to mean we permit randomization exactly where nobody has a reason, which makes the absence of reasons look like a reason, and it makes my own commitee look like it's endorsing the one thing the seminar says can't be a rational act. And the same pattern is everywhere once I started noticing it: schools running lotteries for oversubscribed places, transplant programs allocating scarce organs by lottery among patients who are medically tied, some jurisdictions settling tied elections by drawing lots, hiring committees that say they'll just flip a coin if the two finalists are truly equal.

So what I want from you is a reconstruction of what these practices presuppose. Do they commit to a view about whether choice requires preference — that an agent can act rationally with no preference at all between the options, that indifference isn't paralysis — or do they quietly dodge that commitment by manufacturing a preference (for the procedure itself, or for the outcome of the procedure), so that the lottery is really just the expression of a second-order preference for fairness or for not having to pick? Which rival theories of practical reason does the pattern rule out, and which does it merely sit comfortably with? My hunch is that it's underdetermined, that several very different views would license the exact same coin flip, and I want you to say where, and to keep separate what the practice strictly needs from what we've just gotten used to saying about it. I'm also wondering whether the parts are even consistent with each other: is it the same notion of 'equally good' when we call two candidates tied, two treatments in equipoise, and two haystacks identical? If not, does the practice depend on running those three together?

One more thing I want out of it: if the presupposed view gets replaced — say by a view on which an agent simply cannot choose without a preference, or by one on which any choice is rational so long as nothing else is worse — what actually has to change in the institutions? Does the lottery have to go, does it just have to be redescribed, or does it turn out these practices were never resting on the philosophy I assumed they were? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give me the reasoning; no preamble, no restating the question.
