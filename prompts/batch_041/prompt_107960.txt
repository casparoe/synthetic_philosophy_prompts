I work on the safety side of machine learning systems, and I keep noticing a pattern in how we actually deploy them that seems to rest on some philosophy nobody has ever spelled out to me. Here is the pattern, as concretely as I can put it. We spend enormous effort getting models to narrate their own reasoning, we train them on feedback that is itself partly generated by other models, and we routinely ask them to report their own confidence, their own uncertainty, and their own values. Yet at the moment of deciding whether a system is safe to hand real authority to, we systematically refuse to let it testify about itself. We do not treat a model's "I would never do that" as evidence of anything; we assume the worst about a system we cannot fully inspect; we build monitors, sandboxes, and permission boundaries on the working premise that the model may be acting against us even while it looks cooperative; we deliberately place a weaker but trusted model in the position of watching the stronger one and optimize against that watcher's judgment rather than the strong model's own; and we judge safety by how the system behaves under adversarial pressure rather than by anything it says about itself. So the same words from the same model are, in one part of the pipeline, signal worth training on, and in another part, worthless or worse.

I want to understand what this practice commits us to. What view of the relation between a system's self-report and its actual dispositions does it presuppose? Does it require that whatever is going on inside the model is irrelevant, or merely that it is inaccessible, or does it stay neutral on that and rest only on a claim about what we can verify? Does it implicitly treat safety as a property of the model at all, or as a property of the whole human-machine arrangement, so that the "same" model could be aligned in one deployment and dangerous in another? And is there a coherent story on which the model genuinely has beliefs and values that we simply cannot read off its words, which would justify the same behavior, or does the practice only make sense if we deny those inner states outright?

Tell me what the practice strictly presupposes as opposed to what it merely happens to fit well with, whether its parts presuppose consistent things, and where it is genuinely underdetermined so that several incompatible views would license exactly the same engineering choices. Then tell me what would have to change in how we build and deploy these systems if we adopted a different view of the self-report, one on which a model's sincere claims about itself are weak but real evidence about its dispositions. Please answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, written the way you would explain it to a colleague in an email.
