I'm a second-year PhD student in a PPE-style program, and this term I'm the TA for a seminar whose final unit covers social choice and distributive justice. The piece students consistently misread is Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, because they treat it as a single result rather than a conditional one, and then all the interesting disagreement vanishes into the assumptions. So for the review session I want to hand out a decision tree that walks a student from a starting commitment to a named position, and I'd like you to write it. Please use the following framing so the tree doesn't presuppose knowledge the students won't have. Harsanyi's result, as I will present it: suppose (a) each individual's preferences over lotteries of social states can be represented by a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function; (b) there is a social preference ordering over those same lotteries, and it too satisfies the expected-utility axioms; and (c) the social ordering is indifferent between two lotteries whenever every individual is indifferent between them. Then the social ordering is representable as a weighted sum of the individual utility functions. Everything past that point is where the positions come apart: whether the weights must be equal, whether a social ordering is even a coherent object to posit, and whether the utilities being summed are expected utilities (ex ante) or state-by-state utilities (ex post). To get from the weighted sum to classical utilitarianism, Harsanyi added a further premise, an impartial observer behind a veil of ignorance who does not know which person they will turn out to be and evaluates outcomes by the expected utility of a lottery giving them an equal chance of being each person. That extra premise is doing real work, and I want the tree to make students feel it.

The branch nodes should each be a question a student can answer without already knowing where it leads, and I want them drawn from the assumptions rather than from a list of schools: whether they accept (a), (b), and (c) as stated; whether the impartial-observer/equal-probability device is legitimate or question-begging; whether the aggregated utilities are ex ante or ex post; whether weights may legitimately differ across persons, and if so on what basis; and whether they accept the welfarist premise at all, that what gets aggregated is individual well-being rather than resources or capabilities. At least one node should be built on a concrete case rather than on a principle, so that students answer about the case and not the label — for instance, a single dose of a life-saving drug and a fair coin to decide which of two otherwise identical patients receives it. The tree must reach these leaves even if some are reachable only by denying an assumption: classical sum utilitarianism; weighted utilitarianism where the weights encode something other than equal moral importance; ex ante prioritarianism; ex post prioritarianism; Rawlsian maximin or leximin behind the veil; a proceduralist position that accepts the framework but insists it cannot register a preference for a fair lottery over an unfair one with the same expected-utility profile; a deflationary position on which the theorem is a representation result establishing nothing substantive without the equiprobability premise; and a non-welfarist position that refuses the aggregation of utilities altogether.

Please keep it to about a dozen nodes. Where two leaves land on the same point in the tree, say so explicitly and tell me whether the tree is failing to separate positions that are genuinely distinct or whether the positions are notational variants of each other. Where the tree implies a combination of answers that nobody occupies, note it and diagnose whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has explored it. I also expect the tree to get stuck in one place, and I would rather you flag it than fudge it: ex post prioritarianism appears to require violating one of the very assumptions that generates the theorem, so say clearly whether that leaf sits inside or outside the tree's scope. Be decisive rather than even-handed; I do not want a survey that concludes the answer depends on your axioms, which I can already see. Commit to the leaf you think a careful reader should end up at, and give your reasons for placing them there rather than at the nearest rival. Finish with a final line of exactly the form \answer{leaf name}, naming the leaf you would defend.
