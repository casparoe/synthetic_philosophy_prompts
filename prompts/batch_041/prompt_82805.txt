Write a short story of roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words that dramatizes the no-trade family of results in the epistemology of markets. The relevant ideas, stated plainly: if two agents share a prior, and it is common knowledge between them that both are rational and both regard a proposed exchange as acceptable, the exchange cannot occur; agents with a common prior cannot agree to disagree when their posterior beliefs are common knowledge; and in a world satisfying those conditions, any trade you actually observe has to be explained by hedging, liquidity or risk-sharing motives, differences in endowments or tastes, divergent priors, or a failure of common knowledge, never by two people simply reading the same facts differently. I want a story that takes that seriously as a constraint on plot, not as a slogan to decorate a scene.

Take this setting as given, and do not spend the opening paragraphs justifying it. In a port city, the exchange operates under an old rule: every member posts one number each morning on a public board, their credence that a specified settlement event will occur, and each member knows the others have read the board, and knows that they know, and so on. The posted numbers are identical every day, and nobody finds this remarkable; it is taken as proof that the members are disciplined Bayesians with a common prior. Trade continues regardless. It continues because the board prints credences to two decimal places, and the third decimal is where the market actually lives: it is the sliver of private information that the board is designed to round away.

Tell the story from the vantage of a junior clerk in the board office who has been assigned to a routine precision upgrade. Requirements:

The dramatic engine must be epistemic rather than financial. The clerk's case for full precision should be genuinely good: a board that publishes a number while concealing the number that matters is, on its face, an institution for keeping knowledge un-common, and the clerk should be able to say so with force. Someone with standing in the market should be able to answer, just as forcefully, that the rounding is not a loophole in the exchange but the substance of it — that the institution exists to keep each trader uncertain about what the others know, and that a market whose knowledge is fully common is not a market.

No character may simply disagree with another and have that disagreement carry weight. Every apparent divergence of opinion must be either private, in the sense that each party is ignorant of the other's information, or hedged with uncertainty about what the other knows. Do not let anyone openly agree to disagree; that is the one move the story must not make. A reader who knows these results should be able to audit the story and find no trade that common knowledge would forbid.

Include a scene in which the clerk tries to reconstruct a completed trade from the public record alone and discovers that, on the record, it should have been impossible. Work through the remaining explanations — one party was hedging, one had a liquidity need, one was simply wrong about the other's rationality — and let each carry a cost, whether reputational, moral, or in the clerk's own estimate of the people involved.

Keep the prose plain and concrete. Do not lecture, do not name economists or results, do not use footnotes or citations, and do not explain decision theory in the narrator's voice; the ideas should surface through the board, the clerks' paperwork, the traders' habits, and what people are unwilling to say out loud. End on the clerk's decision and its consequence, with the final beat turning on the difference between disagreement and the absence of common knowledge rather than on a discovery of fraud or a reversal of fortune.
