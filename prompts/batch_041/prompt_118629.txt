I'm in a graduate seminar on the philosophy of history, and we have just moved from Carr and Hayden White to Ranke, which has left me more confused than the sceptics did. Our edition includes the lecture materials for Ranke's universal-history course, and I keep getting stuck on one sentence in the notes. Here it is as it appears there, followed by my own rough rendering, which I don't trust — my German is good enough to know that I may be flattening the syntax:

"[...] wir haben über Irrtum und Wahrheit schlechthin nicht zu urteilen. Es erhebt sich uns Gestalt um Gestalt, Leben um Leben, Wirkung und Gegenwirkung. Unsere Aufgabe ist, sie bis auf den Grund ihrer Existenz zu durchdringen und mit völliger Objektivität darzustellen."

Roughly: we have no business at all passing judgment on error and truth; figure rises up before us after figure, life after life, action and reaction; our task is to penetrate each of them down to the ground of its existence and to present them with complete objectivity.

What I cannot make cohere is the combination. The first clause sounds like a startling abdication — as though the historian were forbidden to call anything false — which is the sort of thing I would expect from a much later and much more sceptical writer, and it sits oddly beside the phrase everyone quotes from the preface to his first book, "wie es eigentlich gewesen". Then the final clause demands "völlige Objektivität", which in ordinary usage is precisely a matter of not letting one's judgments distort the presentation. So is Ranke forbidding judgment and requiring it in the same breath? I would like you to work through the passage closely rather than give me a general summary of Ranke's historicism. Specifically: (1) What does "nicht zu urteilen" plausibly mean in its own context — a substantive metaphysical or relativist claim, a methodological rule about the division of labour between historian and moralist, a theological point about who is entitled to judge, or something else — and what in the passage itself supports or undercuts each reading? (2) What work is "Objektivität" doing there, and is it better read as neutrality of judgment, as fidelity to the sources, or as the sympathetic reconstruction of each epoch's inner necessity? Ranke's line about every epoch being immediate to God seems relevant, though I may be conflating two doctrines that ought to be kept apart. (3) How does the ambiguity of "eigentlich" — which can mean "actually" in the sense of "in fact" but also "properly" or "essentially" — bear on whether the standard charge that Ranke wanted a view from nowhere is aimed at him or at his translators? (4) How would Carr and Hayden White respectively read this passage, and does either of them need to read it uncharitably for the critique to land?

Where you find yourself choosing between readings, say what would decide it — a neighbouring passage from the same lectures, a fact about Ranke's Lutheranism, a fact about the genre of lecture notes as opposed to finished prose — and tell me which single consideration would move you most. I would rather have the argument laid out, with its weak points marked, than a verdict.
