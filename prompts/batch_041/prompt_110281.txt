I'm a fourth-year PhD student in sociology working on social ontology, and in three weeks I give a paper at a small interdisciplinary workshop on social kinds — twenty minutes of argument, twenty minutes of discussion, roughly fifteen people, and a chair who will cut me off mid-sentence and take the first question himself. The paper is done. What I need is the Q&A.

My thesis, compressed. Generations of the Boomer/Millennial/Gen Z sort are real social kinds — not natural kinds, but not merely convenient partitions of a birth-year distribution either. My positive proposal is a distinction I think the literature runs together. An *interval kind* is one whose membership is fixed by a property a person has independently of anyone's classification: birth year is the case in point, since you fall in whatever interval is drawn whether or not anyone has ever grouped you. A *circulation kind* is one whose membership is conferred by the uptake of a public label: what makes someone a member is not only when they were born but that the label is applied to them — by marketers, employers, journalists, and eventually by themselves — and that being so labelled changes what happens to them. I claim that generation talk is a circulation kind systematically misdescribed as an interval kind, and that most of the causal work attributed to generational membership is done by the circulation of the label rather than by the birth interval. Three corollaries: generational explanation should take the label, not the birth year, as its explanatory variable; the label's content is fixed partly by the interests of whoever circulates it, so generational attributes should be expected to track market and managerial needs rather than to be stable psychological profiles; and two societies with the same birth-cohort structure could have different generations, one having a generation where the other has only a cohort. I propose to test the last of these by comparing two societies with similar cohort structure and very different degrees of label circulation, and I claim that comparison is what discriminates my view from the interval view.

The audience. Four people will do most of the damage. A formal demographer, quantitative, who uses cohort as a variable in life-course models and regards generation as a folk category contaminating cohort analysis; for her the real structure is age, period, and cohort, and the identification problem among them is the whole story — she has no interest in my metaphysics and will treat circulation as an unmeasured confound. A cultural historian of the twentieth century who uses generation interpretively, as a name for a shared formative experience; she will hear label-first realism as reducing something lived to something marketed, and will accuse me both of reifying a periodization and of dissolving the very thing I claim to defend. A philosopher of social science who works on interactive or looping kinds — categories that alter their members by being applied to them — and who will ask what work circulation does that the looping idea does not already do, and whether my distinction is a distinction with a difference. And a sociologist of quantification who studies how categories become official by being embedded in surveys, instruments, and HR software; he will press on mechanism and timing, and on whether the label does the causal work or is downstream of changes that also produce the cohort. The chair, a demographer, takes the first question.

What I want is a Q&A prep sheet: not a defence of my view, not a literature review, and no citations, reading lists, or pointers to who has said what — I know the literature and I will not have a browser in the room. Give me the questions this room will actually ask, ordered by how much damage they would do to the paper, each phrased the way that particular objector would phrase it, in her vocabulary and with her presuppositions showing. For each, a two-minute reply, a note on which part of the reply is soft and will not survive a follow-up, and an explicit statement of what I should concede up front rather than defend. Separate the objections that are about the metaphysics from those that are about identification and measurement, since my replies to the two sorts are different in kind, and mark the ones where the honest answer is that my paper does not settle the question and I should say so. Include the objection that is unlikely to be raised but would be fatal if it landed and I fumbled it, and the question I am hoping nobody asks. Finally, tell me where two of these objectors would disagree with each other in a way I could exploit — the demographer and the cultural historian, say — and what to say if I am asked to referee their fight in front of the room. Write it so I can scan it in the ten minutes before I go on.
