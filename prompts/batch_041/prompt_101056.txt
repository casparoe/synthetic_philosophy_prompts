I'm a PhD student in the history and philosophy of economics, and I'm stuck on a dispute I can't get to the bottom of. On one side stand the readers of Keynes's Treatise on Probability who take it seriously as a theory: probability is a logical relation of partial entailment holding between a proposition and a body of evidence, it is objective and independent of anyone's beliefs, and over and above a probability's value there is its weight — how much evidence the relation rests on — so that two hypotheses can carry the same probability and still differ in how well supported they are. On the other side stand the subjectivists in the Ramsey–de Finetti–Savage tradition: probability just is a degree of belief, coherence is the only genuine constraint, and the logical relation and the weight of argument are at best colourful ways of talking about features of an agent's credences. The two camps have been at this since the 1920s. Everyone knows everyone's arguments — Ramsey's objection that there is no such relation to be found, the subjectivist reply that anything a logical relation can do a credence function can do, the Keynesian counter that this simply redescribes the problem. Nobody has moved, and the General Theory's talk of irreducible uncertainty, where no calculable probability exists at all, seems to make the standoff worse rather than better, because now the disagreement is not only about what probability is but about whether everything is probability-shaped in the first place.

Please act as a philosopher of science and decision theorist with a good ear for when a dispute is merely verbal, and give me a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Concretely: is this a substantive disagreement about the nature of probability, a verbal one about the word 'probability', a disagreement about what a theory of probability is for (a logic of evidential support versus a theory of rational betting), or a disagreement about whether 'non-measurable uncertainty' is even a coherent category? My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, not a conclusion — is that the fault line is not objectivism versus subjectivism but a disagreement about what probability attaches to, propositions-relative-to-evidence or the dispositions of an agent, and that the concept of weight is the tell: it only makes sense if probability is about the relation rather than the person. A friend in my reading group has pushed this further and suggested that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading, and that the Keynesian program is really a theory of evidential support that has been mistaken for, and then defended as, a theory of rational action. Please start by laying out each position in its own terms so I can see you have got it before you start cutting, then tell me which of these diagnoses (or which combination) the evidence actually supports, what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — and whether any of this is the kind of dispute that could in principle be settled at all, or whether it is a permanent fork between two legitimate projects.
