A colleague in my decision theory reading group keeps insisting that the Pasadena game is just the St. Petersburg game with a sign flip, and that this settles the matter: whatever machinery we use to tame St. Petersburg — capping utility, discounting large payoffs, weighting small probabilities — should carry straight over to Pasadena, so the failure of the expected-value series to converge in the usual sense is a curiosity about the arithmetic rather than a new challenge for decision theory. I want an audit of that analogy rather than a verdict on the games in the abstract.

Set the two games out precisely enough to expose the structure. In St. Petersburg the expected-value series diverges to positive infinity — a determinate extended value — while in Pasadena the series is conditionally convergent: the payoff on the nth outcome grows like 2^n/n with alternating signs, so the probability-weighted sum can be rearranged to yield any real number, or to diverge, even though every individual outcome has a well-defined probability and payoff. Then give me the correspondence table: what in St. Petersburg maps onto what in Pasadena, and where the mapping is exact. Next, where it breaks. I am especially interested in whether the two games fail in the same way or in different ways — whether infinite expected value and undefined expected value are species of one genus or fundamentally different diagnoses — and in whether the standard St. Petersburg fixes (bounded utility, diminishing marginal utility, probability weighting) actually resolve Pasadena or merely force the series to converge while leaving the underlying order-dependence untouched. Does the dominance reasoning that lets us say the St. Petersburg game beats any finite sure sum have any analogue in Pasadena, given that rearranging the Pasadena series can make the game look arbitrarily good or arbitrarily bad? Which features of the St. Petersburg source is the analogy quietly importing — for instance, that the trouble is a value too large rather than a value absent — and does the target actually have them? Finally, tell me whether the colleague's conclusion survives: is Pasadena a new problem for decision theory or a reskinning of an old one? If the analogy is carrying weight it cannot bear, say what a better analogy would be.
