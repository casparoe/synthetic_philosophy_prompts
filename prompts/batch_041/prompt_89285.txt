I run a 90-minute session for a sixteen-person upper-level seminar that mixes philosophy and biology students, and I want the whole session to be a runnable activity rather than a lecture. The idea I want participants to feel from the inside is the failure of any single-criterion definition of life, together with the stubborn pull of the intuition that there is nevertheless a fact of the matter about which borderline cases are alive. They should collide with that failure themselves, in the middle of a decision they are forced to make, before I name it in the debrief. Design the activity so the difficulty shows up in what happens to them, not in what I say afterward.

Framing. The seminar becomes an advisory panel to a fictional registry that must certify which entities turned up in a survey count as living, for the purposes of a protection-and-regulation treaty. Certification has immediate, stated consequences: an entity certified as living falls under a moratorium on destruction and a research-ethics review; one certified as non-living does not. Every ruling is therefore binding and consequential within the fiction, which is what makes the definitions cost something.

Sequence. (1) Warm-up: split into four groups of four. Each group drafts, on a card, a definition or decision procedure for life and posts it. No prompting toward any particular criterion. (2) Docket: distribute a set of eight entity dossiers, one at a time, each with a one-paragraph description and a designated advocate who must argue for a specific verdict. The eight should be chosen so that between them they break every single-criterion definition the groups are likely to have written. Use: a virus; a prion; a sterile hybrid animal; a synthetic cell with a minimal human-written genome; a self-sustaining autocatalytic chemical set in a vesicle that grows and divides but carries no genes in the usual sense; a flame; a self-replicating digital program that undergoes selection at the hands of its users; and a clonally transmissible cancer lineage that has spread between hosts for centuries. Give each dossier just enough detail that the borderline is genuine rather than a trick. (3) Concession mechanic: after any ruling, a group may amend its definition, but each amendment spends one of three concession tokens, and a group whose tokens are gone is frozen. This is the engine of the activity: it converts the temptation to patch a definition ad hoc into a visible, rationed cost. (4) Roles: a Registrar who reads the frozen definition aloud before every vote and applies it literally; a Treaty Counsel who announces the consequence of each certification; the rotating advocates; and an Ombudsperson entitled to challenge any ruling as inconsistent with the group's own stated definition. (5) Audit: with about fifteen minutes left, check each frozen definition against all eight cases and mark where it contradicts itself.

Debrief. Map the moments onto the idea. The first amendment corresponds to an epicycle that saves a definition by making it vacuous; the token budget corresponds to the felt difference between revising a definition and merely gerrymandering it; the audit corresponds to the counterexample. Surface which criterion each dossier stressed (metabolism, independent reproduction, nucleic-acid information, natural selection, individuality, substrate), and show that no single one survived all eight. Then name the two live responses: that life is a cluster or family-resemblance concept with no sharp boundary, and that the question is partly about a word rather than a fact. Finally, be explicit about the intuition they kept defending — that there is a right answer to each case even when no definition delivers it — and let them sit with the tension rather than resolving it.

What predictably goes wrong. Groups patch rather than rebuild and burn tokens early; freeze the budget from the first round so the scarcity is felt. Someone announces that it is all just semantics and the room deflates; acknowledge the point as a genuine option and immediately require the panel to keep ruling anyway, since the treaty does not wait for the philosophy. The flame is treated as a joke and waved off; have the advocate argue it seriously so the analogy has to be answered. Advocates over-argue toward a verdict the group did not actually reason to, so instruct them to make the strongest case, not the winning one. Consensus pressure silences the minority who think the virus is alive; take a blind vote and reveal the split afterward. Time runs out before the audit; protect those fifteen minutes by capping debate on each dossier.

What the activity cannot reproduce, and that I must supply. It cannot reproduce the empirical dispute over whether viruses and other borderline entities belong to the tree of life, nor the theoretical claim that life is a matter of degree rather than a yes-or-no matter; the treaty forces a verdict and thereby smuggles in a binary that the science may not support. It also cannot make the deflationary position attractive, only available. Flag both in the debrief, and tell the group plainly that the binary they spent ninety minutes enforcing may itself be the artifact worth questioning.
