I'm a final-year undergraduate taking a course on logic and its applications, and a study partner has produced an argument that I can neither refute nor accept; it is now blocking the essay I have to write. I would like you to diagnose it. He begins from a principle he formulated himself, which he calls the reflexivity principle:

(Reflexivity principle) Any rule-governed practice that applies its own rules to its own outputs — a legal system interpreting its own statutes, a moral theory assessing its own verdicts, an artificial agent reasoning about its own inferences — is a formal system in the relevant sense.

He then argues:

(1) By the reflexivity principle, every such practice is a formal system rich enough to encode statements about itself and its own rules.
(2) Gödel's first incompleteness theorem says that any consistent formal system of that kind contains a statement in its language that is true but unprovable within the system.
(3) Therefore every such practice contains a genuine truth of its own domain that it can never establish.
(4) Gödel's second incompleteness theorem says that any such system, if consistent, cannot prove its own consistency.
(5) A practice that cannot establish all the truths of its domain, and cannot establish its own consistency, cannot be a complete or rationally secure account of that domain.
(6) Therefore no formalized ethics, legal code, or computational model of reasoning can ever be a complete or self-justifying account of its domain; every such project is permanently and necessarily unfinished.

He takes this to be a profound result about the limits of any attempt to make a domain rigorous. My instinct is that it proves far too much — it would turn the incompleteness theorems into a licence to declare any rigorous project hopeless in advance — but whenever I try to say which step is illegitimate, each one looks plausible to me in isolation. I would especially like to keep (2) and (4), which I had taken to be straightforward statements of Gödel's results. Tell me which step to reject, explain why that step looked innocent, and tell me what the argument's most defensible descendant looks like once it is repaired. Please take a definite view rather than surveying the options: I need to know what to say in the essay, and I would rather be told where you think the fault lies, with your reasons, than be handed a menu.
