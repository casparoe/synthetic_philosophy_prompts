I am assembling a position paper on the epistemology of knowledge attributions, and before I commit to a line I would like an audit of the view I have put together. Here, roughly in order of my confidence, is what I hold.

1. Whether a person knows something can depend on practical facts about her own situation, and in particular on how much is at stake for her in getting the matter right. Two people with the same evidence can differ in whether they know, if what hangs on the question differs enough between them.

2. The stakes that do the work in (1) are the subject's own practical interests, not the interests of other people who may be affected by what she does on the basis of the belief. A physician's evidence about a diagnosis can be strong enough for her to know, even when a great deal rides on the patient's outcome, provided little rides on it for the physician herself.

3. The truth-conditions of a knowledge attribution — a sentence of the form "S knows that p" — depend on the conversational context of the person making the attribution, and not only on the subject's situation. In a demanding conversational setting such a sentence can be false even when, in a lax one, the very same sentence about the very same subject is true.

4. There is nonetheless a single knowledge relation. "Knows" does not name different properties on different occasions; it names one property, whose extension varies with the subject's situation in the way (1) describes.

5. Knowledge is the norm of assertion and of practical reasoning: one may assert p only if one knows p, and one may treat p as a premise in deliberation only if one knows p.

6. Knowledge does not require certainty. Fallible justification can suffice, so long as it is good enough by whatever standard (1) and (3) supply.

7. If one knows p and knows that p implies q, one is in a position to know q.

I have a nagging sense that not all of this can be true at once, but I cannot locate the fault lines, and I would rather not discover them in the seminar. Please tell me which of these commitments are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be; order the genuine tensions by severity; and for each, state precisely what would have to be given up and what the cheapest repair would cost. I would also like your verdict on a case I suspect my view handles badly: a hiker reads a trail marker, concludes that the footbridge ahead is sound, and turns back because she has no particular stake in crossing; a moment later she describes the bridge to a ranger who is deciding whether to send a rescue party across it. Does she know the bridge is sound, and does the ranger's predicament bear on the answer at all?

Finally, I want a committed response. Do not lay out the options and leave the choice to me; tell me which single commitment you would drop, what the resulting position looks like, and why the loss is worth accepting.
