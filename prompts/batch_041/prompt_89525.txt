A colleague of mine argues that the Holocaust is best understood on the model of a modern factory: it relied on division of labour, bureaucratic routine, technical rationality, and a language of production, and it was made possible by the same processes that built industrial modernity. She uses this analogy to conclude that the Holocaust is not a unique moral rupture but a latent possibility of modern society. I want an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on her politics.

Structure the response as follows. Intended Work (2-3 sentences): what the factory analogy is meant to establish about uniqueness, comparability, and modernity. Mapping (a table): what corresponds to what across factory and genocide, including the roles of raw material, product, waste, management, labour, market, and intention. Where It Breaks (2-3 paragraphs): the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion, especially those concerning the ends of production, the status of the victims, the role of ideology, and the difference between instrumental rationality and exterminatory purpose. What It Smuggles (1 paragraph): the assumptions about modernity, agency, and moral uniqueness that the analogy quietly imports. Verdict (1 paragraph): whether the analogy can support the claim that the Holocaust is not unique, and whether it supports a weaker or different conclusion about modernity; if a better analogy exists for the same historical purpose, state it.
