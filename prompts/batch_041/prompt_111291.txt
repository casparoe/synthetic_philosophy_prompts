I am drafting a short essay on choice without preference and would like critical comments on it. The draft is below.

Draft:

The puzzle of choice without preference is usually introduced with a starving donkey. The donkey stands exactly between two indistinguishable bales of hay, has no preference between them, and therefore dies. I want to argue that the story, as usually told, mislocates the problem, and that once the problem is relocated the puzzle dissolves.

A historical aside first. The starving donkey is not Buridan's image. Buridan's own claim was about the will: that when two courses of action are judged exactly equal, the will cannot break the deadlock, and can only suspend judgement until circumstances change. The donkey is a later caricature of that view, and the caricature is more interesting than the view it mocks.

The standard replies to the puzzle accept its framing and offer tie-breakers: flip a coin, pick arbitrarily, fall back on habit. Each of these assumes the donkey's problem is which bale to eat. It is not. The donkey faces two questions, not one. The first-order question is which bale is better, and by hypothesis it has no answer. The second-order question is whether to go on deliberating, and this one does have an answer. Deliberation costs time, and once the bales are known to be equal, further comparison has zero expected gain. So continuing is weakly dominated by stopping. The donkey is therefore not indifferent after all: it has a strict reason to stop deliberating, even though it has no reason to prefer either bale.

This turns on a principle I will call the satiation licence: when first-order comparison is exhausted, the absence of any further reason to compare is itself a reason to act. The licence does not rank the bales; it authorises the move from deliberation to action. Note that the familiar tie-breakers do no work here. A coin-flip is not itself a reason to act; it is one more thing the donkey would need a reason to prefer to further deliberation. Only if the satiation licence is already in place does the coin-flip help.

I take the upshot to be that the donkey starves only if we insist that action requires a positive first-order preference, and that the real philosophical question is whether having deliberated enough can be a reason. My answer is that it can.

Comments requested: please begin with a single sentence stating whether the central move — shifting from a first-order comparison of the bales to a second-order question about whether to keep deliberating — genuinely dissolves the puzzle or merely relabels it. Then give me the strongest objection you can mount, and say whether you regard the satiation licence as a legitimate principle or as a disguised stipulation. If space allows, tell me whether the historical aside about Buridan is accurate and whether it earns its place in an essay this short. Concrete suggestions for revision are welcome. Please keep the whole response to no more than 450 words.
