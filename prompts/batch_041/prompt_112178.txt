The thesis I'm defending: Lewis's late paper on penal substitution (I never remember where it ran) shows, read charitably, that the structure of the atonement doctrine — one party absorbs suffering so that others don't have to, and we call the arrangement just — is already sitting inside ordinary secular criminal punishment. So the standard liberal move of treating penal substitution as uniquely barbaric, a father beating his son so he doesn't have to beat us, has to either condemn our prisons too or give up the slam dunk. My reconstruction of the Lewis move, which I may be mangling: (a) the attempts-versus-successes sentencing asymmetry is unjust on retributive grounds, and Lewis's fix is to make punishment itself partly a lottery, so the offender faces a chance of suffering roughly in proportion to the chance of suffering he imposed, and can't complain about the chance element because he dealt in chances himself; (b) our real practice of punishing the guilty partly for general deterrence already means the guilty suffer so the innocent needn't, which is the substitution structure; (c) so the disanalogy that matters is innocence, not substitution, and the interesting question becomes whether substitution needs an innocent substitute to be unjust, or whether guilt is doing all the work. If I've got Lewis wrong in a way that sinks the thesis, say so up front — then still write the sheet for the version I just gave, because that's what I'll be defending.
