Write a poem about Hempel's raven paradox that centers on the white shoe and its capacity to disconfirm. The familiar structure is that the hypothesis that all ravens are black is logically equivalent to the hypothesis that all non-black things are non-ravens, so by the equivalence condition a non-black non-raven should confirm the raven hypothesis, though standard Bayesian treatments call the confirmation negligible because non-black things vastly outnumber ravens. The less familiar point, and the one the poem should turn on, is that under some sampling procedures the observation of a white shoe can actually lower the probability that all ravens are black: whether a non-black non-raven confirms or disconfirms depends on how the observation was obtained, not merely on its logical form. Use a short pantoum — four-line stanzas whose second and fourth lines recur as the first and third lines of the next — or a comparable form of strict line repetition, so that a line recurring in a new context changes its evidential valence, the same observation sliding from confirming instance to sign of an unrepresentative sample. The equivalence of the two formulations is not what the poem should question; what it should set in motion is the thought that what counts as evidence for either depends on assumptions about how the evidence was gathered, and it should be accurate enough that a philosopher of science would recognize the moves while remaining clear enough that a reader who has never heard of the paradox comes away with the right picture.
