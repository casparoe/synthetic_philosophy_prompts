I'm making a handout for a small reading group on AI alignment and philosophy of mind, and I want a decision tree that takes someone from 'I accept that HCH is a useful formal idealization of deliberation' to a named position on what it actually idealizes. The group is mixed: a couple of people who know the factored cognition literature, a few philosophers who care about tacit knowledge and the frame problem, and one person who thinks the whole thing is just a restatement of old AI decomposition dreams. The tree should be built from the logical space, not from a list of names. Each branch node should be a question about a specific commitment, phrased so someone can answer it without knowing which label it leads to. For example: does the idealization assume that any deliberation can be decomposed into subtasks that are context-free enough for a fresh agent to solve in isolation? Or does it only assume that the decomposition works for the particular class of problems we care about? Does the human judge at the leaf need to understand the whole problem, or only verify a local implication? Is the recursion well-founded, or does it bottom out in an unjustified primitive? What does the idealization do with the fact that real deliberation involves changing your own concepts mid-stream? And what about the resource constraints: HCH assumes unlimited compute and perfect copies, but does that abstraction preserve the phenomenon of deliberation or replace it with something else? I want the leaves to be positions like 'HCH is a good idealization because deliberation is essentially decomposable,' 'HCH is a useful fiction but not an idealization of human deliberation,' 'HCH idealizes a different thing—collective inquiry rather than individual deliberation,' 'the whole framing is incoherent because it assumes a view from nowhere,' and a few unnamed combinations. For each leaf, one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Also flag any pair of leaves the tree can't really seperate, and note whether an unoccupied combination is empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Keep it to around eight branch questions and six to eight leaves. Don't worry about citations or reading lists—just the tree and the diagnostics.
