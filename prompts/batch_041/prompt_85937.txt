I'm in the second year of a philosophy MA and I've been dragooned into a reading group on normativity that has now spent six weeks failing to get past the wrong kind of reason problem, and at this point I'd like someone to tell me what kind of disagreement we're actually having, because I no longer think its the obvious kind. The shape of it: everyone agrees that some reasons seem to count in favour of an attitude because of what having the attitude would do for you — the demon who'll torment you unless you admire him, the billionaire who'll pay you to think he's a great man — and everyone agrees those aren't reasons of the same sort as the ones that bear on whether the attitude fits its object. But every criterion anyone proposes for drawing that line seems to have counterexamples, and we've split three ways: one camp thinks there's a real distinction and we just haven't found the right formulation; one thinks the distinction is an artefact of trying to analyse value as the fittingness of pro-attitudes, and dissolves once you give up that project; and one thinks it's a verbal dispute about the word 'reason', since a reason to admire him and a reason to get yourself to admire him are different constructions of the same phrase, and the two camps have each grabbed one construction. Since the whole fitting-attitude programme for value hangs on being able to say which reasons for an attitude are the ones bearing on the value of the object, it feels like a lot is riding on a question nobody can state properly.

I've got a hypothesis about the diagnosis and I'd like you to test it rather than just agree with me: I suspect the three camps aren't disagreeing about criteria at all, but about the order of explanation between reasons and correctness — whether an attitude's fittingness is fixed by the reasons for it, or whether we identify the reasons by first seeing which attitudes are fitting — and that each side's counterexamples only look like counterexamples to someone who already accepts their ordering. If that's right, the impasse is a disagreement about what a norm for attitudes is, not about which cases count as data. But I'm not confident, because of cases like this one. I'm a violinist with an audition coming up, and my teacher keeps telling me I'll play well. If I believe her I'll play better, because I'll be less tense, and so believing her makes it more likely that what she said is true. That looks like a reason for the belief that's about the belief's consequences and about its fittingness at once — state-given by one criterion, object-given by another, and neither classification feels right. There are definately a few cases like this in the group's notes (things made true by the attitude, trust that makes someone trustworthy, admiration that improves its object) and we can't agree whether they show the distinction is deeper than anyone thinks or that it was never doing real work.

So: diagnose the impasse rather than taking a side. Is it a substantive disagreement about the norms governing attitudes, a disagreement about which cases count as data, a hidden formal problem with the fitting-attitude analysis, a verbal dispute about the word 'reason', or bedrock disagreement about whether fittingness is a sui generis kind of norm? Say which of these you think is doing the work and why, then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a better criterion, a reason to abandon the analysis, an argument that the right-kind/wrong-kind line is really a cluster of unrelated distinctions, or a case that no progress should be expected here. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. American English, please.
