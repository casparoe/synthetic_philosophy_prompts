I run a 90-minute session for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in social ontology and the philosophy of action; about sixteen students, movable chairs, a plain room, no budget. The session is on joint action, and the knot I want them to feel is whether acting together amounts to anything over and above each person doing their part, each intending to do their part, and each knowing — and knowing that the others know — what the others will do. My students can rehearse the standard positions on demand: the reductionist line that builds shared intention out of individual intentions plus common knowledge, and the non-reductionist line that posits a primitive we-intention or a joint commitment carrying its own normative force. Because they can rehearse them, the positions have gone inert, and I want an activity that puts the difference back into their hands.

Design one runnable session that lets them meet the gap themselves, in stages, before it is named.

Stage one: participants should reach a jointly good outcome while each acts on an independent, self-regarding reason, with common knowledge of the pattern fully in place, so that by the reductionist's lights nothing is missing — and yet the result should not feel like something we did together. They should be able to list the ingredients, see them all present, and still balk.

Stage two: they should form an explicit joint plan and execute it, so that the togetherness is unmistakable; the contrast between the two stages then becomes the thing they must explain. What does the second have that the first lacks, when individual action and common knowledge were present in both?

Stage three: arrange for one participant to act on a private reason that nonetheless delivers their part of the outcome, so the group must decide whether the we survives — and it is here I want the normative residue to surface: the sense that someone who bails on the plan is answerable to the others, not merely disappointing.

Include the rules, roles, materials, a scoring or payoff scheme that needs no real stakes, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes. Give a debrief that maps each moment onto the parts of the problem — the reductionist's ingredients, the non-reductionist's primitive we, and the obligation that seems to cling to joint commitment and has no obvious home in the reductionist picture. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. They should leave able to say what stage two has that stage one lacks, and unable to say it without reaching for something beyond individual intentions and mutual belief.
