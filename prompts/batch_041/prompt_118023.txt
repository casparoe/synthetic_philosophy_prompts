I sit on the clinical competency committee for our diagnostic radiology residency, and I have to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting. Please draft it for me. You're an epistemologist who's spent enough time around clinical training to know how residents actually learn, so think this through carefully rather than giving me a generic AI-is-just-a-tool take. Start by brainstorming on a scratchpad — the competing principles, what's really at stake, what each possible decision would commit us to — and then write the memo.

Background. Two years ago we let residents on the chest CT rotation use an AI reader: it gives a ranked differential plus a short plain-language rationale for each call. The original rule was resident-first — write your own preliminary read, then look at the model. Last July, without any formal vote, we drifted into AI-first: the resident sees the model's call and rationale, then writes theirs. An attending signs off either way, so our liability posture didn't change at all.

The numbers look great. Discrepancies between resident and attending reads are down about a third, and the near misses we actually caught last year — a small pneumothorax, a PE the resident had written off as artifact — were flagged by the model first in almost every case. Alot of what I'm about to say is anecdotal, but this part isn't. Our program director pulled the blinded data: on the quarterly cold-read exams, where nobody gets the model, resident accuracy has dropped every quarter since the switch, and this year's second-years are clearly worse on those cases than the second-years were three years ago. In the exit interviews, residents who got the right answer often couldn't say why the model's rationale was right — they'd say the model is usually right on these, or just rephrase the rationale back at me. One of them said the rationale mostly works as a story that makes the answer feel earned. And when we audited a sample of cases, the rationales were confidently wrong about which feature actually drove the call maybe one time in ten.

Where people stand. A colleague wants to keep AI-first and add what she calls a verification tax: for any finding you accept from the model, you write out independently, blind, the features you'd look for to confirm it. She says that's the scaffold that turns the model into a teacher rather than a crutch. The program director wants to go back to resident-first for everyone, and she admits it will cost us findings in the short run. Our vice chair thinks we're confusing two different goods — producing the right output under supervision, which is what the milestones the accreditors make us report actually measure, versus understanding the image, which nobody has ever measured and maybe can't be.

One more thing that makes this feel urgent: the vendor says the next version, which we'd be on by next fall, drops the rationale entirely and just gives the call plus a confidence number, and it's more accurate. So whatever we decide, we're deciding for about a year.

Write the committee's memo. Give me one concrete course of action with a deadline and a review date, not a menu of options. The argument I personally find hardest to answer is the one for keeping AI-first: if the model is genuinely more sensitive than our residents are, insisting on human-first reads is trading real patient findings for a virtue that mostly benefits the residents. Engage that honestly, and say what we'd be giving up if we reject it. No citations, no literature review — I want your reasoning about this specific case. American English, two pages max.
