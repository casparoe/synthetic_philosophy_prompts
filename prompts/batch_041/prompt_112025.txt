Hey — I'm putting together the take-home for my upper-level seminar and I need one essay question on Carroll's tortoise and Achilles. They've all read the dialogue in class, so I can't just ask them to summarise the regress; the question has to land somewhere the dialogue itself doesn't take them.

Here's the set-up I want to hand them. Tortoise and Achilles are back, and this time Achilles doesn't offer another conditional — he builds a box. The box has two input registers and an output register, and a transition table such that whenever one input holds P and the other holds (if P then Q), the box prints Q, infallibly, for any P and Q you like, including cases where the tortoise is the one feeding it the input. The tortoise looks at the table, agrees it describes exactly what the box does, and then says: fine, but why should I treat what comes out of the box as a reason to believe Q? Achilles says: because that's the rule. The tortoise says: and why should I take that as a reason?

I want the question to force them to say (a) precisely what the tortoise is asking for at that last step, and whether it's the same thing he was asking for in the original dialogue or something subtly different; (b) whether "accepting modus ponens" can itself be a further belief without the gap just re-opening one level up — and if it can't, what the alternative picture is (a disposition, a practice, a piece of hardware, whatever they want to defend), and what that picture buys and what it costs; and (c) whether the regress is actually vicious, or whether the demand generating it is somehow out of order. They should say which way they come down and what would have to be true for the other side to be right.

Then a second, shorter strand: does the same thing happen if we swap deduction for probabilistic updating? I.e. a tortoise who grants the probability axioms, grants that his credences ought to line up with the evidence, and then simply never conditionalises — he endorses every claim about what his credences should be and never actually has them. Same regress, watered-down version of it, or no problem at all? I honestly don't know what I think about that one, so I want them arguing it rather than citing someone.

Deliverables: a model answer of around 700–800 words, then the marking scheme. For the marking scheme I want the specific moves a top answer has to make, marks attached (out of 20), a clear statement of what seperates an A from a B — the moves a B answer will make some of but not all of — and a list of the wrong turns that should cost marks, with the actual mistake named for each. I keep seeing students slide from "the regress never terminates" to "deduction is unjustified", or treat the whole thing as just the Münchhausen trilemma with new characters, and I want those penalised specifically and by name.

Two constraints on the model answer. First, no citations and no reading list — the students already have the bibliography, I just want the moves, so don't hang anything on a name. Second, please be explicit about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on it. If it's disputed whether the regress is vicious at all, or whether the line that accepting a rule is a matter of practice rather than a further premise actually answers the tortoise, then say that it's disputed rather than asserting one side. I definately don't want a contested reading written up as the consensus, and I don't want to grade students down for landing on a live position — so the model answer should make the live bits visible as live.
