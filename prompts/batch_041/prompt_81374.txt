I chair the clinical ethics committee at a mid-sized fertility center. We offer preimplantation genetic testing, and a few dozen times a year a case reaches us in which the question is not whether to screen out a lethal condition but which of the available embryos to transfer, when the differences between them are matters of degree or of value. We have been deciding these ad hoc, and the outcomes drift with whoever happens to sit on the panel that week. I want a written procedure.

The principle it should implement is comparative and defeasible. Among the embryos a couple already has, there is a reason -- some of my colleagues would say an obligation -- to choose the one whose life is likely to go best, where better is understood in terms of the range of opportunities open to the resulting person rather than in terms of any particular conception of a good life. It requires no one to undergo IVF; it does not override the couple's own judgment about what matters; and it never implies that an existing child should have been someone else, because it ranks only the options actually on the table.

The procedure has to be run by a rotating panel of three -- a reproductive endocrinologist, a genetic counselor, and a lay member -- none of whom will have read the philosophical literature, within two weeks, with at most two counseling sessions with the couple. We will also be asked publicly how this differs from the eugenics we repudiate, so our answer to that should be visible in the procedure rather than left implicit.

Write it: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision back to a person because what remains is a question of values rather than of facts. Two design constraints. First, the panel must be able to separate selecting against a condition because of what it does to the life of the future person from selecting against a condition because of what it implies about people who have it; the procedure should be able to block the second without being able to read minds. Second, build in a switch: some of my colleagues think the target is the best available life, others that it is merely a life with a sufficient range of opportunities, and I want that disagreement to show up as an explicit setting rather than as a hidden assumption in the wording.

Then run it on three cases: two deaf parents who want an embryo carrying a variant for deafness; a couple who want to use a polygenic score for a behavioral trait to choose between two embryos, where the score is statistically real in the population but weak and poorly calibrated for this couple; and a couple who want to select against a variant that raises the risk of a treatable condition usually appearing in the sixth decade. At least one of the three should come out in a way I will not like.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind about the core of the procedure -- which finding or argument would make you redesign it rather than patch it -- and where it is most likely to be misapplied, gamed, or to fail the people it is meant to protect.
