I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I have been drafted, reluctantly, into the role of unofficial adviser to a friend — which has left me wishing I had paid closer attention in the one ethics course I took as an undergraduate. My friend, whom I will call Priya, was raised by her grandmother in a large, sagging house that has been in the family for four generations. When Priya was nineteen and her grandmother was dying, the grandmother asked her, in front of the assembled family, to promise two things: that she would look after her younger cousin as though he were her brother, and that the house would never be sold out of the family. Priya promised. Twenty years on, the cousin is thriving and wants nothing from her, and the house has become a genuine burden — a leaking roof, taxes she can barely cover, and a location three hours from anywhere she can earn a living. The family now agrees, with one exception, that selling is the sensible course; the only buyer in prospect is Priya's own brother, which means the letter of the promise could be kept while its evident point was abandoned. Priya has also since come across a letter suggesting that her grandmother, in the 1970s, sold off a parcel of family land in breach of a promise of exactly the same shape.

Her brother's position is that a promise made to a dying woman is a promise made to nobody, and that since the grandmother can be neither helped nor betrayed, Priya is free. Her aunt's position is that breaking it would be a sacrilege, and that the family's sense of itself is bound up in the promise being kept, quite apart from any harm to any person. Priya's own position — this is the part I cannot answer — is that the promise still has a hold on her, and that she cannot tell whether what she feels is a reason or merely an aversion she was trained into as a child; she adds, half joking, that if it turns out to be the latter, she is not sure what to make of any of the promises she has made to people who are still alive to be disappointed. What I want from you is not a general essay on keeping one's word, and not a verdict on whether she should sell the house, but an account of what, if anything, binds her, and what kind of thing that binding is and where it is supposed to reside — given that the person promised is gone, that the reason for the promise has evaporated, that no one now alive has the standing to release her, and that the promisee herself may never have honoured promises of this kind. If she is bound, I want to know what she is bound to do once the promise's literal content and its purpose come apart, and how much weight the family's expectations — expectations that grew up around the promise and would be disappointed by its breach — should carry in that determination. And if she is not bound, I want to know why the same reasoning does not quietly dissolve every other promise she has made.
