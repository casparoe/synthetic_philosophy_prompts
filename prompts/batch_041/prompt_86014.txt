I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of biology — fourteen students, one ninety-minute meeting — and I want to run an activity that makes the units-of-selection problem something they have felt with their hands rather than something they can recite back to me. Take the role of an evolutionary biologist who also designs classroom simulations, and think carefully before you commit to any numbers: sketch the mechanics first, then check the payoff arithmetic yourself, and if one of the phases I describe below would not actually produce the dynamic I claim for it, tell me so and repair it rather than smoothing it over. Two constraints on the framing. First, they should not hear the phrases "group selection," "levels of selection," or "inclusive fitness" until the debrief — no lecture up front, and the rule changes between phases should be announced flatly, as new rules, with no explanation of why I am making them. Second, I am not after the tragedy-of-the-commons lesson about how cooperation is nice; I have a colleague who insists the whole group-selection literature was a long detour and that every one of these cases is gene-level bookkeeping wearing a costume, and I want the activity to leave my students genuinely unsure which of us is right, not comfortably on one side.

Here is the skeleton I want you to develop, with rules, materials, roles, and a minute-by-minute timeline. Divide the class into four groups of three or four; each student has a private score, and each group has a running total. Each round every student secretly chooses one of two options — a self-regarding one and a contributing one — and the payoff table must satisfy three conditions that you should state explicitly and verify: within a group, the self-regarding option out-earns the contributing one no matter what everyone else does; the group's total is strictly increasing in the number of contributors; and the within-group advantage is large enough that contributors visibly decline within about three rounds if nothing else happens. Stakes should be candy or points toward a small prize, never money. Phase 1: reproduction happens only within groups — students' representation in the next round is drawn in proportion to their individual score — and contributors thin out and die. Phase 2: add a group-level event, in which groups whose total falls below a threshold are extinguished and repopulated from the highest-scoring group; contributors should now persist or oscillate rather than vanish. Phase 3: change nothing about the payoff table or the extinction rule, but shuffle a fixed fraction of each group's members randomly between groups at the end of each round; contributors should collapse again. Phase 4: let students choose their own groups, or form groups from students who made the same choice in the previous round; contributors should recover. Phase 3 is the moment I care most about, so make sure the arithmetic really does produce a collapse there for reasons that are visible in the composition of the groups rather than in anyone's incentives, and give me, for each phase, the trajectory of the contributor frequency you expect, so I can tell before I run it whether the phase will work on the day. Include a scorekeeper role, a one-page table I can fill in live, index cards for the secret choices, tokens for scores, and a two-sentence opening for me that does not give the point away.

Then the debrief, which is where most of the value is, and which I want you to write as a script with prompts rather than a lecture. Map each phase onto a distinct claim: a trait's being good for the group versus the group's being a level at which anything is being selected; the difference between a case where the group is merely a vessel whose composition shifts because its members' traits shift, and a case where the group itself is the thing being selected because it reproduces and its offspring resemble it; and the fact that migration destroyed the effect without touching anyone's incentives, which shows the operative variable is the distribution of variation rather than the strength of anyone's selfishness. I also want you to address, honestly and without resolving it too quickly, whether the same four phases could be redescribed in terms of a costly trait spreading because its beneficiaries tend to carry the same variant — discounted by relatedness or assortment — and whether that redescription is a rival empirical hypothesis or just a second way of writing down the same arithmetic; my students should see that the decomposition into within-group and between-group components can be performed at any level, and that this is precisely why the disagreement with my colleague is not a disagreement about arithmetic. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — my guess is that they will moralize, that they will try to bind themselves with promises and thereby smuggle in a within-group enforcement mechanism that is a different explanation altogether, that the student who plays the self-regarding option will be socially punished for it, and that someone will object that my extinction rule is a deus ex machina with no analogue in nature. Say how to head each of those off or turn them into material. And close with a frank paragraph on what the activity cannot reproduce: I am fairly sure it cannot convey that in the real case there is no referee, that the two levels act simultaneously and continuously rather than in alternating blocks I declare, or that the strength of selection at a level is a matter of the ratio of variances rather than a rule anyone sets — but tell me what else I will have to supply myself.
