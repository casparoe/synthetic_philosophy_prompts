I have no training in philosophy and no library to speak of, but there is a puzzle I have been living with for months, and I would like you to write me a poem that makes it felt rather than explained.

The puzzle is about a kind of dependence that has nothing to do with time. Some things are the case because other things are the case — not because those other things happened earlier, but because they are more basic. A pane of glass is brittle because of how its molecules sit; the sitting does not come before the brittleness, it underlies it. Let me call this the in-virtue-of relation: whenever something is so, there is meant to be something else in virtue of which it is so. Now push on it. If every fact holds in virtue of a more basic fact, then one of three things must be true. Either the chain of in-virtue-of stops somewhere, at something that is simply so and so in virtue of nothing further; or it never stops, and there is no bottom at all; or it bends around and rejoins itself, so that the whole chain is what holds up each of its links. A foundation, a bottomless tower, or a circle.

Here is what I cannot get past. Every argument I can find against the circle turns out to be an argument about time — you cannot be your own cause, because a cause must precede its effect — and here there is no time to appeal to. The basic is not earlier, only deeper. So I cannot see why a circle should be impossible; and yet a circle feels like nothing is being explained, because each link holds only because the next one does, and none of them ever touches ground. I want a reader to feel that vertigo from inside it, not be lectured about it.

So: write the poem as a pantoum. In that form the second and fourth lines of each stanza come back as the first and third lines of the next, and the closing stanza folds the opening lines back in, so the poem is itself a chain in which each line leans on the next and the whole thing finally shuts on itself. Let the interlocking do the work of the regress, and let the closing fold be the circle given its full due — not a cheat and not a joke, but the real temptation, the option that has to be felt before it can be dismissed.

Then break the form once. After the last stanza, set a single line apart, one that no later line picks up and that depends on nothing, so that it is genuinely undecidable whether it is the foundation the whole poem was resting on or simply where you, the poet, stopped. That ambiguity is the point, so do not resolve it in either direction.

Two constraints. Do not use words of time — before, then, first, origin, began — because the dependence here is one of depth, not sequence, and the moment the poem sounds like a chain of events it has become the wrong chain. And if it fits naturally, let one stanza quietly raise the question of which way the dependence runs, whether the small parts hold the whole up or the whole is what the parts hang from, and leave that open as well.

Someone who has worried about this should find the three options honestly staged and the circle fairly heard; someone who has not should still come away unsettled and not quite able to say why.
