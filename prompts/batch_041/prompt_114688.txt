I'm a policy analyst who has spent the past year and a half evaluating a guaranteed-income pilot, and I keep colliding with a class of goods that the philosophical debate over what should be equalized seems built to handle but that I can't get to fit. Consider three of the things participants tell us matter most: being the person others turn to in a crisis; having chosen a demanding line of work rather than been slotted into it; and having a job that other people want. In each case the value to the holder seems to depend on how she came to have the thing, or on how many others have it, so that transferring the resources that buy it — or even the opportunity to pursue it — does not transfer the value. I've started calling goods of this kind stubborn: their value is not carried by the resource that normally produces them, and it can be thinned or destroyed by making them more widely available. That last feature is what I find awkward, because the capability approach's central corrective — look at what people can actually do and be, not merely at what they hold — seems to license exactly the broader availability that dissolves the good. I have a working knowledge of the standard positions here (welfarist, resourcist, capability) but no philosophical training, so I may be misdescribing the landscape.

What I want from you is the most important implications of treating stubbornness as a feature of the distribuendum rather than as a measurement problem, specifically for the choice among welfare, resources, and capabilities as the currency of equality. I am less interested in a taxonomy of positions than in what follows from the admission: what each of the three views is forced to concede once stubborn goods are on the table, what every view must give up, whether the debate needs a different question rather than a better answer, and what any of this would mean for how we actually evaluate the pilot. Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
