My old labmate Dave, a physicist, and his daughter's partner, who's finishing a PhD in philosophy, went at it over dinner at my house last month and I couldn't call it. I spent thirty-odd years counting diatoms before I ever opened a philosophy book, so I poured more wine and let them have at it. Roughly how it went:

Dave: there's a theorum of Aumann's that says two people who are rational, who start from the same prior probabilities, and who both know what probability the other assigns to some question — and know that the other knows, and so on up the ladder — can't keep disagreeing. If the disagreement is really out in the open like that, their probabilities have to come out identical. So when two experts dig in against each other, either one of them has slipped a gear, or they're working from different information, or they aren't actually starting from the same prior.

She: all of that holds only for a case that never occurs. Nobody starts from 'the same prior' — I couldn't tell you my own priors, and neither could you — and nobody's opinions are common knowledge in the strict sense, since that means infinitely many levels of knowing, not just everyone sort of knowing. So it's a fact about an idealisation, and it doesn't tell anyone to stop arguing.

Dave: but that isn't an extra assumption you get to drop, it's part of what being rational about a shared world means. If you and I come out differently, the difference has to trace back to something. And in practice we do know roughly what our colleagues think, to within a whisker — I know what my old rival assigns to half a dozen questions to within a percentage point or two.

She: then the honest version of your theorem says we should be within a whisker of agreeing, which is exactly what you see in real disputes — people who differ by a whisker and shout about it anyway. And notice where the work is being done: 'different priors' is the clause you keep having to lean on, and that's precisely the thing your theorem gives you no right to call a mistake. It says rational agents with the same prior can't stay apart; it says nothing about whether they should have the same prior.

So who's right at each step? I definately don't need a winner — I want it move by move, and I want to know what both of them are missing. Under 500 words, please.
