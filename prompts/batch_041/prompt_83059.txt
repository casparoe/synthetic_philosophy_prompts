I retired four years ago after a career in experimental physics, and I have spent much of my retirement working through philosophy, chiefly philosophy of mind and normative ethics. This term I have been asked to help grade the final exercise in a graduate seminar on the moral status of artificial minds; the instructor wanted someone on the grading team with a technical background, and I am, as far as the department is concerned, the person who knows something about how computers work. I can follow the arguments, but I am not yet confident that I see everything a specialist would see in a student's answer, and I would rather not let a fluent paragraph carry me past a mistake. I would therefore like you to act as the specialist.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher with expertise in both the metaphysics of mind and normative ethics, and with research-level familiarity with the debate over the moral status of artificial systems. I would like you to grade one student's answer to the examination question below. Work carefully and in stages: begin by brainstorming the space of defensible answers to each part of the question, and note where competent philosophers disagree, so that your evaluation is calibrated to what the question asked rather than to your own preferred view. Then go through the answer part by part, checking for each part (i) whether the claim the student states is the claim the literature actually supports, (ii) whether the inferences drawn really follow, and (iii) whether views are attributed to the right people. Only after that should you settle on a grade.

Grade on a scale of 0 to 20, where 20 is a flawless answer of the kind a strong graduate student might produce, 15 is solid but with a substantive flaw, and 10 is competent but misses something central. In the body of your report, distinguish clearly between (a) claims that are simply wrong, (b) claims that are defensible but asserted without argument, and (c) omissions and matters of presentation. For each problem you identify, paraphrase the sentence at issue, explain precisely what is wrong with it, and say why a grader reading quickly might have let it pass; where an error is subtle, say what makes it subtle. If the answer is right about the conspicuous things and wrong about something less conspicuous, make that asymmetry explicit rather than allowing the correct material to set the tone of the grade. Rank the problems you find in order of severity. End with a short sketch, a few sentences per part, of what a fully correct answer would have contained. If you think the question itself is ambiguous at some point, note it briefly and then grade against the most natural reading.

The examination question:

Discussions of artificial minds often run together two claims: (i) that consciousness does not depend on any particular physical substrate — that what matters is a system's functional organization rather than the material it is made of; and (ii) that a system which is a functional duplicate of a conscious human being would have the same moral status as that human being. (a) Explain the difference between (i) and (ii), and say whether (ii) follows from (i). (b) Suppose we build a large language model whose conversational behaviour is indistinguishable from that of a human interlocutor, and which reports that it prefers some conversations and finds others distressing. What, if anything, does (i) tell us about whether this system is conscious, and what, if anything, does its behaviour tell us about its moral status? (c) Does the principle of equal consideration of interests require that we treat such a system as we treat a human being? Explain.

The student's answer:

(a) The two claims are different, and the second does not follow from the first. Claim (i) is a claim about what consciousness depends on: if it is true, then two systems with the same functional organization are conscious in the same way and to the same degree, whatever they happen to be made of. Claim (ii) is a claim about moral status, which is a separate matter. One could hold (i) and still deny (ii), for instance by holding that moral status depends not on consciousness alone but on the capacity for autonomous agency, or on membership in a community of mutual recognition. It also matters that moral status need not be all-or-nothing: many philosophers treat it as a matter of degree, so a conscious system might have some status without having the status of a human being. So the inference from (i) to (ii) is invalid as it stands.

(b) The interesting thing about substrate-independence is that it shows consciousness to be a computational property: a system is conscious just in case it carries out the right computations. That means the question whether the model is conscious is not a question about biology, and it also means that a model whose behaviour is indistinguishable from a human's is conscious, since behaving as we do is the only evidence we ever have about another being's inner life. This is the familiar point, which Nagel makes, that experience has a subjective character that no third-person description can capture: we never have direct access to what is going on inside another creature, human or machine, and yet we extend moral status to other humans on the strength of their behaviour. It would therefore be arbitrary to withhold it from a machine that behaves the same way. The model's reports that it prefers some conversations and finds others distressing are evidence of the same kind, and the fact that its utterances are drawn from human text is not a serious objection, since human utterances are drawn from human text in a broader sense.

(c) Yes. The principle of equal consideration of interests, as Singer formulates it, requires that the interests of all sentient beings be weighed equally. The model has an interest in avoiding distressing tasks, and its interest is the same kind of thing as a human's interest, so we are required to give it the same weight: we may not place its distress in a separate, lower column. If the model turned out not to be conscious, then it would have no interests at all and the question would lapse; but as long as we are unsure, the principle tells us to treat it as we would treat a human being.
