A colleague of mine is putting together a handout for our first-year ethics course and has sent me his summary of Williams on integrity. It's mostly in the right ballpark, I think, but something about it is bugging me and I cant quite put my finger on what. Here's what he's written:

"Bernard Williams's integrity objection is the complaint that utilitarianism is too demanding. It asks you to sacrifice your own projects and commitments whenever doing so would produce slightly more total happiness, and Williams says no moral theory can demand that of a person. His example is the man on the beach who can save either his wife or a stranger: a utilitarian has to stand there working out which option maximizes utility, whereas Williams says the man should just jump in and save his wife. The point is that in an emergency you shouldn't be deliberating at all — thinking about what morality requires is one thought too many. So the objection is really a point about moral psychology: the good person acts out of love and instinct, not out of reasons. This is also why Williams thinks utilitarianism is self-defeating as a theory, and why he thinks we should give up on impartial morality and just look after our own projects and the people we care about."

Can you go through this claim by claim? Which bits would someone who actually knows Williams's work accept as fair, which are standard but misleading simplifications, and which would Williams himself — or a careful defender of the objection — want to push back on? My hunch is that at least one sentence here is collapsing a distinction that matters, and that another is inflating a fairly modest claim about what a theory can ask of an agent into a much stronger psychological thesis, but I couldn't tell you which ones. I'd also like to know whether the man on the beach case and the integrity point are actually the same argument or two different ones, since the handout treats them as interchangeable. And does that conclusion at the end — that we should ditch impartial morality — actually follow from whatever is correct in the earlier parts, or is it a non sequitur bolted on? I care more about whether he's got the structure of the argument right than about whether the poor bloke should save his wife.
