I work in software and have no training in philosophy, and I am trying to find out whether there is a name for a pattern I keep noticing in arguments about drug policy. A jurisdiction changes its law — Portugal stops sending people to prison for possessing small amounts for personal use, or a state legalizes cannabis — and afterward both sides quote the same figure. The share of people recorded as using the drug rises: supporters say the law is working, since users no longer have to hide from surveys and clinics; opponents say it is failing, since use went up. Admissions to treatment rise: the same split. A drug is added to standard toxicology panels, and deaths once recorded as unknown cause are now attributed to it. Under prohibition the same thing runs in reverse, with arrests read either as effective enforcement or as proof the problem is growing. Nobody in these disputes seems confused or dishonest; each side has a coherent story, and the figure cannot choose between them. I know about measures that get gamed once people are rewarded for them, and this is not that — nobody is gaming anything, and the problem would survive entirely honest reporting. Nor is it just the warning that correlation is not causation: what strikes me is that the thing being counted changes its meaning at the moment the policy changes, leaving the indicator blind in both directions. Is there an established concept for this? If there is, I want the nearest candidates set out, with an account of exactly where each falls short; if there is not, I want a term and a definition of your own, with its boundaries — what it covers and what it does not. Finally, tell me where I am overstating: what observation or argument would show that such a figure does discriminate after all, and which step in my reasoning is likeliest to be wrong.
