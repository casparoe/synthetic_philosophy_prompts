I teach a 300-level seminar on action theory, 18 students, one 90-minute session, and I want an in-class game that makes them feel the difference between real akrasia and a mere preference reversal over time. The thing that bugs me is that students trot out hyperbolic discounting as if it explains weakness of will, when it maybe just redescribes it -- if my ranking actually flips as the option gets close, there's no knowing-better-and-doing-worse, just a different preference. So I want the game to make them generate both phenomena and then try to sort themselves.

Rough idea, change it if you have something better: everyone gets a wallet of tokens (no real money, just a leaderboard at the end). Then a series of rounds where they choose between a smaller reward now and a bigger reward after waiting some number of turns of the session. Before each choice they fill in a little card with two boxes -- "which is better, all things considered, right now" and "which do I most want" -- and then they choose. They can also spend tokens to "lock" a future choice in advance. Vary the waits and the sizes so reversals actually show up.

What I need: the full rules, the payoff/waiting schedule, the cards, the lock mechanic, a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the akrasia vs. discounting distinction -- including the intrapersonal-game reframing where you treat each time-slice as its own player bargaining with the others, and whether that dissolves akrasia or just relocates it. Don't let the words akrasia or hyperbolic discounting show up until the debrief. Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it (people gaming the leaderboard, "I just changed my mind" denials, nobody buying locks) and how to handle it. And be straight about what the activity can't reproduce -- the first-person felt tug of "I know this is worse and I'm doing it anyway" -- versus what I'll just have to tell them.
