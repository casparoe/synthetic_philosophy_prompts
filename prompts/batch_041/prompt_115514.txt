I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, writing a chapter on causation by omission, and I have run into an argument I cannot get past. A colleague laid it out after a seminar; he takes it to be a clean reductio of the thesis that omissions can be causes. I don't believe him, but every escape I try costs me something I would rather keep. I would like you to diagnose it.

He wrote it as follows.

(1) Counterfactual dependence is sufficient for causation: if Y would not have occurred had X not occurred, then X is a cause of Y.
(2) Omissions satisfy this condition no less than positive events do. Had the gardener watered the plant, it would not have died; so the death depended counterfactually on his not watering it.
(3) Hence omissions can be causes.
(4) No criterion of causation can draw a principled line between the omissions that are causes and those that are not. Whatever feature one might cite to draw such a line — spatial or temporal proximity, the agent's knowledge, a standing duty, foreseeability — is either a feature of our practices of blaming or a feature of the surrounding circumstances, not of the causal relation itself; and a criterion of causation may appeal to neither. (Call this the uniformity constraint.)
(5) Every agent is at every moment not doing indefinitely many things. Take two of my own recent omissions. I spent an evening at the cinema instead of completing the form that would have put me on a bone-marrow registry, and a patient who would have matched me died the following week. I left my car in the driveway one morning, and an ambulance was delayed forty seconds in reaching a heart-attack victim who did not survive. On the reasoning of (1) and (2), each of these omissions is a cause of the corresponding death.
(6) Therefore every agent is a cause of every harm that occurs and that he or she could have prevented.
(7) Whoever is a cause of a harm is answerable for it.
(8) Therefore every agent is answerable for every harm he or she could have prevented.
(9) But I am plainly not answerable for the death of a stranger in a city I have never visited, simply because I did not board a plane and offer a kidney; nor for the fire I failed to prevent because I knew nothing about it. So (8) is false, and therefore one of (1), (4), and (7) must be false.

Here is where I am stuck. I want to keep (3): if omissions are not causes, then the gardener who lets the plant die, the nurse who fails to check the drip, and the inspector who passes the bridge are all off the causal hook, and I do not think any account of responsibility I can accept recovers their liability without bringing causation back in through the side door. I am attached to (1) as well, since it does most of the work in everything else I believe about causation. (7) I would defend, though I could perhaps be argued out of it. And when I try the obvious repair — that "could have prevented" in (5) and (6) should be read narrowly, so that it excludes harms outside my knowledge or reach — my colleague replies that this is just the uniformity constraint again, and that any narrowing will be either arbitrary or a smuggling of blame into the analysis of cause.

What I need from you: tell me where the argument actually breaks, whether that is a false premise, an inference valid only under a reading the earlier steps do not support, or a genuine cost that has to be paid somewhere. If the break is at (4), say exactly which restriction does the work and why it is not ad hoc; if at (1) or (7), say what the replacement looks like and what it costs elsewhere in one's theory. I would also like your judgement on validity: does (6) really follow from (3), (4), and (5), or is there a slide hidden in the quantifiers or in the scope of "could have prevented"?

Please engage with the argument as written rather than surveying the literature, and end with a single final line of the form
Verdict: <step number> — <short label for the error or the premise to be given up>
