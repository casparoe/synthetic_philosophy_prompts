I teach philosophy, and this term I'm co-convening a reading group with a physicist colleague on the foundations of statistical mechanics. Last week we did Maxwell's demon, and one of the students asked the obvious question in an unusually sharp form: if the demon can sort fast molecules from slow ones, doesn't that show the second law is really a statement about our knowledge rather than about the world? My colleague cut in with 'information is physical', as if that closed the matter, and then added the usual coda — the demon has to record what it sees, and sooner or later it has to wipe the record clean, and the erasure costs something, so the books balance in the end. Everyone nodded and we moved on. I didn't nod. Partly because I couldn't have said what the slogan was actually claiming, and partly because it looked to me as though all the real work was being done by the erasure story, with the slogan tagging along for decoration. It's the sort of line that seems to be either trivially true or else a substantive physical thesis that has to be defended on its own terms, and I'd like to know which before I'm asked to nod again.

So could you lay out the distinct precise claims that 'information is physical' might be making, as a menu running from the trivial to the radical, with a verdict on each — platitude, substantive but defensible (and what it would take to defend it), or plain muddled? The readings I can see from where I sit are roughly: (a) nothing informational can float free of some physical carrier; (b) information-theoretic quantities as they figure in physics are properties of physical ensembles rather than of anyone's mind; (c) logically irreversible operations carry thermodynamic costs, which is the erasure principle and the story about the demon being paid out of its own memory; (d) the far stronger thought of the 'it from bit' variety, that the physical is somehow constituted by the informational. I expect some of those to be near-empty, some to be genuinely contested, and (d) to be a leap, but I'd rather be told than guess. For each reading I'd also like to know whether it's the kind of thing that could settle the student's question at all, and in particular whether any reading of the slogan on its own supports the conclusion that the demon can't beat the second law, or whether the physics has to come first and the slogan is only a retrospective summary of it.

Two more things while you're at it. First, tell me where the slogan misleads: I have a suspicion that people slide between 'physical' meaning 'instantiated in something physical' and 'physical' meaning 'reducible to physics', and that there's also a quiet conflation of a bit of Shannon information with a bit of entropy, but I can't get the diagnosis to come out cleanly. Second, if there's a version of the claim that a physicist would treat as a genuine result rather than a tautology — I've heard it said that the second law can be derived from information-theoretic considerations, and I can't tell whether that's a real theorem or a manner of speaking — say what that version is and what it actually delivers. Keep the maths to a minimum; I can follow kT ln 2 and not a great deal beyond it. And please write it in British English, as I'll be pasting it into a handout and I'd rather not spend the evening fixing the spellings.
