After thirty-five years at the bench as an experimental physicist, I have spent my retirement trying to learn philosophy properly, and a local reading group has passed the spring on the metaphysics of causation. I keep returning to a class of case that the literature treats as deeply puzzling and that, in the laboratory and in engineering practice, never once struck me as puzzling: what we mean when we say that something happened because someone or something failed to act. We say that the seal's failure to hold caused the leak, that the technician's failure to recalibrate caused the drift, that the plant died because I did not water it — and none of these utterances has ever cost me a moment's hesitation. Yet the philosophical treatments I have encountered either deny that absences can be causes at all, or admit them by an argument so concessive that almost anything one did not do turns out to have caused almost everything. What I want from you is not a defence of any particular position but the specification that a position would have to meet — the desiderata that an adequate theory of causation by omission must satisfy, where adequacy is judged by what we need when we attribute causes in practice: in a lab report, an engineering post-mortem, or a court.

Set the desiderata out item by item, each with the case or consideration that motivates it. Which cases must the theory get right: the neglected plant; the two bystanders who each could have thrown the rope and each did nothing; the physician who declines to start a treatment she could have started as against the one who withdraws it once begun; the locked door that made a rescue impossible and so seems to annul the omission altogether; and, because these are the cases that most puzzle me, the laboratory and mechanical ones, where a missing component or a controller's failure to energise a relay is cited as a cause without anyone batting an eye. Which distinctions must it either respect or else self-consciously collapse: acting versus omitting, causing versus merely allowing, being a cause versus being a but-for condition, causal status versus explanatory salience, and above all the apparent asymmetry between our easy talk of mechanical and institutional 'failures to act' and our discomfort with personal ones. And which background results must it live with: that absences are not events, that every outcome has an indefinite multitude of unperformed preventions standing behind it, and that we nonetheless single out a few omissions as the causes.

Tell me, too, which of these are genuine desiderata and which are merely the prejudices of one camp dressed up as constraints — I suspect several of the 'requirements' I have listed are really demands for a particular style of answer. Flag any pair you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, with the collision spelled out; I would rather be told that the list is unsatisfiable as it stands than be handed a tidy theory that quietly drops one horn. Begin your reply with your verdict on whether the full set can be met at once — if some pair cannot, name it in your first sentence — and then give the list with its reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
