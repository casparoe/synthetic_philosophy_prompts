I'm building a handout for the final session of my phil of mind unit on higher-order theories of consciousness, and I've thought alot about how to do this: I want a decision tree that actually sorts the views instead of a list of names. My students will have the basic transitivity idea going in -- a mental state is conscious just when a suitable higher-order representation of it exists -- and I want them to end up able to place themselves and the main positions on a map. Roughly eight to ten branch nodes, and the leaves should include: actualist higher-order thought, dispositionalist higher-order thought, inner-sense / higher-order perception, self-representationalism (the one-state view), plain first-order representationalism, and a deflationary leaf for 'there's no real distinction here at all'.

Every node has to be a question a student can answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to. So don't write 'do you think the higher-order state is perceptual?' Write the concrete commitment and let the label fall out at the end.

The axes I care about, in no particular order:
- is the higher-order state a thought (propositional, non-perceptual) or is it itself a perception-like/experiential state? and does the view need it to be non-conceptual?
- must the higher-order state be occurrent right now, or can a mere disposition to have it do the work?
- does the higher-order state have to be itself conscious? (chase what happens either way, including the regress)
- is the transitivity principle a definition of what 'conscious' means for a state, or just a contingent claim about how it happens to work?
- must the higher-order state be reliably about the first-order state, or can it be systematically wrong about it -- and if it can be wrong, what does that do to the view?
- does the first-order state have to be a representation of anything outside itself, or can the whole thing collapse into one self-representing state?

Two things I want in there that I haven't seen in the usual textbook tree, so you'll have to build them yourself:
1. A node about the case where the higher-order state is itself unconscious and there's nothing further representing it. What does each leaf have to say about the first-order state's status in that case?
2. A node about two qualitatively identical first-order states, only one of which is targeted by a higher-order state. Which leaves are comfortable, which are stuck, and does anyone need to say the untargeted one is still conscious in some thinner sense?

Then, underneath: which pairs of leaves can your tree not seperate, and why (same commitments, different vocabulary? or a distinction your nodes can't reach?). And for any combination of answers no one actually holds, say so and diagnose it -- incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. If a leaf needs a name that doesn't exist yet, mark it 'unnamed' and give it the one-line commitment that would define it.

Keep the whole thing to something I can print on two sides of one page.
