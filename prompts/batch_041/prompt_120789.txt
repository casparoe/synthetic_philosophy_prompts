I direct a small centre for social and political thought, and by the end of term I have to commit the centre to a three-year research theme — something defensible to a dean who thinks in terms of external impact, but buildable out of the people I can actually recruit: philosophers, political theorists, and two historically minded sociologists, plus one colleague who works on care ethics and one who has written on platform labour. Nobody in the room will be running regressions or reading national accounts for pleasure. The theme I am leaning toward is the pair of concepts in Marx, exploitation and alienation, because both have become live again in arguments about gig and platform work, unpaid care, prison labour, and the automation of cognitive jobs, and because the philosophical literature on them looks to me badly misaligned with what is actually contested in those arguments. So what I want from you is an agenda, not a survey.

My provisional list of open questions runs as follows. First, what makes exploitation wrong — whether the charge is at bottom distributive, a maldistribution of the product, or relational, a wrong of subjection in which one person's activity is directed by another's ends — and whether the labour theory of value has to be true for either charge to land. Second, whether the two concepts come apart: alienation without exploitation (the self-employed contractor, the unpaid carer, the graduate student who loves the work) and exploitation without alienation (the well-paid worker who is nonetheless a mere means). Third, whether alienation is a normative concept at all or rather a diagnostic one, a claim about a misfit between our powers and our activity that has to be cashed out psychologically, socially, or metaphysically before it can do critical work. Fourth, what the right unit of analysis is, given that the phenomena seem to show up between classes, between firms, and inside a single person's week. Fifth, what the abolition of either condition would look like, given that Marx's own picture of unalienated labour is notoriously thin and that a world without wage labour might still contain hierarchy, drudgery, and compulsion. I will confess a prejudice: I would rather not spend three years on whether Marx's value theory is correct; I suspect that debate has hardened into a loyalty test rather than a research programme, though I am willing to be told I am wrong.

If the centre can support roughly eight people for three years, which two or three of these should the theme be built around, and in what order? I want the reasoning more than the list: for each, why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for people who work with texts and arguments rather than data, how neglected it currently is, and what difference settling it would make to how unions, regulators, and critics of the present arrangement actually argue. Tell me which of my five is overrated and, more usefully, which question I am missing because my list was drawn from the familiar debates. Say what progress would look like inside three years, what evidence or development in the world of work would move each question up or down the ranking, and what you would drop first. No reading list or citations, please; I want the defended agenda.
