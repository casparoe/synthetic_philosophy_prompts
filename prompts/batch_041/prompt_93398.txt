I run a Thursday evening discussion group at the community centre — no entry requirements, no essays due in advance, we just read bits and pieces and argue about them for two hours. We've just finished four weeks on W. E. B. Du Bois and I want to set them a proper end-of-term essay question, with a model answer and a marking scheme, because at the moment I grade these things by gut feeling and I'd rather have something to point at when somebody asks why they got a B.

Here's what's been nagging me. Du Bois gave a speech in the twenties about black art, and the gist of it is that art is always propaganda, whether the purists moan about it or not — he more or less says he's got no time for art that isn't. Fair enough. But in the same breath he says the artist has to be free, and that whoever sets out to serve beauty ends up serving truth and right whether he wants to or not, and that his freedom is bounded by truth and justice. So is he bossing artists about, or is he saying nobody can help themselves? Those are two very different claims and I've been going round in circles about it for weeks and its driving me mad.

And then there's my own hobby horse, which I'd like the question to make them chew on. I've decided there are two kinds of propaganda. The megaphone kind tells you what to think. The window kind just puts something in front of you and says, this is real, look at it. The megaphone argues, the window shows. I reckon Du Bois is mostly a window man who talks like a megaphone man. But I'm worried that if we let him have the window reading, his whole point goes limp — because if propaganda just means "art that shows you something", then saying all art is propaganda is like saying all novels are made of words. True, but so what?

So what I want is one essay question that forces them to deal with that, not just to write "Du Bois thought art should be political", which is what most of them will do if I let them.

Please give it to me as a JSON object so I can paste it into the worksheet template I've been messing about with, with these fields:

- question — the essay question itself, answerable in about 45 minutes of writing. Phrased so a bright adult with no philosophy training can see what they're being asked, but hard enough that a summary of Du Bois won't answer it.
- what_a_top_answer_must_do — a list of the specific moves an A answer has to make, roughly in the order they'd come.
- model_answer — around 650 words.
- total_marks — 20.
- mark_scheme — a list, one entry per thing being marked, each an object with criterion, marks, gets_full_marks_if, and loses_marks_if (say what specifically costs marks, not just "less clear").
- biggest_trap — one line on the mistake that will cost the most people the most marks.
- plain_english_glossary — a list of term and means, covering any jargon that ends up in the model answer, because half the group will be reading this on their phones on the bus.

Couple of things to keep in mind. They're definately not philosophy students, so the question shouldn't hinge on knowing the title of the speech or the year it was given, or on any other name — if they've never heard of the essay they should still be able to answer it from what I've said above plus what they already know. And don't let the marking scheme reward people for agreeing with me about the megaphone and the window. I want to know whether my little distinction survives being taken seriously, so the top answers should be allowed to say it collapses, and the mark scheme should have a way of giving credit for that.
