Question: State Sen's liberal paradox and explain it using his example of Lewd and Prude. Then assess whether the paradox shows that the Pareto principle and liberal rights are genuinely incompatible, or whether it can be dissolved by weakening one of the conditions. Justify your assessment.

Answer:

Sen's liberal paradox is a result in social choice theory. It states that no rule for aggregating individual preferences into a social decision can simultaneously satisfy three conditions. The first is unrestricted domain, which allows any coherent set of individual preference orderings as input. The second is the Pareto principle: if every individual strictly prefers one alternative to another, society must prefer it too. The third is minimal liberalism: at least two individuals must each be decisive over at least one pair of alternatives, so that the individual's preference over that pair determines the social ranking of that pair. Sen's proof shows that these three conditions cannot all hold.

The example involves two people, Lewd and Prude, and one copy of a book. Lewd ranks the outcomes: Prude reads, then Lewd reads, then no one reads. Prude ranks them: no one reads, then Prude reads, then Lewd reads. Minimal liberalism makes Lewd decisive over the pair consisting of Lewd reads and no one reads, so society ranks Lewd reads above no one reads. It makes Prude decisive over the pair consisting of Prude reads and no one reads, so since Prude prefers no one reads to Prude reads, society ranks no one reads above Prude reads. Combining these, society ranks Lewd reads above no one reads above Prude reads. But both individuals prefer Prude reads to Lewd reads, so the strong Pareto principle requires society to rank Prude reads above Lewd reads. That yields the cycle Lewd reads, no one reads, Prude reads, and back to Lewd reads, so no outcome can be selected as best.

The paradox is often presented as a deep problem for liberalism, but it is best understood as a special case of Arrow's impossibility theorem. Arrow showed that no social welfare function can satisfy a short list of plausible conditions, and Sen's result simply adds individual rights to that list. The main difference is that Sen uses the strong Pareto principle rather than the weak one, which makes his conditions more demanding than Arrow's and his theorem correspondingly narrower in scope.

As for dissolving the paradox, the most promising route is to restrict the domain of admissible preferences. The cycle only arises for a particular profile, and a social rule that refuses to accept profiles of that kind will never generate the contradiction. This suggests that the paradox is not a refutation of either liberalism or Pareto, but a warning about how the two interact when preferences are unrestricted.
