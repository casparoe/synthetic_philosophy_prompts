A physicist friend of mine and I spent most of a long drive arguing about the many-worlds interpretation, and I have come away unsure how much of what he told me to take on trust. He is not a foundations person — he is a condensed-matter experimentalist — but he recently read a well-reviewed popular book on quantum foundations and came away convinced, and he is articulate enough that I cannot tell where the popularization stops and the physics starts. His summary, as close as I can reproduce it: "Everett's real contribution was to show that you don't need the collapse postulate at all — the wavefunction just keeps evolving unitarily and every outcome happens in some branch, so nothing is ever genuinely indefinite. He also showed that the Born rule isn't an extra assumption: it falls out of the theory automatically, because once you accept that there are many branches and that all of them are equally real, an observer has to regard herself as a random member of the set of branches, and equal treatment of branches means the probability of an outcome is just the fraction of branches in which it occurs. So there is no objective probability in quantum mechanics at all — 'probability' is just an observer's ignorance about which branch she is in, exactly like not knowing which room you are in — and the whole mystery of the Born rule dissolves. What is left is empirical: pin down the branch structure, the preferred basis, which decoherence hands you, and the interpretation is finished."

When I objected that a 99-to-1 amplitude split would then give me a 50/50 chance of seeing the unlikely outcome, since there are two branches either way, he said the small branch is "thinner" and that I was confusing counting with weighting — but he could not say what "thinner" means, and he insisted the book covers it. I suspect some of this is garbled, and I would like to know which parts, since I actually find many-worlds attractive and do not want to spend the next year defending a cartoon of it.

Please go through the summary claim by claim. For each, tell me what a working Everettian would accept as stated, what they would accept only as a loose popularization of something more careful, and what is simply wrong or a mischaracterization of the position — and where he attributes a specific result to Everett, say whether that is the standard reading or a contested reconstruction. I am especially interested in whether his conclusion — that many-worlds needs no probability postulate, and that the remaining work is empirical — follows from whatever in the summary is accurate, and where the actual live dispute lies. I would rather have the two or three distinctions that matter most, drawn sharply, than a survey.
