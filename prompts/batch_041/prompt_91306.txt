I run the population-ethics desk in the cost-benefit shop of a mid-sized government: four analysts, no philosopher on staff, roughly forty rules a year that change how many people there are or when they are born — a pronatalist tax credit, a road-safety standard, a screening program, an immigration measure. We score everything with a value of a statistical life and a QALY framework, and I have come to suspect that this quietly commits us to the repugnant conclusion without our ever having decided to. I want a decision procedure rather than another round of debate.

Please write one my team can run in two hours per rule: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a finding that the rule is bad, where it must hand the decision to the deputy director because the question is one of values rather than facts, and where our own existing practice — a chain of pairwise judgments we have already made, each of which seemed harmless on its own — has to break if we are to avoid the repugnant conclusion. The procedure should force us to state in writing any critical level, or comparable parameter, that it depends on, so that the number is auditable and binds our later decisions.

Then run it on three rules: a tax credit whose additional children would have lives that are modestly worth living; a safety standard that saves lives and, as a side effect, lowers fertility; and a cheap treatment for a mild chronic condition. Include the case where the procedure tells me something I will not like, and say plainly what it cannot settle.
