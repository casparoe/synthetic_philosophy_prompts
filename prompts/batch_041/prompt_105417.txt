In a seminar on Davidson's radical interpretation, one classmate has now twice ended an argument with the same sentence — her one-line summary of the principle of charity: nobody is wrong all the way down. The first time she used it to block my suggestion that the speakers of a fragmentary ancient corpus might have held a body of beliefs that are, by our lights, systematically false; the second time, much more mildly, to tell me that I should not have read a hostile intent into a colleague's email. I doubt that one sentence can be doing both jobs, and I want to know exactly what it commits her to before I press her on it.

Give me a menu of the distinct, precisely statable theses that sentence could be expressing, ordered from the trivial to the radical. Keep at least these axes separate: what is being maximized or presumed — truth, rationality, agreement with the interpreter, intelligibility, or good will alone; whether the claim is a working heuristic that may be dropped when it stops paying off, a criterion of correct interpretation, or a substantive thesis about belief itself; whether it imposes a threshold (belief is mostly true, inference is mostly sound) or an optimization (as much as possible); and whether the error it rules out is global, wholesale within a single domain, or merely large. For each reading, say what it forbids, what would count as refuting it, whether it is trivial, defensible, or false, and which of the two uses above it can actually license. Mark especially the point at which the reading that maximizes truth and the reading that maximizes intelligibility given what the speakers could have known come apart, since I suspect the equivocation lives there.

Then run the menu against a case of my own, so that the verdict turns on the reading rather than on the case. An interpreter working on an undeciphered corpus finds that the construal yielding the highest proportion of true utterances also yields a lexicon on which the speakers say almost nothing informative, their texts collapsing into platitudes, whereas the construal that makes the texts substantive and informative attributes to them a handful of flatly false cosmological claims. Which readings require the first construal, which permit the second, and which say that the trade-off is not the sort of thing the maxim can adjudicate at all?

Finally, say which reading my classmate most likely had in mind on each occasion, and, if I make her choose one, which one she should be held to.
