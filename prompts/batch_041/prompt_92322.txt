Board game night turned into an argument about rationality, as it does. One of the guys kept repeating 'if you can be money pumped, you're irrational' like it settled the whole thing. Two cases got dragged in. My friend Dana has what he calls a preference loop: she'd trade her red chip for a blue one, then the blue for a green, then the green back for a red, handing over a dollar each time. Then someone said the same line covers people who pay to change what they'll want later -- like paying now to make sure future-you prefers studying to video games. When I pointed out that Dana would probably just refuse to trade once she saw the loop coming, he said 'then shes irrational, just also lucky'.

I've never been able to pin down what a money pump is actually supposed to prove, and I suspect that one sentence is doing about five different jobs. Can you lay out the precise claims it could be making: what 'can be pumped' has to mean (does she have to actually accept the trades? does it need her not to see it coming? does it need a trader whos willing to run the pump forever?), and what 'irrational' means on each reading (preferences that are internally inconsistent? choices that leave her worse off by her own lights? preferences no utility function can represent? a failure to stick with what you decided earlier?). For each reading, say whether it's true, what extra assumptions it needs, and whether it actually gets you anything in the two cases above -- the loop, and the pay-to-change-what-you-want-later one. Do those two come out the same or different, and does it matter that the second one is about her own future self rather than about chips on a table? Which reading do people probably have in mind when they drop the line? Lay it out as a menu I can bring back, verdicts included, and don't skip the ones that turn out to be trivially true -- I suspect at least one reading is just 'people who accept bad trades lose money'.
