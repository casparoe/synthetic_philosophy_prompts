Her version goes: Dennett's point is that natural selection designs organisms that behave as if they were following reasons, and since nobody has to be doing the reasoning, the reasons are free-floating — they don't belong to anyone. Her favourite illustration is the bird that does the broken-wing display to lure a predator away from the nest: there's obviously a rationale there (draw the predator off, make yourself look like easy prey), and the bird is in no position to have it, so it's a reason with no reasoner attached. She says the wasp that keeps re-checking its burrow is the same story, and then she extended it herself to something I actually know about: a trained neural net is the perfect modern case, because there are perfectly good reasons why those weights work and nothing in the network has anything like them, so the rationale floats there unowned — which she says is Dennett's whole thesis.

Where she loses me is the morals she draws from it. (1) She says this makes Dennett an anti-realist about reasons: the rationale is just a façon de parler, a useful story we tell to predict the behaviour, and the intentional stance is the same trick — so when Dennett says the rationale is really there, he only means it's convenient to talk that way, like saying a chess engine wants your queen. (2) She says that if the bird is responding to a reason, it must be representing that reason somewhere, just unconsciously or tacitly — the way I can ride a bike without being able to explain the physics — and that this is what Dennett means by competence without comprehension. I'm pretty sure (3) isn't something Dennett would say about himself, and (1) smells to me like it's using the intentional stance in a way he'd push back on, but I can't tell whether I'm objecting to the philosophy or just to her tone.
