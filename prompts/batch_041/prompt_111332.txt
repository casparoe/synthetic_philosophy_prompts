I teach a 400-level philosophy of mind seminar, and I am setting the final examination. The course has spent several weeks on Dennett, and I want the single essay question to test whether students can hold two of his commitments together rather than recite the three stances. Please write the question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B.

Here is the case I intend to print on the exam paper, and the question should be built on it:

A laboratory has built a device — call it the Chooser — that plays a simple repeated game against a human opponent. Its only input is the last three moves of the game. It has no model of the opponent, no memory beyond a three-move buffer, and no internal component that could plausibly be described as representing anything about the opponent's intentions. Its behaviour, however, is predicted with near-perfect accuracy, and by a description far shorter than any physical-stance or design-stance description of comparable accuracy, if we attribute to it the belief that the opponent will repeat a losing move and the desire to exploit that tendency. A second attribution — that the Chooser believes the opponent is bluffing and desires to call the bluff — predicts equally well and is equally short, and the two attributions are incompatible with one another. The Chooser was assembled by an engineer who had no interest in the game and built it to pass a hardware test; no one ever intended it to play well.

The essay task should ask students to do three things at once, and to take a definite position rather than survey the options: first, to say whether Dennett's compression criterion from his 1991 article Real Patterns entitles him to call the Chooser's attributed beliefs real, given that two equally compressive and mutually incompatible attributions are available; second, to say whether that answer can be reconciled with his claim that the physical stance is the most accurate in principle; and third, to say what the case shows about whether real patterns are real independently of any stance, or only relative to a descriptive vocabulary and a prediction task. Students should be told that a good answer will not simply summarise the three stances, and that they must commit to a position and defend it.

The model answer should argue a line rather than hedge. I want it to take the view that the two commitments can be held together, but only at a price — namely that real pattern has to be read as a stance-relative notion — and to say clearly what that price is. It should not present the rival attributions as a knockdown objection, and it should not treat the physical stance's in-principle accuracy as settling which patterns are real.

The marking scheme should total 20 marks and name the specific moves a top answer must contain, with the marks each is worth. It should separately list the moves that distinguish an A from a B: for instance, whether the candidate recognises that the compression criterion is comparative and vocabulary-relative rather than a correspondence test; whether they distinguish the reality of a pattern from the reality of an inner belief-state; whether they diagnose what in principle is doing in the physical-stance claim (a claim about entailment by an ideal predictor, not a ranking of degrees of reality); whether they notice that an equally good rival description does not by itself refute the criterion; and whether they engage the fact that the Chooser's game-playing rationale belongs to no one. Then give a short list of the specific mistakes that should cost marks — for example, reciting the three stances as a hierarchy of accuracy, treating a real pattern as a useful fiction, treating the tie between rival attributions as a decisive refutation of Dennett, or answering the second part by saying that the physical stance is more accurate and therefore that intentional patterns are less real.
