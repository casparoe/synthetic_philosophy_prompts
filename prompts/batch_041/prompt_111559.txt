I'm in the second half of a decision theory reading group — we've been through the standard axiomatics and have now reached the paradoxes — and I've been asked to write up my own position so the others can go at it. I'd rather you stress-tested it first. Here is roughly where I've landed. (i) Expected utility maximization, with the standard axioms (completeness, transitivity, continuity or Archimedean, independence), is the correct account of instrumental rationality; I don't think it needs to be patched with an extra boundedness axiom, and I'd rather not give up any of the standard axioms, which don't look arbitrary to me. (ii) Utility is unbounded: there is no finite ceiling on how good a state of affairs can be, and the utility of money increases without limit, albeit at a decreasing rate. (iii) The St. Petersburg paradox is dissolved by that decreasing rate: the infinite expectation is an artifact of measuring the prizes in money, and once diminishing marginal utility is in play the gamble is worth a modest finite amount and no more. I have a nagging worry here, which I've never been able to reconstruct properly — something about mere concavity not being enough, about the marginal utility needing to fall away at a particular rate — but it seems to me that if each successive doubling of the prize adds less and less, the series simply has to converge. (iv) On the other side I hold what I take to be a common-sense line: it cannot be rational to stake a substantial finite sum on a vanishingly small probability of a payoff, however enormous the payoff, and anyone who hands over their savings for a one-in-a-googol chance at a vast prize has fallen for a Pascal-style wager rather than seen something deep. (v) Methodologically I'm content with standard real-valued probability and countably additive expectation, and I don't think we need infinitesimals or non-Archimedean decision theory to sort these cases out. (vi) As a verdict on the case itself: I would pay at most about £20 to play the St. Petersburg gamble, and I think that is what a rational agent does.

What I want is an audit rather than a lecture. Tell me which of these commitments are genuinely in tension and how badly, ordered by severity, with a note on each as to whether it is a real conflict or merely an apparent one that dissolves once the commitments are stated precisely. For each genuine tension, say what the cheapest repair would be and what it costs me elsewhere in the list; for each apparent one, say exactly what makes it dissolve. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, which commitment I'm most likely to be wrong about, and what the strongest argument is for the reading I've rejected. I'm not attached to any particular item and I won't tell you in advance which one I'd surrender, so don't spare any of them.

One last thing: please write your answer in British English, and keep the mathematics explicit wherever the disagreement turns on it — the group will not accept a hand-wave at the point where the series either does or does not converge.
