I've been sitting in on a seminar about Wittgenstein's remarks on religion and it's turned into a running argument I can't settle, so I want to get something out of it. Here's what I keep circling. He seems to say that religious belief isn't a hypothesis about how things are, held up by evidence — it's more like a picture that regulates a whole life, and that if you tried to support it with evidence you'd have changed the kind of thing it was rather than helped it. And the language-game the believer is in doesn't rest on anything underneath; you just reach the point where this is how we go on, and asking for a further justification is asking the wrong thing. He also spends a lot of time being annoyed at Frazer for treating magic and ritual as bad physics, insisting the rite isn't a failed experiment or a mistaken causal claim, so it can't be wrong the way a false prediction is wrong. Fine. But then everyone in the seminar asks the obvious thing — does that mean you can't argue with a religious person at all, that God exists and there is no God aren't even contradictories, because contradiction needs shared criteria — and half of us think he's quietly protecting religion and half think he's emptying it out. I want that tension put somewhere concrete.

So: write me the welcome packet / FAQ a newcomer would be handed at a small invented congregation whose vicar has read too much of this and is genuinely trying to be straight with people about it. Deadpan, no winking, around 650 words: a short cover note from him, then numbered questions in the newcomer's voice with his answers, and one appended reply to a letter from the local secular society asking, perfectly reasonably, what exactly the congregation is claiming. The comedy has to come out of the philosophy being right rather than out of the vicar being a fool — he should be warm and sharp and should never actually assert anything about how things are, and if he ever almost does, the withdrawal has to be the joke. The secular society's letter should land the real objection (that a belief nothing could count for or against isn't a belief, it's a mood or a taste), and his answer should be good enough that the reader can't just wave him off, while still making the cost visible. Work in the groundlessness point, the not-a-hypothesis point, the Frazer / not-a-failed-experiment point, and the business about whether the believer and the atheist are really contradicting each other. Include a sidebar of ordinary parish notices — jumble sale, boiler fund, the rota for the churchyard — where the theory quietly collides with the fact that these are people who do things on Tuesdays. Don't put any quotations from Wittgenstein or anyone else in it and don't cite any books or lecture notes, just paraphrase, since I can't check quotes and neither can you. I want someone who's read his remarks on religion to laugh at how well it lands, and someone who hasn't to still follow it.
