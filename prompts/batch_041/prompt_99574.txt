I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training, so apologies in advance if I've reinvented a wheel badly here — I've been argueing about the ethics of suffering in a group chat for about two weeks and I've ended up with a position I've been calling (half-jokingly) lexical threshold negative utilitarianism. Before I keep defending it to people who actually know this literature I want to know if the thing hangs together. Here's the package as plainly as I can state it: (1) hedonism — the only things that matter intrinsically are pleasure and suffering, nothing else (autonomy, knowledge, relationships, beauty) matters on its own; (2) below some intensity, pleasure and pain are commensurable and sit on one scale you can trade across — I'd take a nagging headache for a decent slice of cake, I'd pay twenty quid to skip a day of flu-ish misery, and that seems like a real exchange rate rather than a category mistake; (3) above that threshold suffering is lexical — no amount of anyone's pleasure, however large, compensates for it; I usually put this as the marginal value of pleasure above the threshold being zero rather than infinite; (4) where the threshold sits is a brute structural fact about the value function, but I also think we locate it by intuition, comparing experiences and asking whether the compensation intuition still holds, and I think it's a bit fuzzy — a borderline zone rather than a knife edge; (5) I'm a totalist about positive welfare, so adding a life that's net happy is always an improvement, and a much bigger population of mildly happy lives beats a smaller population of ecstatic ones — yes, I know, the repugnant conclusion thing, I bite that bullet on the pleasure side; (6) verdict on a case: if a hospital could either cure five thousand people of moderate chronic pain or cure one person of agony, I'd cure the one person of agony without hesitation, and I'd think splitting the resources was a moral error; (7) also I think it's fine to have kids knowing they'll certainly have some bad days, because the expected pleasure is real and enormous.

I'm fairly sure at least one of these is quietly leaning on another in a way that doesn't survive contact, and I can't see which. What I want is an audit, not a lecture about why negative utilitarianism is spooky: which of the seven actually conflict, which only look like they conflict once you state them properly, and how severe each real conflict is. For each real one, tell me what specifically has to give and what it costs me to give it, and then tell me which single one of the seven I should drop if I want to keep the other six with the fewest knock-on changes — right now I can't even tell which beam is load-bearing.
