Thirty-odd years of doing physics, and now that I'm retired I've been reading moral philosophy badly. Here's whats bugging me. Every time the Hiroshima and Nagasaki question comes up — at dinner, in the essays I keep reading — someone reaches for the trolley problem. Five people tied to the track, one on the siding, you throw the switch, and presto: Truman was just the guy at the switch, the bombings were obviously justified, and anyone still agonizing is just being sentimental. I want to know how far that comparison actually holds. Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the track, the switch, the five, the one, the person throwing it — then show me where the mapping breaks, and be honest about which breaks actually matter for the argument it's being used to make versus which are just scenery. I'm especially curious whether the bombings are really the switch case or more like the version where you shove somebody off a footbridge, and whether that difference is doing real work or is just a philosophers party trick. Then tell me straight: does the conclusion — obviously justified, stop agonizing — survive once the disanalogies are laid out, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if the trolley is the wrong tool for this job, what would be a better comparison?
