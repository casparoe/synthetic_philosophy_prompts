I've been reading the philosophy-of-science work on models and the rationalist blogosphere's recurring fights about whether a model is just a model, and before I read another account of idealization I want the specification. Set out the desiderata any adequate theory of idealization in scientific modeling must meet: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the results it must stay consistent with — with the consideration behind each requirement. The cases I care about are frictionless planes, infinite populations, perfectly rational agents, and the models where a false assumption is load-bearing rather than merely convenient. The distinctions I don't want collapsed are idealization versus abstraction versus approximation versus plain error; the model as a representational claim versus the model as a computational tool; and de-idealizable cases, where some limiting procedure returns you to the target, versus ones where no such procedure exists. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the prejudices of a particular camp, and if some subset of them cannot be jointly satisfied, say so — that is the result I most want. Then, briefly, how Friedman's F-twist and the fictionalist line about models fare against the list.
