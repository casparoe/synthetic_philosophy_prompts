I'm putting together an application for a small internal grant — six months, half my time, the money is basically buying out some teaching — and I want to use it to turn a vague irritation into a dissertation chapter. Two pages max. So what I need from you is the proposal document itself, not advice about how to write one. No bibliography, I'll do the citations.

The irritation: everyone who writes about Turing and machine learning goes straight to the imitation game and then, if they're being thorough, to the bit where he says we should build a child machine and educate it rather then program an adult mind. Fine. But the education part of that gets treated as a cute metaphor for training, and nobody asks what Turing actually thinks education is doing. And here's the thing that's bugging me. In one of his machine-intelligence papers he draws a distinction between a genetical search and a cultural search — evolution on one side, the transmission of ideas by teaching and imitation on the other. That distinction is doing a lot of work. If education is a cultural search, then teaching isn't conditioning, it's a way of making an impossibly vast search space tractable by handing the learner results it could never have reached on its own. But the picture of education in the learning-machines discussion is much thinner than that: a big memory, reward and punishment, a teacher who is supposed not to be able to follow most of what the machine is doing, and a lot of hand-waving about the machine picking things up from experience. I want to know whether those two pictures are consistent, and if they aren't, which one is doing the real work in his account of how a machine becomes intelligent.

So the question I want the proposal to nail down is roughly: on Turing's account, is the education of a machine a matter of shaping its behaviour by reinforcement, or of transferring into it the accumulated results of a cultural search — and does his account have the resources to distinguish those two, or does it slide between them? Give me a sharper, more determinate version of that if you can.

Why the existing discussion doesn't settle it: the secondary literature is mostly about whether the imitation game is a good test, whether the child-machine idea anticipates modern ML, and whether Turing was a behaviourist. Those are the obvious alternatives and the proposal should say why neither gets at this question. Nobody seems to have taken the cultural-search remark seriously as an account of education, or noticed the pressure it puts on the reward-and-punishment story. That's my claim, and I'd like it stated in a way that doesn't overclaim — I havnt read everything.

Method, and this is the part I need to be realistic about: I'm not going to an archive. The work is textual and reconstructive. Month 1: get clear on the two pictures and pin down where they actually conflict, including the awkward bits — that the machine is supposed to be allowed to make mistakes and take its time, that the teacher's role is to run the process rather than to inspect the results, that the machine starts from very little and how much has to be built in from the start. Month 2: work out what a cultural search would even look like for a machine — what gets transmitted, what still has to be rediscovered, whether the teacher supplies content or a curriculum. Months 3 and 4: write the argument that the two pictures can't both be right, or that they can be reconciled by reading one as about the mechanism and the other as about the epistemic situation of the learner. Month 5: draft the chapter. Month 6: workshop it and revise.

Lay out the possible conclusions as live options, with what would decide between them. Option (a): the account is inconsistent, the reinforcement picture is the real one, and cultural search is a throwaway remark about how human science progresses rather than about machine learning. Option (b): the cultural-search remark is the serious one, and the reinforcement talk just describes how the teacher interacts with the machine, not what is being learned. Option (c): they're compatible — cultural search names the thing education makes possible, reinforcement names the mechanism that makes it happen — and then the interesting question becomes what Turing thinks the machine cannot get from its teacher. For each, say what would decide it: mostly textual fit, plus whether the account can handle a machine that produces a genuinely new result rather than a recombination of what it was taught.

Failure conditions: if it turns out Turing is just inconsistent in a boring way and there's nothing to reconstruct, say so and call it a negative result — a short paper arguing that the child-machine story is not a theory of education would still be worth having. What would really count as the project failing is if I can't get a version of the question that has a determinate answer, or if the whole thing collapses into a terminology dispute about what counts as learning.

Scope: six months, part-time, honestly. If the reconstruction starts needing a general theory of cultural transmission, that's too big, cut it. If it needs me to adjudicate between readings of every passage, too big, cut it.
