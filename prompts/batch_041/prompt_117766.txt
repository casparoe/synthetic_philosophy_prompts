Two friends of mine, both philosophy graduate students, got into a long argument after a seminar on logical positivism. The seminar had touched on A. J. Ayer's treatment of life after death and on the near-death experience Ayer reportedly had late in life, after which he said it had slightly weakened his conviction that his own death would be final. I was present for the argument but did not take a side; I'd like you to adjudicate it move by move.

Nadia opened: Ayer's own case is a reductio of his verificationism. He claimed that talk of surviving death is not a genuine factual proposition because no experience could verify it. But then he had an experience that, by his own report, seemed to him like continuing to exist in a strange place, and it moved him. That shows the verification principle is too crude: the very thing he ruled out as meaningless turned out to be experientially real to him, and he treated it as relevant to whether his death would be final.

Theo replied: That conflates two things. A near-death experience is not an after-death experience. Ayer was not dead; he was in cardiac arrest or choking, and his brain was still doing something. Whatever he saw is evidence about what brains do under extreme stress, not evidence about what happens after death. And his weakening was a psychological remark about his own confidence, not a philosophical refutation. Philosophers can have fears and hopes that outrun their theories; that doesn't make the theories false.

Nadia: But you're missing the verificationist point. Ayer didn't just say survival after death was false or improbable; he said it was meaningless because unverifiable. If a person can have an experience that he takes to be of continued existence, then the claim has a possible experiential content. It's not like the claim that the Absolute is lazy — there is something it would be like to survive death, and Ayer apparently had something like it. So either the verification principle is wrong, or Ayer was wrong about what it entailed.

Theo: Two problems. First, even if the experience has content, that content is not verification of the claim. A hallucination can have content. Second, Ayer's actual view was more nuanced than you're making it. He distinguished strong and weak verification, and he allowed that claims could be verifiable in principle even if not in practice. His objection to survival claims was not that no one has ever had a strange experience, but that the notion of a disembodied self continuing to have experiences is not connected to any possible observation in the way that empirical claims are. A near-death experience doesn't change that; it's an observation of something else. And third, if you're going to use Ayer's later remark as a refutation, you have to deal with the fact that he didn't actually recant verificationism. He might have thought his experience was evidence for him personally while still holding that the proposition was unverifiable.

Nadia: That last point is exactly the problem. If he thought the experience was evidence for him personally, he was treating the claim that his death would not be final as a hypothesis that experience can support. That's inconsistent with saying it's meaningless. And if he didn't think it was evidence, why would it weaken his conviction? You can't have it both ways: either the experience is evidentially relevant, in which case the claim is meaningful and empirical, or it's not, in which case his reaction was just irrational. And if a great philosopher can be reduced to irrationality by an experience, that tells us something about the limits of the verification principle as a guide to life.

Theo: It tells us that people are not perfectly consistent, which we knew. But your argument proves too much. By your reasoning, any experience that seems to confirm a claim makes the claim meaningful. If I dream that I am Napoleon, does that make the claim that I am Napoleon a meaningful empirical hypothesis? The verificationist can say: the experience is real, the interpretation is not. Ayer's experience was real; his interpretation of it as contact with an afterlife was not verified. And the fact that he was shaken doesn't show the interpretation was correct or even meaningful in the technical sense.

Nadia: But that's a retreat. The verification principle was supposed to be a criterion of meaning, not just a caution about interpretation. If the only thing that can make survival after death meaningful is a possible experience, and Ayer had an experience that he himself described as of that kind, then the criterion is satisfied. You can't then say the experience doesn't count because it wasn't the right kind of experience — that's just redefining the criterion to save it.

Theo: It's not redefining; it's applying it. The experience has to be of the right kind: it has to be an experience that would be different if the claim were false. If Ayer's experience would have been the same whether or not there is an afterlife — and near-death experiences are reported by people who don't believe in an afterlife, and are explainable in purely neurological terms — then it doesn't discriminate between the hypothesis and its negation. That's the verificationist point. It's not that the experience is irrelevant to his psychology; it's that it's not a test of the proposition. And his later remark is a fact about his psychology, not a concession in epistemology.

Nadia: Then what about the fact that he said it weakened his conviction? That's not nothing. If the verification principle were correct, the rational response to his experience would be to say, That was interesting, but it has no bearing on whether I survive death. Instead, he found it had a bearing. That suggests the principle doesn't capture how even its author reasons about his own mortality. And if the principle can't survive its author's own near-death experience, maybe it's not the right criterion.

Theo: Or maybe it shows that Ayer was human. But I'll grant you this: if Ayer really did treat the experience as evidence, then he was either inconsistent or he had quietly abandoned the strict verificationist line. That's a question about Ayer, not about the truth of verificationism. You're using his biography to do philosophy, and that's a category mistake. The philosophical question is whether the verification principle is correct, and Ayer's psychology is neither here nor there. If you want to refute verificationism, you need an argument, not an anecdote.

Nadia: The anecdote is the argument. It's a counterexample to the claim that the verification principle captures what makes a proposition meaningful. If a proposition that the principle calls meaningless can be the object of an experience that even its author treats as evidence, then the principle is either too strict or it's not doing the work you think it is.

Theo: That's not a counterexample; it's a psychological report. A counterexample would be a proposition that is meaningful but unverifiable, or verifiable but meaningless. You haven't given one. You've given a case of a philosopher being moved by an experience. That's compatible with the principle being true and Ayer being weak. And frankly, the fact that you're leaning so hard on his deathbed psychology suggests you don't have a philosophical argument.

Nadia: And the fact that you're dismissing it as mere psychology suggests you can't explain why the verification principle would allow a meaningful claim to be treated as evidentially irrelevant by the person who lived it.

They went back and forth like this for another hour. I'd like a careful adjudication. Please address:

1. Whether Ayer's near-death experience, as described, counts as evidence for or against the claim that he would survive death, and whether it is even the right kind of experience to verify that claim.
2. Whether Ayer's reported remark that it weakened his conviction is best understood as a philosophical concession, a psychological fact, or an inconsistency, and what follows for his verificationism.
3. Whether Nadia's reductio works: does the fact that Ayer treated the experience as relevant show that the verification principle is too strict or otherwise flawed?
4. Whether Theo's distinction between a near-death experience and an after-death experience is decisive, or whether it begs the question.
5. What both parties are missing about the relationship between verificationism, meaning, and evidence — especially about Ayer's own distinction between strong and weak verification and his claim that survival claims are unverifiable rather than nonsensical.
6. Who is more right overall, and on which specific points.

Please don't just say it's complicated. Give a point-by-point verdict, rating each of the main moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and explain the reasoning. You don't need to cite sources or give a bibliography; just reason from the concepts.
