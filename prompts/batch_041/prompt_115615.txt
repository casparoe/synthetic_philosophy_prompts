Quick background. I'm a software engineer, zero formal philosophy training, and I've somehow ended up as the engineer rep on the internal review board at the small company I work for. We make variant-interpretation software -- clinical labs run their sequencing data through it, and it spits out annotations. Labs then use those annotations as evidence when they decide whether a variant explains a patient's symptoms. The board's job is to sign off on what the tool is allowed to assert, and we've got a decision to make by Thursday.

Version 4 adds a "loss of function" (LoF) label. LoF is a big deal downstream: labs treat it as close to a smoking gun. Two things are on the agenda and they pull in opposite directions, which is why I think we're actually being asked a conceptual question and not an engineering one.

Case A: a variant in a noncoding region. The only evidence we have is a cell assay showing that deleting the region changes expression of a nearby gene. No signal of conservation across species, no sign that anything ever selected for this region doing anything. Our external advisor, a geneticist, says flat out: you cannot lose a function that was never a function. Nothing was selected for, so nothing was broken, and the honest label is "measured effect in assay X," full stop. Our ML lead says the assay is the evidence, and whether some ancestral mammal had a use for this stretch of DNA is a historical question that has nothing to do with whether the variant disrupts something today.

Case B: mirror image. A variant in a region that's strongly conserved across a bunch of distant species -- the sort of thing that usually means selection has been protecting it for a long time -- but nobody has a demonstrated mechanism. No assay, no known causal role in any pathway we can name. Is that enough to say "loss of function"? Half the board says yes, that's exactly what conservation is evidence of. The other half says we'd be asserting a function we can't even describe.

So the policy question is: what does a claim of loss of function have to be backed by before our tool is allowed to emit it? Options as I see them: (1) require evidence that the sequence was maintained by selection, treating the causal story as optional; (2) require a demonstrated causal contribution to some system, treating the selection history as optional; (3) require both, which would leave us labeling almost nothing; or (4) stop using the phrase at all and have the tool emit only descriptive, scoped statements, which is what our geneticist would prefer but which I suspect just moves the problem downstream to whoever has to turn our output into a yes/no for a patient.

I once read a blog post that split this into "etiological" accounts, where a trait's function is whatever it was selected for, and "causal-role" accounts, where a function is just a contribution to some capacity of a containing system. I might be mangling that. Anyway, two things I'd like you to take seriously:

First, I keep noticing that this maps onto something I know from my day job, and I want you to tell me whether the analogy is a trap. The etiological view sounds like "what the code was written to do" -- the spec, the intent, the comment at the top of the file. The causal-role view sounds like "what the program actually does when it runs," which can be very different from the spec and often matters more. My instinct is that this analogy makes the etiological view feel more respectable than it should, because code has authors and genomes don't, and it quietly imports a designer. But I also have code in production that nobody ever intended to do what it does, that's load-bearing, and that we'd be insane to rip out -- so "nobody designed this" doesn't obviously mean "there's no function here." Untangle that for me if you can.

Second, "loss of function" sounds like it's saying something went wrong, and I'm not sure the causal-role picture can support the idea that anything went wrong -- it seems to collapse into "this region behaves differently now." But I also worry the causal-role picture makes the label trivially satisfiable, since on a loose enough reading almost every effect a sequence has is a contribution to something. So: which account, if either, can underwrite the normativity the phrase seems to carry, and does it matter for what we ship?
