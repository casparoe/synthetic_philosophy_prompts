Read William Golding's Lord of the Flies through the Mencius–Xunzi debate about human nature, and say which of the two the novel vindicates, if either.

The obvious move is to call the book Xunzian: strip away the schoolmasters, the law, and the policeman, and the untransformed desires of a group of boys produce precisely the contention and violence Xunzi predicts. But I want the reading to take seriously what the obvious move skips. For Xunzi the diagnosis is not that human beings are depraved but that they are unformed: the crooked wood needs the press-frame and the blunt blade the whetstone, goodness is the work of deliberate effort and ritual rather than of nature, and yet the transformation is available to anyone — so the island would show him a failure of instruction, not a proof of innate evil. Mencius holds instead that the sprouts of compassion, shame, deference, and moral discrimination are present in everyone, that they are only sprouts, and that a bad environment can strip them away: the mountain everyone calls barren is barren because the axes and the grazing have been at it, and it would recover if left alone. Neither would accept the novel's most common gloss — that the boys simply reveal an innate and ineradicable depravity — though they reject it for opposite reasons.

Which of them, if either, accounts for the book's actual shape? Consider Roger's arm, still held back by the habits of a civilization he no longer has; the chant and the dance, in which ritual does not tame the killing but licenses it; the fact that the moral failure at the climax is total, since Ralph and Piggy are complicit, and yet the boys do not fail alike. Does the novel dramatize Mencius's claim that the sprouts can be overwhelmed, Xunzi's claim that order is a fragile artifact that must be made and maintained, or something neither can hold — a settled corruption that both philosophers deny? Be honest about where the novel resists the reading, and finish by saying what it contributes to the debate that the two texts do not.
