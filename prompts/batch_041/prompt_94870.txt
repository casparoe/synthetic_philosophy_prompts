For two years a reading group I belong to has argued about path dependence without moving, and I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. The lock-in camp holds that certain historical outcomes — a technical standard, a legal rule, a pattern of settlement — become entrenched through increasing returns, so that an early and possibly arbitrary choice fixes what comes later, and a different early choice would have yielded a different and perhaps better equilibrium; on this view history is a branching tree and the branch we took was never inevitable. The convergence camp holds that the appearance of lock-in is mostly survivorship bias and after-the-fact storytelling: had the entrenched outcome been genuinely inferior, some pressure would eventually have eroded it, and the claim that things could have gone otherwise is an unfalsifiable counterfactual dressed up as an explanation. We share the same case studies and disagree about what they show. Take a deliberately invented example so that we cannot lean on any famous one: two rival port cities adopt incompatible rail gauges in the 1880s, both technically sound; a century on, one standard dominates and the other city's network is stranded. The lock-in camp reads this as contingency entrenched by increasing returns; the convergence camp reads it as the eventual victory of the better standard, with the arbitrary early choice washed out by later selection. My own hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that this is not an empirical disagreement about the past but a disagreement about what a historical explanation is for — the lock-in camp wants the actual explained as one of several live possibilities, the convergence camp wants it explained as the likely or forced outcome given the structure — and that neither can refute the other because they are answering different questions under a single name. I also suspect the term path dependence is doing at least two jobs, one about the causal weight of early events and one about the irreversibility of their effects, and that sliding between them keeps the argument warm. Diagnose the dispute: say what kind of disagreement it really is, whether a hidden empirical or formal question is doing the work, whether the two sides are talking past each other or equivocating, and what, if anything, would count as progress — a distinction, a test, a formal result, or a demonstration that none should be expected. Give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion.
