I teach a 90-minute session on computability for a mixed group of about 18 upper-year philosophy and CS students, and I want them to feel why the Church-Turing thesis can't just be proved -- why it's a claim about an informal notion rather than a theorem. Build me a runnable activity. My rough sketch, improve on it if you have something better.

Split them into two offices that can't talk to each other. Each office gets a different rulebook for what a clerk may do: one gets register-machine style rules (add one, subtract one, jump if zero, halt), the other gets string-rewriting rules on a paper strip. Give both offices the same handful of jobs -- double a row of tally marks, decide whether a string is a palindrome, add two numbers -- and have them run the jobs by hand. Then bring the offices together; they'll notice they got the same jobs done, and it'll feel like evidence for something big. Don't say the words Church or Turing until the debrief, and no lecture up front.

Then the part I actually care about. Introduce a third role: a "clerk" allowed to do anything -- guess, follow a hunch, phone a friend, consult an infinite lookup table, flip a coin, run forever. The group has to write the rule saying which clerks count as mechanical. They'll write something, and then I spring cases on them: the oracle clerk with a magic phone that answers yes/no instantly, a clerk who can rewrite the strip but never erase, one working in parallel with fifty hands, one who just sees the answer. Every rule they produce will be either vague or circular (mechanical means could be done by a machine).

I need the setup and materials, a timing breakdown for the 90 minutes, the exact adversarial clerks to spring and in what order, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the thesis's epistemic status -- informal left side, formal right side, convergence as the only real support. Tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time and how to salvage it, and be honest about what the activity can't reproduce, so I know what I have to supply myself.
