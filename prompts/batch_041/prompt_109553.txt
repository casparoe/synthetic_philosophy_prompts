I'm a TA for a graduate seminar on vagueness, and for the session on the epistemicist/supervaluationist divide I'd like to open with a poem rather than a handout. Could you write me a poem in terza rima — Dante's interlocking tercets, aba bcb cdc and so on, closing with a single line that rhymes back into the chain — of roughly eighteen to twenty-four lines. I want the form itself to carry the argument: because the tercets chain on indefinitely and have no natural stopping point, the poem has to decide where to stop, and I want the reader to feel that the stopping point is a choice the poem makes rather than one the material forces. That is the supervaluationist's point that no single precisification is privileged, sitting right beside the epistemicist's reply that there is a unique fact about where the last tercet ought to end and we simply cannot know it. If you can manage it, let the poem's final line be the one that would have to serve as the cutoff, and let it be genuinely unclear whether the poem is telling us that it is.

The subject should be a sorites series in the flesh — a heap shedding grains, a shade sliding from red toward orange, dusk draining out of a sky — and I would like the speaker to be one of the borderline items itself, addressing the series it belongs to and trying to work out which side it falls on. It should be accurate enough that a specialist recognizes the machinery rather than hearing it name-dropped: the margin-for-error thought that if the speaker is, say, still a heap, then its nearest neighbor must be one too, so no single grain can be the culprit; the idea that on the supervaluationist picture a borderline sentence comes out true under some admissible ways of drawing the line and false under others, which is why the speaker can't be told yes or no; and the higher-order worry that even the edges of the penumbra refuse to hold still, so the poem can't simply point to where the vagueness stops. Someone who knows the debate should be able to tell which of the two views the poem is quietly arguing for, and ideally should find that the closing tercet makes that hard to say. Please keep it under about thirty lines, and don't resolve the tension at the end — the seminar is supposed to do that, and I expect it to do it badly.
