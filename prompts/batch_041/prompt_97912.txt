I teach a 75-minute session in an MA seminar on metaphysics and action, with sixteen students, and I want a runnable activity that makes them feel the force of the view that omissions can be causes, and then the countervailing worry that if every omission is a cause, causation loses its explanatory and normative grip. I have spent years in rationalist blogs and forums where this distinction is litigated, so I want the activity to do the work rather than a lecture. The participants should run into the problem themselves before it is named. Set up a simple shared-resource scenario with turns, private choices, and a harm that occurs only if nobody intervenes; arrange the payoffs so that each participant can truthfully say I did not do it, while the bad outcome is massively overdetermined by many omissions. Keep a ledger in which every player's non-action is recorded as a line item, initially with no distinction between a failure and a choice. Then run a second round in which the same abstention is embedded in an explicit duty, rota, or role, so that the same not-doing suddenly looks like the cause. I want them to feel the pull of counterfactual dependence — if I had acted, it would not have happened — and then to feel how cheap that makes causation, since each of them can say the same. The debrief should map what happened onto the distinction between making and allowing, the role of norms in causal selection, and the difference between explaining an outcome and assigning responsibility. Give me rules, materials, roles, a timeline, a payoff scheme that does not need real money, and an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. Also be frank about which feature of the metaphysical debate the activity cannot reproduce and that I shall have to supply in the debrief. No lecture up front, and do not let the terms of art appear until the debrief. Please write the answer in British English.
