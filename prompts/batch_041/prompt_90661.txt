I'm a teaching assistant for a graduate seminar on knowledge and self-knowledge, and I'm marking my share of the final exams before the instructor and I calibrate our grades. The exam was built around a small toy model that the instructor put on the board in the last week of term, right after we finished Williamson's anti-luminosity argument; the model isn't drawn from any published paper, so there is nothing for you to look up and everything you need to grade the answer is stated here. The model, exactly as the students received it, was this: "Experience varies along a discrete scale with levels i = 0, 1, 2, ..., N, where N is large. At level i the subject's evidence confines the level to the three adjacent values E(i) = {i-1, i, i+1}; values below 0 and above N are simply absent. A belief is safe at level i when its content is true at every level in E(i), and the subject knows a proposition at i just in case the belief is true there and safe there. For each threshold t, the subject is disposed to believe 'the level is at least t' exactly when i ≥ t, and to believe 'I know that the level is at least t' exactly when the first-order belief has its margin, i.e. exactly when i ≥ t+1. Nothing else is built into the model."

The question on the exam read: "(a) At which levels does the subject know that the level is at least t? Justify your answer. (b) At which levels, if any, does the subject know that they know that the level is at least t? (c) Does the KK principle — if one knows that P, then one knows that one knows that P — hold in this model? In answering, say what the model shows about the luminosity of the condition 'the level is at least t' and about the luminosity of the condition 'the subject knows that the level is at least t'."

Here is one student's answer, transcribed in full: "(a) The subject knows 'the level is at least t' exactly when the evidence rules out every level below t. At level i the evidence is {i-1, i, i+1}, whose lowest member is i-1, so the claim is true throughout the evidence — the belief is safe, that is, it is not a belief the subject would have held had it been false — exactly when i-1 ≥ t, i.e. i ≥ t+1. So the subject knows the first-order claim at every level from t+1 up to N. (b) The higher-order claim 'I know that the level is at least t' is true exactly at the levels where the first-order belief is safe, i.e. at i ≥ t+1. To know it, the subject needs it to be safe, i.e. true throughout E(i); the first-order belief is safe at a level j exactly when j ≥ t+1, so the higher-order claim is safe when i-1 ≥ t+1, i.e. i ≥ t+2. So the subject knows that they know at every level from t+2 up to N. (c) KK therefore fails, and it fails at exactly one level for each threshold, namely i = t+1, where the subject knows the first-order claim but not that they know it. But this failure is an artifact of the discrete scale: the trouble is only that t+1 is a sharp boundary, and if the scale were continuous then no single level would sit exactly on the boundary and the failure would disappear, so a subject with a continuous margin would satisfy KK. The same goes for luminosity — the condition 'the level is at least t' fails to be luminous at i = t for the same boundary reason — and this shows that the anti-luminosity phenomenon is really about the edges of the space of cases rather than about the structure of knowledge. The wider lesson is that self-knowledge is always hostage to such boundaries, so a subject can never be fully secure about their own states."
