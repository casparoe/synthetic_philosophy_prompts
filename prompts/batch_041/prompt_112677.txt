I'm putting together a handout for the second meeting of a small reading group on Kant's ethics, and the first meeting went badly — everyone already knows the line about Kant saying you can't lie to the murderer at the door, and we spent an hour going in circles because it turns out five people in the room were defending five different things and none of them noticed. So for next week I want a decision tree that takes someone from the bare commitment that there is something to the thought that you must not lie even here, to a specific named position: each branch labeled with the question that splits it, each leaf carrying the position's name plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. The sorting is the whole point, so every node has to be a question someone can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. I'd like the branches built out of the distinctions that actually do the work in this debate, roughly: is the relevant duty owed to the murderer (a right to the truth), to humanity at large (the thought that a lie vitiates the source of right and damages the institution of assertion), or to the speaker's own dignity (lying as self-degradation); is the question the juridical one of whether you can be held liable for what follows, or the ethical one of what you may do, since Kant's 1797 reply to Constant looks like it's about the former; is a lie analyzed as knowingly saying what is false, or as making a declaration to another, so that some answers turn out not to be lies at all; whether the case involves a genuine conflict of duties at all, and what it costs you if you say yes; and whether the murderer is addressing you as a rational being or merely operating on you as a threat, which is what lets some people say the utterance isn't a declaration and so isn't a lie. I also want leaves for views people actually hold that aren't just 'Kant was right' and 'Kant was obviously wrong': the one where you may stay silent or say something true but misleading; the one where the murderer forfeits his claim by his own violation; the one where Kant's considered position is that the strict answer holds in the doctrine of right while the ethics of virtue permits the lie; and the one where the example is simply underdescribed and the answer turns on whether the man at the door is a private citizen or an agent of the state. Flag any two leaves your tree can't really separate, and note which combinations of answers come out empty, and whether they're empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Somewhere between six and nine leaves, please.

Two things about how to write it. First, dont bother with references, citations, or 'so-and-so argues that' — I don't need a reading list and I can't check any of it anyway, so just give me the positions. Second, and this is annoying but I mean it: write the whole thing in continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no little arrows — narrate the tree in sentences the way you'd explain it to someone over coffee, the first split is between those who think such-and-such, and if you say yes to that the next question is whether, and so on. And be straight with me about what's settled versus what's genuinely contested among people who know this material. A lot of what gets said very confidently about what Kant 'really meant' in the murderer case is one contested reading among several, and I don't want a live interpretive fight handed to me as if it were the consensus; where a branch of the tree depends on a disputed reading of Kant, say so right there in the sentence.
