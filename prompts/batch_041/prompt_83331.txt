Here's the angle I want to push. There's this well-known move where you take akrasia and say it's basically just hyperbolic discounting: the agent prefers the smaller-sooner thing when it's close and the larger-later thing when it's far, and "weakness" is just the discount curve being steeper than the agent's own long-run ranking. But I've started to think this doesn't explain akrasia so much as dissolve it. On that picture there's never a moment where the agent judges A best and then does B anyway, because the judgement itself slides with the delay — so there's no conflict left to explain. If that's right, a chunk of the literature is answering a question the model makes disappear.

What I want from you: a tier list of the main ways of accounting for akrasia, ranked by how much of the pheonomenon each one actually explains once you hold the synchronic/diachronic split fixed. Synchronic = judges A best and does B in the same breath; diachronic = the ranking flips over time while the agent is stable at any given instant. Say up front what you're taking "explains" to mean here, since I know that word is doing a lot of work.

Include at least these, and add others if you think they've earned a slot:
- akrasia as the will failing to execute a settled judgement
- akrasia as intrapersonal bargaining between time-slices, where the fix is a committment device that changes the choice set rather than the will
- the pure discounting model, on which there's no conflict at all, only preference reversal
- akrasia as a failure of attention or salience rather than of valuation
- akrasia as a social / virtue-theoretic vice, i.e. something you're criticizable for, not just a mechanism that fires

Put them in tiers (S/A/B/C, whatever you like), one or two sentences each on why it lands where it does, and flag which ones only really work for one of the two kinds of case.
