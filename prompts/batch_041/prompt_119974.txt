I teach a graduate seminar on the political thought of the Goethezeit, and I want to run Act V of Goethe's Faust, Part Two as political philosophy rather than as a literary monument. Take the role of a scholar who works on both Goethe's political thinking and the philosophy of the German Enlightenment, and who is suspicious of treating a poet's fictions as mere illustrations of his opinions. Think carefully. Begin with an explicit brainstorm of three or four candidate readings of Act V, noting for each what it would have to claim about the rest of the play, and only then commit to the reading you find most defensible.

The context you should assume. Goethe spent most of his adult life as a minister of a small German duchy; his settled political temper is usually described as anti-revolutionary. He distrusted rupture, valued order and gradual development, admired Napoleon, and is remembered for the maxim, recorded in his account of the siege of Mainz, that he would rather be guilty of an injustice than endure disorder. Read Act V against that temper. In the act, Faust is an old man, blind, holding a coastal fief granted by the Emperor, who has spent his last strength reclaiming land from the sea, driving dikes, and settling people on the new ground. His project is, in his own mind, a great one: he wants to stand on free ground with a free people. Against it stands a remnant of the older world: Philemon and Baucis, an old couple in a cottage on a hill, with linden trees and a chapel bell, who shelter a wanderer they once saved from shipwreck. The bell, and the fact that these few trees are not his, torment Faust. He orders Mephistopheles and the three mighty men to move the couple to a better farm; instead the hut and the lindens are burned, and the old couple and their guest die. Faust recoils and blames his agents. The grey hags come, among them Care, who blinds him. He dies with his vision of the free people still before him, speaks the words of the wager in a form that may or may not count, and angels carry off his soul.

What I need from you.

First, the central question: does Act V dramatize the political ethic Goethe actually held, or does it expose that ethic? To sharpen this, distinguish in your own terms between order as a condition of freedom and order as a substitute for freedom, that is, between a politics that secures the ground on which people can act together and a politics that treats the absence of friction, including the friction of a bell one does not own, as the highest good. Which of the two does Faust's dike-building serve? Which does Goethe's own temper favor? Say clearly where the play refuses to decide.

Second, Faust's exculpation. He claims he wanted persuasion, not violence. Is that exculpation, self-deception, or an accurate description of a division of labor in which the sovereign's clean intentions depend on dirty hands? Does the play treat the three mighty men as Faust's agents or as the truth of his project? And why does the wanderer, the guest who remembers an old kindness, matter to the political reading?

Third, the wager and the last speech. Faust addresses the passing moment in the optative rather than the indicative. Does that grammatical fact matter politically? Is the vision of free people on free land a genuine political ideal within the play, or a self-flattering image that the fire and the blindness expose? Tell me whether to teach the ending as endorsement, indictment, or deliberate suspension, and what each choice costs.

Fourth, be honest about resistance. Name the scenes, motifs, or structural features that pull against reading Act V as a verdict on Faust's project, and say where the political lens distorts the play. If the drama holds two political ethics in tension rather than resolving them, say so and say what the tension costs.

Fifth, close with what the drama adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not. Is there something about the experience of ordering a world, or of being the person who must choose between injustice and disorder, that Goethe shows but cannot argue?

Write in continuous prose, with brief headings if they help. No bibliography, no citations, and no German quotations; paraphrase the play rather than quoting it, since I care about the reading and not the wording. Near the end, describe two or three scenes I should put in front of the seminar first, and explain why those.
