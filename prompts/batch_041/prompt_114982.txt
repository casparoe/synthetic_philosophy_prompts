I'm making a handout for a grad methods seminar on the science of consciousness, and I want a decision tree that walks a student from a bare starting commitment (consciousness should be studied empirically, like any other natural phenomenon) down to a named methodological position. The one I care most about getting right is heterophenomenology — Dennett's move of taking what a subject says about their experience as data about what they believe and are disposed to say, while staying neutral about whether the experience is really like that (bracketing it) — but I want its rivals on the tree as live options, not straw men.

Build it from the logical space, not from a list of names: each branch node should be a question a student can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and each leaf should be a position plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Nodes should be concrete enough to actually answer. For example: a subject in a binocular-rivalry task reports that she saw the face and then the house, alternating. Do you file that as evidence about her visual experience, as evidence only about what she believes about her experience, or as verbal behavior to be explained by something else? Put at least these leaves on it — heterophenomenology itself; classical introspectionism, where trained observers' reports about their own experience are the primary data; neurophenomenology, where first-person reports and third-person measures are supposed to constrain each other in both directions; front-loaded phenomenology, where you use phenomenological distinctions to design the task up front instead of collecting reports afterward; illusionism, where the reports are real but the phenomenology they seem to be about isn't; and a hard behaviorist/eliminativist leaf, where reports are just verbal behavior and never evidence about experience. If there's an obvious position I've left off (contemplative first-person traditions, second-person or interactive methods, whatever), put it on the tree and tell me where it forks.

Things I want the nodes to actually seperate:

- whether a report is treated as evidence about experience, or only as evidence about the subject's beliefs and utterances;
- whether the experimenter's own experience is allowed in as data at all;
- whether subjects can be trained to report more finely, and whether that training makes their reports more trustworthy or less;
- whether first- and third-person data constrain each other one-way, two-way, or not at all;
- whether bracketing the truth of the report is just a method, or is quietly a metaphysical claim that there's nothing more to experience than what gets reported;
- whether consciousness is the thing to be explained or the thing to be explained away.

Then give me at least two leaves that the logical space allows but that nobody seems to occupy, mark them as unoccupied, and say for each whether it's empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And flag any two positions the tree still can't seperate with these nodes — I'd rather see the gap than have it papered over.

Audience is grad students who know the hard problem but not the methods literature. American English. Ten to fourteen nodes and six to nine leaves is about right, but don't pad it just to hit a number.
