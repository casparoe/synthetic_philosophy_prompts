I'm a doctoral candidate in urban planning, and I've been trying to work out what my own field's central decision procedure is actually committed to. The practice I have in mind is this. In much of the United States, and in recognisably similar forms elsewhere, the rules governing what may be built are set out in a citywide plan adopted by the whole council and justified in the language of the general welfare. But the decisions that determine whether a particular building actually gets built are made project by project, through variances, conditional use permits, design review, and other discretionary approvals. In those proceedings, the parties who are formally notified, given the right to speak, and given the right to appeal are the owners and residents in the immediate vicinity of the site. The people who would live in the building, and the people who would move to the city if more of it were built, have no standing at all. Among the grounds on which a project can be refused are its effect on the neighbourhood's character and its effect on nearby property values. The asymmetry I keep running into is that the plan is written for everyone, while the permit is decided by the few who are already there.

What I want is an account of what this arrangement presupposes. What principle of political inclusion does the standing rule commit the city to: that all affected interests should be represented, that only those subjected to a decision's coercion should be, or something territorial and proprietorial that maps onto neither? What conception of property does the neighbour's objection presuppose? Does the objection make sense only if ownership carries something like an easement over the uses other owners make of their own parcels, and can that be rendered coherent on a view of property as a bundle of separable rights, or does it require something closer to a natural right to the existing state of one's surroundings? Is there a defensible ground for the incumbents' claim, such as sunk investment, attachment to place, or the value of stable communities, and how much weight can it bear against the claims of those excluded altogether?

I would also like you to sort out which parts of the practice are strictly presupposed by it and which merely happen to fit with it. A purely rent-seeking story, in which incumbents use the permitting process to protect their asset values and the moral vocabulary is decoration, would generate the same pattern. If that story explains the practice as well as any theory of legitimacy does, say so, and say what follows about whether there is an implicit theory here at all. Then tell me where the practice is underdetermined: which rival theories would license exactly this arrangement, and where they would diverge if pressed.

Finally, take the view you judge the practice to presuppose, whatever it turns out to be, and replace it with each of three serious alternatives: that the relevant demos for a neighbourhood decision is everyone affected, including would-be residents; that land-use decisions are matters of property right and should not be made collectively at all; and that the relevant unit is the region rather than the neighbourhood. For each, say concretely what would have to change in the notice rules, the permissible grounds for refusal, the standard of review on appeal, and the level at which decisions are made. I am less interested in a survey of the planning literature than in a reconstruction of the theory the machinery is already committed to, and an honest verdict on whether it holds together.
