I retired a few years back from a career in geophysics and I've been working my way through philosophy in a reading group with a couple of old colleagues, and last week we did Kavka's toxin puzzle, which has been bugging me ever since. As I understand the setup: a billionaire will wire you a million dollars tomorrow if he becomes convinced that today you genuinely intend to drink a vial of some toxin a day later -- the toxin won't kill you, it'll just make you wretchedly ill for a day -- and the money is yours regardless of whether you actually go through with drinking it. So the clever move is to form the intention today, collect the million, then simply not drink, since by then there's nothing more to gain and a day of misery to avoid.

But heres what keeps nagging at me: it seems like the whole thing is trivially easy, and I can't get anyone to tell me why it isn't. Intentions are just things I can decide on, like deciding to floss or to finally learn Italian -- I say to myself, right, I intend to drink it, and there I am, intending. And if I can simply decide to have an intention the way I decide to have a plan, then there's no puzzle left at all: I decide to intend, take the cash, and skip the toxin. My reading group split right down the middle and nobody could explain why my "just decide" move doesn't dissolve the whole thing. So what am I missing? Is the puzzle really demonstrating that intentions are nothing but beliefs about what I'll end up doing, and that a rational person should therefore be able to talk himself into holding one? Or is there something about intending that makes it different in kind from deciding -- because from where I sit, if the will can't be commanded like that, half of what I've been reading about practical reason falls apart.
