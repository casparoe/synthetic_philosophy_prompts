I teach a second-year "Mind and Brain" module that mixes philosophy and psychology students, and I run a 90-minute session early in the term whose whole job is to make the access/phenomenal distinction land before I have said either of those words out loud. What I want from you is a runnable activity — part parlour game, part staged experiment — for roughly eighteen students in an ordinary seminar room with a projector, in which they discover for themselves that there is a gap between what they can report and what seems to have been there. I have a rough mechanic in mind; build it out if it works, and replace it if you can keep what it delivers. Each student is briefly shown a card bearing a grid of a dozen distinct symbols, long enough to see and far too brief to take in, and then writes down what they saw, which is always humiliatingly little. Then they do it again, and this time, immediately after the flash, I call out one of three colours, each picking out a row, and they write down only that row — and almost everyone gets the whole cued row. The moment I am after is the one just after that, when I ask them to wager points on how many of the twelve items were "there for them" in the way the reported row was, and the room splits between those who want to say that all twelve were present and simply out of reach, and those who suspect the whole thing is a conjuring trick. I would like a third round with a delayed cue, late enough that the row they were about to report has drained away, because the felt contrast between the immediate and the delayed cue seems to me to be where the philosophical pressure actually lives. Please give me the rules and materials (what goes on the cards, how the flash is timed without a tachistoscope, how the cue is delivered, how points or some small stake is awarded so that wagering feels like wagering, and how to keep the session from collapsing into a memory quiz); a minute-by-minute running order for the 90 minutes, with the activity itself taking no more than about forty minutes and leaving me a full twenty-five for the debrief; roles if you think they help (a scorer, a recorder, and perhaps a designated sceptic whose job is to interrogate the room's introspective reports rather than accept them); and explicit instructions about what I must not say or do beforehand, since the point is that they arrive at the phenomenon rather than being briefed on it. Assume nobody in the room has read anything about this, that I will be doing the theory myself afterwards, and that the activity should therefore be self-contained and should not name any theory, paradigm, effect, or researcher — including in the materials handed out. If it can be done without overloading the session, I would also like a short final stage in which the group has to work out what one of their number saw without receiving any report from them at all, so that they run into the fact that a report is the only instrument they have, which is precisely the methodological trouble in this area.

The debrief is the part I care most about, and I want it to leave the room holding two readings side by side rather than choosing one. Map each stage of the activity onto the difference between what is available to a system for report and control and what is present in experience, and make them feel both the pull of the thought that the unreported items were nevertheless experienced — that the cue reached back into something that was already there — and the pull of the deflationary reading, on which the cue reaches into a rich but non-conscious buffer, and the only thing ever conscious was the little they could report. I would like them to see why their own wager, made from the inside and on the basis of how things seemed, cannot settle the question either way, and why the immediate-versus-delayed contrast is the kind of evidence that both sides can accommodate. If you can fold in a second, shorter stage that stages the global-workspace idea — some task in which information counts only once it has been broadcast to a shared surface, so that the group discovers how much each of them was holding that never made it to the surface, and then has to decide whether the unbroadcast material was conscious or merely stored — I would welcome it, provided it does not eat the debrief. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it: the student who has met the paradigm before and announces it; the one whose reports are systematically unreliable; the projector timing that ruins the flash; the group that converges on a single introspective verdict by social pressure. And be frank about the feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce — my own worry is that anything a participant can tell me about is by definition reportable, so the exercise may stage the access side of the distinction vividly and only gesture at the phenomenal side, leaving me to supply the rest. I want the activity, not the literature: no citations, no reading list, and nothing that assumes the students will look anything up.
