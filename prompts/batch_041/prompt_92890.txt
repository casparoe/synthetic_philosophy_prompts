I'm writing a chapter on du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness and I need to say something about how her program looks from here. Her line, as I read it: happiness is something you manage rather than something you find — keep your illusions, swap the grand passions out for steadier tastes, get your health sorted first since nothing works without it, don't try to overhaul your circumstances, and the love of study is the most dependable passion going. Now imagine she gets a briefing on three things that postdate her by a couple of centuries, and tell me what she'd actually do with each:

(a) the empirical happiness literature — self-report life-satisfaction scales, and the finding that people adapt back toward a baseline after both windfalls and disasters, so measured happiness turns out to be oddly sticky;
(b) the modern happiness toolkit — mood-tracking apps, gratitude journaling and similar deliberate exercises, and mood-enhancing drugs;
(c) the idea of adaptive preferences, i.e. that contentment with a deprived lot is often a symptom of injustice rather than the real thing.

Where would she say thats what I was after, you just lacked the instruments, and where would she say we've got happiness wrong? And does the set-point finding actually threaten her view or is she insulated from it?
