I'm giving the closing toast at a farewell dinner for my old boss, who's retiring after about thirty years running a small research shop whose whole reason for existing was to tell people their historical analogies were garbage. He's the guy who'd get a memo comparing some fresh crisis to Munich and send back a two-line note asking *which* Munich, and whether the author could name a case with the same setup that went the other way, and why nobody ever writes the memoir about the crisis that quietly fizzled because everyone kept their heads. Half the room is his former analysts, who will absolutely notice if I do the exact thing he spent his career complaining about. The other half is his family, who are lovely, have no idea what the Thucydides trap is, and don't need to.

I want the toast to be warm, obviously, he's a good man and people love him. But I also want it to actually *do* what he did — to be about why the lessons-of-history business keeps churning out confident predictions that don't hold, in a way the analysts nod at and the family still enjoys. Problem is I'm going to have to use analogies to pull it off, which is precisely the trap. So please write it so it notices that and doesn't pretend otherwise — ideally the recursion is the spine of the whole thing rather than a cute aside at the end.

Some things I want in there, because they're his actual obsessions and they're the part everyone gets wrong: that the famous cases everybody learns from are the ones that got *noticed*, so the sample is rigged from the start; that the counterfactual is quietly doing all the work and nobody ever admits it; that an analogy which can't turn out wrong isn't a lesson, it's a mood; and that the genuinely instructive cases are usually the boring non-events nobody commemorates. He also had one famous call where he leaned on an old analogy and got it right, and he'd always insist that was luck, not skill — I'd like that in there somewhere.

Don't turn it into a lecture with bullet points, it's a dinner and I've got maybe six minutes. At least one joke, at least one moment that actually lands emotionally, and keep it tight — I ramble when I'm nervous.
