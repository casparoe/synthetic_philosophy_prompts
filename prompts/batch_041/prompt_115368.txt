You are an expert in both classical Islamic philosophy — the kalām tradition, and Ashʿarite theology in particular — and the contemporary analytic metaphysics of causation, and you have sat through more interdisciplinary question periods than you care to remember. I need you to act as my behind-the-scenes preparation coach.

I am presenting a paper next month at a joint session of my department's metaphysics reading group and its Islamic thought seminar; there will be roughly forty minutes of questions, and I will have about two minutes per answer. The thesis I am defending is this: the standard reading of al-Ghazālī as an occasionalist — God the sole genuine agent, created things wholly inert, cause and effect merely conjoined in our experience — misdescribes the position he actually commits to in the Tahāfut al-falāsifa. What al-Ghazālī denies is that the connection between a cause and its effect is necessary in the philosophers' sense, that is, grounded in the natures of the things themselves. What he puts in its place is a necessity of a different kind, grounded in God's unchanging habit (ʿāda): the order of nature is a standing divine commitment, contingent in itself — God could have ordered things otherwise, and on the occasion of a miracle does — yet stable and knowable precisely because God does not change his practice. I mark the contrast with two labels of my own: constitutive necessity, which al-Ghazālī denies, and decretal necessity, which he affirms. My central claim is that this is not a relabelling of occasionalism, because on my reading created things are real secondary causes with genuine powers, whose efficacy depends at every moment on divine conservation and whose connection is fixed only by decree. My secondary claim is that this makes him an unrecognised option in the present landscape: not a Humean about laws, not a contemporary powers theorist, but something closer to a primitivist who locates the modal force of laws in a divine will.

The room will be two audiences who almost never share a room, and I want to be ready for both.

The first group are analytic metaphysicians of causation: mostly Humean regularity theorists and counterfactualists, with two or three dispositionalists and one person working on grounding and dependence. None of them has read the Tahāfut; for them al-Ghazālī is a name attached to the slogan that causation is not necessary connection, which they regard as a crude anticipation of Hume. They speak in the vocabulary of laws, counterfactuals, supervenience, explanatory idleness and the epistemology of modal knowledge, and they are quick to suspect theology of doing work that metaphysics should be doing.

The second group are Islamicists and historians of kalām. They read Arabic, they know the Tahāfut as a dialectical work aimed at the falāsifa rather than a statement of constructive doctrine, and they will be suspicious of a philosopher extracting a position from a text of that genre. They know the Ashʿarite apparatus well — atomism, substances and accidents, the denial of any real secondary causation, the vocabulary of divine habit — and they will ask why I do not simply call al-Ghazālī an Ashʿarite occasionalist with an unusually careful epistemology. They will also want to know how my reading squares with the constructive theology he sets out in his later works, and whether I am making him consistent at the cost of making him contemporary.

Before writing anything, spend some time thinking on the page about each audience: what they already believe, where they think the burden of proof lies, which moves they regard as cheap, and what they will hear when I say necessity, power or habit. Resist the temptation to make the Islamicist objections purely textual and the metaphysician objections purely technical; the objections that will hurt are the ones where a textual point has metaphysical teeth or vice versa.

Then give me a preparation sheet — not an essay, and not a defence of my view:

1. Eight to ten objections, ranked by how much damage they would do if they landed, not by how likely they are to be raised. For each: which audience raises it and in what form; a reply I can actually say out loud in about two minutes; a note on the weakest joint in that reply; and whether I should concede the point up front rather than fight it.
2. The one or two objections that both audiences will raise, each in its own vocabulary. Tell me which version is the more dangerous, and why.
3. The three objections I should prepare thoroughly, and the ones I can safely deflect in a single sentence.
4. What I should concede in my opening remarks, before questions begin, to take the wind out of the most predictable attacks.
5. The question I am hoping nobody asks, and why it is dangerous.
6. The one reply that has to land for the thesis to survive the session, and what I should do if it does not.

Give me the strongest version of each objection, not the version I can answer easily, and phrase each one the way its author would actually phrase it. Do not give me a reading list, bibliography or citations; if you want to refer to an interpretive position, describe it generically (the standard occasionalist reading, the dialectical reading of the Tahāfut) rather than attributing it to a named scholar, unless you are confident of the attribution. Do not invent quotations, page references, or Arabic technical terms you are unsure of; use only terminology that is common currency in this discussion, and gloss it where you use it.
