Write a satirical advertorial of roughly 650 words — headline, byline, and a small-type notice that it is a paid placement — for a trade publication whose readers are compliance officers and machine-learning engineers. The voice is that of a confident corporate copywriter, and the piece is written from inside the vendor's own marketing rather than from outside it: deadpan throughout, no winking, no narrator stepping forward to tell us what the joke is. The target is a practice, not a person — the fantasy that one more technical fix will dissolve the fairness problem, and the compliance culture that has learned to advertise the impossibility away. Invent every institution you mention; do not name real companies, regulators, scholars, or papers, and cite no literature.

Treat the following set-up as given, and elaborate it rather than explain it:

- The vendor is Halyard Analytics, Inc., and its mark is "Parity+ Certified," awarded to hiring and credit-scoring models that, in the brochure's phrasing, satisfy "within-group calibration, equalized error rates across groups, and predictive parity — all three, by construction."
- The mechanism is a two-stage pipeline. Before any model sees an application, a "Base-Rate Alignment Layer" runs, described in the brochure as a community outreach and applicant-preparation program, complete with the vendor's own recruitment statistics. After scoring, a "Dynamic Threshold Equalizer" adjusts decisions — which the brochure calls "an individually calibrated lottery of opportunity" and defends on the ground that two identical applicants may receive different outcomes, since uniformity of treatment "was never the goal."
- A state ordinance, the Algorithmic Hiring Disclosure Act, requires every certified tool to carry a "Fairness Facts" label, modeled on a nutrition label, on which the vendor must declare which of the three properties the tool does not have. The brochure reproduces Halyard's label, and the label declares no exemptions.
- Include a pull quote from the Director of Equity Analytics at a fictional regional workforce board, a boxed "How Parity+ Works" sidebar laying out the three steps, and at least one footnote that quietly concedes something the body text depends on.

The humor has to come from getting the mathematics right, so that someone who has audited or built such a system laughs and finds the satire fair rather than wincing. The result being satirized is the one holding that within-group calibration, equalized false-positive and false-negative rates across groups, and equal precision across groups cannot hold jointly when the groups' base rates differ, so a certification claiming all three is claiming something arithmetic forbids. Do not state that objection in your own voice, and do not let the brochure confess it; the label, the sidebar, the outreach statistics, the pull quote, and the footnote must carry the whole burden. A general reader who knows nothing about the field should still be able to follow the piece and feel that something is off, without being able to say exactly what.
