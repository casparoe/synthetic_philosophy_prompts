I work on the historical Jesus, and the long argument over the criteria of authenticity — dissimilarity, embarrassment, multiple attestation, coherence, Aramaic and Palestinian environment, and the rest — has been stuck for a generation in a way I would like diagnosed rather than settled. The critics appear to have won on paper: it is now widely conceded that the criteria are neither necessary nor sufficient for a saying's going back to Jesus, that dissimilarity in particular selects for material the earliest communities had least reason to invent and so yields a distorted, artificially eccentric figure, and that the whole enterprise presupposes a model of transmission — discrete units of tradition, isolable and testable one by one — that historians of other ancient figures would not accept. The case was pressed in the early 1970s and largely ignored, restated at book length in a 2012 collection that announced the criteria's demise, and by now the objections are near-commonplace. Yet the practice has barely changed: commentaries still sort pericopes into authentic and inauthentic, dissertations still run the criteria, and no replacement has been agreed on. The alternatives on offer — reading the Gospels as evidence about the communities that told the stories, social-memory accounts of how traditions about a recent figure form and mutate, holistic judgments about the general shape of Jesus's career instead of the pedigree of individual sayings — strike their critics as either abandoning history for something else or smuggling the criteria back in under new names.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the impasse is not really about which criteria survive scrutiny. It is that at least two different questions are being answered under one heading — what a particular first-century Galilean said and did, and how the earliest traditions about him took shape and functioned — with standards of evidence internal to each question, so that what settles one is nearly irrelevant to the other; and perhaps that the sides disagree about something still more basic, namely whether the Gospels are evidence about a person or about a movement, and whether "authentic" is a property of individual units at all. Diagnose it. Tell me what kind of disagreement this is, using what the critics and the practitioners actually claim rather than the labels they wear: verbal, substantive, two questions under one name, or a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work (whether the criteria are probabilistic indicators being used as proofs, say, and whether any evidential framework could make the aggregate judgment rigorous). Say whether the impasse shows the question is ill-posed or merely hard, and then what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a change of question, a formal result about what the evidence can and cannot support — or argue that none should be expected, and why.
