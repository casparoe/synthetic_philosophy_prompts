I'm preparing a handout for a graduate reading group on decision theory under risk, and I want a decision tree that takes a reader from the bare acceptance that the expected value of a gamble can be undefined — not infinite, but genuinely without a unique sum — to a named position on what to do with such gambles. The point is to sort people by what they say about concrete cases, not by which label they like, so no node should just ask whether they are an expectationalist or a non-expectationalist.

To fix the cases, here is the kind of gamble I mean. A fair coin is flipped until it lands heads; if it takes n tosses, the payoff is arranged so that the expected value is a conditionally convergent series. Taken in the natural order of outcomes, the series sums to a specific finite number — for the standard version, sometimes called the Pasadena game, the natural logarithm of two — but rearranging the outcomes can make the series sum to any real number you like, or diverge. A stronger variant, sometimes called the Altadena game, makes the positive and negative parts of the series both diverge, so there is no natural ordering that gives a finite sum at all. I want the tree to handle both the weaker and the stronger cases, because some positions that work for the first fail for the second.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. First the questions, then the combinations of answers they allow, then the actual positions located in that space, with unoccupied combinations noted and briefly diagnosed — is nobody there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? Each branch node should be a question a reader could answer without already knowing which position it leads to. For example, one node might ask whether the undefinedness of the expected value means the gamble has no determinate value at all, or whether it has a determinate value that the usual expectation formula fails to capture. Another might ask, if the value is determinate, whether it is given by a canonical summation convention — the natural order of outcomes — or by a rule that is invariant under rearrangements. Another might ask, if there is no determinate value, whether that makes the gamble incomparable with sure options, or whether a non-expectation principle can still rank it; and if a non-expectation principle ranks it, whether that principle is dominance-based, risk-based, or something else. A further node might ask whether the undefinedness is a defect in the gamble — the gamble is ill-posed — or a defect in expected utility theory — the theory is incomplete.

The leaves should be named positions, each with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I expect the tree to include at least the following, though you should add any others the space contains: the view that expected utility theory is simply silent and the option is incomparable; the view that the gamble has the value given by the natural ordering of outcomes; the view that the value is undefined because rearrangement can yield anything, so the gamble is not comparable; the view that the gamble is the limit of its finite truncations and inherits their limiting expected value; the view that a dominance-like principle ranks the gamble despite the undefined expectation; the view that undefinedness is a reason to avoid the gamble; the view that the problem shows expected utility theory is incomplete and needs a richer theory; and the view that the gamble is not a legitimate option at all. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate — for instance, the natural-order view and the truncation view may coincide for the weaker game but come apart for the stronger one, and the silence view and the incompleteness view may be hard to tell apart from the answers alone.

The audience is a graduate reading group in decision theory, so the tree can be technical, but every node should still be answerable in plain terms. Aim for ten to twelve leaves. Please write the whole thing in continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — the way one would write an email or a letter.
