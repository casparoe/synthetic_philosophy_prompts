I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy, in the late stages of a dissertation on the reception of Stoic ethics in the contemporary literature on self-management, and I have been asked to open a graduate conference paper on the dichotomy of control with a short joke. The audience will be specialists and near-specialists in ancient philosophy and ethics. What I need is a joke whose humour is internal to the doctrine — one that someone who has actually worked on this material would find funny, rather than the standard Stoicism-flavoured fare about statues, memento mori, and the serenity of not checking one's email.

The most promising feature, I think, is a structural one. The dichotomy tells us that our judgements, impulses, and desires are up to us and that everything else is not, and it is meant to be therapeutic precisely because it settles what we should and should not invest ourselves in. But the dichotomy is itself a judgement about where the boundary falls, and judgements are on the up-to-us side, so whether the dichotomy is a dichotomy appears to be one of the things the dichotomy is supposed to adjudicate. The popular revival compounds the problem: it adds a third category of things we control only partially, and it converts the doctrine into a practice with progress and setbacks, so that one can now be anxious about whether one is applying the remedy correctly — and therefore fail at the one discipline whose entire point is that this sort of failure is a mistake.

Please write a single joke, short enough to deliver from a lectern and recognisable as having a setup and a punchline, that turns on either the self-application problem or the transformation of the doctrine into a performance metric. It should land without explanation, and it should not be a joke that would work equally well for any other self-help regimen. I would like the audience to conclude that the speaker knows this material, not merely that the speaker has heard of it.
