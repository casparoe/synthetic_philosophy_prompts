I'm in a metaphysics reading group that has now spent an unreasonable number of weeks on the statue and the clay, and I'd like to close the term with a poem rather than another round of the same four objections. The puzzle, in case it's worth restating: a quantity of bronze is cast into a figure, and afterwards we seem forced to say that the bronze and the figure are in the same place, made of the same atoms in the same arrangement, indistinguishable to any instrument, and yet two things — because the bronze would have survived being squashed, or melted down into an ingot, or recast into something else, and the figure would not. Different conditions of identity, so they can't be one thing; and yet nothing about the atoms explains how there could be two. I've sat through enough comment threads on this to be suspicious of the move where the problem is declared to dissolve into a fact about our words, since that still leaves us unable to say which of the two countings tracks the world. The wrinkle I keep circling is that sameness of matter and sameness of form come apart in both directions: melt the figure down and recast it in the original mould and you have, by most accounts, the same bronze and a new figure; cast a copy from that same mould in different bronze and you have, by most accounts, the same figure and different bronze; melt it into an ingot and you have all the bronze and no figure at all. I want a poem that makes that independence feel like a fact about the world rather than a quirk of our nouns, and that leaves the grounding question open instead of quietly answering it.

So: a pantoum, four to six quatrains, done properly — the second and fourth lines of each stanza returning as the first and third of the next — and closed in the standard way, with the final quatrain drawing its second and fourth lines from the opening quatrain so that the poem's last line is literally its first. Pantoums need not rhyme, so don't force it or trade sense for a rhyme. Give it a concrete subject: a figure on a plinth, a foundry, a museum's records, the interval when the metal is liquid and there is no figure at all, whatever the poem needs. It should work as a poem for someone who has never heard of any of this — about casting and copying, restoration, memory, the way one thing can be present twice.

The philosophy has to live in the repetition. Put the lines that come back at the places where the two countings diverge — lines whose subject is deliberately left unfixed, so that the same words, in a new stanza, are about the bronze in one pass and about the figure in another — and let someone who knows the debate point to the exact line where the two sets of identity conditions cross, without the poem ever saying 'constitution', 'sortal', or 'coincidence'. No thesis in the last line. If it can end on its own first line and still leave the question open, that's the poem. If you title it, let the title read like a museum caption — material, date, provenance — and let the ambiguity sit there rather than in the verse.
