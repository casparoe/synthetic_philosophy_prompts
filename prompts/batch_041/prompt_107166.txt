For several years now I have been thinking about the problem of evil on my own — I have no training in philosophy, only a shelf of books and a habit of arguing with friends — and I keep running into the phrase "the best of all possible worlds." I have seen it offered as a serious answer to the question of why a good God permits suffering; I have seen it used with open contempt, as a way of mocking anyone who tries to console the bereaved; and I have seen it used almost casually, as a kind of consolation in its own right. It has occurred to me that these people cannot all mean the same thing, and that some of them may not mean anything definite at all. I would like a careful inventory of the distinct claims the sentence could be making, so that I can tell which one is in play in any given argument.

For each reading, please make clear what work the word "best" is doing — what it measures, and whether it measures the world as a whole or the life of some particular creature inside it. Distinguish the reading on which the claim is about what God could have chosen differently from the reading on which it is a claim about what is good or bad for us, since these seem to come apart: a world might be optimal in the eyes of a cosmic accountant while remaining wretched for the people living in it. Say also whether the sentence is meant as a description of the world, as a justification of God, or merely as a consolation offered to someone in pain, and whether each reading holds up on its own terms.

I am especially puzzled by one thing. If the possible worlds form an infinite series in which every world can be exceeded by a better one, then there seems to be no best at all — just as there is no largest number — and the phrase would name something that cannot exist. I want to know how a serious user of the slogan escapes this, and whether the escape costs them the comforting part of the claim. Then tell me which readings are near-trivial, which are substantive and defensible, which are simply false, and which one a person is most likely invoking when they use the phrase to reassure someone who is grieving. Lay it out as a menu with verdicts rather than choosing a favourite; I want to be able to look at any use of the phrase and say which item on the menu it is.
