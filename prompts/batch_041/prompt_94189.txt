I am preparing a short position statement for a workshop on the epistemology of markets, and before I circulate it I would like an audit of its internal consistency. Here is roughly where I have landed.

(1) A price is a compressed summary of dispersed private knowledge. The price system outperforms any centralized alternative at aggregating such knowledge, and a quoted price is therefore among the better epistemic instruments we have for claims about the future.

(2) I accept the no-trade result in the following form: where traders share a prior, the structure of the market is common knowledge, and the initial allocation offers no gains from trade independent of information, no trade can arise from private information alone. Any offer made to me at the prevailing price reveals the offeror's information before I can act on it, and the bare fact that someone is willing to take the other side of my position is, if anything, reason for suspicion rather than confidence.

(3) In practice markets exhibit enormous volume and prices that move on order flow. I take this as evidence that the aggregation described in (1) is working, on the grounds that heavy trading is heavy information processing.

(4) Two equally rational and equally well-informed participants can hold different valuations of the same asset. This disagreement is not a defect of the market but a precondition of one.

(5) What distinguishes a price from a poll or an expert forecast is that it is backed by money. Its epistemic authority derives from the costliness of the commitment.

(6) Prices can be informative only to the extent that those who gather information can profit from doing so, which requires that prices not fully reveal what is known. There must be residual noise, and someone must be trading at the wrong price in order for the informed to earn a return.

(7) Aggregation improves accuracy when individual errors are independent and roughly centered on the truth.

Please tell me which of these are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be. For the apparent tensions, state the distinction that dissolves them, so that I can see I am not merely papering over a real conflict. For the genuine ones, rank them by severity, and for each say precisely what would have to be revised and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. I would also like to know which single revision would resolve the greatest number of the genuine tensions, and what it would leave unresolved. If any of the commitments cannot be retained in its present form at all, say so plainly rather than hedging it into compatibility. I would rather be told one thing to abandon than five things to qualify.
