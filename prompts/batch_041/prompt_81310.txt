Here is the question:

"(a) Explain how epistemicism and supervaluationism each account for borderline cases, and say what each view implies about bivalence and about the law of excluded middle. (b) Consider the sentence 'there is some number n such that n grains of sand make a heap and n − 1 grains do not.' What verdict does each view deliver about the truth value of that sentence, and which verdict better fits the intuition that vague predicates have no sharp boundaries? (c) Briefly: which of the two views is better placed to handle higher-order vagueness?"

And here is the student's answer:

"Both views agree that vagueness is a semantic phenomenon — it has to do with the meaning of predicates such as 'heap' and 'bald', not with any haziness in the objects themselves — but they disagree about whether a borderline case has a truth value.

According to epistemicism, every borderline sentence has a determinate truth value. Bivalence holds: for any n, 'n grains of sand make a heap' is either true or false, and the boundary between the heaps and the non-heaps is perfectly sharp. What explains the appearance of indeterminacy is the inexactness of our knowledge. On the margin-of-error model, a belief counts as knowledge only if it is true in all cases sufficiently close to the actual one; because the predicate switches at a single grain, the cases on either side of the boundary are too close together for us to know which side we are on. The sorites paradox is then diagnosed as having a false premise: exactly one instance of the induction step is false, though we cannot know which. Vagueness is thus a form of ignorance rather than a gap in the world.

Supervaluationism denies that borderline sentences have truth values. There are many admissible ways of making a predicate precise, and a borderline sentence is true on some precisifications and false on others, so it is neither true nor false. Truth is identified with supertruth — true on every admissible precisification — and falsity with superfalsity. Since every precisification is itself fully classical, the law of excluded middle is valid, and indeed supervaluationism preserves all the classical logical truths and the classical consequence relation. And because each precisification assigns a definite truth value to every sentence, every sentence comes out true or false once the interpretation is fixed, so the semantics is bivalent; all that has changed is that truth is relativised to a precisification. One genuine cost is that the connectives are no longer truth-functional: a disjunction can be supertrue even though neither disjunct is supertrue.

When we turn to the sentence 'there is some number n such that n grains make a heap and n − 1 do not', the supervaluationist has an appealing line. Since no particular boundary is admissible on every precisification, no sentence of the form 'the boundary is at n' is supertrue, and the existential claim about a sharp boundary is not true either. The view therefore vindicates the intuition that vague predicates have no sharp boundaries. The epistemicist, by contrast, has to insist that the sentence is true, and can only add that we are unable to locate the boundary.

On higher-order vagueness the supervaluationist is in the weaker position, since the notion of an admissible precisification is itself vague, and it is not clear that this can be handled without an infinite regress. The epistemicist can simply say that vagueness about where the boundary lies is more of the same ignorance, subject to the same margin-of-error treatment. On balance I would give the edge to epistemicism, though the supervaluationist's treatment of the sorites is the more natural one."
