I've read it six times now and I honestly can't tell wether I understand it or I've just memorized it. Here's the passage, copied out of the translation he lent me:

"For which purpose we must see what a lie is. For not every one who says a false thing lies, if he believes or opines that to be true which he says. Now between believing and opining there is this difference, that sometimes he who believes feels that he does not know that which he believes, (although he may know himself to be ignorant of a thing, and yet have no doubt at all concerning it, if he most firmly believes it:) whereas he who opines, thinks he knows that which he does not know. Now whoever utters that which he holds in his mind either as belief or as opinion, even though it be false, he lies not. ... Wherefore, that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind. Whence also the heart of him who lies is said to be double; that is, there is a double thought: the one, of that thing which he either knows or thinks to be true and does not produce; the other, of that thing which he produces instead thereof, knowing or thinking it to be false. Whence it comes to pass, that he may say a false thing and yet not lie, if he thinks it to be so as he says although it be not so; and, that he may say a true thing, and yet lie, if he thinks it to be false and utters it for true, although in reality it be so as he utters it. For from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie. Therefore he who utters a false thing for a true, which however he opines to be true, may be called erring and rash: but he is not rightly said to lie; because he has not a double heart when he utters it, neither does he wish to deceive, but is deceived."

And a line or two after that: "But the fault of him who lies, is, the desire of deceiving in the uttering of his mind."

So what's breaking my brain: he says you can say something false and not be lying, and then that you can say something true and still be lying, because what matters is what's going on in your head and not whether your words match the world. Fine, I buy the first half. But the "double heart" definition he gives right before that seems to be about holding onto a true thought you don't say while producing a false thought you do say — and in the say-a-true-thing-while-thinking-it-false case there's no true thought he's sitting on, so the definition doesn't seem to fit the very example he's using it for. Then he turns around and says the liar's fault is "the desire of deceiving," which sounds like it's about wanting someone to believe something false, full stop. Those two tests disagree on a case I actually run into: my teammate asks whether I looked at his PR and I say "I've been heads down on the migration all week," which is true, and I am fully hoping he'll take it as yes. I'm trying to produce a false belief in him, but nothing I said is something I think is false. Is that a lie for Augustine or not? Same question for a joke that gets taken seriously, and for a support reply that's technically accurate and designed to be misread. What I want out of this is his actual test for a lie, stated precisely enough that I can run it on examples like those — plus what "opines" is doing in the first sentence (a guy in the group said it's just "thinks he knows," but then the rest of the paragraph starts getting weird).
