Next month I am to interview, for our departmental colloquium, a social theorist who has spent his career defending a contemporary version of the thesis Weber made famous: that the values a society holds are among the causes of its economic institutions. His paradigm case remains the Protestant ethic — more precisely, the this-worldly asceticism he locates in certain Calvinist sects — which he holds helped bring about a spirit of capitalism that its adherents neither intended nor foresaw, and which then reproduced itself in an order that no longer required the religious convictions that had first animated it. He is impatient with the objection that he has shown at most a correlation, and with the further objection that the ethic's disappearance alongside the capitalism it supposedly caused shows the causal story to run backwards; he says that both mistake his claim for a cruder one than he is making.

Please draft the eight to ten questions I should put to him, in the order I should ask them, each followed by a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, and arranged so that the easier escape routes are closed before the harder questions arrive. Every question should force him to commit where his position is otherwise underdetermined; none should be answerable by someone who does not hold it; none should invite him merely to restate the thesis.

I want him, first, to locate his claim on a distinction I have found useful in teaching this material: values as a standing background condition (necessary for the institution to arise, but not by itself productive of it), values as a proximate trigger (what tips a society into the new form), and values as a selector (what determines which of several live paths a society takes, given its circumstances). Press him on whether he can coherently claim more than one of these.

Second, the mechanism: how does a belief held by individual agents generate an outcome at the level of institutions? Ask whether he owes a micro-story, or whether he thinks a culture can be a cause in its own right rather than a shorthand for aggregated individual states.

Third, the gap between reasons and causes: that a norm was a reason for someone to act does not yet show that it caused him to act. Ask whether he is trading on that ambiguity, and whether a normative item can enter a causal explanation at all, or only its being believed.

Fourth, the rival explanations — reverse causation, a common third factor, and the possibility that his Protestant ethic is an ideal-constructed composite that no actual group ever embodied, so that the ideal-type method quietly renders the thesis unfalsifiable.

Fifth, a case I think has been under-discussed in this connection and which I would like to see him confront: a single religious community that splits into two branches differing on precisely the doctrine he treats as load-bearing, the assurance of election, while their economic circumstances, kinship networks, and political conditions remain as nearly identical as any historical case permits, and whose economic trajectories then diverge. Ask whether he would regard such a comparison as close to decisive, and what he would predict before the outcome is known.

Sixth, elective affinity: whether his claim is stronger than the claim that the ethic and the economic form merely fitted one another, and if so what the difference comes to in observable terms.

Seventh, transmission: by what route the values persist across generations, and whether that persistence is itself causal or merely inertial.

Finally, disconfirmation: what evidence would make him abandon the thesis; and, to close, whether his causal claim bears at all on whether a culture can be praised or blamed, or whether he keeps the causal and the normative registers strictly apart.

I want no questions about his intellectual formation, and nothing that lets him answer with a summary of the thesis. Where a standard reply exists, the follow-up should be the reply to the reply.
