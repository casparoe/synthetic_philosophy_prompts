I'm a backend engineer at a mid-sized machine-learning company, and I have no formal training in philosophy — what I know about the science/non-science line comes from blog posts, one half-listened-to audiobook on my commute, and arguing with a colleague who did a philosophy minor. I'd like you to draft a satirical piece for me, because the version I'd write myself would be a rant rather than a joke.

The piece should be an internal engineering RFC, written in the flat house style of a real design document — Summary, Motivation, Definitions, Policy, Exceptions, Appendix — of around 800 to 900 words, with numbered sections. In it, a fictional team proposes a "Falsifiability Gate": before any claim about the product may appear in an internal memo, a customer email, or a sales deck, the team making the claim must register in advance an observation that would show the claim to be false. The rest of the document then describes, in the same even procedural voice, the waiver process, the escalation ladder for when a failed prediction gets blamed on the test harness rather than on the claim, and the schedule of internal artifacts that are permanently exempt from the gate. Give the RFC a repeal condition that nobody could ever satisfy.

Here is what I need you to get right, and it's the whole reason I'm asking instead of writing it myself. The humor has to come out of the actual structure of the debate over falsification, not out of a generic register of corporate nonsense. So:

- The gate is meant to separate science from non-science, and the RFC should never quite notice that this is not the same as separating true from false, or sense from nonsense. A claim that passes the gate can be false; a claim that fails it can be perfectly true. Let the document behave as though failing the gate amounts to being wrong, without ever asserting it.
- Nothing is ever tested alone. Make this visible in the procedures: a prediction is registered against a claim, but when it fails there is always a list of other things it might have been — the eval set, the prompt template, the data pipeline, the definition of the metric — and the escalation ladder should give each of them a rung, in a way that is formally fair and practically inexhaustible.
- Some commitments in the document should be placed beyond revision. Say so out loud, in the reasonable voice of someone protecting a load-bearing assumption, and then handle the difference between legitimately adjusting a supporting assumption and rescuing a dying theory with a procedural rule that, on inspection, permits everything.
- The RFC should assume, without argument, that research proceeds by bold conjecture and attempted refutation — and then quietly document an incentive structure under which nobody registers a bold prediction twice. A short section on normal operations should let a reader glimpse the rival picture, long stretches of puzzle-solving punctuated by anomalies that get filed as tickets rather than treated as refutations, without the document ever conceding it.
- The RFC should be as hard on itself as on anything else. It is a claim about what the company ought to do, and it should be subject to its own gate in a way that leaves it unavoidably exempt. Don't have the narrator remark on this; just build it in.

Include two or three concrete claims under review — the kinds of things this company really says about its models, such as that a model's chain of thought reflects its actual computation, or that a benchmark measures what it is named after — and make the hardest-to-falsify ones the claims the company would defend to the death. Add one short block quotation from a fictional staff engineer dissenting in the appendix, an invented Bayesian complaint about what a failed prediction actually does to a probability.

Constraints: no citations, no quotations from real works, no real people or real organizations named — everything in the piece should be fictional, including the name on the RFC. Stay deadpan: no winking, no italics for irony, no "of course" or "needless to say." Don't state any of the standard objections outright; the reader should be able to tell what is being sent up only from what the procedures do. A reader who has never heard of the demarcation problem should still follow it, and a reader who has should find it uncomfortably accurate.
