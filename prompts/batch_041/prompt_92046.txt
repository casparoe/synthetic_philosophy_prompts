Question 3 (short essay, 20 marks)

A female digger wasp drags a paralysed cricket to her burrow, leaves it at the entrance while she goes inside to check that all is well, comes back out, and then drags the cricket in. If you nudge the cricket a few inches away while she is inside, she surfaces, finds no cricket at the entrance, hunts for it, drags it back to the entrance — and goes inside to check the burrow again. Repeat as often as you like; she never catches on.

In the lecture we used this case to distinguish two kinds of flexibility in a control system:

- re-entry flexibility: the same routine can be left and re-entered at a different stage (or restarted) depending on conditions the agent can detect. The stages at which such conditions are tested we called checkpoints, and the number of checkpoints in a routine we called its grain.
- revision flexibility: the routine itself can be altered — stages added, dropped or reordered — so that the agent can do something for which it has no stage.

We also noted that in a fixed environment a high-grain checkpoint agent and a revision-flexible agent may be behaviourally indistinguishable: both get the cricket into the burrow, so success on its own doesn't reveal which sort of system you're looking at.

(a) Explain the distinction and say what the wasp's routine looks like in these terms.
(b) An engineer claims: "The wasp only looks sphexish because her grain is too low. Give her enough checkpoints and she'll stop being sphexish." Assess this claim.
(c) What, if anything, does any of this show about free will?

Student's answer

The wasp's behaviour is a fixed sequence of stages with a single checkpoint, so her grain is 1: when she surfaces she tests whether the cricket is at the entrance, and if it isn't she re-enters the routine at the fetching stage. She never adds a stage, so she has re-entry flexibility of the thinnest kind and no revision flexibility at all. That's why the trick works — nothing in her routine represents the possibility that the cricket is being moved on purpose, so each pass through the loop is a fresh instance of the same routine rather than a lesson learnt.

The two kinds of flexibility come apart because the first concerns where a fixed sequence may be entered and the second concerns whether the sequence can change. A high-grain agent can be steered round almost any obstacle, but the space of stages it can occupy is still the one it started with. That said, the engineer is basically right. Grain is a matter of degree and there's no principled stopping point: put a checkpoint at every stage and there's no fixed routine left to be stuck in, because the agent's behaviour is then settled stage by stage by what it finds, and that is what flexibility consists in. The wasp's failure is an engineering failure — insufficient grain.

As for free will, the case shows that the interesting question isn't determinism but the shape of the control structure, and that sphexishness comes in degrees — we're sphexish in plenty of our subroutines, which is why the story is funny rather than alien. Dennett's reading of the wasp is deflationary: she's a mechanism and nothing more. But Hofstadter's intentional stance is available here, and if we can predict the wasp by attributing to her a desire to provision her burrow, then she has that desire; a creature with desires isn't a mere mechanism, so the wasp isn't really sphexish after all. The term only applies to systems we refuse to interpret. So the case doesn't settle whether we're free — it just tells us rigidity comes in degrees.
