In a graduate seminar I co-teach on Indian epistemology, a guest lecturer opened his session on Jain anekāntavāda with the parable of the blind men and the elephant: each man, feeling a different part, declares the elephant to be like a rope, a wall, a fan, or a spear, and each is said to be partly right and partly wrong, the moral being that reality has many sides and no single report exhausts it. He then drew what struck me as a considerably stronger conclusion — that on the Jain view each of the blind men's reports is a genuine and equally legitimate perspective, that the whole point is the impossibility of adjudicating between them, and that anekāntavāda is thus an ancient anticipation of a thoroughgoing pluralism in which no one is ever in a position to call another's view false. Several students have since written this up as the doctrine, and I would like a careful audit before I have to respond to it.

Please lay the analogy out explicitly — a table of correspondences will do — saying what plays the role of the blind men, the elephant, its parts, the shared object, the rope and the wall, and the act of declaring. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in whether the parable locates the multiplicity in the right place: it appears to place it in the perceivers' blindness, whereas I understand anekāntavāda to place it in the object's own infinite determination, and to distinguish the standpoint doctrine, which concerns what a single standpoint can assert, from the conditional-predication doctrine and its seven modes, which concerns how any such assertion must be qualified. If that is right, say what the parable gets wrong about the relation between the two, and whether the plurality of reports corresponds to the standpoint doctrine at all. I would also like to know what, if anything, corresponds to the elephant as a whole — since on the doctrine there is an omniscient standpoint the parable seems to leave out. And then, most importantly, tell me whether the lecturer's conclusion — equal legitimacy, no adjudication, no one entitled to call another mistaken — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether it was the parable, and not the doctrine, doing the work. If the analogy cannot carry that conclusion, say what a better analogy would be, or whether the doctrine is better left without one.
