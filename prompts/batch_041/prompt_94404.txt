I have just come from a long argument with a friend about the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem and strategy-proofness, and I would like your help adjudicating the dispute that grew out of it. The occasion was a proposal for a departmental election. The proposed rule is this: each voter assigns every candidate a score from 0 to 5; the two candidates with the highest total scores advance to a runoff; in the runoff, each voter is counted as voting for whichever finalist she scored higher, with equal scores split evenly; the finalist with more votes wins. My friend and a third friend, whom I will call M and N, took opposite sides, and I could not referee the exchange. It went roughly as follows.

M: The rule is strategy-proof. Honest scoring is weakly dominant. You can only affect which two candidates reach the runoff, and raising a candidate's score can only help that candidate. Since the runoff then selects the finalist you prefer, you never gain by scoring insincerely.

N: That is false. Suppose your true scores are A=5, B=3, C=0. The other voters' scores are such that, if you score honestly, the runoff is A versus C, and C wins. If you instead score B=5, the runoff becomes B versus C, and B wins. You prefer A to B, but you prefer B to C, and you know A cannot beat C, so you gain by making B the finalist. You had to report B as equal to A, which you do not believe, in order to change the finalists. That is a strategic vote.

M: It is not a misreport. The ballot asks for scores, not a ranking. If you prefer B to C, and A cannot win, giving B a 5 is a truthful way of saying that B is the candidate you want to advance. There is no fact of the matter about a true score independent of the strategic situation. Strategy-proofness is about misrepresenting a fixed ordinal preference; your example assumes a ranking the rule never asks for.

N: There is a fact of the matter: you prefer A to B to C. That is a fact about your preferences over outcomes. The rule may ask for scores, but the voters still have preferences over who wins. If you give B a 5, you are saying you are indifferent between A and B, which you are not. The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem tells us that any non-dictatorial rule with at least three alternatives and unrestricted domain can be manipulated. Your rule is not exempt just because it uses cardinal input.

M: The theorem assumes the rule takes ordinal rankings. Our rule takes cardinal scores. The theorem does not apply. And your counterexample assumes I know the other voters' scores. Strategy-proofness is about dominant strategies, not about what I can do with full information. If I do not know the other scores, honest scoring is the safest.

N: The theorem's assumptions not applying does not mean the rule is strategy-proof; you need a separate proof, and you have not given one. Your example shows the rule is not dominant-strategy incentive-compatible. If strategy-proofness is weakened to Bayesian incentive compatibility, it can still fail for some beliefs. And the runoff does not rescue it: it only changes which two candidates you are choosing between; it does not remove the incentive to manipulate who reaches the runoff.

M: Even if there is some profile where a voter could gain, that does not make the rule bad. The theorem says every non-dictatorial rule has such a profile. The relevant question is whether the rule is more robust than plurality. The runoff protects against vote splitting, which is a real virtue. You are treating a theoretical possibility as a practical defect.

N: I am treating strategy-proofness as the property you claimed it had. If you now say the rule is not strategy-proof but has other virtues, fine. But you began by saying it was strategy-proof. The counterexample shows it is not. The theorem is relevant because it tells you not to expect a non-dictatorial rule to be strategy-proof; your rule is a case in point.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. In particular: (i) Is M right that the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem does not apply to a cardinal rule, and if so, what follows? (ii) Is N right that the example is a genuine manipulation, or is M right that there is no independent score to misreport? (iii) Is honest scoring weakly dominant as M claims, or does N's counterexample refute that? (iv) Does the runoff make the rule more robust in the way M suggests, or does it leave the strategic problem intact as N says? (v) What, if anything, are both missing? Rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and explain the basis for each rating. Do not simply declare an overall winner; I want the specific points separated.
