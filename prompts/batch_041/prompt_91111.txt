I run a four-person threat-response office at a firm of about four thousand employees. We receive roughly a dozen credible extortion demands a year: a party claims to hold material or to be able to harm us, names a price, and sets a deadline of twenty-four to seventy-two hours. The sums run from fifty thousand to five million, and the consequences often fall on people who are not parties to the dispute — customers, employees, a supplier. Most demands are ordinary, in the sense that paying or refusing visibly changes what happens next. Two recurring shapes are not, and I have no procedure for them.

In the first, the demand arrives with the claim that the sender has already settled, by a process we cannot observe, whether the harm lands on us or on an unrelated third party, and that the price is the same either way. On that account our answer cannot change whether we are harmed at all; it can only determine whether we turn out to be the sort of party who pays. In the second, the note claims that the sender pays parties like us in the situations where it does not ask us for money, and that whether it pays us then depends on what we would do now, if it asked. We cannot check the claim and cannot afford to dismiss it.

Our outside advisor says that in both shapes my decision cannot affect today's outcome, and that what it can affect is which of these situations we keep being placed in, because the senders are selecting for a particular kind of responder — and that people who study decisions disagree about whether that consideration is a reason or a confusion.

Please write a decision procedure my office can actually run. Fix the questions we ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with a no; and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values or law rather than decision theory. It must be runnable in under an hour by people with no formal training in the area, produce a written record the board can read, and be honest about which steps are judgments dressed up as procedure. Address in particular what it should do about the selection consideration: whether to treat being the kind of party who refuses as an asset to be protected, how a commitment can be made credible when the only party we can bind is ourselves, and what to do when the honest answer is that we do not know whether the consideration applies.

Then run it on three demands: an ordinary pay-or-we-release demand against a subsidiary; the first of the two shapes above, where the sum is small; and a case in which the procedure tells us to pay, which I expect to dislike. Close with the questions the procedure cannot settle at all.
