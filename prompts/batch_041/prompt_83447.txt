A friend in my reading group dropped this on me and I still can't tell if it's a real refutation or a parlor trick. Heres it tidied up:

(1) Strong linguistic relativity: the concepts a person can entertain are fixed by the language they speak -- there's no thinking outside the categories your language supplies.
(2) So if two languages carve things up differently, their speakers inhabit different conceptual worlds.
(3) Suppose an anthropologist claims the community she's studying lacks our concept of, say, individual ownership, or that it obligatorily marks every statement for whether the speaker saw the thing firsthand or only heard about it (picture a language with grammatical evidentials built into every sentence).
(4) To make that claim she has to have gotten inside their scheme -- otherwise she's just projecting our categories onto them.
(5) But by (1) she can only entertain concepts her own language supplies.
(6) So either her language already had those concepts, in which case what she found was a difference in words and not in concepts, or she could never have found the difference in the first place.
(7) Either way, there is no case where anyone observes a genuine conceptual difference between two languages.
(8) Therefore strong relativity, the only version worth arguing about, can neither be stated nor detected -- it refutes itself. Only the bland "language gives thought a nudge" reading survives.

I'd like to keep (1) if I can, because I do think language shapes thought more than the nudge people admit, but the conclusion eats itself and I don't want to just hand-wave. Which line do I drop, and what does dropping it cost me? And if you say it's the anthropologist bit, tell me why that same objection doesn't also gut perfectly respectable claims about understanding other cultures or other minds -- because it feels like the same move would prove too much everywhere.
