In my graduate seminar on social choice we have just finished Gibbard–Satterthwaite, and a colleague in the department has taken to explaining the result to nonspecialists with a mechanical analogy. A voting rule, on his telling, is a machine: voters feed in their preferences, the machine aggregates them, and out comes a social ranking or a winning alternative. Strategy-proofness, he says, is the design requirement that no voter can do better by feeding in a lie than by feeding in her true preference — the machine, if well built, makes honesty each voter's best play. The theorem, he concludes, is then an engineering impossibility result: you cannot build a machine that is responsive to everyone's preferences, non-dictatorial, and immune to strategic misreporting; something has to give.

The students found this intuitive and are now asking whether it is actually accurate, and I would like a careful audit before I let the picture stand in the seminar. Please do the following. First, give me the correspondence table explicitly: for each element of the machine metaphor — the input, the output, the design requirements, the failure modes, the impossibility — say what it maps onto in the formal theorem, and flag any element of the metaphor that has no clean counterpart. Second, identify where the analogy breaks down, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the work the analogy is being asked to do from the ones that are merely decorative. Third, and most important, tell me whether the conclusion the analogy is used to support — that the impossibility is a contingent limitation of a particular design, to be engineered around — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the mechanical framing quietly imports a picture of the theorem's force that the theorem itself does not support. I am particularly interested in whether casting the result as a defect of a machine obscures the sense in which the constraints are constitutive of the problem rather than accidental features of one attempted solution, and in what a better analogy would be if the machine picture is misleading.
