Ok so I've landed on a position I'm pretty sure about, and I want you to try to actually knock it down rather than just survey the literature around it.

The position: the identity of indiscernibles is false, and false as a matter of possibility, not just as a description of how things happen to be. The case that does it for me is the old two-sphere one — an otherwise empty universe with exactly two iron spheres in it, same size, same mass, same surface texture, same everything, some distance apart. There's no fact about which one is which, but there are two of them and not one. So being numerically distinct doesn't require being qualitatively different.

But I don't want to just recite the textbook version, so here's my own twist, which is the part I actually believe. Describe that world completely and what you get is a description that is invariant under swapping the two spheres. The swap isn't a change in the world; it's a redundancy in the describing, the same situation written out twice. And I think that's exactly what it means for there to be two of something with no difference between them — the world has a symmetry that isn't a symmetry of anything, it just has more structure in the description than there are facts to go around. If that's right, then any version of the principle that says distinct things have to differ somehow is just a prejudice in favour of there always being enough facts to individuate everything.

So: what would count against this? I want the defeaters, not a summary of the debate. Which arguments, intuitions, cases, or formal results should actually move me — things like the Leibnizian move from sufficient reason, the thought that describing my world already smuggles in a thisness for each sphere, the worry that "being two metres from *that* sphere" is a property one of them has and the other doesn't, the reading on which a symmetric universe doesn't contain two individuals at all but one structure with two positions, and whatever the identical-particles business in quantum mechanics is supposed to show here. Sort them from fatal to merely awkward. Tell me which of them the committed defender of my view would themselves concede as counting against them, and which they'd wave away as begging the question. And be honest about whether my position actually risks anything or whether I've set it up so nothing could touch it — and if it's insulated, whether that's a real problem for a claim like this or just what you'd expect for a claim about possibility.

I'll tell you what would genuinely move me, since that's the bit I keep going back and forth on: if it turned out that any complete description of that world has to include a bare thisness for each sphere — that I need the very haecceity I'm denying just in order to say there are two spheres there at all — then my case presupposes the thing it's supposed to refute and I'd have to drop it. I don't know if that's true and I suspect it's the crux.

One more thing: please seperate out what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's a live disagreement, and don't hand me one camp's line dressed up as the consensus. I've definately read enough popular stuff on this to be suspicious of confident summaries.
