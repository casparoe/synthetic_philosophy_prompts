I'm a novelist — three books in, mostly literary, but the one I'm drafting now has a speculative streak and it's gotten me reading way outside my lane. For a while now I've been chewing on something I half-rememebr from the AI safety blogosphere, a term like "ascription universality": roughly, a mind counts as universal in this sense when it can model any other agent *as an agent* — as something with beliefs and goals and plans — rather than merely as a physical system or as a pattern it has memorized. The claim I've talked myself into, and that the whole book is quietly resting on, is stronger than the definition though. I think this kind of universality is what *wins*. Whatever mind can ascribe agency to the widest range of other minds, including minds shaped by totally alien selection pressures, ends up epistemically dominant — it out-predicts them, out-negotiates them, and eventually out-lasts the narrow specialists and the memorizers, and any competitor that isn't universal in this way either gets folded into its model of the world or gets out-competed by it.

So play skeptic for me, because I suspect I've fallen for something flattering. What would actually count against the view? I want the real defeaters, not the hand-wave that "generality has costs." Which observations or arguments would be decisive and which would be merely awkward? The things nagging at me: whether a narrowly specialized mind can just out-perform a universal one in every domain that actually matters, so that generality turns out to be a luxury that loses; wether modeling everything as an agent is sometimes exactly the wrong move — the way we anthropomorphize weather or markets and get burned for it — which would make ascription a liability rather than a superpower; and whether some powerful non-agentic process (evolution, a market, a plain optimization loop) could dominate all the minds without being a mind at all. I'm also a bit worried the thesis is unfalsifiable — that "ascription universal" has quietly come to just mean "wins," so nothing could ever disconfirm it. If that's what's happened, say so, and then say whether that's fatal or just the price of admission for a claim this broad.

What would move me most is anything that's arguably already happened — a real case where a generalist mind met a specialist and lost, or the reverse — or a place where the ascription story predicts something its rivals don't. Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me what a defender of the view would themselves concede counts against it, as opposed to what only critics on the outside would say.
