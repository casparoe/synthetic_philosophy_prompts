I am designing the final session of a seminar on Confucian ethics, and I want to build it around Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day. The interpretation I have been teaching all term is the role-ethical one: on this view a person is not a discrete individual who subsequently enters into relations, but is constituted by their roles and relations — son, servant, host, friend, subject — so that the virtues are excellences in the performance of a role rather than properties of a will standing behind it, and the familiar Confucian vocabulary (humaneness, ritual propriety, appropriateness, family reverence, the ideal of the consummate person) is best read as a vocabulary for doing roles well rather than for obeying rules. My students can recite this and cannot use it, which is why I want to put Stevens in front of them. He is a man who has evidently made himself into nothing but his role: he keeps serving dinner while his father lies dying upstairs, he answers Miss Kenton's approaches with the impeccable politeness of the pantry, he takes the employer's purposes as his own to the point of being unable to notice what Lord Darlington is doing politically, and he calls the whole thing dignity. What I cannot decide, and what I want help with, is whether Stevens is a reductio of role ethics or a case the role ethicist can diagnose from within. If it is the latter, I want to know which resources do the work and whether they are genuinely in the tradition or quietly imported from liberal individualism: whether appropriateness is really a matter of judgment that can outrun what the employer wants, whether the plurality of roles (the novel stages a real conflict between Stevens as butler and Stevens as son) generates any principle of priority or only a plea for tact, whether the tradition's insistence that one may remonstrate with a superior and decline a corrupt office is available here, and whether ritual propriety without humaneness — the perfect surface of service with nothing behind it — is exactly what Confucius thought was worthless.

Two further things I would like pressed hard. First, Lord Darlington: he is a man of roles too, host and English gentleman, and he is loyal to the wrong people for reasons he can articulate in role terms, so the question is whether role ethics can condemn him without appealing to an impartial morality that role ethics exists to reject, or whether it must say he misconceived his role (a host is not a man who lies for his guests). Second, the novel's pathos seems to depend on there being someone behind the role who is suffering and who might have chosen otherwise — on precisely the private self that role ethics denies — and I want to know whether the book is tacitly individualist, whether that makes it a better or worse witness against role ethics, and whether a role ethicist can give an honest account of Stevens's waste without smuggling in the romantic conception of a life that was his to spend.

Please write the reading rather than a plot summary, and be willing to say where the lens fails: if some scenes resist being read this way, or if the role-ethical reading can only be made to fit by distorting either Ishiguro or the Confucian texts, I want that stated plainly. Tell me which scenes to put in front of the group and what to say about each, keep the reading faithful in both directions, and finish by saying what the novel adds to this debate that the arguments alone cannot — I would rather end the term with a genuine puzzle than with a tidy verdict on whether Stevens was right to give his life to a house.
