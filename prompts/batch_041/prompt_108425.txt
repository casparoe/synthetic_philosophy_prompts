I sit on the quality-and-ethics committee at a mid-sized hospital — I'm a nurse by training who drifted into health-services research — and we've been going round in circles for months on something I suspect is one problem wearing two hats. The first hat is our pain protocol. Every patient gets asked to put a number on it, 0 to 10, and we're audited on wether the number gets charted and acted on; meanwhile the pain clinic hands out a questionnaire that splits pain into sensory, affective and evaluative sub-scores, which seems to assume pain isn't one dimension at all. The second hat is the cost-per-QALY spreadsheet our commissioners use, the league table where a hip replacement, an antidepressant and a hospice package all get priced per quality-adjusted life year and lined up against a threshold. What nags at me is that we tell patients, sincerely, that pain is subjective, that only they can say how bad it is, that your 7 isn't my 7 — and then the same institution pools thousands of those numbers, treats them as though they lie on a single scale, and lets the pooled result decide what gets funded. I keep hearing there's an old philosophical puzzle behind this: whether all pleasures and pains really have anything in common, or whether the word pleasure is just a blanket over a pile of utterly unlike states — a warm bath, a joke, a solved proof, a scratch finally itched — that share nothing but the label. Someone told me the traditional name for the alleged shared ingredient is a common hedonic tone, and that other people think pleasure isn't a feeling at all but an attitude, liking or wanting-to-continue, which would make the common element structural rather than felt.

What I want is not a literature survey or a reading list — I won't read it, honestly — and I don't care who said what. I want to know what our practice is actually comitted to. So reconstruct the implicit theory of pleasure and pain that the 0–10 scale plus the QALY league table presupposes: what exactly has to be true about what pleasures and pains have in common for that whole apparatus to make sense. Then separate what the practice strictly needs from what merely happens to fit it. My hunch is that it doesn't need a shared felt quality at all, only a shared measurable something, and that the attitude people could run the same hospital; tell me if that's right. Which theories does it genuinely rule out — do the pluralists and the incommensurabilists lose, and do they lose at the rating step or only once we start aggregating? Where is the practice merely silent and fillable in several ways? And are the two halves of what we do — pain is subjective and only the patient can report it, versus pool everything into one index — actually in conflict, or just two commitments that happen to live in the same building? If the presupposition turned out false, what would have to change on the ward and in the spreadsheet? (We've also had a visiting ethicist claim the QALY quietly discounts disabled lives, which may or may not be the same fault line.)

Give it back to me as a JSON object with exactly these fields: presupposed_claim (one sentence, the single thesis the practice most needs); strictly_presupposed (array of the commitments it cannot do without, each with the bit of the apparatus that would collapse without it); fits_but_not_required (array of rival theories the practice is perfectly compatible with, each with a one-line reason); ruled_out (array of the views it excludes, each with where exactly they break the machinery); where_underdetermined (string, naming the choices the practice leaves open); internal_tension (string, your verdict on whether the subjective-report half and the pooling half conflict, and if so at precisely which step); what_changes (array of concrete changes to the scale, the aggregation, or the funding rule).
