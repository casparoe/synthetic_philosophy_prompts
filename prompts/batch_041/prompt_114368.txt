I run the assessment desk at a small open-source research firm — four analysts, none of them trained in philosophy — and we publish short "what we know" notes for clients who act on them. Each claim in a note carries one of three labels: known, assessed, possible. Our recurring failure is the chain. A document says something, that something implies a further claim, and by the end of the paragraph we are writing the further claim as known. Our house rule is that each step is solid, so the conclusion is solid. I have come to think this rule smuggles in a principle that a well-known theory of knowledge denies, and I want a procedure that stops us doing it.

Please write a decision procedure — an ordered checklist an analyst can run on a single claim before it is allowed to carry the label known. Ground it in the tracking account of knowledge (the one associated with Nozick): a claim counts as known only if it is true, believed, and (i) we would not have believed it had it been false, and (ii) we would have believed it had it been true. Build in the following, and write it so that someone who has never heard of the theory can run it:

- For each question, what counts as a sufficient answer, and what to do when we cannot answer — which, in our line of work, is most of the time.
- An explicit rule for chaining, which must not assume that knowledge is closed under known entailment. Say which of the two conditions, if either, a conclusion can inherit from a premise, which it cannot, and why.
- A rule for fixing the description of the method by which a belief was formed. We have noticed that whether a claim passes (i) depends on whether we describe the method as reading this document or as trusting this source; the procedure must either tell us how to fix that description or tell us plainly that fixing it is a judgment call.
- A non-vacuity check. Some of our claims would have been believed no matter what, so (i) is satisfied trivially; the procedure must catch these rather than pass them.
- A fallback for the case where (i) cannot be answered but the claim matters, if there is an honest one; if the only honest fallback is a weaker label, say so.
- The point at which the procedure stops with a no, and the point at which it stops and hands the claim to me, because the question that remains is one an analyst cannot settle.

Then run the procedure on three cases and give the label each ends with: (a) an unnamed source we have used before says a minister attended a meeting; attendance implies a decision was taken; we write that the decision was taken. (b) A source with an excellent twenty-item track record has sent a report, and we are inclined to call the report accurate on the strength of the record. (c) A source who, we have recently realized, would have told us the reassuring thing whether or not it was true; the procedure will end somewhere I will not like, so do not soften it.

Close by saying what the procedure cannot do: the kinds of claim on which it will misfire, and which of its steps are judgment dressed up as procedure.
