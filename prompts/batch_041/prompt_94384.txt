My father died in March. Since then I have been playing the same slow movement nearly every night, the one that was played at his funeral, and I have noticed that I want to, which has unsettled me enough that I have begun keeping track of my reactions the way one keeps a food diary. I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, is doing the work in the verdicts below, and then to press that principle hard against cases I have not considered. I am not asking for a survey of what others have written and I do not want citations; I want the principle stated precisely enough to be applied, with the case that forces each of its clauses, and an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot save.

The verdicts.

At the funeral, hearing that movement, I felt only grief. Nothing about the experience was good, and I would not have chosen to hear it again. At home, the same movement, the same tears, and I seek it out; I would say the listening is good, and I have played it a fourth night running. The difference cannot lie in the music or in the tears.

A film in which a child dies: I cry, I think the film is excellent, I would watch it again, and I would not say I enjoyed the death. I would say the film earned what I felt.

A documentary about a real atrocity, made with comparable skill and producing comparable tears: I call it valuable, I do not call it enjoyable, and I do not want to see it again.

A ninety-second advertisement in which a dog is reunited with its owner, engineered to produce tears: the tears come, I resent them, and I do not count the experience as good.

A song in a language I do not speak, whose subject I cannot guess: I cry, and I enjoy it, and I suspect this is the purest case of the pleasure, precisely because the sadness has no object at all.

The fourth hour of the same movement: by then I believe it is bad for me and that I should stop, and I still call the listening good while I continue.

Crying at a film while suspecting that what pleases me is not the film but the fact that I am the kind of person who cries at such films.

The same film on a happy afternoon: tedious. On a bad evening: overwhelming, and good.

A story written for me by a machine, assembled out of my father's name, his illness, and the street where he lived: I cry, and I feel handled. The crying does not count for anything.

Here is my own tentative principle, which I would like you to test rather than adopt: sadness is pleasant when it is deserved, when I enter it of my own accord, and when it has been detached from any particular object and offered to me as a shape I can inhabit. That fits the movement at home and the song in the unknown language. It seems to fail at the funeral and at the documentary, and I cannot tell whether the advertisement fails it because the sadness is undeserved or because I never consented.

So: state the best principle, or the two best competitors, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it; say which of my verdicts it cannot keep, and whether I should drop the verdict or complicate the principle; apply it to at least three cases I have not listed — I am especially interested in one where I am complicit in the sad event, one where I am myself the maker of the sad thing, and one where the loss depicted is still open; tell me whether enjoyment and worth are one judgment or two, since the fourth hour and the advertisement seem to pull them apart; and say what the principle implies about the machine-written story, which I take to be the case that should decide between theories and which I cannot fit at all.
