I am a novelist, and for most of a year I have been living with a character who keeps a ledger for the person she will be in the morning — not a to-do list but a set of rulings: that whatever she concludes about her brother before breakfast is not to be trusted after it, that the first hour's tenderness is a mood and not a judgment, that a verdict reached late at night is to be honored at noon even when the noon self would reach another. What has kept me at this book is less the drama than a problem I cannot state cleanly. She is not withholding evidence from her future self, since the morning self will know everything she knows and rather more. She is not promising, because a promise binds conduct and she is trying to bind belief. And she is not deceiving herself, because she is entirely candid about the arrangement. What she distrusts, precisely, is not the information her future self will have but the quality of its judgment — she expects the one who wakes to weigh the same considerations worse, being more pliant, more tired, more eager to be loved — and so she tries to fix in advance a conclusion that her future self will not be free to reconsider. It is a kind of self-government, but the governed self is, by the governor's own admission, the better-informed of the two.

I would like to know whether there is an established name and body of work for this — the deliberate, present-tense management of one's own future beliefs by someone who distrusts the future believer's competence rather than the future believer's evidence — or whether I have assembled something for which there is no home. A friend in a philosophy department tells me there is a formal principle to the effect that one must treat one's future opinions as authoritative, on pain of a peculiar kind of inconsistency, and that my protagonist would therefore be irrational; but I have the impression that the principle carries a proviso, and that she may be standing exactly on it. If there is a name, I want the thing itself and not merely the word: what distinguishes it from a Ulysses-style precommitment, from ordinary self-deception, and from the humdrum case of trusting tomorrow's evidence over today's; and if the proviso is real, how it is stated and whether it marks a principled boundary or an escape hatch that lets the principle off every hook. If nothing quite covers this configuration, I would rather be told so plainly, and given a proposed term with a definition sharp enough to sort the cases — which fall inside it and which outside. The label interests me less than the line.
