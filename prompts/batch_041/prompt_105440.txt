I teach an undergraduate seminar in environmental ethics and want a diagnostic decision tree for the debate over individual contributions to collective harms. Begin from the premise that a single person's climate-relevant action, meat consumption, or vote makes no perceptible causal difference on its own, and build the tree from the logical space of responses rather than from a list of familiar labels. Each branch node should be a concrete question on which the positions genuinely come apart: whether the relevant causal relation is threshold, linear, probabilistic, or constitutive; whether the objection concerns token acts or act-types; whether the moral question is about making a difference, being complicit, honouring a duty, or expressing solidarity; whether the agent's reason must be universalisable; and whether the remedy lies in individual abstention, collective coordination, or political action. Phrase every node so a student can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to. Leaves should name positions such as expected-impact consequentialism, rule-consequentialism, Kantian universalisability, complicity or authorisation views, virtue-ethical views, expressive or participatory views, and political-obligation views, each with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any leaves the tree cannot separate. Include a short note distinguishing what is settled in this literature from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Aim for eight to ten leaves, and mark combinations that are unoccupied and say whether they are incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
