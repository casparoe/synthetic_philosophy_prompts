I'm putting together the research plan for a three-year fellowship I've been awarded on moral formation through life-writing, and I need to decide which of the genuinely open conceptual questions in that area deserve the time. The constraint I've set myself is that the output should be useful to people who actually design programmes that put exemplary lives in front of students — seminaries, medical humanities, military ethics, character education in schools, the sort of institution that still assigns Plutarch's paired lives or a saint's life and hopes something will happen — rather than a contribution to a debate only other philosophers read. I've been circling a tension I can't state cleanly, and I suspect it is the hinge of the whole area. Call it the legibility/fidelity trade-off: the more a life is shaped so that its moral can be read off it — the hagiography, the encomium, the edifying obituary — the more the reader can extract a lesson, but the more the account has been purged of precisely the contingency, ambiguity and luck that make a real life worth learning from; the more faithfully a biography restores that contingency, the less it delivers anything that could be called a lesson. Every pedagogy of exemplars seems to sit somewhere on that trade-off without anyone admitting it, and I suspect the historical genres I'm drawing on have quietly inherited one side of it.

Beyond that hinge I have half-formulated several further questions and no idea which are load-bearing: whether an exemplar teaches by proving a way of life possible rather than probable, so that the single case functions as a possibility proof rather than as evidence; whether the moral of a life is available to the person who lived it or only to the biographer who outlives it; whether admiration is a reliable faculty of moral perception or merely tracks status, charisma and whatever the readership already reveres; whether the form of the account — praise, confession, denunciation, warts and all — fixes what can be learnt from it, so that certain genres can only teach certain virtues; and whether anything at all transfers from a life set in circumstances radically unlike the learner's, or whether the genre is only ever a mirror that returns the reader's existing commitments.

What I want from you is a defended research agenda rather than a survey or a reading list. Given three years and one person, which of these questions — or others I have failed to name, including any that would make my legibility/fidelity framing look naive — should come first, and in what order? Make the criteria explicit: which questions are upstream of which, which are tractable with the tools of philosophy rather than needing empirical collaborators, which are neglected, and which look urgent but would change nothing downstream if settled. I should also like to know what evidence or developments over the next two or three years would move a question up or down the ranking, and where you think I have mis-framed the area altogether. Please write in British English.
