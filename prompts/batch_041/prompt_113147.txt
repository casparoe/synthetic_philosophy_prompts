I lecture on a third-year philosophy of physics unit and I'm putting together the exam for the spacetime week. Every year I get the same answer to anything touching Newton's bucket: the water climbs the sides, so rotation is absolute, so space is a substance — full marks, next candidate. I want a question that kills that recitation stone dead and forces them to seperate what the argument actually shows about rotation from what it shows about space.

Here's what I want to hang it on. Strip the universe down. Universe A: the bucket, the water, nothing else, the two of them spinning together as one rigid lump. Universe B: same bucket, but now there's also a thin spherical shell of matter way out beyond it, and it's the shell that's spinning while the bucket hangs still. Universe C: bucket and shell both there, spinning in opposite directions. For each, what does the relationist say the water does, what does the absolutist say, and — the bit I actually care about — does the bucket argument on its own give you any way to decide? A strong student should be able to say: fine, Newton's case refutes the view that rotation is relative to the bucket, but it doesn't touch the view that rotation is relative to the total distribution of matter, and it doesn't by itself buy you space as a substance — it buys you absolute acceleration, which is a cheaper thing to buy. I want the question to force that move, and then force them to say what a theory of spacetime would have to look like to make the bucket come out right without substantial space, and what would count as a reason to believe or disbelieve it.

Can you write it for me? The exam question itself (parts a, b, c is fine, 60 minutes total), then a model answer of about 750 words written the way a strong candidate would actually write it, then a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves a first-class answer has to make and the specific moves that should cap someone at a 2:2. I'm expecting the classic error is treating the claim that rotation is absolute as if it were already the claim that space is a substance, but tell me what else you'd penalise, and give me a one-liner for each band on how candidates typically bugger it up.

Before you draft anything, brainstorm properly: lay out the live positions (naive relationism, the Machian line that inertia comes from the distant matter, the view that the argument only ever establishes absolute acceleration, anything else you think belongs) and say which of them the question is really testing. Then draft. Write as someone who knows the philosophy of physics literature on spacetime structure inside out, so be careful with the physics — don't just assert that the water bulges in universe A, say what each position predicts and why, and don't pretend the shell scenario has a settled answer, because it definately doesn't.

One last thing. At the end of the model answer, add two or three sentences clearly marked as not part of the exam answer, saying which step of your verdict you're least sure about and what would change your mind about what the bucket argument actually establishes. And if you think my framing is wrong-headed — say I'm begging a question by calling one of these positions naive — tell me so and say what I should ask instead.
