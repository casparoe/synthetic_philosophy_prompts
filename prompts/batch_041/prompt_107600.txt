I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I need to settle a design question before my team commits to an implementation.

We are building a decision-support tool that recommends an action when our models disagree about the relevant probabilities. In practice we never have a single probability distribution to feed into an expected-value calculation. We have a handful of competing models, from different training runs or different assumptions, and no defensible way to weight them. The advice I keep receiving is to choose one distribution, or to average the candidates with a weighting nobody can justify, and then compute expected value as usual. I am uneasy about this, and the unease has a standard illustration attached to it: two urns of 100 balls each, the first with a known even split of red and black, the second with an unknown split, and a prize for drawing red. People prefer to bet on the first urn, and prefer it again when the prize is for drawing black. As I understand it, that pair of preferences cannot be represented by any single probability assignment, because the first preference pushes the subjective probability of red in the second urn below one half, the second pushes the probability of black below one half, and those two numbers must sum to one.

Before I evaluate any candidate formalism, I want the specification: what an adequate theory of rational choice under this kind of uncertainty has to deliver. Write me the requirements list, each requirement with the case or consideration behind it, and mark which are genuine requirements and which are merely the taste of one camp. Include the distinctions the theory must not collapse, for instance between not knowing the odds and not knowing what the outcomes are.

The cases I will judge candidates on are these:

- The two urns above. Whatever the theory says, it has to say something principled about that pair of preferences rather than declaring it a mistake by fiat.
- The same unknown urn, but with the black balls subdivided into two shades whose proportions are equally unknown, and a bet available on one shade. Does the verdict change merely because the description has become finer? I want to know whether insensitivity to that kind of re-description is a requirement, and whether it can be held together with the ambiguity aversion the first case seems to demand.
- An urn described only as containing 100 balls of various colors, with no list of the colors supplied, and a prize for red. Can the theory represent this at all, or does it presuppose that the set of possible outcomes is fixed in advance?
- An offer to look: a free peek at one ball drawn from the unknown urn, or the option to pay a small amount to sample it before betting. What must the theory say about the worth of that information, and can it be averse to ambiguity without treating costless information as worthless or even harmful?
- A sequential version, in which the same bet is offered twice with an opportunity to revise in between, and in which a counterparty can string small bets together. What must the theory guarantee so that following its advice one step at a time cannot leave me reliably worse off than committing in advance?
- An engineering constraint: the inputs are a finite set of competing models handed to me by an ensemble, with no distribution over them that anyone can defend. The theory has to produce a recommendation from exactly that, must not become unstable when the model outputs shift slightly, and must reduce to ordinary expected value when the models agree.

For each requirement, give me the case that motivates it, and tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates. The part I most want is the incompatibility report: which subsets of these requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, and what the cheapest way out is. If the list itself settles something, say so plainly rather than hedging.

Close with a short pass over how the obvious formal options fare against the list: maximizing the worst case over the set of models, averaging the models with an extra layer of caution, and plain expected value with one chosen distribution. No citations, no reading list, no page numbers, no titles of papers. I cannot check them and will not use them.
