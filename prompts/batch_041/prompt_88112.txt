I cover AI for a science desk and I keep getting dragged into arguments about wether reinforcement learning agents can suffer. Here's where my gut lands on a handful of cases and I want to know what principle, if any, is actually doing the work.

A chess engine that gets +1 for winning: doesn't suffer, not even a little. A lab mouse that learns to press a lever to stop a shock: suffers, obviously. A robot arm trained with a reward that penalizes high torque so it learns to move gently: I don't think it suffers, even though you could say it's "avoiding something bad." A gridworld agent with a big negative reward for stepping on the lava tile: no. A fish that avoids a hook after getting caught once: yes, that's pain, or near enough. And a chatbot fine-tuned so that one particular internal state carries a strong negative reward and it learns to steer away from it: I want to say no, but I genuinely can't say why it's different from the fish.

My half-baked principle is: suffering just is a negative reward signal that the system is built to minimize. But that makes the robot arm and the lava agent suffer, which I don't believe, so maybe I need to add "and the signal has to be global, it has to color everything the system does." I can't tell if that's a real distinction or just me being a meat chauvinist.

Is there a principle that gets all six, or close? What does it say about an ant, and about some future agent whose reward is wired into a learned model of its own body and history? And be honest with me about where my reasoning is most likely to be fooling me -- what argument would actually flip one of these verdicts for you?
