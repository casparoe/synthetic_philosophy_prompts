I am presenting a paper at a two-day workshop on the ethics of office next month, and what I want from you is a question-period preparation sheet, not a defense of the paper. My view, which I call the warrant-depletion account of dirty hands, runs roughly as follows. There is no moral remainder that attaches to the individual who authorizes a wrong in an official capacity. Something real happens when an official does so, but it is mislocated by everyone who describes it as a stain on the person: the wrong is booked against the legitimacy of the institution whose warrant the official spent. Institutions confer standing to act on the collective's behalf; that standing is finite and consumable; when it is spent on a wrong, the institution is poorer by exactly that much. The official experiences the depletion from the inside, which is why the phenomenology reports itself as guilt, but the correct description is that the account has been drawn down, not that the agent has been soiled. Three consequences I draw in the paper: first, the permission in question is not an exception carved out of a moral rule but a transfer of who holds the debt; second, the reluctance and anguish of the official are not evidence of wrongdoing but an accurate reading of the balance; third, officials who act without institutionally conferred warrant -- putschists, vigilantes, the head of a faction, anyone whose authority is self-supplied -- should report remainders, and the fact that they reliably do is a prediction of my view rather than an embarrassment to it.

The room will be about fifteen people and I know roughly who they are. A political realist of the Weberian stripe, who will hear the whole thing as the ethic of responsibility with a ledger attached. Two Rawlsians, one of whom works on public reason, who will press on whether a debt can be transferred without the consent of the party to whom it is transferred, namely the wronged. A Kantian who denies that an office can be a bearer of moral standing at all, and who will say I have reinvented the excuse/justification distinction and then blurred it back out of existence. Someone who has spent years on truth commissions and reparations, whose questions will all be about the audit: who reads the ledger, what becomes of the debt when the institution holding it is the one that committed the wrong, and whether the institution's legitimacy is anything other than a euphemism for the convenience of the state. And a moral psychologist who studies guilt and shame, who will reject the phenomenological claim outright and will want to know how, in the first-person case, I would distinguish warrant-depletion from ordinary agent-remainder.

Give me the objections in the order I am likely to get them, weighted by how much damage each would do, phrased the way each of these people would actually phrase them and not the way they would appear in a survey article. For each: what I should concede up front, what I should hold, a two-minute reply, and a note on where the reply is soft -- in particular, which replies survive only because I have been granted a premise I have not earned. Tell me which single concession costs me least and buys me most, and which single reply has to land for the session to go well. If two of the objections are the same objection wearing different clothes, say so and tell me which of the two I should answer. Flag any place where my own formulation, rather than my position, is doing the damage. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and say why that one is the one that hurts. No reading list, no citations, and do not tell me who has said what before -- I know the literature and I will not be able to check it from the podium.
