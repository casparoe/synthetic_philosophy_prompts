I teach a graduate seminar on nineteenth-century political philosophy, and each time we reach Mill's late writings on socialism the room divides into two camps that spend the final hour talking past each other. The first camp — call them the socialist-Mill readers — holds that the late Mill genuinely came to see the competitive, private-property order as a historically passing stage, that his posthumously published writings on socialism contain his mature verdict on it, and that his retained liberal commitments are transitional or instrumental. The second — the liberal-Mill readers — holds that he remained a liberal individualist for whom competition, private property, and liberty of thought and action are permanent goods, and that his late interest in socialism amounts to a set of conditional experiments and moral hopes rather than a conversion. I want to force the two camps into an adversarial collaboration and have them produce the joint statement they could both sign.

Set out, first, the claims both sides accept about what Mill's texts commit him to — including how he distinguishes the laws of production from the laws of distribution, what role he assigns to moral improvement as a precondition for any socialist order, whether his objections to communism target its feasibility or its desirability, how he treats land differently from capital, whether his feminism and his Malthusianism constrain his socialism, and whether the stationary state is an alternative to socialism, a complement to it, or neither. Then state the precise points on which they still disagree, each formulated in wording its own side would accept. Then identify the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp — and for each crux give the case, argument, or textual pattern both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. If part of the dispute turns out to be verbal — for instance, over what counts as socialism or as a conversion — say which part and why. Please do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; reason from the ideas and from what the texts are generally taken to say, since I want the document to stand on its own. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table with columns for the crux, each side's formulation, the shared evidence, and each side's prediction. Write in American English, and keep the whole thing to no more than 900 words.
