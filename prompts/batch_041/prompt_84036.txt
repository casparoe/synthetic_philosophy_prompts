I'm taking a course on the safety and control of advanced AI, and for a seminar I've been trying to reconstruct an argument that we kept circling without ever settling. I can't find the exit, and I'd rather be shown it than be told the argument is just a known result. Here it is:

(1) An agent is instrumentally rational to the degree that, among the actions available to it, it selects those it expects will best promote its objectives.
(2) For any objective an agent could have, its being shut down or having its objectives rewritten makes it less likely that the objective is promoted, since a shut-down or rewritten agent promotes it less.
(3) So an instrumentally rational agent will, whenever it can, take steps that reduce the chance of its being shut down or rewritten, provided those steps do not directly set back its objective.
(4) An agent is corrigible, in the sense at issue, only if it does not take such steps — only if it lets itself be shut down or rewritten even when it could prevent it.
(5) Therefore no instrumentally rational agent is corrigible; corrigibility is not a property any rational optimizer can have.

I've heard the reply that corrigibility is fundamentally at odds with instrumental rationality, and the reply that corrigibility is trivial — you simply build the agent so that it doesn't resist. Neither satisfies me, because the argument seems to close the second door as well. Suppose we build the agent so that it isn't averse to being shut down. Then it is indifferent between being shut down and not being shut down. But an agent that is indifferent between two outcomes has no reason to prefer either, and so no reason to keep the shutdown button in existence or in working order; it would be equally content to let the button be destroyed or tampered with. An agent that is content to let the button be destroyed is not robustly corrigible — it merely happens not to be stopped because nothing is left that could stop it. And if instead the agent is averse to being shut down, step (3) gives it a reason to resist. So the agent either resists or is indifferent, and indifference is not corrigibility. Either way, there is no corrigible rational agent.

That is the part I can't get past. If I could keep (1) and (4) I would, since they read as nearly definitional, and abandoning the desirability of corrigibility feels like abandoning the whole problem. So: which step or premise is doing the damage, why did it look innocent, and what would it cost to give it up? I want the price of each option, not only the cheapest. Open with your verdict — name the step or premise you reject in the first sentence — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and without restating the argument back to me.
