For a series we are running on how people get trapped in bad decisions, a colleague has filed a draft sidebar on the dollar auction, and I need to know how much of it can go to press. The gist of his draft, near enough to his own words, is this:

The dollar auction is a game theorist's party trick, designed to show how easily sensible people can be led into ruin. The auctioneer offers a dollar. Bidding is open and ascending, and the twist is that the two highest bidders both pay what they bid, though only the winner receives the dollar. Two bidders will push each other past a dollar, past five dollars, past twenty. The reason is the sunk-cost fallacy: having invested so much in the chase, neither can bear to write off the loss, and each new bid is a way of refusing to admit that the earlier bids were a mistake. This is what psychologists call escalation of commitment, and the dollar auction is the purest laboratory demonstration of it — the same pathology that keeps companies pouring money into failing projects and governments into unwinnable wars. What is most striking is that rational choice theory has nothing to say here: a rational bidder would never bid more than a dollar for a dollar, so the bidding war is a straightforward refutation of the assumption that people maximise expected value. The practical lesson for our readers is to set a limit before they start bidding and stick to it.

Two of my colleagues have already weighed in. One says the whole thing is the sunk-cost fallacy in fancy dress. The other says the dollar auction is really a war of attrition and that the psychology is a red herring. My own instinct is that it is a story about stubbornness, though I suspect that is just the folk explanation the draft has already planted in me.

Please go claim by claim through the draft and tell me what a specialist would accept as stated, what they would accept only with a qualification, and what is simply wrong — and in each case what the correct statement would be. I am particularly interested in whether the escalation in the auction really is a sunk-cost phenomenon, whether "escalation of commitment" is the right label for it or a different thing that has been welded on, and whether the trap survives if the rules are altered — say, if only the winner pays, or if the bids are sealed, or if the auctioneer refunds the runner-up. Then adjudicate between my two colleagues: which of them, if either, has located the mechanism, and why. Finally, tell me whether the draft's closing lesson is the right lesson.

I want a committed answer rather than a survey. Take a position on whether the escalation is best explained by the psychology of the bidders or by the structure of the game, say which sentences I should cut, and do not hedge by telling me that both factors matter in their own way unless you can also say which one is doing the work here. You need not cite literature; I am after the substance, not a reading list.

After the audit, give me a table with these columns: Claim (the draft's claim, in a few words); Verdict (one of sound, needs qualification, misleading, wrong); Correction (one or two sentences stating what should have been said); Load-bearing (whether the draft's overall conclusion depends on that claim).
