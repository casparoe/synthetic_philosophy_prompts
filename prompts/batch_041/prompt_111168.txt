I have no academic training and no university affiliation; I read philosophy in my own time, mostly in the evenings, and I have been stuck on one problem for about two years. I am applying to a programme at a foundation that makes small grants to independent researchers working outside institutions, and I need a proposal of no more than 1,200 words for a nine-month, part-time project. Please write it.

Here is the idea, in my own words, since I do not know whether it has a settled name. Suppose two people have never met and never will, and nothing either of them does can reach the other. Suppose also that they are so alike in how they reason — the same habits, the same evidence, the same way of working out what to do — that when one of them settles on a course of action, that is a sign of what the other will settle on. Then when I choose to do something I am doing it, and I am also making it more likely that my unseen counterpart does the same thing. If the thing is a sacrifice made for someone else's sake, we have between us paid for two goods while each of us paid for one. And if the world is large enough — if there are many minds like mine, or many that converge on the same reasoning — the number of unseen counterparts could be very large, so that a small cost to me might be worth a great deal in aggregate. I have been calling this cooperation at a distance, though I expect others call it something else.

My half-formed worry is this. The whole force of the idea seems to rest on how likely it is that my counterpart acts as I do, and on how many counterparts there are. Both of those seem to depend on how I draw the boundaries of the case: on how similar I insist a counterpart must be, on whether I count only minds that already exist or also ones that merely could, and on whether the reasoning we are said to share is described broadly or narrowly. If I can shift the numbers by shifting the description, then I can make the case for cooperating come out as strong or as weak as I like, and a principle that can justify anything justifies nothing. So the question I want the proposal to make precise is whether there is a version of the argument in which the similarity and the numbers are fixed by facts about the world rather than by the describer's choices — and if there is not, whether that is the end of the idea or whether it can still be defended as a practical rule that does not pretend to be a discovery.

Please give me a proper proposal: the question stated so that it has a definite answer rather than being a mood; why the two replies I keep meeting — that this is wishful thinking dressed up in arithmetic, and that it simply follows from some general principle about acting on reasons — leave it open; the method, month by month, and why reasoning about cases is the right approach here rather than, say, trying to gather evidence about whether such counterparts exist; what a good outcome would look like and what would count as the project failing; and a realistic scope for nine months of part-time work by someone with no training and no library. If you think a diary of my own decisions over those months would add anything, say so and say why; if you think it would be self-indulgent, say that instead. I would also like a short paragraph on why a foundation should fund someone outside the university to do this, since I expect the panel to ask.

Do not give me a reading list or references — I cannot get hold of most of what is written on this and I would rather the proposal not pretend to know. Write in British English.
