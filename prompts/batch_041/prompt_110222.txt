I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy — I work on memory, so this is adjacent to my stuff — and I'm sitting in on a mind seminar that spent two weeks on the old Malcolm/Dennett fight over whether dreams are experiences. A friend of mine in the department, a linguist who thinks the whole debate is people hypnotizing themselves with there own vocabulary, built the following argument out of the Malcolm half of the reading, and I've been carrying it around for a week trying to find the crack in it. Here it is, the way he put it on the whiteboard:

(1) When I dream that I'm in the garden, my report of the dream answers to nothing. Nobody can check it, I can't be shown to have gotten it wrong, and there's no fact about the dream that outruns what I'm willing to say about it. (2) If something in my mental life is an experience, then there's a fact of the matter about it that my report of it has to get right — the kind of thing I could be wrong about, and that someone else could in principle catch me out on. (3) So when I dream, nothing is experienced. "I dreamed I was in the garden" doesn't report an experience; it tells you what story I'm going to tell about last night. (4) Which is why it's a mistake to ask whether I was really terrified in the dream — whatever I say about the terror goes, and with real terror that's not how it works.

The problem is that I can run (1)–(4) on alot of cases where the conclusion is obviously false. Take me sitting here visualizing a garden: nothing my report — "I'm imagining a garden" — answers to either, nobody can check it, I can't be wrong about what I'm seeing in my mind's eye. So imagining isn't an experience, and by the same token neither is a hallucination, since the hallucinating person's report isn't answerable to anything out in the world. But visualizing a hedge is about as experiential as it gets — there's something it's like. So some link in the chain has to be broken, and I want to know which one and what I'm paying when I drop it. I'd like to keep as much of (1) as I honestly can, because there's something in it: you can't interrogate a dream report the way you can interrogate a perceptual one (ask whether the shed was to the left of the tree in my dream and the question feels broken; ask about the actual shed and it's a perfectly good question). And someone else in the seminar keeps insisting the whole thing dissolves once you take seriously the idea that the dream was never played in real time — that the whole thing, apparent hours and all, gets composed at or just before the moment of waking, so the apparent duration is itself part of the content, like a backstory in a story you're told. I can't tell whether that's a real move or just a way of changing the subject. So: which step goes, why did it look fine, and what does dropping it cost me? (And a side thing I keep tripping over: does it matter that the sleep lab can tell when someone is dreaming? I have a feeling that should bear on (1) somehow and I can't see how.)
