Thirty-five years I did geophysics — paleomagnetism mostly, then a long stretch on the geodynamo — and since I retired I've been reading history and philosophy of history, which is a different kind of fun. Here's what keeps bugging me. Whenever a historian or a pop-science writer wants to make the point that some huge outcome was contingent — the storm that scattered the fleet, the letter that arrived late, the sentry who was asleep — they reach straight for chaos theory. Butterfly effect, sensitive dependence on initial conditions, a wingbeat in Brazil and a tornado in Texas. And the moral they draw is always the same: history is contingent, there are no laws of history, the whole thing turns on a horseshoe nail.

I know my way around nonlinear dynamics from the geodynamo and turbulence work, and my hunch is that a good chunk of what their borrowing doesn't actually make it across the gap. Can you audit the analogy the way I'd audit a bad physical analogy in a seminar paper? I want the correspondence spelled out first — in a chaotic system, what exactly plays the role of the initial condition, the map, the Lyapunov exponent, the attractor, the cloud of nearby trajectories — and then where the mapping fails. In particular I want you to sort out whether contingency in history is supposed to mean unpredictable-to-us or genuinely unsettled, because those are wildly different claims and chaos theory only gives you the first one; whether there's any meaningful analogue of a phase space or a measure when the state is a society; and whether the whole move backfires, since a deterministic chaotic system is still deterministic, so if the analogy holds it looks like it undercuts the could-have-gone-otherwise conclusion rather than supporting it. Is the horseshoe-nail story even the same kind of claim as the butterfly story, or are two different pictures being run together under one heading?

Then tell me whether any conclusion about laws of history, or about counterfactual history, survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the real work was being done by something else all along and the chaos talk is just decoration.

One ask on top of that: say how confident you are in the main claims, and single out the point you're least sure of. I'd much rather have a flagged uncertainty than a smooth answer.
