I am a high school student writing a long paper on the philosophy of the social world, and I have run into a comparison I cannot resolve on my own.

It is often said both that money is socially constructed and that race is socially constructed. The first claim is usually unpacked in a coordination idiom: a banknote is worth something because everyone expects everyone else to treat it as worth something, and each of us has reason to conform to that expectation, so the arrangement is self-sustaining but also arbitrary — a different arrangement would have served just as well, and in principle the whole thing could be replaced. The second claim is usually unpacked in a different idiom: race is the residue of a history of classification, hierarchy and exclusion, and the classification does not merely describe people but shapes them, so that those sorted into a category tend to become what the category says they are, and the category hardens into bodies, institutions and life chances.

My question is whether these are two versions of a single idea of social construction or two different ideas that have been run together under one phrase. Please compare them. I would like you to address, at least:

1. Whether the arbitrariness of money is the same property as whatever people mean when they call a racial classification arbitrary. Can a kind be socially constructed without being arbitrary in the money sense, and can a kind be arbitrary without being constructed in the race sense?

2. Whether the coordination account has anything to say about kinds that carry moral weight and are costly to leave, and whether the historical account has anything to say about kinds that are morally neutral and cheap to change. If each account is silent about the other's paradigm case, is that a defect in the accounts, or evidence that the phrase covers two distinct things?

3. A thought experiment I intend to use: a society re-denominates its currency overnight, versus a society abolishes a racial classification overnight. The first is disruptive but feasible; the second is not, or is feasible only in a way that leaves the kind intact. What does that asymmetry reveal — the number of people who must coordinate, the sanctions for nonconformity, the fact that one kind is embodied and the other is not, or something else?

4. Where a kind introduced as a convenient administrative fiction — morally neutral at the outset and coordination-like in its working — later becomes identity-bearing and hierarchically loaded, falls on your comparison. Which account explains that trajectory better?

Finally, tell me which of the two accounts you think does the real explanatory work in each case, whether either is parasitic on the other, and whether your answer would change if the kind in question were gender rather than race.
