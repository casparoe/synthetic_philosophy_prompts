So a reading group I'm in got onto wild animal suffering last week and it turned into a proper arguement, and now I'm stuck on something someone said half as a joke that I can't shake. The gist was a chain of reasoning, and I've tried to write it out cleanly below. It goes:

(1) Whether a being's coming into existence counts as a benefit or a harm to that being is settled entirely by the welfare the life actually contains — net positive, a benefit; net negative, a harm.
(2) Most sentient animals in the wild are r-strategists: they produce enormous numbers of offspring, the vast majority of those offspring die young, and many of those deaths are genuinely bad — starvation, being eaten while still alive, disease, exposure.
(3) So for a randomly selected individual of such a species, the suffering in its life outweighs the good in it, i.e. its lifetime welfare is negative.
(4) Therefore each time one of those animals is brought into existence, that existence is on balance a harm to it.
(5) If you can prevent a large amount of harm at a small cost to yourself, you ought to — or at the very least it's seriously good to.
(6) And this is now within reach, via contraception, gene drives, or just letting the relevant populations quietly stop reproducing, at moderate cost.
(7) So we ought, or it's seriously good, to stop most wild animals from reproducing, which over enough time means winding down most of the sentient life in nature.
(8) Nothing in the reasoning cared that the victims were non-human; run the same steps on any future population whose lives come out net negative and you get the same verdict.
(9) So the same logic that begins as a plea to reduce animal suffering ends up recommending we engineer the planet into something empty of conscious life, and maybe that we bring that about sooner rather than later.

I'm fine with (1)–(3), I'm sympathetic to (5), and I don't think I'm just being sentimental about nature when I say I can't accept (7) or (9) — the conclusion is monstrous. My gut says the slippage is somewhere around (4), that treating non-existence as a rival state that the animal turns out to be worse off than is doing something illegitimate, but I can't make that precise and I'd rather keep (1) and (5) if I can. Where does this actually break? Name the step you'd reject, explain why it looked fine, and be straight about the price — especially for someone in my corner who wants to keep (1)–(3) and still refuse the conclusion, since that's the position I'm actually in.
