I'm an applied machine learning engineer — tabular models, evaluation, attribution — and I have no biology beyond high school. I keep meeting claims about the heritability of traits and I want to know whether the machinery I use every day for variance accounting and feature attribution can actually carry the content of what quantitative geneticists mean, or whether I'm about to make my field's mistakes in a new register. Assume I'm fluent in bias–variance decomposition, variance explained on a held-out set, permutation importance, Shapley-style additive attributions, ablations, train/test and covariate shift, aleatoric versus epistemic noise, and enough causal inference to keep conditioning and intervening apart. Assume I know nothing about genetics.

Here is the position I want restated. Heritability is a ratio of variance components: the share of the phenotypic variance in a specified population, at a specified time and across a specified range of environments, attributable to genetic differences among the individuals in that population — in the narrow sense, to the additive part of that variation. It is a property of a population, not of a trait, an individual, or a genotype. It is compatible with the trait's mean moving enormously across cohorts; I'm told the standard illustration is height, where the twin-based figure is high and the mean has nevertheless moved a great deal over the same era. The estimate is not invariant: it depends on the distribution of environments, on the assumption built into the twin design that identical and fraternal twins share environments to the same degree, on assortative mating among the parents, and on interactions and correlations between genetic and environmental differences — so the same genotypes under a different environment would yield a different number. Additive variance recovered from family designs is usually much larger than what direct molecular estimates find, a gap with a name of its own. And the whole apparatus decomposes variation, which makes it silent about levels, about which interventions would work, and about how much of any particular individual's trait is due to anything.

Restate that in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can: the population as a data distribution, the phenotype as the target, the genotype as a very high-dimensional and heavily correlated input, the environment as the rest of the input space or as the distribution the data is drawn from, heritability as a share of explained variance under that distribution, the additive/dominance/epistatic split as a decomposition of a fitted model's contributions. Say which of my standard moves — retraining on a shifted distribution, permuting a feature, holding other features fixed, reading off a variance-explained number — corresponds to which geneticist's move, and where you had to fudge.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. What does not survive the translation? I have suspicions I'd like tested rather than confirmed. First, that my notion of a feature is too well-behaved: my predictors are correlated, the geneticist's variants are correlated by linkage, and the environmental difference is correlated with the genetic one, which is exactly the regime where permutation importance and additive attributions are known to mislead — and I'd like to know whether my canonical fix for the non-uniqueness of decompositions under interaction, the Shapley-style one, says anything useful here or rests on a baseline that genetics does not have. Second, that environment in genetics is a residual category defined by exclusion, while my inputs are partitioned on principle, so the second component of that decomposition may be doing work nothing in my toolkit does. Third, that the reference point for an attribution is a choice I make and is not a choice the geneticist makes. Fourth, that my habit of reading a high variance-explained figure as this is what matters and cannot be moved is a fallacy my own field commits routinely, so the translation may transmit the disease rather than cure it. For each item, say whether it looks like a limitation of my vocabulary, something a better formulation would capture, or a sign that the original claim is doing something my vocabulary rightly refuses to express, or a sign that the original is less clear than it sounds.

Don't be diplomatic. If the translation works better than I expect, say so; if my suspicions are wrong, say that. Tell me where this reasoning is most likely to fail and what would change your verdict — which fact about the genetics or which fact about my toolkit would flip a piece of the residue report from a defect in the translation to a defect in the original, or the reverse. Write it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — the way you'd write a long letter to a colleague who asked a slightly odd question.
