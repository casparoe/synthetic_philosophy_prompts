I have been trying to fix my own view of where a mind stops, and I have reached the point where I can report my verdicts on a run of cases but cannot see the principle behind them. I would like you to find it, or to tell me that there is none, and which of my verdicts I should surrender.

1. Otto, the Alzheimer's patient from the extended-mind literature, who writes his directions in a notebook, consults it without hesitation, and trusts it without checking: the notebook is part of his memory. This seems to me correct.

2. My own notebook, kept and consulted in much the same way, though I have an ordinary memory: a tool, not a piece of me. If it burned I would be annoyed and would remember the contents anyway.

3. The calendar on my phone. I do not know my own week; if the phone vanished I would miss appointments for a fortnight. I consult it dozens of times a day without hesitation and I never double-check what it says. I want to say that it is not part of my mind. I notice that this sits badly with the first case: the dependence looks the same from the inside, and the only difference I can name is that Otto's brain cannot do the job while mine has merely contracted the job out.

4. The assistant in my earpiece, which I query all day and which is usually right: not part of my mind, because I catch myself treating its answers as claims rather than as things I know, and I re-check when it contradicts me.

5. My partner, who holds the appointments I never write down, whom I ask without hesitation and never doubt: not part of my mind, since her remembering is her doing something for me. But I am excluding her by pointing to the fact that her reports arrive in her voice, as her acts, and I cannot say whether a voice marks a real difference or a merely stylistic one, given that the information and the trust are the same in her case as in the notebook's.

6. A cochlear implant is part of the person's hearing; my reading glasses are not part of my seeing. I can defend this only by saying that one is inside me and always on and the other comes off.

7. A silent implant that supplies names and dates, which the person cannot distinguish from recall: part of that person's memory. This troubles me, since functionally it is case 3 moved under the skull.

8. A notebook kept by a couple, both writing in it and both consulting it constantly: it seems to belong to a memory that is theirs and neither's, and I do not know what to say.

The criterion I have read for the first case is this: "If, as we confront some task, a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is (so we claim) part of the cognitive process." Taken literally, it seems to count cases 3, 5, 7 and 8 as extended too, which would overturn two of my verdicts. My own attempt is that something joins my mind only when I have no independent grip on what it holds and it answers without mediation, with no voice and no interface I have to work; that keeps 1, 2, 4, 5, 6 and 7, and it also keeps 3, which I resist.

So: is there a single account that gets all of these, or close to all? Please state the principle, or the two or three candidate principles, precisely, say which case forces each clause, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and say what it predicts for cases I have not put to myself: thinking with a pen, where the writing is the thinking and I almost never reread it; a password manager holding the only copies of passwords I have never memorized; the screen reader and refreshable Braille display a blind friend reads with; and a research group's shared wiki, which no single member could reconstruct.
