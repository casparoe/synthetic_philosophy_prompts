I'm a novelist and I'm stuck on a scene. Two people are doing something together and I want the reader to feel the togetherness is real — but I don't want it propped up by the usual crutches: no agreement hashed out beforehand, no coercion or threats, no deception, no money, nobody doing it purely as a favor to the other, nothing heroic or life-or-death. Two ordinary people, a kitchen or a car, both sincere, neither confused about whats happening. Build me that case, then run it through the three accounts I half-remember: Searle's we-intention, Gilbert's plural subject and joint commitment, Bratman's shared intention. I want it to come out Searle yes, Gilbert yes (obligations and all), Bratman no — and if Bratman can only say no by way of a technicality I've rigged, tell me that instead of rigging it. Then: which single feature of my scene is doing the work, what happens when I delete it (do I just land back on the standard textbook cases?), and where would a Bratman defender attack the scene if they wanted to save him? I need to know whether my reader's sense that these two aren't quite doing it together is tracking something real or just sentimentality.
