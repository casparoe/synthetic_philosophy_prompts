I spent thirty-five years doing molecular biophysics and retired a couple years ago, and since then I've been sitting in on a philosophy seminar that's slowly taken over my life. The dept has kindly let me set one question for the final of the upper-level "Minds, Machines and Morals" course I've been auditing, and I want it to be one of the nasty ones — the kind a student can't get through by reciting funcionalism at me.

Here's the thing I want them to chew on. Take the old puzzle where somebody clever shows that a rock (or a bucket of water, or the whole galaxy) "implements" a finite-state machine, just by assigning the right mapping between the thing's states and the machine's states — so on paper the rock is running the same program as you or me. Now the leap I actually care about: suppose it really is running the same program as a system we're sure can suffer. Does that mean the rock can suffer, or matters morally? I don't think the leap goes through, but I want the question to make them say *why* in a way that survives a bit of pushing, and to seperate two things I keep seeing mashed together: the question of whether a system is *performing* a given computation (call it attribution) from the question of which computations, if performed, would *ground* moral status. I want them to notice that if you answer the attribution question the lazy way — anything can implement anything — then moral status ends up depending on the observer's choice of mapping, which is horrible; and that if you answer it the strict way (only "real" causal implementations count), you now owe me an account of what makes an implementation non-trivial, and that account had better not boil down to "whichever ones happen to be made of neurons."

I also want them to feel the lookup-table problem: a system that produces exactly the right outputs by a giant table of pre-stored responses looks functionally equivalent to the real thing but doesn't look like it feels anything. So equivalence at the level of inputs and outputs can't be what matters. And I'd like them to say something honest about whether the moral question even turns on the computation, or on something the computation merely tends to bring with it.

Please write the exam question itself (aimed at a 3rd/4th-year undergrad, answerable in about ninety minutes), a model answer of maybe 500–600 words, and a marking scheme. Give it back to me as exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no intro, no wrap-up. Item 1 is the question. Item 2 is the model answer. Items 3 through 5 are the marking scheme: 3 = the moves a top answer has to make, 4 = the moves a solid-but-not-top answer makes, 5 = the one mistake that should cap a student below that. And on the model answer — I've been burned by confident philosophers before, so have it own its weak spot: say where its reasoning is most likely to give out, and what would actually change its mind about whether the rock matters morally.
