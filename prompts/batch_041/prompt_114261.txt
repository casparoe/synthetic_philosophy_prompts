I'm a first-year PhD student in philosophy who has drifted into a social-choice reading group, and I keep meeting a summary of Harsanyi's aggregation theorem that I suspect is being made to carry more weight than it can bear. The version in front of me comes from the opening chapter of a well-regarded trade book by an economist writing for a general audience, and its gist is this: Harsanyi proved that a rational society must be utilitarian. The proof is supposed to be that a society whose collective preferences obey the expected-utility axioms, and which prefers whatever all of its members prefer, must rank social states by a weighted sum of individual utilities; and because each person behind the veil of ignorance has an equal chance of turning out to be anyone, the weights come out equal and the sum becomes an average, which the chapter says is just utilitarianism. It goes on to claim that this is why Arrow's impossibility theorem, which the chapter calls the central problem of welfare economics, is a problem only for ordinal rankings: once utilities are cardinal rather than ordinal, the impossibility evaporates and the social welfare function is uniquely pinned down as the utilitarian sum. The chapter closes by saying that Rawls's difference principle is now widely regarded as a mistake traceable to an unjustified assumption of extreme risk aversion on the part of the parties in the original position, and that most economists therefore treat utilitarianism as the correct standard for evaluating policy. There is also a footnote I don't follow which credits the same result with showing that people who share the same information cannot rationally disagree about values, so that persistent moral disagreement must be either ignorance or irrationality.

I don't want to dismiss the chapter wholesale, since I assume most of it traces back to something real, but I'd like to know which sentences a social choice theorist would sign off on, which would make them wince, and which are simply wrong. In particular I want to know which of these conclusions are actually in the theorem and which are extra premises the summary has quietly added: if the equal weights, or the uniqueness, or the move from a total to an average each depends on something further, I want to know whose premise it is and how much work it does, and whether anything shifts once the population whose welfare we are aggregating is itself variable in size. I'd like the same treatment for the Rawls sentence and for the footnote, since I have a suspicion that the chapter's opponent is being set up in a way that Rawls himself would not recognise.
