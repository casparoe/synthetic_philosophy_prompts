I'm a second-year PhD student in comparative politics, and for the past two years I've been keeping a mental tally of the verdicts my advisor hands down whenever democratic backsliding comes up in workshop. She has been at this for thirty years, she is impatient with the term, but she does use it, and her applications of it are unusually consistent. Hungary from 2010 onward is, for her, the clearest case there is. Poland from 2015 to 2023 is also a case, and she will not let the change of government in Warsaw in late 2023 count as a retraction of that judgment: the recovery, she says, is a separate event, and the erosion stands as it happened. Turkey from roughly 2008 to 2016 she calls backsliding, but she insists the case stops there, and after the 2017 referendum she codes the country as a different kind of regime and stops using the term for it. India since 2014 is a case, and she says the government's continuing electoral success, along with its record on growth and poverty, is beside the point. The United States between 2017 and 2021 is not a case; she calls it a stress test, gets visibly irritated when people treat it as the paradigm, and says the guardrails held. Brazil between 2019 and 2022 is not a case either, on the same reasoning. Israel's judicial overhaul in 2023 she also declines to call backsliding, on the grounds that the attempt was blocked. Venezuela she refuses to discuss under the heading at all: by the time one would want to apply the term, she says, the thing being described had already become something else. When I finally asked her to state her criterion outright, she said that what she watches is whether the damage sits in the machinery or only in the mood.

Now our reading group has a new case to argue over, and I want to predict her before she says it. Because I don't want the answer to collapse into pattern-matching to a country you have read about, I'll describe it as a composite and ask you to reason from the features. Call it Kestrel: a middle-income democracy of about nine million where a broad center-right coalition has governed for twelve years, winning four consecutive elections with between 45 and 52 percent of the vote under proportional representation. Turnout has stayed above 70 percent; the electoral commission is appointed by all parliamentary parties, has never been credibly accused of fraud, and international observers describe the elections as free and fair; the opposition controls the capital, several regions, and a substantial bloc in parliament, and it wins and loses races in the ordinary way. The private press is lively and adversarial, and the government has not touched the electoral law. What the coalition has done is different in kind: it expanded the constitutional court and rewrote the appointment rules until it had a reliable majority, which has since blessed several of its initiatives; it installed a loyalist as prosecutor general, and investigations have been opened against opposition mayors, three of whom have been convicted and two of whom have left the country; it dissolved four autonomous regulatory agencies and moved their functions into ministries; it passed a law requiring NGOs that receive foreign funding to register as foreign agents, which has already shut several of them down; and it has replaced the senior civil service with political appointees. At the same time, corruption prosecutions are up, tax collection is up, poverty is down, and the prime minister is a bland figurehead whom the party has replaced twice without any visible change in policy.

I would like you to tell me what she will say about Kestrel and, more importantly, what rule her earlier verdicts are most naturally read as expressing, which other rules fit those verdicts just as well, and what each of those rules predicts here. Give me your best guess with your reasons and your confidence, and tell me which further case would be the cleanest way to tell the candidate rules apart. One of her colleagues, incidentally, insists that the whole label is a partisan instrument that Western academics reserve for governments they dislike, and I want to know whether her verdicts, taken together, give that charge any purchase. If the record does not actually constrain the answer as much as I have assumed, or if I am asking you to recover a single rule from someone who may simply be judging case by case, say so plainly rather than handing me a tidy answer. Please write your answer in American English.
