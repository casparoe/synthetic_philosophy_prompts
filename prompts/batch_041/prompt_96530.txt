I've spent a lot of time in the old rationalist archives — Overcoming Bias, LessWrong, the comment threads underneath — and one of the recurring asides there is that al-Ghazālī beat Hume to the problem of induction by six centuries, in the seventeenth discussion of the Tahāfut al-Falāsifa, where he denies that there is any necessary connection between what we call a cause and what we call an effect. Every time I try to line the two up, the comparison slips through my fingers, and I'd like you to sort it out properly. Hume's denial of necessary connection is, at least on its surface, a claim about what we can perceive and about how the idea of causation gets generated in us, and it leaves the world alone; al-Ghazālī's denial reads as a claim about what is actually going on in the world, with fire and cotton as the test case — the cotton is burned not by the fire but by God, at the moment of contact, and the regular sequence we observe is a matter of God's habit or settled practice rather than any necessity residing in the things themselves. Take the role of someone who works on both medieval Arabic philosophy of nature and early modern empiricism, and think this through carefully before answering rather than reaching for the nearest available summary. I want to know what exactly separates the two positions, which parts of the apparent parallel are genuine and which are an artefact of the fact that both authors are usually rendered into English with the same word, custom, and what each of them is actually committed to that the other is not.

The piece I'm genuinely stuck on is what I've been calling the particularization problem, and it's the part I'd most like you to work through. As I understand the Ash'arite background, the created world is atoms and accidents that God recreates at every moment, and the atoms are, so to speak, interchangeable — there is nothing in the fire itself, no power or form or nature, that could explain why it burns this cotton and not that stone, or why it burns cotton rather than freezing it. Any such difference has to be traced to God's will singling one outcome out rather than the other. But that looks like a bad bargain: the occasionalist secures the stability of science by treating the regularities we observe as brute facts about what God has gotten into the habit of doing, so that fire burns cotton now says no more than when cotton is near fire, burning reliably happens — which is precisely the accusation the falāsifa leveled at the theologians, that a God who can do anything at any moment makes the world unintelligible. Please explain whether that charge is fair, and be concrete about three things: whether an occasionalist can distinguish a lawlike regularity from an accidental one; whether the appeal to God's habit does any explanatory work that constant conjunction does not already do; and whether there is a defensible reading of al-Ghazālī on which created things are genuine secondary causes even though the connection between them is not necessary — that is, whether the choice between necessary connection and no causal contribution whatsoever is a false dilemma. I've seen that last possibility gestured at in the secondary literature but never spelled out, and I'd rather have the argument than a survey of who holds what.
