Dana started with the shopkeeper thing. She said imagine a shopkeeper who never cheats his customers, but only because cheating would ruin his reputation. He does the right thing, but he doesn't get any moral credit, because he's just being prudent. Then imagine a shopkeeper who doesn't cheat because he thinks cheating is wrong. Same action, different worth. She said that shows moral worth has to come from the motive, specifically from doing it because it's right.

Marcus said fine, but then she has to admit the person who doesn't cheat because he genuinely likes his customers and would feel awful hurting them also gets no credit on her view, and that seems wrong. Marcus said that's a bizarre move. He said: you're saying we can never know for sure whether someone acted from duty, so we should give credit only for the motive we can never verify, and deny it to the motive we can see right in front of us. That can't be right.

Then Marcus brought up this example from a book he read. A kid in the old South believes with his whole conscience that helping a runaway friend escape is a sin, he feels guilty the whole time, but he does it anyway because he can't stand to see his friend sent back. Dana said that kid is not acting from duty at all — he's acting from feeling, and he even thinks he's doing wrong, so his action has no moral worth. Marcus said that's crazy, the kid did the right thing for the reason that made it right — his friend needed him — and the fact that his moral beliefs were messed up by his upbringing shouldn't erase the worth of what he did. Dana said then you're not talking about moral worth anymore, you're talking about praiseworthiness or something, and those are different.

Marcus also said Dana's view makes the loving parent who cares for their kid out of love morally inferior to the cold parent who does it out of duty, and that's backwards. Dana said that's a caricature, and anyway the loving parent isn't doing anything morally impressive, they're just following their feelings, and if the feelings went away they might not do it. Marcus said the dutiful parent might not do it either if their upbringing had been different, so why is one lucky and the other virtuous?

Dana said the difference is that the dutiful parent is responsive to the reasons themselves, not to how they happen to feel. Marcus said the loving parent is also responsive to reasons — the reason is that the kid needs them — and you don't need the extra thought that this is what duty requires on top of it. Dana said you do, otherwise you can't distinguish the loving parent from someone who just enjoys parenting, like a hobby.

Then Marcus said something about how if a person has both the duty motive and the affection motive, Dana has to say the action is only partly worthy or that the affection contaminates it, which is weird. Dana said no, if the duty motive is doing the work then it's fine, the affection is just a bonus. Marcus said but you can't tell which one is doing the work, so by your own epistemic standard you can't give credit to anyone.
