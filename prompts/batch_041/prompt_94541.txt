The examination question was as follows:

Teleosemantic theories of content hold that a state's representing that p consists in its having a certain proper function — of indicating, of corresponding, or of being consumed — so that content is fixed by a history of natural selection rather than by the state's current causal relations to the world. (a) Set out the two most prominent versions of this strategy and identify exactly what does the content-fixing work in each. (b) Some teleosemanticists hold that a single representation can be at once descriptive and directive, as when a signal both conveys where the nectar is and instructs the hive to go there. Explain how such a state is supposed to be possible, and say whether each of the theories from (a) can allow it. (c) Consider two cases: (i) a state that reliably covaries with the presence of a toxin and now controls avoidance behaviour, produced by a mechanism that arose as a by-product of genetic drift and has never been selected for producing it; and (ii) a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a frog, assembled by chance in a swamp, with no evolutionary history. For each case, say what each theory implies about the content of the states involved, and whether that implication should count as an objection to the theory. (d) Assess whether either theory escapes the indeterminacy problem, the charge that it cannot distinguish a state's representing F from its representing some broader condition that always accompanies F.

The answer I have to mark is as follows:

The two leading attempts to ground content in biological function are Dretske's and Millikan's. Dretske begins from information: a state carries the information that F when it is reliably correlated with F as a matter of natural law. Mere correlation is not enough for content, since a state can covary with indefinitely many conditions at once. Dretske therefore adds a function condition: a state represents F only if it has the function of indicating F, and it acquires that function by being recruited into the control of behaviour, so that its job becomes to indicate F. Content is thus fixed by the state's function rather than by what it happens to covary with on a given occasion, and this is what makes misrepresentation possible: a state with the function of indicating F can be tokened when there is no F, and then it is false rather than merely uninformative.

Millikan's account is evolutionary rather than informational. On her view, a state's content is fixed by the proper function of the mechanism that produces it. A representation has the proper function of corresponding to some state of affairs, and the mechanism acquired that function when producing such states contributed to fitness in the lineage. Since proper functions are conferred by a history of selection, whether a state represents anything at all depends on the selective history of the representing device, which is why her view is called biosemantics. The systems that consume a representation — the mechanisms that read it and act on it — must of course have their own proper functions, but on Millikan's account they do not fix content; they only have to be able to use whatever the producer supplies. Content is settled on the production side.

Both views therefore make content depend on something other than current causal connection, which is their main selling point against pure indicator semantics. Millikan's framework also allows states that are at once descriptive and directive: a signal may both correspond to a state of affairs and have the function of eliciting a response, and she treats this as a distinct category rather than a problem, since nothing in her account requires representations to be belief-like or detached from action. Dretske's framework has no natural place for such states, because for him a state's job is to indicate, and any directive element has to be located in a separate desire-like state.

The theories differ over the two cases in (c). On Dretske's view, the drift case turns on whether the state has been recruited as an indicator in the control of behaviour: if it has, it represents the toxin; if the mechanism is a hard-wired by-product that merely happens to covary, it does not. Millikan's view gives a cleaner verdict: no content, because the mechanism has no proper function, and drift confers none. That is a real cost, since the organism behaves in every way as though it represented the toxin. For the swamp frog, both theories deny content, since neither the frog nor its mechanisms have any selection history. This consequence was originally pressed against Millikan by Dretske, and it is generally treated as close to fatal, since it makes content hostage to facts about ancestry that appear irrelevant to what the organism is doing now.

Finally, indeterminacy. The frog's state covaries with flies, with small dark moving things, and with frog food, and the objection is that no teleological theory can say which of these it represents. Dretske's function condition is supposed to narrow the field, but functions are themselves described in ways that inherit the indeterminacy. Millikan's account fares no better: if content is fixed by what the producer mechanism was selected for corresponding to, and corresponding admits of many readings, then her account relocates the disjunction problem rather than solving it. My conclusion is that neither theory succeeds in naturalizing content, and that the teleosemantic programme is best seen as an interesting failure.
