After thirty years of classifying organisms, I have lately been reading about family resemblance — the suggestion that a concept like game need not have any single feature common to all its instances, only overlapping similarities — and I find the idea both attractive and suspicious. Before I read further, I want the specification. Set out what any adequate theory of concepts in this vein must deliver: the phenomena it has to explain, each with the case behind it, such as how we sort a novel instance, why some instances feel more central than others, and how we reason at all with a term that resists definition; the distinctions it cannot collapse, such as extension versus stereotype, vagueness versus open texture, and resemblance versus mere disjunction; and the findings it cannot contradict, including what is known about how children acquire categories and about the behaviour of the classifications I spent my career on, where categories are routinely revised on good empirical grounds and yet still used as though they cut nature at its joints. Tell me which of these are genuine requirements and which are prejudices of one camp, and say plainly which single requirement does the most to eliminate candidates; I suspect at least two of them cannot be satisfied together, and I want you to name the pair and defend the claim. Take a definite position on which account — definitional, prototype, or resemblance-based — survives the list best, and do not hedge. Write it as continuous prose, without headings, lists, or tables.
