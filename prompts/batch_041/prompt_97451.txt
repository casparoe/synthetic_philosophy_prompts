I got into a stupidly long argument with my mate Anders last night and I'm still annoyed about it, so I want you to help me work out what we were actually disagreeing about.

Background: I've been living in Sweden for a few years, Anders is Swedish, and before that I was in the States. The argument was about which attitude towards government is the more reasonable one — the Scandinavian one, where people basically assume the authorities are acting in good faith and that the system broadly works, or the American one, where people assume the people in charge are probably lying to them and need watching like hawks. I said the Scandinavian attitude is more reasonable because it's actually been earned by decades of competent, non-corrupt institutions and it makes everything from tax compliance to public health work. He said no, the American suspicion is more reasonable because it's the correct default in the face of any concentration of power, and high trust just means you aren't paying attention. Here's what's driving me mad: we agreed on all the facts. We agreed Swedish institutions are less corrupt, that the US has had genuine abuses, that high-trust societies function better on loads of measures, that suspicion can tip over into conspiracy thinking. And we still ended up on opposite sides, and every time one of us made a point the other just said "yeah but that's not the point". Two hours, round in circles.

My hypothesis (and I want you to test it, not just agree with it) is that we weren't really disagreeing about governments at all, but about what 'trust' is and what it's for. A few things I've picked up from reading that I think are doing hidden work here, and I want you to bring them in explicitly:

- Trust as a three-place relation: A trusts B to do X. On that picture 'trust in government' is already a bit of a category error, since you don't trust an institution, you trust particular people in particular roles to do particular things. Maybe Anders and I were each smuggling in a different object.
- The difference between trusting someone and merely being confident in them or relying on them — like the difference between trusting a friend to keep a secret and being confident a vending machine will dispense a KitKat. The first seems to involve the other party's attitude towards you; the second is just a prediction. Is 'trust in government' ever the first kind, or always the second kind wearing a nice suit?
- The difference between a general disposition to trust (a kind of moralistic default you carry around) and a strategic judgement about whether this particular body is likely to do this particular thing. Both get measured by the same survey questions and I suspect they're different animals.
- The idea I've seen floating about that falling trust isn't a crisis at all but a sign of citizens becoming more critical and less deferential — i.e. that the 'good old days' of high trust were partly just deference to elites who hadn't been caught yet.
- A move I want you to take seriously: maybe American suspicion isn't a lack of trust but trust placed in a different object — trust in the constitution, in checks and balances, in markets, in the very idea that power should be distrusted — so the two countries might not differ in how much they trust but in what.

So: you're a political philosopher and social epistemologist who works on trust. Before you give me a diagnosis, spend a bit of time brainstorming the candidate diagnoses in your own words — a verbal dispute about the word 'trust', a substantive disagreement about the facts, the two of us answering different questions under one heading, a disagreement about what 'reasonable' means here (tracking the truth vs. being good for a polity), a disagreement about what democracy is even for — then commit to the one you think actually fits and say why the others don't. I don't want a verdict on whether trust or suspicion is better; I want to know what kind of disagreement this is and why it's so stable. Be concrete about what Anders and I each actually said, not about the literature — no reading lists, no citations, I can't look anything up anyway.

A distinction, some piece of evidence, a reframing of the question — or is this one of those disagreements where we should accept we're answering different questions and stop expecting either of us to move?
