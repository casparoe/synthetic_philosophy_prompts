Some friends and I have been working through Derek Parfit's teletransporter thought experiment in a reading group, and I have been trying to fix my own view by running through variations on it, because my verdicts keep refusing to settle behind a single principle. I would like you to work out what principle, if any, they add up to, what it implies about a case I have not decided, and which of my verdicts it forces me to give up.

The version we have been using is a service, call it Meridian, that scans your entire body and brain, transmits the blueprint at the speed of light, and assembles a new body at the far end carrying all your memories and dispositions; in the ordinary case it also destroys the original, painlessly. My verdicts are these. First, the ordinary trip — scanned here, original destroyed, the person who wakes up there with all my memories — I would take it, and I would call whoever arrives me; the loss of the original body costs me nothing I care about. Second, a slow variant in which, over a decade, my neurons are replaced one at a time by silicon equivalents while I stay awake and myself throughout, ending with no biological neuron left: plainly the same person at the end, no question. Third, the split variant, in which the original is destroyed and two replicas are assembled on two different planets, each with all my memories: I think I survive — I am the one on Mars and I am also the one on Titan, and the fact that there are now two of me does not mean that either of them died. Fourth, and this is the one that unsettles me, the copy variant, in which the original is not destroyed and a replica is made while I remain alive and conscious: I do not think the replica is me at all; the original is me, and the replica is a stranger who happens to remember my life. Fifth, a backup case: the company scans me at thirty, I live to forty and then die in an accident, and they restore the thirty-year-old scan into a fresh body; I do not think I survive the accident, since the person they build is a version of me but ten years of my life ended for good.

I am fairly confident about each of these taken alone, yet the third and the fourth look as though they cannot both be right. In the third I accept that a replica assembled from a scan is me even though the original is gone; in the fourth I refuse to let a replica assembled from exactly the same kind of scan be me, and the only difference is that in the fourth the original is still around. If a replica can be me when the original has been destroyed, why can it not be me when the original is still breathing? I suspect the honest answer has something to do with continuity and with branching — perhaps what matters is that my psychological life continue, and that it may branch, but not that it coexist with the original still running — but I cannot state it without its sounding like a trick. So tell me which principle, or small set of principles, fits the five verdicts best; tell me what it predicts for the case I keep dodging, in which Meridian keeps the original body alive but permanently unconscious and unable ever to wake, so that there is one walking, talking replica and one breathing husk; and tell me plainly which of my verdicts, if any, the principle cannot save.
