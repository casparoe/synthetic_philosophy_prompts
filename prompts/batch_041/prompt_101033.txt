The fitting-attitude analysis holds that for something to be valuable just is for it to be fitting, or appropriate, to take up a certain favourable attitude towards it — to admire it, to desire it, to cherish it. Value is not a further property sitting behind the appropriateness of the response; to call something good is to say that the pro-attitude is warranted by the thing.

The standard objection runs as follows. A demon tells you that unless you admire a certain saucer of mud he will torture you. You now have an excellent reason to admire the mud, so the admiration is fitting. But the saucer of mud is not valuable; it is a worthless lump of mud. Since it can be fitting to favour something that has no value at all, fittingness cannot be what value consists in.

The trouble is that the demon's threat is a reason of the wrong kind. It is a prudential reason, aimed at the consequences of your having the attitude, and not a reason that bears on the mud itself. The right kind of reason — the sort that could make the attitude fitting in the relevant sense — is a moral reason, one grounded in the evaluative features of the object. Restrict the analysis to reasons of the right kind, and the counterexample is blocked.

A defender might try to save the analysis by building the restriction in from the start, saying that something is valuable when the pro-attitude towards it is fitting for reasons of the right kind. But this is plainly ad hoc: it stipulates the counterexample away instead of explaining why the demon's reason fails to count. Because the analysis cannot handle the case without that stipulation, we should conclude that value is not a matter of fitting response at all, but a simple, indefinable, non-natural property that we grasp by intuition.
