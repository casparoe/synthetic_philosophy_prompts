I keep running into the following argument and can neither refute it nor accept it, so I should like a verdict on where it breaks. (1) The heritability of a trait is the share of its variance in a given population that is due to genetic variance in that population. (2) If a difference between two people is caused by their genes, then no change to their environments will remove that difference. (3) So for a trait with substantial heritability, the differences among people in it are largely gene-caused and therefore largely immune to environmental change. (4) Adult height is among the most heritable traits we know. (5) Yet mean adult height has risen by several centimetres within a century in industrialised countries, far more than any change in the gene pool could explain. (6) So a trait can be highly heritable and still be substantially altered by changing the environment. (7) Therefore heritability tells us nothing about whether a trait can be changed, and anyone interested in policy should ignore it. I would sooner keep (4) and (6) than accept (7). Tell me which single step is doing the illegitimate work, why it looked innocent, and what it costs me to give it up — decisively, not as a menu of options. Write in British English.
