In 1957 B. F. Skinner published Verbal Behavior, an attempt to account for language as operant behavior — verbal responses shaped and maintained by reinforcement — and in 1959 Noam Chomsky reviewed it in a piece usually treated as the moment the behaviorist program lost its claim on linguistics. Chomsky's objections, as they are ordinarily summarized, were that Skinner's central terms were extended metaphorically, with no independent criteria for what counted as a stimulus, a response, or a reinforcement, and that no history of reinforcement could explain a child's production of novel utterances that are nonetheless appropriate to their occasion; the program that followed — an innate, species-specific faculty, together with the argument that the data available to the child underdetermine the grammar — became the core of generative linguistics. Six decades on, the most capable language technologies are statistical systems trained on large corpora, resembling neither the child of the nativist story nor the rat of the behaviorist one, and their admirers describe them as competence without comprehension.

Read that episode — the review, the collapse of the behaviorist research program, and the statistical sequel — through the pair of inversions of reasoning associated with Darwin and with Turing: the claim that design can be produced without a designer, and the claim that rule-governed competence can be exercised without comprehension of the rules. I do not want a survey of the dispute or a verdict on who was right; I want the mapping. Which of the two inversions, if either, is Skinner's program an instance of, given that reinforcement is itself a selection process operating on variation? Which is Chomsky's counter-program an instance of, given that an innate faculty is a Darwinian object — design without a designer at the level of the species — while the child's use of that faculty requires no comprehension of the grammar it embodies? Say precisely where comprehension sits on each side: in the reinforcement history, in the learner's priors, in the linguist's theory, or in the corpus. Then tell me what the episode shows about the inversions themselves: whether the statistical sequel vindicates them, whether it vindicates only one of the two and not the other, and what the persistence of systematicity-style objections reveals about the gap between competence and comprehension that the inversion was supposed to close. I want an honest account of what the inversions cannot adjudicate here — including whether the two sides were ever answering the same question, and whether the real disagreement concerned where the structure comes from rather than whether comprehension is needed — and a verdict on what would count as evidence that comprehension is doing work the mechanism cannot do without.
