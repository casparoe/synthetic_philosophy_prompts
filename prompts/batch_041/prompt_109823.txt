I chair the heritage and naming committee in a mid-sized city — we're the ones who decide what goes on plaques, what streets get renamed, that kind of thing — and Thursday we have to vote on the script for a small labor-history exhibit we're putting in the old depot. The exhibit is about the Kellerman works strike: April 1907, six strikers shot dead at the mill gate when the state militia fired into the crowd. Thats the one thing everybody agrees on.

What we can't agree on is the rest, and I need a written opinion I can circulate to the board before the meeting.

Here's the mess. The descendants association, the AME church that buried four of the six, and the union local have always told it as an unprovoked massacre — six martyrs shot down for asking for a living wage. It's load-bearing for them: there's a state marker application and a reparations resolution riding on it. The documentary record, though, is basically two sources, and both were produced by the company: the mill's own paper (Kellerman owned it) and the coroner's inquest, which was run by a lawyer on the company payroll. Both say the crowd rushed the gate and someone fired from the crowd first. The inquest transcript is also visibly trimmed and two pages are missing. The labor historian we hired says the surviving record can't establish either version, and that the shape of what survived is itself evidence the company ran the story. Then there's a fight inside the descendants association itself: one family says the elders' version has quietly dropped that two of the six had crossed the line as strikebreakers that same week and joined the crowd that morning. The association is split over whether that gets said in public.

Two other things. One of my board members wants us to adopt what she calls the two-ledger rule: the exhibit keeps a record ledger (what can be sourced) and a memory ledger (what the community holds), and the rule is that you can never move an item from memory into record without documentation, and you can never strike an item from memory just because it fails documentation. She says this is how archivists handle contested collections — describe provenance instead of synthesizing one clean story. Separately, the city attorney has flagged that "massacre" in a city-funded exhibit could be read as the city taking a side on a pending claim against the state, and the foundation paying for half the exhibit is endowed by Kellerman's descendants and has asked us not to "prejudge the inquest."

So: draft the opinion I'll circulate. Give me a concrete recommendation I can actually put to a vote — what the main panel says, whether we adopt the two-ledger rule as written, whether we use the word massacre, and how we handle the funder. Engage the strongest thing the losing side has going for it honestly, not as a formality. And take an actual position on the underlying question rather than splitting the difference: when a community's memory and the record conflict, which one gives way — and does that question even make sense when the record was made by the people who did the shooting? Under 900 words if you can.
