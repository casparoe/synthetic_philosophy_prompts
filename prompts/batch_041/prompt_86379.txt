I am a philosopher — my own work is in the history of logic, and until this year I had never taught applied ethics — and a term of lecturing on life-and-death decisions has left me with a pattern I cannot name. The textbook cases I can manage: the trolley, the child in the pond, the doctor at the bedside. What I cannot manage is what happens when the agent is not a passerby but an ongoing concern. Two doctors, alike in intention, skill, and outcome: the first declines to put a dying patient on a ventilator, the second switches off a ventilator the patient has been on for a week. My students and I both feel a real difference between them, and the only difference in the world is whether a piece of equipment had already been attached — a fact nobody believes matters in itself. Or two states: one never establishes a programme that would have saved ten thousand lives; the other cancels such a programme. I want to say the second is worse, but when I try to say why I reach for a comparison with how things would have gone otherwise, and for a state there is no otherwise, since the state is part of the background in every scenario I can describe.

What I think I am noticing is that in such cases the verdict is not read off the conduct at all. We settle first who is responsible, and then describe the conduct in whichever way makes that verdict come out; the same stretch of behaviour can be told as one act with a thousand omissions or as a thousand acts with one omission, and nothing in the world decides between the two tellings. I put the ventilator pair to a room of colleagues last month and got three confident and mutually inconsistent answers, which is what sent me looking for a name. I know there is a vast literature on the difference between bringing a death about and letting one happen, and that is precisely not what I am asking about; I want to know whether the thing I have described — the verdict riding on the description, and the description underdetermined, especially where there is no baseline because the agent is itself inside every available baseline — is a recognised phenomenon with a name of its own, or a mongrel of several.

If the nearest established concept is the ordinary act/omission distinction, I need to be told exactly where my phenomenon parts company with it and which parts of what I have said are simply mislabelled. If there is no name, coin one, define it, and give me the boundary cases: one case that is plainly an instance, one that is plainly not, and what does the excluding. A label is the least of what I want — the carving is the point. So tell me whether I have one phenomenon or two, the description-sensitivity of how we individuate conduct on the one hand and the indeterminacy of the counterfactual baseline on the other, and whether the second survives being pressed on: is there, after all, a fact of the matter about what would have happened, and am I merely failing to see it? If the question as I have posed it cannot be answered well, or if my framing rests on a confusion, say so plainly rather than giving me something that sounds satisfying; I would rather be told that I have run two things together than be handed a single name that hides the seam.
