I spent thirty-five years as an experimental chemist, and the five since my retirement reading philosophy — slowly, and without much guidance. Last autumn I was appointed as a lay member of the advisory panel that our government convenes whenever it wishes to discuss national wellbeing, and I have found myself in an argument I do not know how to adjudicate.

The panel is deciding whether to fund a public campaign whose declared aim is to lift this country's place in the international happiness rankings. In a single meeting I heard the same four words used to argue both sides. One member said 'money can't buy happiness', and concluded that the campaign is a waste of public money, since the thing the ranking supposedly measures is not for sale. A second member, from the finance ministry, used the identical words to reach the opposite conclusion: precisely because money cannot buy happiness, he said, we should stop treating growth as the measure of national success and start managing the ranking instead. Nobody in the room appeared to notice that they were saying the same thing.

What I want from you is not a verdict on the proverb but a careful disambiguation of it. Set out, as a numbered list, the distinct and precise claims that 'money can't buy happiness' could be making, ordered roughly from the ones that are barely worth asserting to the ones that would be substantial and controversial. For each, say whether it is trivial, defensible but contested, or false, and what kind of evidence would bear on it. Please attend in particular to the distinctions that I suspect are doing hidden work: between a claim about causation and a claim about mere association; between an individual's income and the average income of the country they live in; between a change in income and a level of income; between how people judge their lives as a whole and how they feel from moment to moment; and between what holds of a person and what holds of a nation.

Three further things. First, the ranking in question is built, as I understand it, from answers to a single life-evaluation question — where respondents place their current life on a ladder from nought to ten — with other variables used to explain differences between countries rather than to compute the score. I should like to know which of your readings of the proverb, if true, would actually count against our chasing such a ranking, which would leave the project untouched, and which would tell against the ranking as a measure of happiness at all. Second, say which reading each of my two colleagues must have had in mind for his argument to work, and which reading an ordinary person is most likely to mean when saying the thing at a dinner table rather than in a committee room — including the reading that is in play when the words are used simply to close a discussion. Third, flag whether aiming at a ranking could change what the ranking measures.

Please write in British English.
