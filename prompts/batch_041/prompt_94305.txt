I am preparing a seminar on self-reference and grounding, and I would like a critical reading of Borges's story "The Circular Ruins" through the idea of the tangled hierarchy and its inviolate level — the level that, in Hofstadter's discussion of Escher's "Drawing Hands," is what keeps a loop of mutual drawing intelligible: the two hands draw each other, but the drawing as a whole is made by Escher, who is not himself drawn by the hands. Borges's story looks like a textbook tangle. A man dreams another man into existence, teaches and loves him as a son, sends him away, and at the end walks unharmed into fire and understands that he too is dreamed. My difficulty is that the story appears to offer no inviolate level. Every candidate ground — the dreamer himself, the fiery deity of the ruined temple, some outermost dreamer, Borges as author — either falls back into the regress or lies so far outside the story that the story cannot represent it.

Please give me a reading that takes the story and the concept equally seriously, and that is prepared to conclude that here it is the concept, not the story, that has to give way. Specifically:

First, does the story have an inviolate level, and if so, where? Is it the author, the temple's Fire, the reader, or some level of the narration? If it has none, is it still a tangled hierarchy at all, or does the very notion of a tangle presuppose a level to be tangled relative to? I want you to say plainly whether a work of fiction can be a genuine strange loop with no ground.

Second, distinguish two readings of the term and tell me which one the story needs: an ontological inviolate level, a level that really is not produced by the loop; and a merely formal or pragmatic one, the level at which the rules are stated, inviolate only because we agree to stop asking who wrote it. My suspicion is that Borges collapses the first into the second — that the story presents the inviolate level not as a fact about the world but as a fact about where we stop asking — and I would like you to argue for or against that.

Third, the story is also, unmistakably, about creation: a maker who labors over a son, instructs him, dreads losing him, and is finally humiliated by learning that he is himself a made thing. Where does the inviolate-level reading illuminate this, and where does it flatten a story about a creator's love and terror into a diagram? Tell me where the honest critic has to stop.

Where the story resists the reading, please say so rather than forcing it, and end by telling me what the story contributes to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not — whether it dramatizes something about grounding that a paper could not, or whether it merely illustrates a puzzle I could have posed without it. Skip plot summary I already know, and do not quote the text; I want the argument.
