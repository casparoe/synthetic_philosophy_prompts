I'm a third-year PhD in phil of bio and I keep getting dragged into arguments about what a heritability estimate is even supposed to be a fact about. The thing that set it off: a labmate re-ran a twin analysis on a personality measure and got h² around .65 in one cohort and around .15 in another — same instrument, same country, different decade — and now the whole group wants to know which number is "the" heritability of the trait, and a policy person I talked to at a workshop wants to use the second number to argue the trait is basically plastic and the first to argue its basically fixed. I know the glib answer (neither, it's population-relative, stop reifying it) but I don't have a principled list of what an account of heritability would actually have to deliver, and I want that list before I read one more paper that waves the coefficient around like it settles something.

So write me the desiderata: what any adequate account of heritability has to get right, for the purpose of someone who wants to use heritability claims to say something about (a) what causes differences between people and (b) whether a given trait is a good target for environmental intervention. I want the requirements list — the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it can't collapse, the findings from biology and genetics it can't contradict — with the specific case or consideration behind each requirement, not just the slogan. Things I already suspect belong on the list and want you to either defend or kick out: that it's a property of a population in an environment and not of a trait or a person; that "heritable" must not slide into "genetically determined" or "unmodifiable" (the disease that's highly heritable but fully managed by diet is the obvious pressure test); that the coefficient has to stay separate from the reaction norm; that the "environment" side is a residual and not a measured thing; that passive/evocative/active gene-environment correlation and assortative mating can inflate the estimate; that twin-based, pedigree-based and SNP-based estimates are answering somewhat different questions and the gap between them isn't just statistical noise to be wished away; and that within-group heritability tells you nothing about between-group differences. Flag which of these are genuine desiderata and which are just prejudices of one camp, and — this is the part I most want — tell me whether any pair of them is jointly unsatisfiable, and which single requirement does the most work in knocking out the popular accounts (classical twin-study h², the reaction-norm view, the deflationary "it's just a descriptive statistic" line). A table is fine for the scoring bit if you keep it short; the weight should be on the criteria and why each one is there.
