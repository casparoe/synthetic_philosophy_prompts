Next month I have to teach Nāgārjuna's tetralemma in a single ninety-minute session, to a cohort of graduate students in analytic metaphysics and logic who have read no Buddhist philosophy at all. I want the teaching plan rather than the explanation, and I do not want a reading list, a set of citations, or a survey of scholarly positions; I want the architecture of the explanation, in the order I should actually deliver it.

1. The order. What has to be in place before the four corners can register as anything other than a logical stunt, and what depends on what? My working hunch is that the audience needs some grip on the idea of an intrinsic nature or own-being -- an essence a thing is supposed to have independently of everything else -- before the four corners can be seen as turning on a question rather than on a predicate, and that only once that is in place does the distinction between the two levels of truth earn its keep instead of arriving as mysticism. Tell me where the hunch is wrong, and what must precede what. Assume this audience will not be moved by the suggestion that Madhyamaka runs on a different logic; they will reach immediately for paraconsistent or intuitionistic readings as the charitable option, and I need the sequence to make that move unnecessary rather than merely forbidden.

2. The carrying example: one case that does the work for the whole session, together with an account of what it must have and what it must lack. I do not want the chariot, the fire and its fuel, the dream, or the mirage. I want something from ordinary contemporary life that the room already has a stake in, and I want to know whether the candidates I would reach for -- an artifact with parts, a university, a river, a crowd at a station -- satisfy the constraints. In particular, I am worried that any example of a whole composed of parts invites the audience to hear a reductionist or eliminativist moral, which is the wrong lesson; tell me what the example must not invite, and how to block that inference inside the example rather than after it.

3. The misconceptions, in the order they will form, each with the point in the sequence at which it should be headed off. My rough list: that emptiness is nihilism; that it is idealism or mind-only; that the fourth corner is a third truth-value or an ineffable both-and; that the tetralemma is a thesis and therefore refutes itself; that dependent origination is just causation; and, the one I fear most, that the tetralemma is a device for proving that reality is contradictory. Say which of these must be pre-empted before the example, which during it, and which only afterwards, and what must already be true of the sequence for each head-off to land.

4. The check. One question or task for the last ten minutes that will separate a student who has understood what the four corners are doing from one who has merely learned to say that everything is empty. I want it to fail specifically for the student who has understood only that all four corners are rejected.

5. What I should openly decline to cover at this level, and say so out loud.

Two further constraints. Do not let the plan depend on contested textual scholarship or on the historical development from one thinker to the next; if a step requires me to take sides on a disputed reading of Nāgārjuna -- for instance on whether he is entitled to a thesis of his own -- flag it as such or route around it. And the session should leave the students able to do something, not merely to have heard something.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in the plan as a whole, and single out the one point in it you are least sure of, whether because the pedagogy is speculative or because the reading of Madhyamaka it assumes is contested.
