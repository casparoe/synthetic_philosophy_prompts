Next month my old boss is retiring from the copy desk after thirty years, and I've been volun-told to give the send-off at the newsroom party. She was an Orwell person down to her bones — fuzzy writing lets you get away with fuzzy politics, jargon and dead metaphors are how people avoid meaning what they say — but her real heresy, the thing she'd corner you with at the coffee machine, was that the whole plain-style gospel is a bit of a con: that "just say it clearly" is itself a move in a game, the voice of whoever already has the mic, and that some true things about power can only be said sideways, through indirection, allusion, even a little deliberate murk. Half the room are old reporters who'll hear any defense of murk as a betrayal of everything she drilled into them; the other half are her family, who've never read a word of Orwell and just want it to be warm and funny. Write the five-minute speech. It has to actually make her case rather than just gesture at it, and here's the hard part — she'd want no notes on the prose either: no dead metaphors, no prefab phrases, nothing ducking behind the passive voice. If honoring her argument means the speech has to break the very rule she'd have marked in red, do it on purpose and let the room feel why.
