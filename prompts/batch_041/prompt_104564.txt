I am preparing a seminar on the concept of mental disorder in which students will assess Szasz's claim that mental illness is a myth, and I want them to argue against a specification rather than against a slogan. Before they evaluate any position, they need the standard it is being held to. Set out the desiderata that any adequate account of mental disorder must satisfy: the cases it must classify correctly (conditions once counted as disorders and now treated as ordinary variation, conditions now counted as disorders that were formerly vice or crime, conditions that respond to treatment but whose status as disease is disputed, and borderline presentations such as prolonged grief or attenuated psychosis); the distinctions it must not collapse (disorder versus deviance, disorder versus disability, the fact of distress versus the judgment that some capacity is impaired); and the constraints it must respect from medicine and law (that a diagnosis licenses treatment, that it can excuse conduct, and that one category has to serve clinicians, insurers, and courts at once). Give the consideration behind each requirement, mark which are non-negotiable and which are commitments of a particular camp, and identify the requirement that does the most to exclude candidates. Then indicate briefly how Szasz's position and a naturalistic alternative such as the harmful-dysfunction analysis fare against the list; keep the weight on the criteria, since the scorecard is the part I will do with the class.
