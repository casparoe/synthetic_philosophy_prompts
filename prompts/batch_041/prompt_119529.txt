ok so I'm writing the final for my upper-level seminar (it's cross-listed, half philosophy of mind people, half AI ethics people) and I need one big essay question plus the answer key. I want it built around something they definitely haven't read, so it actually tests thinking rather than recall. Here's the setup I want you to use, roughly as I've got it in my head:

There's a fictional startup called Halcyon. They've built a compiler they call "valence-preserving compilation." You write your program at a high level, with variables like damage-signal, aversive-valence, relief, report, and the compiler guarantees that whatever hardware it lands on, that abstract feedback structure is preserved — the thing that makes some of the system's states count as good-for-it and others bad-for-it. They've built a chip called ORPHEUS and they're marketing it as "sentience-compatible": not sentient, they're careful about that, just structurally in the clear.

Two critics. Critic 1 says: fine, the valence structure survives compilation, but it isn't what's doing the causal work. The real causal transactions in the chip are transistor voltages, and the mapping from those to your valence variable is a coarse-graining the engineers picked; you could carve the same silicon into a different abstract machine tomorrow and the valence would evaporate. So ORPHEUS's valence is decorative, epiphenomenal. Critic 2 says: doesn't matter, silicon can't be sentient, end of story.

The thing I want the question to force: Critic 1's argument looks like it also cuts against the functionalist story about brains. A brain's pain state is also a coarse-graining over ions and vesicles that some neuroscientist chose. So either the objection proves too much and has to be weakened in some principled way, or the student has to find something that makes the brain case different that isn't just "it's made of meat" — because if that's all they've got they're just Critic 2 in a labcoat. That's the crux. Make the question live or die on it.

So what I need from you:

1. The exam question itself. 90 minutes, essay-length. It should tell them to (a) say what Halcyon's claim actually amounts to, (b) say whether Critic 1's objection succeeds and, if it doesn't, exactly where it has to be weakened so it doesn't also sink brains, and (c) say what a criterion for "morally relevant computation" would have to look like given all that. Make it clear they get credit for the honest answer if the honest answer is "we can't know yet, and here's what we do under that uncertainty" — I dont want them to feel they have to fake a criterion they can't defend. But "we can't know" can't be a free pass either: the uncertainty answer has to earn its marks by saying what would actually reduce the uncertainty. And the question should not be answerable by reciting the functionalism-vs-biological-naturalism debate; that's the trap I'm trying to avoid.

2. A model answer, 600-700 words, written the way a strong student would write it. No citations, no name-dropping of papers or authors — they can't look anything up in the exam and I don't want the model answer resting on stuff they'd have had to memorize. It can use whatever standard vocabulary is fair game for the course (functionalism, multiple realization, the worry that a system's computational description might not be its causally efficacious description, the intentional stance, precautionary reasoning, whatever) but it has to do the work with it, not gesture at it. Also: don't let it just declare a winner in the Halcyon case. The point of the question is the criterion, not the verdict. If it lands on a verdict, it should land there as a consequence of the criterion, and it should be honest that the verdict is underdetermined.

3. The marking scheme. 20 marks total, broken into the must-hit moves with the marks each is worth, one line on what separates an A from a B answer, and — this is the part I care about most — for each must-hit move, a line on how students typically get it wrong. The failure modes I specifically want caught: (i) "silicon can't be sentient" smuggled in as a premise; (ii) using the causal-imprecision objection against the chip without noticing it hits brains too; (iii) treating "we can't know" as an answer instead of a starting point; (iv) conflating the question "which computations matter morally" with "which systems do we have reason to treat as if they matter" — those are seperate questions and I want a student who spots that to get credit for spotting it.

Couple of small things. I've got one very literal student who will ask "is ORPHEUS one chip or a family of chips," so make the setup tight enough that the question is well-posed. And keep the exam question itself in one voice, no headers or bullet points — students find that clinical. The model answer and marking scheme can be structured however's clearest.
