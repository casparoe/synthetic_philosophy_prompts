I'm putting together the last session of my undergrad seminar on the sunk cost fallacy, and I want to close it with a poem instead of a summary, because the whole point is that the recieved version — only marginal costs and benefits count, what's spent is spent, anyone who says we've come too far to turn back is committing a fallacy — is too tidy. I want the poem to hold three objections that people usually mush into one, and I want them kept separate:

first, the past as a reason. Not a cause — a reason. If I promised someone who's now dead that I'd finish the thing, then the years already gone aren't simply gone, and stopping looks more like a betrayal than a saving. That's not the same as saying the money is a reason; it's saying some reasons face backwards.

second, the past as evidence. The fact that I put so much in is a signal that I once had private grounds for thinking it would work — so treating the past as nothing throws away information, even though information isn't itself a reason.

third, the past as a device. A blanket rule of never quitting can be the rational thing to adopt in advance even if each particular act of honouring it is irrational on the day; and in a standoff, the fact that you can't get your stake back is exactly what makes your threat credible.

Can you write me a villanelle? The two refrains should be the two things people actually shout at each other in this argument — the cut-your-losses line and the we've-come-too-far line — and the form should do the work of showing why they keep passing each other: same lines, different contexts, slightly different sense each time round, until the closing quatrain makes it clear the two refrains aren't contradicting each other but answering different questions (one's about a single decision, the other about a rule, or a relationship, or a reputation). Give me one stanza from inside the trap — the voice that can see it's throwing good money after bad and goes on anyway, because stopping now would mean admitting the years were wasted. I'd rather the phrase sunk cost never appears in the poem; it should earn it. And please don't resolve it at the end — the last two lines can meet, but they shouldn't pretend the two sides were secretly the same all along. British spelling, and it has to be accurate enough that someone who's actually read the escalation-of-commitment literature wont wince.
