I am a novelist, and for the better part of a year I have been in correspondence with a friend who teaches logic about a proof she showed me and which I have not been able to put down: the one that begins with the innocent thought that whatever is true could in principle be found out, and arrives — by way of almost no machinery at all, only the plain fact that knowledge cannot be false and that whoever knows a conjunction knows each of its parts — at the conclusion that every truth is already known. She tells me the derivation is sound as far as it goes, and that the only live question is what the anti-realist, who wants truth to be answerable to inquiry, is to do about it. I spend my working life arranging facts that no reader will ever be told, so a picture on which nothing true can escape discovery forever is not a neutral one for me.

Here is the proposal I would like you to weigh. My friend suggests that the knowability principle should not be read as a claim about the bare possibility of knowledge — that some possible knower, somewhere, might know — but as a claim about inquiry itself: a truth counts as knowable only if there is a route to it, a constructible sequence of epistemic improvements, each step licensed by what is already known or observed, running from where we now stand to knowledge of it. On that reading the offending proposition, that p is true and that no one will ever know it, is not knowable, and not because of a stipulation, but because no route to it exists: any route that ended in knowing p would have destroyed the second half of what it was supposed to deliver. She takes this to be a principled rescue rather than a rescue by fiat, and I am not so sure.

Give me your credence, and give me the arithmetic behind it. Take the proposition to be this: there is a principled restriction of the knowability principle which blocks the derivation and leaves the anti-realist thesis contentful — that is, the paradox does not refute anti-realism. Please decompose the number, with a rough probability and a line of justification for each term: how much turns on whether the anti-realist is in fact committed to the unrestricted principle, one applying to every proposition, including those that speak of knowers and of ignorance; how much on whether a restriction can be motivated by something other than the paradox itself, which is the whole difference between a principled restriction and an ad hoc one; how much on whether the inquiry-path restriction she favours earns its keep, and in particular whether it ever diverges from the simpler restriction to propositions whose being known is not ruled out in advance, since if it never diverges it is only a relabelling; how much on whether, once restricted, the position still asserts something a realist would deny, rather than collapsing either into realism or into the trivial claim that we know what we in fact know; and how much on what exactly the paradox is a paradox for. Before any of that, tell me whether you think the derivation itself has a weak link, since if it does the rest is moot. I would also like to know which term the final number is most sensitive to, and which single argument or consideration would move you most.

For what it is worth, I put myself at about 0.2 and she puts herself at about 0.75. Tell me which of those terms our disagreement most likely lives in.
