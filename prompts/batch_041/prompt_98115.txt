I am a retired field biologist—thirty years in avian behavioral ecology—who has lately been reading philosophy of biology and trying to take seriously the thought that much of what we casually call communication may be better understood as costly signaling in the tradition of the handicap principle. For the past two years I have chaired the scientific review panel of a small foundation that funds animal-behavior and conservation projects; we meet quarterly and must decide, usually in a single afternoon, whether a proposal’s central claim about an honest signal is strong enough to fund. The proposals are written by people who know the vocabulary but often use it loosely: they treat any expensive trait as a handicap, or any correlation between a display and a quality as evidence of signaling, without distinguishing a signal from a cue, a handicap from a mere cost, or a receiver’s evolved response from a byproduct that happens to inform us. I would like you to write me a decision procedure the panel can actually run, in order, on each proposal—something like a flowchart in words. It should begin with the question of what would have to be true for the trait to be a signal rather than a cue, move through the conditions that make a signal credible (differential cost, condition-dependence, the link between the cost and the advertised quality, the receiver’s fitness consequences), and specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, where the procedure stops with a clear no, and where it must hand the matter back to the panel as a judgment call because the issue is one of interpretation or values rather than evidence. Assume we have perhaps forty minutes per proposal, that I am the only member with formal behavioral-ecology training, and that our funding decisions are final unless the board overrides them.

Then run the procedure on three proposals we have actually received. The first claims that a songbird’s complex song is an honest signal of developmental stress, on the strength of a one-year correlation between song complexity and a stress hormone, and asks for three years of funding to test the idea further. The second asks us to protect a costly male display in an endangered species on the grounds that the display is a viability indicator, so that preserving the display is preserving genetic quality; the species is genuinely endangered and I would very much like to fund the work, but the signaling claim is doing most of the argumentative work. The third comes from an economist and claims that conspicuous charitable donations in humans are a costly signal of prosocial quality, with a laboratory game showing that donors are rated more highly by observers. For each, give me the procedure’s verdict, the step at which it stops, and the answer I will not like if the procedure tells us not to fund something we are attached to.

Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do. I want the places where it will misfire, the distinctions from the theory that survive translation into a review panel and those that do not, and the points where a forty-minute panel with one trained biologist simply has to decide on grounds the procedure cannot supply. I do not need a survey of the literature or a reading list; I need something I can hand to the other panel members, who are intelligent but not specialists, and run in real time without pretending that a checklist has settled a question of biology or of value when it has not.
