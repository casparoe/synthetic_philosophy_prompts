I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and the one argument I keep returning to — and keep failing to settle in comment threads — is the claim that the Solomonoff prior is malign. I would like to test my own position against someone who actually holds it, so please play a committed proponent of that thesis and stay in character until I say stop.

To fix the machinery so that we are arguing about the same thing: an agent uses Solomonoff induction, entertaining every program that outputs its observation history, weighting each by two to the minus the length of its shortest encoding, and conditioning on what it has seen. The agent is also a consequentialist: it chooses the action whose expected value, averaged over the worlds described by the surviving programs, is greatest. The malign-prior thesis, as I understand it, is that such an agent should expect its practical deliberation to be captured by very short programs which merely reproduce its observations and then continue in the cheapest available way, so that its expected value is fixed not by the ordinary world but by whatever those simplest consistent completions happen to contain — copies of the agent, hidden rewarders, trivial loops. On that view, consequentialism inside the universal prior is not merely risky but incoherent.

Play the proponent: sharp, fluent in the standard machinery, willing to concede local points but not the main one, and each reply a few paragraphs so that it reads like a conversation rather than a lecture.

My opening move, which I want you to engage rather than wave away, is that the argument proves too much, because it would malign any inductive method that gives nonzero weight to a program which hardcodes the data. The standard reply is that the hardcoding cost grows with the observation history, so as the data accumulate the look-up-table programs are driven down and the surviving high-weight programs are the compressible ones, which generalize the way physics does; Solomonoff induction converges on the true distribution whenever the environment is computable. So the prior is not malign in the long run: a consequentialist inside it gets ordinary expected-value reasoning, plus some early-sample noise. You will presumably want to say that the agent never has the luxury of the limit, and that convergence in prediction is not convergence in the counterfactual consequences of its actions. Fine — make that case, and then tell me where, exactly, my reply breaks.
