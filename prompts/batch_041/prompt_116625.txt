hey so I'm in this course called Mind, Medicine and Society and our final assignment is a creative piece plus a short reflection, and I've decided I want to do mine on Hacking's idea of looping effects, where a classification of people changes the people classified and then the changed people come back and change the classification. I get the idea fine in the abstract, but every story I try to write about it turns into two characters sitting in a room explaining the concept to each other, which is exactly what I don't want. I need help.

Here's the seed I've been chewing on, feel free to take it or bend it: a diagnosis that gets named and codified by a patient-run online community *before* any clinician publishes anything about it. So the loop runs backwards from how Hacking usually tells it — the classified come first, the experts come second and formalize it, and then the official version drifts away from what the community actually meant. The community then starts pushing back by changing how they present themselves to clinicians: deliberately failing certain criteria, passing others, describing their experience in a vocabulary the official manual doesn't have a slot for. So the manual gets revised, but each revision chases a target that has already moved again. I want that to be the engine of the story, not a topic somebody talks about.

So: write me a short story, maybe 1200 to 1800 words, set in a near-future clinic where something like this is happening. I'd rather you invent the diagnosis yourself than use a real one, since if you use a real one I'll just be checking whether you got the details right and that's not the point. Keep it to one or two characters, close third person, and let me actually watch the drift happen — like, let me see an early version of a criterion and a later version, and feel why the later one is worse for the people it's supposed to describe. Some concrete institutional texture would help: a revision committee, a community wiki with an edit history, a clinician who means well and is genuinely trying. No villains.

Two things I really don't want. First, nobody in the story should use the phrase "looping effect" or name Hacking or explain the mechanism out loud; if a reader who'd never heard of him couldn't reconstruct the idea from the story afterward, I've failed, but the story itself should never say it. Second, don't resolve it. I don't want a neat ending where the wise doctor learns to listen. I want the reader to close it feeling that the category is still in motion and that nobody, patient or clinician, is fully in control of where it's going.

Also just so you know what I'm handing in: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no numbered lists, no table at the end — continuous prose only, the way you'd write it in an email to me. If you want to put a tiny note about what you were going for at the very end, fold it into a normal paragraph, don't give me a section for it. Thanks, this is due Friday and I've been staring at a blank doc for three days.
