I am a novelist, and for the past year I have been writing a book that follows one family from the late 1960s to the present. The book keeps forcing a question I cannot settle for myself: when I write a character as a member of a generation — as someone formed by the moment she came of age — am I describing something real about her, or am I dressing her in a category that a marketing department invented and that I would be foolish to build a life on? To get some purchase on this, I asked two acquaintances to talk it through with me. They ended up arguing for an hour. I have no stake in who was right, and I have tried below to reproduce the substance of what they said without favoring either of them.

Mira works with population data; Teo writes cultural criticism. Mira went first, roughly as follows. Generational labels are not discoveries, she said. The line between one label and the next is drawn a year or two either way depending on who is drawing it; the same label is applied to whole countries whose young people grew up under utterly different conditions; and, worse, the pattern the label claims to name cannot be identified from the data at all. Because a person's birth year plus her age just is the calendar year, age, period, and cohort are three descriptions of two quantities. You cannot separate a cohort effect from an age effect or from a period effect without importing an assumption that is not itself in the data. Every generational story can be rewritten as a story about what it is like to be twenty-five, or as a story about what happened in a particular year, and nothing in the evidence chooses between the rewritings.

Teo replied that this is a fact about what our instruments can separate, not about what exists. Real kinds in the world, he said, are usually clusters rather than essences: their members share a bundle of properties that tend to go together because some mechanism holds them together, and such kinds are graded at the edges rather than fenced. A cohort that comes of age inside the same economy, the same schools, and the same media can acquire precisely that kind of clustered resemblance. A boundary drawn arbitrarily can still cut a real gradient. And the label is itself one of the mechanisms: once a cohort is named, its members are told what they are, institutions begin to address them as a bloc, and the resemblance tightens. That makes the kind more real over time, not less.

Mira answered that if the label is part of the mechanism, then the kind is real only relative to a naming practice that could have gone otherwise and could be withdrawn — which is what a market segment is. On that account the interesting causal unit is the label together with the institutions that take it up, not the people; the people are the surface on which the category does its work.

Teo said that was too strong. Formative conditions and the label are both mechanisms, he said, and a cluster held together by several mechanisms is the paradigm of a real kind rather than of a construct. To insist on one mechanism and a sharp boundary, he said, is to reinstate the essentialism that the cluster view was supposed to retire.

Mira granted the point but said it had a price: once real just means that there is a resemblance worth noticing, the question whether generations are real kinds has no content left beyond whether the resemblance is useful. Teo replied that usefulness is not the only test; the resemblance also has to be produced by mechanisms rather than by the act of counting.

I would like you to adjudicate this exchange point by point rather than tell me which of them I should believe. For each of the moves above, say how strong it is and why, and mark clearly where a move is mistaken, where it is right but overstated, and where it is right as far as it goes. I am especially interested in whether the two threads they keep pulling — the statistical point about what can be identified from the data, and the metaphysical point about what makes something a kind — are genuinely connected, or whether one of them is quietly being used to settle a question it does not touch. Tell me what each of them is missing that the other cannot supply. And since this is ultimately a question about how I should write these people, close by saying what, if anything, the exchange should change about the way a novelist treats a generational label: a fact to be discovered, a convention to be used knowingly, or something in between.

End with a single final line of exactly this form, with nothing after it: \answer{...}, where the braces contain one of these four labels — cohort-realist, label-realist, conventionalist, no-fact-of-the-matter — chosen to state your overall verdict on whether generational kinds are real.
