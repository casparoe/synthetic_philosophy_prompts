An argument I keep running into in discussions of forgery goes like this: a symphony is the same symphony whether it is played in Vienna or Tokyo, by this orchestra or that one; nobody calls a good performance a counterfeit, and nobody demands to see the composer's manuscript. If the musical work can be fully present in any faithful performance, why should a painting be any different? On this view a perfect replica of a Vermeer delivers everything the original delivers, and the market's premium on the original is snobbery dressed up as aesthetics.

I would like that analogy audited rather than dismissed. Set out the correspondences explicitly — what plays the role of the score, the work, the performance, the performer, and the audience — then mark where the mapping breaks, and separate the breaks that are load-bearing for the conclusion from those that are merely decorative. Two complications deserve particular attention: that the very people who are relaxed about copies of paintings will pay handsomely for period instruments and original tempi, and that some art forms (prints, casts, photographs, and arguably literature) seem to sit between the two cases rather than on either side. Then say whether the conclusion survives — that a perceptually indistinguishable replica is worth just as much as the original, and that the premium on originals is unjustified — and, if it does not survive intact, whether some better analogy would support a weaker version of it. Argue it on the merits; I don't need references.
