I'm a novelist — historical stuff, mostly — and I'm about 200 pages into a book set in Dublin in the 1730s, a family of linen merchants, and there's a subplot about a scheme for a national bank that's tearing the family apart. Research rabbit hole: I've been reading Berkeley's Querist, which isn't really an argument so much as a few hundred questions in a row, and one of them stopped me cold: "Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?" A bit later he asks whether "less money swiftly circulating, be not, in effect, equivalent to more money slowly circulating" — and I realized I don't actually know how to make two people fight about this in a room.

So here's what I need. Write me the scene as a dialogue between two of my characters: (1) a hard-money merchant, fifty-ish, who's spent his life accumulating silver and is convinced that a coin's worth is in the metal, that paper is a scheme for making debts disappear, and that tampering with the standard is a form of theft from anyone who ever lent or was owed; and (2) a younger curate with a mathematical bent who's swallowed the ticket view whole — money's only job is to stir up industry, wealth is really the produce of land and labour, tickets are just counters for recording and transferring claims, and a bank that issues them could put the poor to work instead of leaving them to beg. I want roughly 800 words of them actually arguing, not trading speeches: each should pick up what the other just said and turn it, and neither should be a fool or a mouthpiece — I want the merchant to land at least one blow the curate can't fully parry (something about promises, or about who bears the loss when the tickets turn out to be worth less than what they were traded for), and I want the curate's view to cost him something too, not just win. Keep it in the 1730s — clipped coin, bills of exchange, absentee landlords, a man's credit resting on his neighbours' opinion of his character — and don't let them start talking like modern economists with spreadsheets. If the argument bottoms out somewhere genuinely unresolved, even better; I'd rather the scene end on a question than a thesis. I'm a novelist, not an economist, so no lecture please, just the scene.
