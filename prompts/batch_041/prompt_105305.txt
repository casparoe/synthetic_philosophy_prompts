Last term I sat in on a working meeting of a group drafting diagnostic criteria, and two of the senior members spent most of the hour arguing about a single proposed category. The category — call it affective overload syndrome — is defined entirely by reported experience and observable behaviour, with no biomarker and no agreed physiological marker. The pilot data on the table had two features. When the drafters lowered the threshold to include a milder presentation, the number of people meeting criteria roughly tripled, while two independent measures of impairment — missed work days and clinician-rated severity — stayed flat. And in the follow-up interviews, a good number of the newly qualifying participants had started describing themselves in the manual's own vocabulary; several said they had always been this way, though they had not used that description before they encountered it.

Marta opened. This, she said, is Hacking's looping effect in its purest form: the classification has recruited its own cases, and the recruits have taken up the description and made it true of themselves. Nothing here is being discovered, because the thing being measured reorganises itself around the measurement. A category that partly manufactures its own extension cannot be validated the way a disease is; the target moves while you aim at it.

(1) Dev answered that the label changed who fell above the line, but falling above a line is a bureaucratic fact, not a clinical one. Loosening criteria always changes the count; that is arithmetic, not metaphysics. If the impairment measures did not move, that is information about the sensitivity of the measures, not about whether the condition exists.

(2) Marta replied that "insensitive" is doing all the work in that move. Any measure that fails to budge when prevalence triples can be defended by declaring it the wrong measure, and then no evidence could ever count against the category.

(3) Dev said no: we can test it. If the newly qualifying group is genuinely ill, it should show the same correlates as the original group — family history, course over time, response to treatment. If it does not, we have a boundary problem, not an ontological one.

(4) Marta objected that looping makes even that circular, since the correlates are themselves often ascertained by self-report in the diagnostic vocabulary, and as the vocabulary spreads the correlates spread with it. She then offered a test of her own, which she called the subtraction test: ask what the population would look like if the term had never circulated, holding everything else fixed. If the profile changes, the category is partly constituting its own cases; if it does not, it is tracking something independent of us.

(5) Dev said the test is incoherent, because you cannot hold everything else fixed while removing the very thing that is doing part of the work; the counterfactual is undefined. You cannot ask what the placebo effect would be if no one believed anything. Marta answered that by that standard no controlled trial asks a meaningful question either, which is absurd. Dev said it is not absurd — the answer is just a range rather than a point, and it is the width of the range that tells you how stable the category is.

(6) Marta closed by saying that even a narrow range is sustained by the label, and that this is a reason to prefer descriptions that do not feed back into their subjects, or at least to count the feedback as a cost. Dev said the feedback cuts both ways, since a name can also give people access to care and to one another, and whether that is a cost depends on whether the condition is worth naming — which the looping argument by itself cannot settle. Marta said that on his view the category is vindicated by its usefulness, and usefulness is exactly what a social kind has; he had conceded her point. Dev said he had conceded only that psychiatric kinds are historical, and that historical is not the same as unreal.

I was there as a guest and have no stake in either side. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, rating each numbered move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justifying each rating briefly. Then tell me what both of them are missing, in particular: whether the subtraction test can be made coherent, whether the tripling of prevalence with flat impairment is evidence of looping or evidence of nothing in particular, and whether the fact that a category partly makes its own cases is a defect in the category or simply a feature of psychiatric kinds that any adequate account of them has to absorb.
