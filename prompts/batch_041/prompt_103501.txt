Imagine a finite spectrum of one thousand possible lives, L1 through L1000. Each life differs from the next by an imperceptible increment in a single dimension, say, mild chronic pain, so that no adjacent pair seems to differ in betterness, yet L1 is clearly better than L1000. Consider the following six proposed principles for the betterness relation when applied to such spectra:

1. Strict transitivity: if A is better than B and B is better than C, then A is better than C, without exception.
2. Contextual threshold: differences count as betterness only when they exceed a threshold fixed by the context of comparison; sub-threshold differences are treated as ties.
3. Parity: betterness is transitive, but some pairs are neither better, worse, nor equal; they are incommensurable.
4. Local comparability: each adjacent pair in the spectrum is comparable, but transitivity can fail across sufficiently long chains.
5. Supervaluationist sharpening: there is a precise betterness ordering, but language and evidence leave it indeterminate which precise ordering is correct.
6. Lexical plateau: every sub-threshold difference is a tie, but once enough such differences accumulate, the later item is better by a discrete jump.

Rate each principle from 0 to 10 on (a) fidelity to the intuitive data of spectrum cases, (b) resistance to preference-cycle and exploitation objections, and (c) fit with ordinary comparative language. Then rank the six principles by their overall plausibility as a theory of betterness in spectrum cases, and for each say in one sentence why it lands where it does. Finally, identify the principle you would be most willing to abandon and describe the single most important revision you would make to it to answer the strongest objection against it.
