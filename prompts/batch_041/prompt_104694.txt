Our debate club's briefing sheet summarizes the precautionary principle as follows. It says that an activity or technology should be banned unless it has first been proven safe, whatever the cost of the ban and however small the risk; that the principle's core claim is a reversal of the burden of proof, so that the innovator must demonstrate safety rather than the public demonstrate harm; that it was written into environmental treaties and has since been extended to pesticides, nuclear power, and now artificial intelligence; and that because it demands certainty before anyone may act it is unscientific, while because it would equally forbid the technologies that might solve the problems it fears — and, once precaution's own costs are counted, forbid precaution itself — it cannot be a serious guide to policy and is at best a slogan. Is this a fair summary? I have a vague sense that there is more than one version of the principle and that the sheet may be describing the strongest one, but I cannot tell. Please go claim by claim and tell me what a careful defender would accept, what is a common simplification, what is a genuine misrepresentation, and whether the closing claim — that the principle cannot guide policy — follows from anything true in the summary.
