I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, working on a dissertation chapter about how contemporary popular Stoicism has absorbed Epictetus, and I've hit a stretch of text I can't get to sit still. Here is the opening of the Enchiridion in the public-domain translation I've been working from:

"Some things are in our control and others not. Things in our control are opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion, and, in a word, whatever are our own actions. Things not in our control are body, property, reputation, command, and, in one word, whatever are not our own actions."

And then, in the short section on desire and aversion:

"Use only the appropriate actions of pursuit and avoidance; and even these lightly, and with gentleness and reservation."

Three things puzzle me, and I'd like help thinking them through rather than a summary of the handbook.

First, the second sentence of the opening looks as though it wants to be a substantive division of the world into two classes, but the way it is closed off — "in a word, whatever are our own actions" ... "in one word, whatever are not our own actions" — threatens to make the division exhaustive by definition, and therefore empty, unless "actions" is doing technical work. Is the claim informative, or is the appearance of a dichotomy generated by the phrasing?

Second, the reservation clause. If pursuing and avoiding are themselves among the things said to be in our control, why must the pursuit be undertaken "with reservation"? It reads to me as though the object of pursuit, and not merely its outcome, is being held at arm's length, which sits awkwardly with the first passage. I'd like to know what work "reservation" is doing and whether it presupposes something the opening passage denies.

Third, and this is the one I most need help with: a colleague in my seminar has suggested that the standard rendering of the key phrase as "in our control" smuggles in a causal notion — what I can bring about or influence — whereas the underlying expression is closer to "up to us" or "dependent on us," and that the dichotomy is therefore a claim about what is attributable to us as agents, not a claim about the causal structure of the world. On her reading, the self-help use of Epictetus is not a simplification of the text but a different thesis altogether. I can see the appeal and I can see how it might be an overcorrection. Please adjudicate: does the causal reading or the attribution reading fit the passage better, what in the text bears on the question either way, and if the attribution reading wins, what precisely does the popular use get wrong and does any of it survive?

Please say how confident you are in each claim you make, and single out the one point you are least sure of — I would rather have your hedges than false precision. I don't need a reading list or citations; I have the editions, and what I want is your reasoning about the text as it stands.
