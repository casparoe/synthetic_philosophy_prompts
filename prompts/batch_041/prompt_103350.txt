I am presenting a paper at a decision-theory reading group next month and need a Q&A preparation sheet. My thesis: Death in Damascus is not a counterexample to causal decision theory but a case of cutting the act too finely. The object of rational choice is not the act of staying or fleeing but the agent's standing disposition, settled before the prediction is made; once the causal graph is drawn at that grain the prediction sits downstream of the disposition, so causal decision theory on its own delivers a determinate recommendation and no retreat to evidential reasoning is required.

The audience: roughly three-quarters causal decision theorists, fond of pressing the why-ain't-you-rich objection against evidentialism and allergic to anything that looks like choosing your own algorithm; two committed evidentialists who treat Death in Damascus as their best advertisement and will say I have simply relabelled their view; and one statistician convinced the whole literature is a muddle about what it means to condition on one's own action. None of them will have read the paper.

Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way that member of the audience would put it, with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is soft; then name the single weakest point in my position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody notices, and include the one question I am dreading. My sketch, so the objections can aim at it: the predictor has already placed Death according to its prediction, so I hold the placement fixed and argue the disposition was the real locus of choice all along; I concede the disposition is not chosen in the moment, but claim that is equally true of every decision, and that the causal link runs through the predictor's simulation of the disposition rather than through the present act.
