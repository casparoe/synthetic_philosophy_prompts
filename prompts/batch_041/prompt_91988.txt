I run a seventy-five-minute session for about fifteen undergraduates in a philosophy-of-biology seminar, and I want an activity that makes them feel the pull of the view that life has no essence: that any definition they reach for will either count something they are certain is alive as dead or something they are certain is dead as alive, and that the choice of criterion is doing work no further fact can settle. Arrange the rules so the group has to produce a single verdict, alive or not, on each item in a fixed list, and only afterwards discovers that their verdicts commit them to a criterion several of them will reject on reflection; I want no lecture up front and no mention of cluster concepts or family resemblance before the debrief. Build in a forced choice or a scoring rule that makes the tension bite, and give me the materials, the order of play, a timeline that fits the seventy-five minutes, and a debrief that maps particular moments onto definition by necessary and sufficient conditions, family resemblance, and the possibility that life is a concept we should engineer rather than discover. Propose the cases I should use, and tell me if you would swap any of these: a virus, a prion, a sterile mule, a frozen seed, fire, a self-replicating computer program, and an organism engineered in a lab to metabolize but never reproduce. Then tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. Please write your answer as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a letter.
