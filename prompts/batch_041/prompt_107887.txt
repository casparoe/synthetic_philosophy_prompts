I'm writing a novel — my narrator is an anesthesiologist who was the attending on a case where the patient remembered the surgery afterwards, and I've spent the last month reading my way into how the field actually decides whether somebody is conscious on the table, and its stranger than I expected. The dose they titrate to is defined by movement: the standard target is the concentration at which half of patients don't move in response to the surgeon's incision, which is a fact about bodies, not about anyones experience. Most patients also get a paralytic, so a patient who is aware and terrified cannot move a muscle; the reason anyone knows awareness happens at all is that some of them mention it days later, or because a study put a tourniquet on one forearm before the paralytic went in, so that arm could still squeeze a hand on command. So the working rule in the OR is basically: no response, no experience. But the same people will tell you without blinking that a dreaming sleeper is having experiences — vividly, sometimes more vividly than waking life — and a sleeper is every bit as unresponsive as any patient, and can't report anything until morning, if at all. I keep tripping over that.

A friend of mine who did a philosophy degree says the practice is quietly committed to a higher-order theory of consciousness — the idea that a state only counts as conscious if something else in the system represents it, or if it's available to be reported on — and that this is why unresponsiveness gets read as unconsciousness, and why the patient's retrospective report gets the last word. Another friend says no, it's really a global-workspace or access view, or maybe no theory at all, just triage pragmatics dressed up. I don't know nearly enough to tell who's right, and I'd rather not put something in my narrator's mouth that a real anesthesiologist would wince at.

So what I want is this: what does the practice actually presuppose about what consciousness is? Reconstruct the implicit view as carefully as you can, and say which rival views it genuinely rules out versus which ones it merely sits comfortably with. Tell me whether its parts are consistent with each other — in particular, whether the OR rule and the dream case can be held together, or whether the practice is smuggling in two different criteria and just not noticing — and where it's underdetermined, i.e. which bits would survive under any of the going theories. Then tell me what would have to change in the clinic if you took the higher-order view seriously, and separately if you took a first-order view, something like recurrent processing, where the experience is there whether or not anything higher-order is going on. No citations needed, I just need to know what the thing assumes, because my narrator has to say something smart in chapter nine and I want it to be right.
