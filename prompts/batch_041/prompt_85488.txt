I'm the engineer behind the rules engine that adjudicates claims at a mid-sized insurer, and I've spent four years inside it, so I'm fluent in its vocabulary and not much else: Datalog rules over base tables (policy, claimant, procedure, provider, authorization), stratified negation, semi-naive evaluation, magic sets, dependency graphs, incremental view maintenance for the dozen or so derived tables that have to be current at all times, and a nightly batch job that recomputes everything else to fixpoint. Two features of it are the ones I keep coming back to. The first is scope: a rule can read only the tables named in its scope declaration, so there are facts that are derivable from the database as a whole but that a given rule will never see. The second is a planning budget: the optimizer prunes any rule whose estimated cost exceeds a threshold, which means that on a bad night some consequences of the data are simply not computed — and, this is the part that bothers me, an uncomputed consequence and a consequence that is false are represented identically in the output, as absence of a row, which is by design, since we take the closed-world assumption. My formal background is thin (one undergraduate course in discrete mathematics), so if you need a logical symbol, please define it as something in the engine.

Lately I've been arguing about this at lunch with a colleague who took a course in modal logic and has strong opinions. He says the problem the philosophers worry about is a non-problem: agents in possible-worlds models believe every logical consequence of what they believe, this is called logical omniscience, it is obviously false of any machine, since a machine computes some consequences and not others, and the only lesson is that possible-worlds models were never meant as models of computation and nobody serious confuses them. I half agree and half think he is missing the point. It seems to me that the idealization is built into the semantics rather than bolted on as an extra assumption, so you cannot simply delete it and carry on — and I suspect my engine is, in some sense, an explicit-belief machine by construction, with the implicit beliefs being whatever the closure would have been, which makes me wonder what the philosophers are actually disagreeing about.

What I want is a translation exercise rather than a tutorial, and you need not name anyone or cite anything: describe the strategies, not the literature. Take the problem of logical omniscience as it is ordinarily set up — why it is supposed to be embarrassing, and the main families of repair that have been proposed — and restate each piece in the vocabulary of my engine: tables, rules, scope declarations, stratification, the cost budget, the nightly fixpoint job, incremental maintenance. For each piece, say what maps cleanly, what has to be distorted to fit, and what has no counterpart at all. Then give me the residue report: is what is left over a defect of my vocabulary — something my way of thinking about belief cannot express — or a sign that the original problem was less clear than it looked? And finally, tell me where my colleague is right, where my hunch about explicit and implicit belief goes wrong, and whether the thing I called the absence of a row is doing any real philosophical work or is merely an implementation detail.
