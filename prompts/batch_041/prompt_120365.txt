I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, though I have spent a fair amount of the past year reading decision theory on my own, and I have reached the point where I would rather watch an argument than read another summary. I keep running into the Allais paradox. I can reproduce the choices and I can see that they conflict with something called the independence axiom, but I cannot tell whether that conflict is a defect in the people who make the choices or a defect in the axiom, and every exposition I find either assumes the answer or refuses to commit. What I would like you to write is a dialogue between two people who understand the material well and who genuinely disagree, with both of them arguing at full strength and each responding to the other's specific points rather than talking past them. I am not interested in a debate in which one side is quietly set up to lose; the second character should be at least as persuasive as the first.

Use the following pair of problems, which I gather is the classic version. In the first you choose between A, a sure $1,000,000, and B, a lottery paying $1,000,000 with probability 0.89, $5,000,000 with probability 0.10, and nothing with probability 0.01. In the second you choose between C, a lottery paying $1,000,000 with probability 0.11 and nothing otherwise, and D, a lottery paying $5,000,000 with probability 0.10 and nothing otherwise. Many people choose A over B and D over C, and the standard observation is that no assignment of numerical utilities to the outcomes can accommodate both choices once we impose independence. Let the first character be the one who holds that independence is a genuine requirement of rationality and that the A-and-D pattern is a mistake, and let them make the strongest available case, including the intuition that a rational agent's preferences should not change when a common branch of two lotteries is replaced by a different common branch. Let the second character be the one who holds that the pattern is defensible, and let them make the strongest available case, including the thought that independence quietly assumes that only the final probability distribution over outcomes can matter, so that a person is never permitted to value the elimination of risk as such.

Two specific things I would like the dialogue to reach, because they are the parts I cannot resolve on my own. First, I am repeatedly told that violating independence exposes an agent to a money pump or a Dutch book, but the Allais choices seem to me to be made independently of one another and at the same time, and I cannot see where the exploitation enters unless the two choices are connected in some way; I want the two characters to argue about whether the money-pump objection really bears on the Allais pattern or only on some sequential version of it. Second, I have been trying to construct that sequential version and would like them to fight over it. Imagine the first problem as a two-stage game: with probability 0.89 the game stops at once and you are paid $1,000,000, and with probability 0.11 you continue to a second stage in which you choose between a sure $1,000,000 and a lottery paying $5,000,000 with probability 10/11 and nothing with probability 1/11. Now imagine the second problem as the same two-stage game with the terminal payoffs rearranged: with probability 0.89 the game stops at once and you are paid nothing, and with probability 0.11 you continue to a second stage whose choice is exactly the one I have just described, a sure $1,000,000 against a 10/11 chance of $5,000,000. As far as I can work out, choosing A over B amounts to taking the sure thing at the second stage of the first game, while choosing D over C amounts to taking the gamble at the second stage of the second game, so that the Allais pattern requires the same second-stage decision to be answered differently depending on which game one is in. I would like the characters to say what, if anything, this shows and to disagree about it honestly, and I would like them to tell me where their disagreement finally bottoms out: in the axioms themselves, in what the word rational is supposed to mean, or in what a preference is taken to be. If there is something either of them could say that would actually move the other, I would like to see it; if there is not, I would rather they say so than manufacture a resolution.
