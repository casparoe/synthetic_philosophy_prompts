I cover cognitive science and philosophy of mind for a monthly ideas magazine, and I also host its long-form interview segment; next month's guest is a philosopher of perception who has spent the better part of a decade defending an unfashionable reading of the color phi phenomenon, and I want the conversation to be genuinely adversarial rather than a friendly walk through her book. The phenomenon, as I understand it, is this: you flash a blue dot near the top of a screen, then a red dot near the bottom a fraction of a second later, and at the right spacing and timing subjects report not two dots but a single dot that travels down the screen and changes color abruptly somewhere in the middle of its path, even though there was never any middle. The standard philosophical lesson, drawn from Dennett's discussion of it, is that there are two ways to tell the story and no way to choose between them: either the brain revises the content of the experience itself before anyone is conscious of it, so that the experience is edited in advance, or it leaves the experience alone and edits only the memory or the report that is produced afterward. Dennett's own conclusion was that these two stories are empirically indistinguishable and that the difference between them makes no difference. My guest's new book insists that this is wrong: she thinks the two stories can be pulled apart by pitting a continuous, time-stamped motor response, such as a button held down and released at the moment the color seems to change, against the ordinary retrospective verbal report, and she claims the two measures come apart in the color phi case in a way that vindicates the first story, the one on which the experience itself is edited, over the second.

What I need from you is a set of ten to twelve interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, that will press her account where it is actually vulnerable. Please make sure the set includes: at least one question that forces her to say precisely what her motor-versus-report dissociation is supposed to show, and why it is not just one more piece of post-experiential processing, since if the button release is itself prepared and executed by the brain, why should it be any closer to the experience than the words are; one that raises the deflationary alternative, that no revision of any kind is needed because the two stimuli simply produce identical internal states through ordinary inhibitory or associative mechanisms, and asks what her data say against it; one on whether her view commits her to experiences having a definite temporal location at all, and if so, what becomes of the specious present and of the intuition that we live in a moving now; one that asks her to distinguish her position from the broader family of postdictive effects, such as the flash-lag illusion and the findings about when a voluntary movement seems to begin, without letting her treat the analogy as a free pass; and one that asks, in concrete experimental terms, what result would count as refuting her. No icebreakers, nothing that invites her simply to restate the book, and no request for what first drew her to the problem. I would rather have questions she finds irritating and has to work to answer than questions she can field on autopilot.
