Bit of an odd one. I'm running a reading group session on Aristotle's natural slavery and I want to come at the theory sideways, through a novel, because in the Politics it's easy to mock and hard to actually feel. The book I've picked is Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day. Stevens seems like a near-perfect test case: superb at working out *how* to do a thing and constitutively unable to ask *whether* the thing should be done — the Darlington business, his father dying upstairs while he keeps the conference running, Miss Kenton, all of it runs on that one incapacity.

Aristotle's picture, in Book I of the Politics, is that there are people who can take in a reason and follow it but can't give themselves one, that such a person is a kind of living tool, a piece of property with a soul, that being ruled is actually good for them, and that their virtue isn't the free man's virtue — the slave needs only as much virtue as keeps him from botching the job. And then in the Ethics he says something like there's no friendship with a slave *qua* slave, only *qua* man, which is interesting given how much of this novel is about Stevens failing to be bantered with, failing to get inside the easy intimacy his employer keeps offering him.

What I want is a reading that pushes both ways. Does Stevens vindicate the theory — a man genuinely suited to service, flourishing in it, so the natural slave turns out not to be a fiction — or does the novel expose the theory as a machine for making manufactured docility look like nature? I'd also like you honest about where the book resists the reading: by the end Stevens has no master left, his loyalty is to a role rather than a person, the house goes to an American, and none of that fits the master-slave bond Aristotle needs. Finish by saying whether the novel shows us something the argument can't see — what's lost even when servitude really is the servant's element.

Under 700 words please, and don't summarise the plot at me, assume i've read it.
