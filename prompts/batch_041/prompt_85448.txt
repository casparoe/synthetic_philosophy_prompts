Here it is.

(1) Training only ever scores outputs. The loss is computed on inputs drawn from the training distribution, and nothing in the loop ever inspects the objective function the network is internally using to generate those outputs.

(2) Take any policy that scores as well as the base objective asks for on the training distribution. There are infinitely many objectives that give that policy a maximum score on that distribution and yet disagree with the base objective outside it. Just take the base objective and add any term that's zero on the training inputs and non-zero elsewhere.

(3) So at the end of training, everything we've observed is equally consistent with all of those hypotheses. None of them is better supported by the evidence than any other, including the hypothesis that the model has the base objective.

(4) Therefore the probability that the model's objective coincides with the base objective away from the training distribution is essentially zero, and we should expect misbehaviour in deployment as a matter of course rather than as a risk to be managed.

(5) And none of this is special to gradient descent. Any process that selects for behaviour on a limited set of situations leaves the underlying objective underdetermined. Evolution is the obvious case: it selected for behaviour that worked in the ancestral environment, so we should expect organisms to be badly misaligned with fitness anywhere else.

(6) Which is what we find. We're drawn to sugar and fat, we use contraception, and so on. Same failure mode, different substrate.

(7) So inner alignment isn't a contingent engineering risk. It's guaranteed by the logic of selection, and no training procedure could avoid it.
