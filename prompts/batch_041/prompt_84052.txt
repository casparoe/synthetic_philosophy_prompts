(a) Say what bad faith is, and distinguish it from (i) lying to someone else and (ii) simply being wrong about yourself. (b) Sartre says bad faith is possible. And here's what the student wrote:

Bad faith isn't lying. A liar knows the truth and keeps it from someone else; the person in bad faith keeps it from herself. And it isn't just being wrong about yourself either, because being wrong is something that happens to you — you pick up a false belief — whereas bad faith is something you do, a project you take up. Put crudely, it's a flight from the anguish of freedom. We are "condemned to be free", so we can never hand our choices over to anything or anyone, and that's unbearable; bad faith is the pretence that something else is doing the choosing for us.

The café waiter is the clearest case. His movements are a little too crisp, a little too eager; he is performing "waiter" as if it were a thing he is, rather than a role he keeps taking up. He isn't lying to the customers — nobody is deceived except himself, and he doesn't fully deceive himself either, since he has to know what he's doing in order to do it. That's the puzzle Sartre sets up: to lie to yourself you'd have to know the truth you're hiding and not know it at the same time. His answer is that the for-itself isn't one simple thing; it has two sides, facticity and transcendence, and bad faith plays them off against each other. The waiter collapses his transcendence into his facticity. Something similar happens with the young woman on the first date, who leaves her hand in her companion's without responding to it: she lies to him about what she wants by treating her hand as a thing, so that she never has to choose.

I don't think Sartre's solution really works, and his discussion of Freud is where that shows. He can't simply dismiss the unconscious, because the analyst's censor is exactly the kind of agency that would explain how someone can hide something from herself. So Sartre takes over the idea of a censoring agency, and that's what lets him say that bad faith isn't something we do deliberately. But if it isn't deliberate, it's hard to see how it can be a fault: on this picture the person in bad faith is closer to someone with a bad habit than to a liar, and I think Sartre would have to agree that she isn't really responsible for it.

Dana: yes, she's in bad faith, on the "that's just how meetings go" side. Saying that anyone in her chair would do the same is the waiter's move — she turns herself into a function of her position, so that her choices look like the position's choices rather than hers. The "that isn't me" part is a different thing. That's just a false belief about her own character, and false beliefs get corrected by evidence; they aren't bad faith.
