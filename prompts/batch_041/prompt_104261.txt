In a graduate seminar on game theory, the instructor treats 'common knowledge of rationality' as a single, well-understood assumption, and he has twice used it to justify backward induction — most recently in a long centipede-style game, where he invoked it to brush aside my objection that a player who actually arrives at a late node has, by that very fact, evidence that the earlier players did not play as the assumption requires. I suspect the phrase is doing several jobs at once, and that at least one of the claims it can be made to express would undercut his argument rather than support it.

What I want is a menu rather than a verdict on the phrase as a whole: the distinct precise claims it could be making, separated along the dimensions that matter here — how much is known (that each player is rational, versus an infinite hierarchy of mutual knowledge, versus a fixed point of the knowledge operator); what the knowledge is about (the players' rationality, their beliefs, the payoffs and structure of the game, the absence of mistakes); what 'rational' is being predicated of (ex ante expected-utility maximization, sequential rationality at each node actually reached, or simply the absence of any deviation); and whether the assumption functions as a substantive hypothesis about the players, an idealization that no finite agent satisfies, or a stipulation that defines the solution concept rather than describing anyone. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and whether it would actually deliver the backward-induction conclusion he wants.

Give me exactly five numbered items, one reading per item, ordered from the weakest claim to the strongest, each a sentence or two, with the reading you take the instructor to be relying on flagged as such. No introduction or conclusion.
