I'm a third-year undergraduate in an epistemology seminar, and I've volunteered to make the handout for our unit on the epistemic regress problem. I don't want yet another three-column table that just lines up foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism and recites their slogans — everyone in the room can already do that, and it doesn't actually tell you where a person's view lives. What I want is a diagnostic instrument that sorts someone by what they say about the cases, not by which label they like. Please act as an epistemologist who works on theories of justification and is at home in the abstract logical space of this debate rather than in a memorized list of names. Think carefully and, before you draft the tree, brainstorm for a few sentences about the design questions that genuinely separate the positions — I'd rather see the scaffolding than have you jump straight to the finished diagram.

The deliverable is a decision tree (a taxonomy with labeled branches is fine, or a flowchart that leads from a starting commitment to a position). Build it from the logical space, not from a roster of -isms: first identify the questions that carve the space, then show which combinations of answers they permit, and only then locate the actual positions inside that space. Every branch node must be phrased as a concrete case or a claim that someone could answer without already knowing which position it leads to — so no node may read "do you accept foundationalism?" or "are you a coherentist?". Each leaf should carry the name of the position (or "unnamed" if nobody defends it) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

I want the tree to be able to place at least the following, though you should add others if the space demands it: the strong version of foundationalism that terminates the chain in indubitable, non-doxastic givens; the modest version that terminates in fallible, defeasible basic beliefs; pure coherentism, on which the chain never terminates because justification is holistic and mutual; infinitism, on which it never terminates because the chain runs on forever without repeating; the hybrid that wants both a foundation and coherence; the externalist or reliabilist view on which the regress never gets started because justification was never a matter of having reasons in the first place; the quietist view that the regress is an artifact of a faulty principle and dissolves once we drop it; contextualism, on which the stopping point is fixed by the conversational context; and outright skepticism. Where two of these leaves turn out to be indistinguishable on your questions, say so explicitly instead of papering over it, and for any combination of branch answers that no one occupies, note it and diagnose whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has bothered.

Three distinctions I specifically want the tree to exploit, because I think they are where the real action is and they keep getting lost in the textbook version. First, whether the items in the chain are beliefs or propositions — the doxastic versus propositional reading of the regress — since that quietly changes what could even count as a stopping point. Second, whether justification is transmitted along the chain by inferential support or instead accrues to the whole all at once, since rejecting transmission is one way to make the regress vanish rather than answer it. Third, whether the chain may terminate in something that is not itself a justified belief (an experience, a seeming, a fact about how the belief was formed), as opposed to something that is a justified belief but not justified by anything further. Use a concrete test case to probe each of these rather than stating them in the abstract.

Keep it to roughly seven to nine branch nodes and ten to fourteen leaves, and aim the whole thing at a smart classmate who has read the standard material but hasn't thought about these three distinctions. Do not give me citations, a reading list, or a bibliography — I can't look anything up while I'm building this, so if you name a position, make sure the name is doing real work and isn't standing in for an argument you'd otherwise have to make. Write in plain prose, and where a node is doing something subtle, say in a sentence why the positions actually come apart there.
