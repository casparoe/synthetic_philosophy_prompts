I'm in my third year of a philosophy degree, and this term I'm taking a seminar on moral worth — on what it is for an action to be not merely right or useful but to have the kind of value that makes the agent admirable — and, somewhat to my surprise, I've just been appointed the student member of my college's Beacon Prize committee, which meets this Thursday to choose the recipient of an annual award for "exemplary moral conduct by an undergraduate." Two nominations are before us and the committee is split down the middle. The first is Priya, who during an accident in the chemistry building ran back into a smoke-filled lab and dragged a trapped technician out, badly burning her hands; everyone agrees she saved his life and that she will likely carry the scars for years. Her own account, though, is that she did not really decide anything — "my legs just went" — and she has told a close friend that what she chiefly remembers feeling was dread at how it would look if she, the safety officer of her club, were seen standing outside while someone burned. The second is Daniel, who for two years has run a free weekend tutoring program for the children of university staff, and who, when a landlord moved to evict a family he had never met, spent most of his savings on a lawyer for them. He says he acted because it was simply not acceptable that such a thing should happen, and that he would have done it even if no one ever learned of it; but the eviction was in fact reversed for unrelated reasons before his lawyer accomplished anything, the family says his help changed little, and the tutoring program has, by the numbers, raised no one's grades.

I've been arguing in seminar for the view — I think it is the one on which an action has moral worth only when the agent is moved by the very considerations that make the action right, rather than by a good-hearted impulse or a general wish to do right — and the committee chair has taken to calling me the inquisitor. Her position is that the Beacon is a beacon and not a tribunal: it should reward whoever produced the most measurable good, full stop, because motives are private, self-reported, and in any case unknowable. The Dean's office, meanwhile, keeps saying the Prize is "for character, not outcomes" without saying what character is or how a committee is supposed to see it. I find I cannot defend my view against two cases our seminar kept returning to: the person who does the right thing while believing it to be wrong, out of sympathy rather than principle, and the person whose good deed is so overdetermined — training, habit, temperament, and a little vanity all pushing the same way — that no single motive can be picked out as the reason. I am also unsure whether the Prize should reward acts or agents.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete decision — which nominee, if either, and whether the Prize should be given at all this year — and, more usefully, propose a criterion the committee could apply to future nominations, defended against the obvious objection that any motive-based test turns a prize committee into an inquisition into students' inner lives. Engage the chair's results-only position at its strongest rather than as a caricature, and say plainly what is lost if we reject it. I would much rather have a committed recommendation with reasons I can argue from than a survey of the options.
