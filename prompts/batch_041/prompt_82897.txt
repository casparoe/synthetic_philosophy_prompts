Having spent my career in the biological sciences and come to moral philosophy only in retirement, I find myself preoccupied by the nonidentity problem, and before I read further into the proposed solutions I should like a specification of what an adequate theory of the ethics of bringing people into existence must deliver. In my own field a theory earns its keep by covering the cases, and I want the analogous list here: the cases the theory must get right, the distinctions it may not collapse — among them the difference between harming someone and wronging him, and between comparing a person's life with the life he would otherwise have had and comparing one outcome with another in which he never exists — and the constraints it must not violate, each with the consideration that motivates it. Three cases should be handled explicitly: a woman whose decision about when to conceive determines which child she has, the child she does have enjoying a life worth living but carrying a serious handicap she could have avoided by waiting; a policy that lowers the well-being of future generations while also determining which people will constitute them; and a choice between a smaller number of people living very well and a far larger number whose lives are barely worth living. Please mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which express the preferences of one camp, and tell me whether some subset of the desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the point I most want to see. I do not need a scorecard of the existing theories; I shall attempt that myself.
