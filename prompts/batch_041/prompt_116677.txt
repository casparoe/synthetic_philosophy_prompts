I have no formal training in philosophy, law, or history, but I have spent several years reading first-person accounts from countries that traded criminal trials for testimony after periods of political violence, and I also volunteer at a neighbourhood mediation service, where I have watched people accept far less than they were owed because the alternative was years of nothing at all. Between those two things I keep meeting the argument that amnesty for serious crimes is always a sell-out, a betrayal dressed up as pragmatism, and I have begun to suspect that the strongest case against amnesty might be made from the side that normally defends it. I would like you to build me a thought experiment that tests this, to my specifications.

Please take the role of a philosopher of law who works on transitional justice and knows the retributive, consequentialist, and restorative approaches to punishment and repair well. Think carefully before you answer, and do not reach for the first case that comes to mind. Begin by sketching three or four candidate structures for the case in note form, say briefly why each fails to satisfy the specifications below, and then develop the one that survives.

The case must satisfy all of the following.

1. It should be a single paragraph of setup, in a country you invent, with people you invent. Do not use any real country, commission, trial, statute, or person, and do not cite or recommend any literature, since I have no way to check references and do not want any.

2. Scale it down. Not a nation deciding its future, but a handful of people: one perpetrator, one or two people harmed, one or two officials or neighbours whose decisions matter. It should be a situation a person could plausibly be inside.

3. Nobody in the case disputes that the crime was serious. The argument must not turn on how bad the crime was, on whether the person harmed is sympathetic, or on whether the perpetrator is likeable. Hold those fixed and vary something else.

4. The amnesty must be conditional on full public disclosure, and that disclosure must be the thing that produces the only surviving record of what happened, so that the amnesty is not merely the price paid for the truth but the cause of it. No other route to the record may exist in the story.

5. Three verdicts are required, and each must fall out of the theory rather than being written into the facts:
   (a) a retributive, desert-based view must reject the arrangement;
   (b) a forward-looking view concerned with future stability must accept it;
   (c) a restorative or relational view, the framework that normally favours truth-telling over punishment, must come out against the arrangement, and must do so because of something structural in the case rather than because the process was botched or the officials were insincere. This is the verdict I care most about, and the one I expect to be hardest to earn honestly.

6. Do not smuggle the result in. Nothing in the case may state or imply that the arrangement is degrading, shameful, or a betrayal; if the restorative objection is real, it has to be visible to a reader who has not been told to look for it. No ticking bombs, no mind-reading, no omniscient narrator, no invented technology, and no appeal to what history will eventually say.

Then, in this order:

- Restate the case in two or three sentences as a bare structure, stripped of colour.
- Give the verdict of each of the three views and the reasoning that gets it there, showing which premise in each view is doing the work.
- Identify the single feature of the case on which the whole pattern depends. Then run the removal test: say what happens to each of the three verdicts if that feature is deleted, and again if it is inverted, and say whether the pattern survives in either case.
- Say what a defender of the restorative view would say to escape the verdict you gave it, giving the strongest reply rather than the weakest.
- Tell me honestly whether the case has any moral interest beyond generating this verdict pattern, or whether it is only a machine for producing one.
- Finally, tell me if the specifications are jointly unsatisfiable, or if the case had to cheat somewhere to meet them, and which constraint I should relax if so. Do not smooth over a failure. I would rather have a partial case and a clear diagnosis than a tidy one that quietly stipulates its conclusions.

Write plainly, define any term of art in the sentence where it first appears, and keep the whole answer to roughly a thousand words.
