Read Borges's short story "The Library of Babel" — the one collected in Ficciones — through the lens of algorithmic information theory: Kolmogorov complexity, Solomonoff induction, and Occam's razor understood as a preference for short programs. I want a piece of criticism rather than a summary, so assume the story is common knowledge, do not quote it, and do not send me to any secondary literature or reading list.

The story gives us a universe containing every possible book of a fixed format over a small alphabet, so that every truth and every falsehood is present, almost all books are gibberish, and the one volume that would serve as a perfect index or justification of the whole — the object of the librarians' cults and pilgrimages — is unfindable. I want you first to take the following reading seriously and then to stress-test it. As a set of strings, the Library is algorithmically almost information-free: a short program suffices to enumerate every string of the stipulated length. So the entity that contains all information has nearly zero information content, while almost every individual book is incompressible. On this view the perfect index the librarians seek simply is the minimal description of the Library — a short program whose existence is guaranteed and whose identification is uncomputable — so their despair is not a mood but a theorem.

Then give me the other half of the reading: where does this lens distort Borges, whose register is metaphysical and theological rather than computational? Is the story's totality genuinely the set of all strings of a fixed length, or is it something the formalism cannot capture? And here is the question I care most about: the Library's implicit prior is uniform over strings, whereas Solomonoff's prior is biased toward the compressible — so is the Library the friend of Occam's razor or its reductio, and does the story know which? Point me to the scenes and images that carry the argument, and close by saying what the story dramatizes that the formalism cannot: what it is like to live where these theorems are true.
