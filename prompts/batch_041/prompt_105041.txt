I am designing a single ninety-minute session for a formal epistemology reading group: six or seven doctoral students in philosophy who are fluent in Bayesian confirmation theory and comfortable with the rudiments of decision theory — states, acts, expected utility, dominance — but who have never met a scoring rule, proper or otherwise, and who will read nothing in advance. I have to build the session from scratch, and I would like you to design it rather than explain the material to me.

The topic is the accuracy-first approach to credence: the proposal that the fundamental epistemic good is the accuracy of a credence function, that accuracy is graded rather than binary, and that the central normative results — above all the argument that credences must satisfy the probability calculus — are supposed to follow from accuracy considerations alone, without betting behaviour, representation theorems, or a prior distribution over the possibilities. I want the audience to leave able to say exactly what that argument establishes and what it merely presupposes.

Two things are fixed, and I want the session arranged around them. First, the hinge is the difference between a dominance argument and a maximisation argument; the audience knows both from decision theory and will, I predict, read the accuracy argument as expected-utility reasoning in disguise, so the plan should be ordered such that the moment this misreading becomes available is the moment it is closed off. Second, I want the final third of the session to land on the gap between showing that a credence function is accuracy-dominated and showing that it is irrational, and on which assumptions are doing the work at each stage — in particular the strict propriety of the scoring rule and the assumption that the accuracy of a whole credence function is the sum of the accuracies of its individual credences. I am unsure how much of this the students can be brought to see in the time available, so tell me where earlier in the sequence the ground for it must have been laid.

What I need from you:

1. The order of ideas, with dependencies made explicit: what must be in place before what, and for each step what it would cost to reverse the order. Assume nothing can be introduced as a lemma to be taken on trust.

2. The one example or case that carries the session. My candidate is a credence function over three mutually exclusive and exhaustive possibilities that assigns a half to each, set against the uniform function assigning a third to each, so that the students can score both in each of the three possibilities and see the second beat the first in all three. Tell me whether this can carry the whole ninety minutes; what it must have and must lack in order to do so; where it will break down and what would have to replace it there. I want it to do three jobs at once — make accuracy feel graded rather than binary, make the audience feel the pull of the incorrect function instead of dismissing it as absurd, and make the world-by-world structure of the argument visible — and I suspect it can do at most two.

3. The misconceptions to expect, in the order the audience will form them, and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. My own list, which I would like corrected and reordered: that accuracy is simply truth; that any reasonable measure of closeness to the truth will serve, so the choice of scoring rule is a matter of convention; that the argument is circular because it needs probabilities over the possibilities; that it shows expected accuracy to be the fundamental epistemic norm; that it tells you which credences to have rather than constraining only their structure; and that being dominated and being irrational come to the same thing. Tell me which of these I have got wrong, and which misconception I have failed to anticipate.

4. A check for understanding for the last five minutes — one question put to the room whose answers will separate the students who have grasped the argument as a dominance argument resting on a bridge principle from those who have grasped it as an expected-accuracy argument. If one question cannot do that, tell me what pair will.

5. What to leave out, and how to say so. I do not intend to attempt the diachronic extensions, to enter the question whether accuracy is the only epistemic good, or to do anything with epistemic utility theory as a general framework beyond what the argument requires — including whether to present accuracy-first as merely a special case of it. Tell me whether any of that can be deferred without damage, and whether anything on that list turns out to be load-bearing after all.

One constraint throughout: the students cannot look anything up during the session, and I do not want the plan to depend on their having memorised names, dates, or titles. Everything needed should be reconstructible from what is on the board.
