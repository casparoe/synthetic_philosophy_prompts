I have no formal training in philosophy, economics, or mathematics, but I have spent a long time thinking on my own about the two-envelope puzzle, and I have come to believe that the mistake it exposes is one people make every day in forms they would never recognize. I would like to write a satire of it, one that someone who has actually worked on the problem would find fair rather than embarrassing.

Please write it as a client-facing document from a small firm called Envelope Arbitrage Partners: a plain-language prospectus with an FAQ, deadpan from beginning to end, about 650 words, pitched at ordinary retail customers rather than professionals. The firm's central claim should be that a customer who swaps envelopes always stands to gain, set out exactly as the argument is usually set out, with the same symbol doing duty on both sides of the comparison. The comedy should come from the accumulation: a customer who follows the advice again and again, a fee schedule that is entirely above board, and the founder's unshaken conviction that he is doing his clients a service. Nobody inside the document may notice anything wrong, and the firm must never appear dishonest or stupid — its sincerity is the joke. The fee schedule should be where the firm actually makes its money, described so that a careful reader sees it quietly doing the work the mathematics does not. No one in the piece may call the situation a paradox, and the piece must never explain or resolve it: a reader who knows the puzzle should be able to point to the single sentence where the sleight of hand happens, and a reader who doesn't should still find the thing funny and slightly unsettling. The target is the style of reasoning, not any person or school.

Use these headings, in this order: "What We Do" (about 120 words); "The Mathematics, in Plain English" (about 200 words); "Frequently Asked Questions" (three questions, about 200 words in total); "A Letter from Our Founder" (about 130 words). Then, under a final heading "Notes on the Choices" (about 150 words), tell me what you were doing — where the equivocation sits, what you deliberately left out, and what a reader is meant to feel by the end.
