ok weird request but here goes. I've been lurking on LessWrong and a couple rat-adjacent discords for like eight years, and the thing that still gets me is how people talk about the principle of indifference like it's a solved problem. So for a writing workshop I want a satire about it.

Format: an Onion-style news article. Headline plus ~500 words, deadpan, no winking, and the jokes have to come from the actual math rather than from "ha ha these people are dumb." I want someone who's read Jaynes to laugh, not wince. A reader who doesn't know the material should still be able to tell what's being ironized, they just won't get every layer.

Setup I'm imagining: some institution — a city, an insurance company, a forecasting startup, whatever you think is funniest — has made "if we don't know, split it evenly" into official policy, and it turns out to be a nightmare. The article should quietly assume the reader already knows the classics, so work them in through the quotes and the details instead of explaining them:

- the random-chord thing, where you get three different answers for the same event — 1/3, 1/2, 1/4 — depending on whether you randomize over endpoints, over distance from the center, or over the midpoint in the disk
- the water/wine thing, where a uniform prior on one ratio and a uniform prior on its reciprocal give you 1/2 and 1/3 for the same event
- non-conglomerability, i.e. a case where every conditional probability is at least 1/2 but the unconditional one comes out below 1/2
- the way people try to wave all of this away with "just use maximum entropy" and how that doesn't actually save them

Include at least one quote from a "concerned" statistician (invented) and one from a spokesperson who is completely unbothered. End with the institution doubling down on the policy. The target is the practice, not any real person.

Dont cite anything — no papers, no author names for me to go look up, it should stand on its own.
