Two members of a nine-person committee on which I sit fell into a long disagreement after we made a decision last week, and I would like you to adjudicate their exchange move by move. The decision was mundane: choosing between two competing scheduling systems for our department. Each of us had looked into the two options beforehand, so each of us arrived with a private leaning formed from our own reading rather than from anyone else's. Because the chair wanted the discussion to stay orderly, we went around the table in a fixed order, each person stating a preference aloud after hearing everyone before them. The first two speakers preferred the same system; all seven speakers after them preferred it as well. Two colleagues — call them Elena and Ravi — argued afterward about what had just occurred. Their exchange ran roughly as follows.

Elena: What happened was a textbook case of irrational herding. Two people independently looked at the options and came out for the same system; the other seven then fell in line without their own evidence ever entering into it. The committee decided on the strength of two people's research and discarded the other seven. That is herd behavior, and it is a failure of rationality.

Ravi: The seven who followed did not discard their own evidence; each weighed it and then updated on the fact that the first two had independently arrived at the same conclusion. Two independent judgments pointing one way is real evidence, and it can outweigh a single contrary leaning. Choosing what the group had already chosen was, for those seven, the rational thing to do given what they knew. This is rational herding, not a lapse.

Elena: Grant all of that, and you have shown only that rationality can lead a group to a bad result. The committee's decision was no more accurate than if the first two had decided alone — the other seven's work contributed nothing. And the arrangement is a house of cards: had a single person announced one contrary piece of evidence, the whole group would very likely have swung the other way. That fragility is a symptom of a broken process.

Ravi: Two things. First, the fact that a person's information goes unused by the group is not that person's fault; each acted correctly given what they knew, and it would be perverse to ask them to choose less accurately so as to keep the group better informed. Second, the fragility you describe is exactly what a rational Bayesian should expect: once everyone is choosing the system for the same reason — that everyone before them chose it — no one is adding information, so a small public shock can indeed reverse the group. That is not a paradox; it is the mechanism working.

Elena: But the split you keep drawing between the individual and the group cuts against you. If the crowd has stopped carrying any information, as you just conceded, then someone who follows it is not tracking evidence at all, so following is not obviously rational either. And much of what you are calling rational herding is, I suspect, mere self-protection: no one is blamed for choosing the system everyone else chose. That is not evidence-tracking; it is blame-shifting.

Ravi: Those are two different things and you are merging them. Following the crowd because you believe the crowd is right is one phenomenon; following it while privately believing it wrong, to avoid blame, is another — and I agree the second is a different and worse story. But the second is not what happened at our table.

Please adjudicate this exchange. For each of the six moves in turn, place it on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justify the rating briefly, attending to what the move actually claims rather than to the conclusion it is meant to support. Then say which of the two is right about which specific points, and where each goes wrong. Finish by identifying what both of them are missing — the considerations that would matter to a correct account of what happened and that neither has raised — and say which of the two is closer to right overall.
