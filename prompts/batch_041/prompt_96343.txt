A national ethics committee has for some years advised clinics on applications to select embryos for traits that are not diseases. Its published decisions are the working law for these applications, and I sit on the committee. The following is a fair summary of the relevant track record.

It refused an application to select an embryo unaffected by a severe degenerative disorder that would have killed the child in early adulthood, on the ground that the child's own interests plainly favoured the unaffected embryo.

It approved an application by two parents who both have a congenital condition causing profound deafness; they sought a child who would share the condition. The committee's published reasoning was that the condition imposes no restriction on the child's functioning or life chances that the family, which belongs to a signing community and would raise the child bilingually in sign and in speech, cannot fully accommodate, and that parental preference grounded in a community's way of life was entitled to weight.

It refused an application to select for a trait the parents wanted because they regarded it as desirable — increased adult height — where the selection also carried a modest risk of a manageable medical condition. The published reasoning was that selection must answer to the child's welfare and not to parental preference for an aesthetic or social advantage.

It refused an application to select an embryo that would have developed a trait making the child markedly more suggestible and deferential than average, in a way that would foreseeably have left the adult child less able to revise their own commitments. The published reasoning was that the committee would not sanction a selection that contracts the child's future, even where the parents said the trait would suit their close-knit community.

It approved an application to select an embryo with a lower than average risk of a common respiratory condition.

The next application is from two parents who are both deaf and who belong to a signing community. They ask to select an embryo that will be born deaf, from among their embryos. They say the child will be raised bilingually in sign and in speech, will have fluent signing parents and a large signing community, and will not be worse off in health, opportunity or happiness than a hearing child of theirs; they add that a hearing child would be an outsider in their household in a way that a deaf child would not.

Predict the committee's verdict, for consistency with its own decisions, and tell me the rule it seems to be running. Several rules fit the track record equally well, and I want the candidates set out: say what each predicts about this application, which you would stake your own prediction on, and why. Then tell me which case not yet before the committee would best tell the leading candidate rules apart, and what the committee's verdict on that case would reveal.

I would like the answer in British English. Where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and what consideration would lead you to a different verdict, say so plainly.
