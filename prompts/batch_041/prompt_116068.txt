I teach a graduate seminar that pairs science fiction with the methodology of forecasting, and I want to open the term with Isaac Asimov's original Foundation trilogy. The first unit is on the outside view: reference-class forecasting, base rates, and the difficulty of assigning a unique case — a declining empire — to a class of cases that have already happened. I would like a reading of the trilogy through that lens, written for students who know the forecasting literature but have not read the novels.

The questions I want it to take seriously. First, psychohistory is an outside view in its purest literary form: it licenses predictions about trillions of people precisely by saying nothing about any individual. What does the trilogy show about what such a view buys and what it costs? Seldon's forecasts are point forecasts with dates that arrive on schedule, whereas real reference-class forecasting yields ranges and probabilities and is normally invoked only after the inside view has failed. Is that difference a literary convenience, or does it mark a conceptual gap — a wish for the outside view to be stronger than it can be?

Second, the hard cases. (i) The Mule: an individual whose action falls outside the model's reference class. Is the Second Foundation's response — relocating the anomaly in a wider class — a principled correction, or an immunizing move that leaves psychohistory unfalsifiable? (ii) The Foundation's own conduct: its leaders know the Plan, act on it, and govern by trade and by a technological religion, so the forecast is also a cause. Can an outside view survive being published and acted upon, or does the trilogy quietly stage the performativity problem — that a base rate stops being a base rate once the case knows it is a case? (iii) The reference class problem in earnest: the Galactic Empire is one empire among many in the reader's history and one of one in the fiction's. If one took the historical base rate seriously and treated it as a draw from the class of large, long-lived, agrarian empires, what would a reference-class forecast have said, and would it have resembled Seldon's schedule? What must the trilogy assume for the Roman analogy to carry the weight it is asked to carry?

Where the novels resist the reading, say so plainly: I do not want the trilogy reduced to an illustration of a method, nor the method trimmed to fit the plot. End by telling me what the trilogy dramatizes about the outside view that the forecasting literature cannot state in argument, and which episodes I should assign alongside it.
