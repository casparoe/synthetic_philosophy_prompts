I am preparing a recorded interview for a public-philosophy series. My guest is a scholar of nineteenth-century thought who has spent years defending a revisionary reading of Kierkegaard's writings on the press and on the public — a reading on which those writings are not early media criticism but a diagnosis of a spiritual condition. Her position can be summarized in four claims, which I would like the questions to take as a starting point rather than to ask her to restate: that what Kierkegaard calls the public is not an audience or a readership but a solvent, an abstraction to which no one belongs and in which anyone can be everywhere and nowhere at once; that the operative mechanism is not anonymity as such but the collapse of the distinction between the significant and the trivial, so that the momentous and the idle become equally discussable and equally forgettable — this is what he calls leveling, and its characteristic speech is chatter; that this condition has an inverted successor in the present, where an abstract public belonging to no one has given way to a concrete audience belonging to everyone, from which the individual cannot withdraw; and that the single individual set against the public is a religious category rather than a liberal one, so that anyone who keeps the critique while discarding its theology owes an account of what is left of it. (The sustained analysis of the present age appears in his 1846 review of a novel by Thomasine Gyllembourg.)

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press this position at its points of greatest strain rather than inviting a summary of it. Include at least one question on each of the following, and for each of those supply a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give:

- whether the contrast between a public and a crowd, or between a public and an audience, does any independent work, or whether the critique reduces to a familiar complaint about anonymity, scale, and distraction;
- whether the critique is bound to the print ecology in which it was written — signed authorship, one-to-many address, the periodical, and the satirical weekly he deliberately provoked into attacking him — and if so, whether it can survive translation into a medium whose address is many-to-many;
- the self-undermining problem, namely that Kierkegaard wrote under pseudonyms, published ceaselessly, and made his own case in the very medium he condemned, so that a critique of publicity appears to require the publicity it rejects.

At least one further question should press on whether the single individual can be detached from its religious setting without collapsing into a thin conception of personal authenticity. Please phrase the questions so that they cannot be answered by reciting biography or by naming sources, and do not ask her for reading recommendations.
