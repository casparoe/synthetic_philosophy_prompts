I cover artificial intelligence and the behavioral sciences for an ideas magazine, and I am writing a feature on how machines are taught to infer what people want from what they do. The researchers I interview keep insisting that the bottleneck is not the mathematics but the prior question of which behavior deserves to count as evidence. For the piece I would like a tier list. Below are seven signals from which one might read off a person's preferences; sort them into tiers by how much weight you would give each as evidence of what that person actually wants, and say in a sentence, for each, what it can reveal and what it cannot. Two things matter to me especially: be explicit wherever a signal confounds what someone wants with what they merely believe or are able to do, and flag any signal whose evidential value is destroyed or inverted by the very act of using it — that inversion is the spine of the article.

(a) What a person chooses in private, when the stakes are trivial.
(b) What a person pays to avoid, or pays a premium to obtain, in an ordinary market.
(c) The pattern of a person's suboptimal choices on a task — reading their errors as noisy evidence about a hidden objective rather than discarding them as mistakes.
(d) What a person arranges in advance to be prevented from doing later.
(e) What a person says they want when you simply ask them.
(f) What a person does after being told that a model has already predicted their choice.
(g) What a person does when their behavior is being scored against a rough proxy for their preferences.

Close with a single line of the form \answer{Tier 1: ...; Tier 2: ...; ...}, naming signals by letter.
