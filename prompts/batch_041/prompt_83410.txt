My friend and I spent most of Saturday night arguing about whether frontier model weights should be released openly, and it turned out we disagreed less about the present than about a precedent: we both kept invoking the cryptography fights of the 1990s and drew opposite lessons from the same events. I would like that episode analyzed properly, as a case, rather than summarized back to me.

The facts I am working from, in case they help fix the reference: public-key cryptography moved from academic publication in the 1970s into freely redistributable civilian software by the early 1990s, most famously when an individual released a strong encryption program on the Internet in 1991 and later found himself the subject of a criminal investigation over munitions export. Over roughly the same period the US government proposed a national encryption standard built on a classified algorithm and a key-escrow arrangement, under which copies of each user's key would be held so that authorities could decrypt with legal authorization. Cryptographers attacked the escrow design in public and largely dismantled it technically; courts were asked whether published source code is protected speech; industry complained that export limits were costing it sales; and by the end of the decade the controls had been relaxed almost to nothing.

What I want is an analysis of that episode through the security-studies idea of an offense-defense balance — whether a technology, once diffused, favors the party attacking or the party defending, and how easily the two uses can be told apart — applied to the present question about open-sourcing frontier models. Make the mapping explicit: what plays the role of the capability, of the release, of the state, of the adversary, of the bystander. Then tell me where the analogy actually bears weight and where it is doing rhetorical work.

I have a suspicion I want tested. Cryptography seems to satisfy three conditions that a technology must meet before the 1990s precedent transfers: the secret that matters is small, separable from the design, and rotatable, so publishing the design does not publish the capability; the marginal use of the technology is predominantly defensive or self-regarding, so diffusion does not simply arm the aggressor; and the technology's trustworthiness depends on public scrutiny, so that secrecy degrades the very good it is meant to protect. Cryptography arguably meets all three — a cipher has to survive disclosure to be worth trusting, and its key can be changed at will. Say whether it really does, whether frontier models do, and which of the three conditions is doing the real work in my friend's argument, since he takes the crypto episode to prove that openness wins and that the security services are always wrong.

And I want the historical lesson kept separate from the historical facts. The controls collapsed under a combination of cheap global distribution, foreign availability, commercial interest, litigation, and a change of administration — not obviously because anyone was persuaded. If the outcome was inevitability rather than vindication, say what that does to the analogy, given that distributing a model is even cheaper than distributing a cipher while the capability at stake is far less reducible to a short string of bits.

Throughout, keep the line between what is settled and what is genuinely contested among people who know this material. Where the history is well documented but its lessons are disputed, flag the dispute rather than reporting one reading as the consensus, and be explicit about when you are reasoning by analogy rather than from evidence.
