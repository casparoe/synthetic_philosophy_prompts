OK so I'm teaching logical uncertainty again this term and I keep hitting the same wall, and I'd like you to help me get past it.

The two things I want compared are (a) the textbook Bayesian idealization and (b) the "logical induction" picture. Let me say what I take each to be, because I don't want you answering some other version of them.

(a) The Bayesian idealization: the agent's credences are a probability function, it gives probability 1 to every logical truth and 0 to every logical falsehood, and it's logically omniscient -- nothing that follows from what it accepts is ever missed. This is the standard thing we wave our hands at in decision theory and confirmation theory.

(b) The logical induction picture: credences are more like prices in a market over sentences. There's a deductive process that gradually spits out theorems; the agent's prices update day by day; the guiding constraint is roughly that no computably-specifiable trader can make unbounded profit against those prices (the fine print is about efficiency classes, I know, don't get bogged down in it); and the upshot is that prices for provable sentences drift toward 1 without the agent ever being logically omniscient at any finite stage.

Now, two concrete things I want pinned down, and I want it concrete rather than vibes.

First: my toy reasoner can do 10^6 deduction steps a day, full stop. There's a sentence S that is in fact provable in PA, but the shortest proof is about 10^9 steps, so our reasoner is years away from it. What does each picture say about its credence in S today? The Bayesian answer looks like "1, because it's logically true" -- but that seems to sever the credence from the fact that the agent has no earthly idea, and if you say that for every truth then you've got an agent that can never represent the activity of learning logic as learning anything. The inductor says something like "strictly between 0 and 1, drifting up as deduction finds more". Is that a theory of rationality, though, or just a redescription of what a bounded reasoner does anyway?

Second, and this is the one I actually care about: self-reference. Take a sentence that says of itself "my price will be less than 0.5". In the market picture, price is supposed to be credence, so calibration-ish considerations want the price to be 1 if it's below 0.5 and 0 if it isn't, which is, well, awkward. Does the logical-induction machinery handle that cleanly, or does it just get it wrong? And what does the omniscient-Bayesian picture have to say about the case at all, given that it's meant to describe an agent that's already closed under consequence -- is the sentence even a live object for it, or is the whole question something only the bounded picture can pose?

So: which is the better idealization for a bounded reasoner, what does each buy that the other can't, and where does each simply refuse to answer. And please don't give me the "they're answers to different questions" line unless you can name the questions precisely, because I've heard that move a dozen times and I dont buy it yet.
