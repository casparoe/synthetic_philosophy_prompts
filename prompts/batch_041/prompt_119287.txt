I'm reading George Eliot's translation of Feuerbach's The Essence of Christianity — she did it as Marian Evans, before she became George Eliot — because I keep seeing people say her novels are Feuerbachian and I wanted to see the source for myself. There's a passage near the start of the main body that I cant get my head around. Feuerbach has just been saying that religion is a kind of disunion: man sets God before himself as the antithesis of himself, God is everything positive and man everything negative, God is infinite and man finite, and so on. Then he writes:

"But in religion man contemplates his own latent nature. Hence it must be shown that this antithesis, this differencing of God and man, with which religion begins, is a differencing of man with his own nature."

I dont understand how this is supposed to work. If God is just man's own nature projected outside himself, then in religion man is contemplating himself — but he doesn't know it's himself. Is that the point? And what does "latent" mean here — that man's true nature is hidden from him, or that it's something he hasn't yet become? That seems like a big difference: on one reading religion is an illusion about something already there, on the other it's a confused anticipation of something not yet realized. Which is it, and why does Feuerbach say "hence" — how does the second claim follow from the first?

Also, since Eliot spent years on this translation, I'd like to know how much of it she actually signed on to. People talk as if she just swapped Christianity for a religion of humanity and kept the structure, but I'm not sure that's the same as what's going on in this passage. I want a definite reading, not a survey of options: tell me what you think the passage is actually saying, where you're confident and where you're guessing, and what it would mean for Eliot's own position if she accepted it.
