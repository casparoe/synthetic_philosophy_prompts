I'm not a lawyer or a philosopher, just someone who reads a fair amount of history of science on the side, and I keep running into the same strange pattern: the calculus gets worked out twice, natural selection gets discovered twice, oxygen turns up three times, the telephone turns up twice. When I raise this with friends we always end up in the same argument. One side says it proves the discovery was inevitable — that the pieces were lying around and whoever got there first was merely the one holding the pen. The other side says that's hindsight talking: the fact that two people found it tells you nothing about what would have happened if both had died young, and the particular mind that made the connection is exactly the thing we're trying to explain. I don't know how to settle that in the abstract, and I suspect it can't be settled in the abstract. What I'd like to understand instead is what our institutions have already decided, since they have to act as though they know.

So take the patent system, which I've been reading about because my brother-in-law is a patent attorney. The whole justification for handing someone a monopoly, as I understand it, is that the invention has to be non-obvious — that it's more than what an ordinarily skilled person in the field would have arrived at as a matter of routine. The system is therefore committed, in its own self-description, to the idea that the inventor contributed something the field could not have produced by itself. But here is what puzzles me. If a second person independently arrives at the same invention — which strikes me as the strongest possible evidence that the thing was reachable — it does him no good whatsoever: I'm told independent invention is no defense to infringement, so he is simply an infringer. Until recently the system at least ran a hearing to decide who had invented first; now the patent goes to whoever files the paperwork first, and the only remaining proceeding of that kind asks whether one person took the idea from another. There is a narrow exception for someone who was already using the invention in his own business before the application was filed. So the law seems to care a great deal about who was first in the queue and almost not at all about the fact that the thing was going to exist regardless.

What I want to know is what theory of invention this bundle of rules presupposes. Does the system actually need the lone-genius picture it advertises, or could everything it does be justified by a much thinner story about administrative convenience and the disclosure of knowledge? Which views of discovery does it quietly rule out — if discoveries really are inevitable, is any room left for desert, and if there isn't, does the patent system still hold together on its own terms? Are its parts consistent with one another, or is the rhetoric of the brilliant individual doing no real work while the rules are quietly built on inevitability? I'd also like you to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit, since I suspect several quite different philosophies of invention would license the same set of statutes. And finally, suppose we took the inevitability side seriously: what would have to change — nothing, because the system already assumes it, or everything, starting with who gets the reward?
