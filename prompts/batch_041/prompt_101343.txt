For a seminar on consciousness I am supposed to take a side on the knowledge argument, and I have found that the standard framing does not match what I actually think when I go case by case. A few of us argued about it for an hour and the labels on offer — the reply that she gains an ability, the reply that she gains a new concept, the reply that she learns a self-locating fact — each fit part of what I said and none fit all of it. I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, is behind my verdicts, and where that principle forces me to give one of them up. Here are the cases and what I think about each. (1) Mary, who has lived her whole life in a black-and-white room and has mastered every physical fact about colour vision, is let out and sees a ripe tomato for the first time. She learns something, and I want to say she learns a fact about the world, not merely a skill. (2) Her twin, raised in the same room but with normal colour vision, has simply never bothered to attend to anything coloured; the first time she really looks at a tomato she gains nothing, because she already knew what red was like. (3) A man blind from birth, who knows the physics of vision inside out, receives a retinal prosthesis that gives him a coarse grid of grey phosphenes; he learns what it is like to see a shape, and I count that as the same kind of gain as Mary's even though his experience is nothing like normal sight. (4) A woman has always been able to tell two very similar shades apart but has no word or concept for the difference; she is taught the concept and the word for it, and can now think of the one shade as distinct from the other. I think she gains knowledge too, a genuine fact, although nothing about her experience changes. (5) A novelist writes a long and exact description of the taste of a fruit I have never eaten; I read it, and when I finally taste the fruit I find I already knew what it would be like and learn nothing new from the tasting. (6) A disembodied system is given the complete physical description of a colour experience and asked what it is like; it produces a fluent and accurate-sounding report, but I do not think it knows what it is like at all.

Two of these are hard for me to hold together, and I suspect they are where the real work is. In (1) I feel that what Mary gains is something no description could have given her and that she cannot pass on; but in (5) I feel that a description did pass it on. And in (3) the gain seems to require a new experience, while in (4) it seems to require only a new concept. The honest answer may be that I am running two different notions of knowing what it is like together, and I would rather find out which one I care about than pretend all six verdicts fit a single story.

Please give me the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I can apply it to new cases, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it. Then apply the best candidate to cases I have not listed — an adult who acquires an entirely new sense modality, say magnetoreception; a pair of identical twins one of whom is a philosophical zombie; and a person who has known what red is like all her life and only then learns the complete physics of colour vision — and say what the principle predicts in each. Finally, tell me honestly which of my six verdicts it cannot accommodate, and whether that verdict is the one I should give up. If one of the replies we were arguing about turns out to be what I have been groping for, say so and show which of my cases actually supports it, but do not assume I have already chosen one of them.
