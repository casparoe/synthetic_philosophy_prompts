I'm a quant — I build life-cycle portfolio choice models for a living, so I'm fluent in the standard machinery: utility over consumption streams, time-separable and recursive preferences, discount factors and the elasticity of intertemporal substitution, state-contingent claims, complete versus incomplete markets, bliss points and non-satiation, and the usual bag of tricks for stuffing extra arguments into a utility function when the data insists. I have no philosophy training whatsoever. Over the holidays I got into an arguement with a friend who's an economic historian about Keynes and what he thought a good life was — the essay where he imagines the economic problem being solved within a century and wonders what people will do with the freedom, and the memoir where he looks back on his Moorean youth and says the only things that ever really mattered to him were states of mind, chiefly one's own. My friend thinks there's a substantive view in there and that I'd like it. I tried writing it down myself in my own vocabulary and it felt far too easy: I ended up with a utility function whose arguments are friendship, love, beauty and knowledge instead of consumption, a modest discount rate, a satiation point, and a public-goods-ish term to handle the fact that he clearly thought the good life was something a society achieves rather than something an individual buys. But that version doesn't look like a view anyone could disagree with — it's just one person's tastes written in math, and my friend says I've missed the point entirely.

So what I want is a faithful translation, not a flattering one, followed by the residue report. Take the main claims in that picture — that the ultimate goods are states of mind and everything else is instrumental; that a life organized around accumulating means is somehow degraded rather than merely unusual; that the love of money as a possession is a disease and not just a taste; that the present is not merely raw material for the future; that plenty would change not just what we consume but how we think about wanting — and put each one into my vocabulary as precisely as you can. Then tell me which of them survived intact, which I could only make fit by bending something (and what got bent in the process), and which have no counterpart at all in a preference-based, forward-looking, expectation-taking framework. The residue is the part I actually care about, so be specific about what kind of thing is left over: for each leftover, say whether it's a limitation of my vocabulary — something a better formalization could still say — or evidence that the original claim was doing something other than describing a state of affairs, like recommending an attitude or issuing a verdict that no utility function could contain. One thing to check along the way: Keynes had his own theory of probability, and as I understand it, it wasn't the subjective-expected-utility picture my models run on, so if the translation depends on treating uncertainty my way, flag it and tell me whether any of it survives on his own terms. Please don't send me a bibliography or point me at secondary literature, I won't read it — I want the translation and the wreckage.
