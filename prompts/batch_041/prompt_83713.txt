ok, so I've been reading a lot about predictive processing and the free energy principle and I've ended up with a position that I suspect doesn't hang together, but I genuinely can't tell where the fault line is. Here's roughly where I've landed, and I want you to audit it rather than be nice to me:

(i) Predictive processing is right about the brain. Perception is top-down inference, prediction error gets passed up the hierarchy, precision-weighting does the job attention is supposed to do. I don't mean this as a metaphor or a "useful redescription" — I think that's roughly the actual algorithm.

(ii) The free energy principle is true of any system that manages to keep existing, not just brains. It's closer to a mathematical fact about self-organising systems than to an empirical hypothesis about nervous systems.

(iii) I'm a realist about the generative model. When I say the brain has priors over hidden causes, or that a system has a Markov blanket, I mean those are real features of the thing, not bookkeeping devices we project onto it to make the maths come out.

(iv) I also think the FEP is unfalsifiable, and I don't think that's a defect. Frameworks that can't be wrong are doing a different job from theories that can.

(v) Active inference is the right story about action. Agents act so as to bring about the sensory states they predict, which means you don't need a separate reward or value machinery bolted onto the side.

(vi) I know the standard reply to the dark room objection is that you give the agent prior preferences it expects to encounter, so it heads out looking for food instead of sitting in the dark. But I think if the framework only works once you've helped yourself to a prior that says "you will enjoy lunch", then something has gone wrong — that's the value system coming back in through the window and pretending it wasn't invited.

(vii) I'm fairly sympathetic to the radical enactivist line that the whole framework smuggles in a Cartesian picture, and that a lot of what gets called representation in this literature is a useful fiction at best.

(viii) And I think when I see a cup I see the cup. I'm not seeing an inner model of a cup; I'm in perceptual contact with the world.

So: which of these can actually be held together, which pairs only look like they conflict, and which single one has to go? I'd like the tensions ordered by how bad they are rather than a flat list, and for each one tell me the cheapest fix and what it costs me. I'm not attached to any particular item yet, so don't assume I'd rather keep (ii) than (v) or whatever — just tell me where the load-bearing mistake is.
