I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training at all, so go easy on the jargon. Something's been bugging me about the games I play and occasionally work on.

Here's the pattern I keep noticing. In a big open-world game, most NPCs are basically scenery. You can shoot them, they ragdoll about, blood decals, done, and thirty seconds later the street repopulates with identical blokes. But a handful of characters have an 'essential' flag on them — you can shoot them all day, they'll flinch and shout at you, but the game simply will not let them die. And then there's a small set where the opposite happens: the death is scripted, it's meant to land, the game autosaves over it so you can't undo it, there's a funeral, other characters keep mentioning it for the rest of the game, and sometimes the devs put an 'are you sure?' box in front of the action that leads to it. Some studios add an achievement for getting through without killing anyone. Players hold in-game memorials for NPCs, modders build gravestones. And yet the same studio will tell you in interviews that it's all just code and you shouldn't feel bad about any of it.

What I want to know is what all this presupposes about whether these characters matter morally. Is the practice actually committed to anything, or does it just sit comfortably with lots of different views? My gut says there are three things it could be up to: (1) it's not about the characters at all, it's about the player — you're meant to feel something, which is an aesthetic or psychological goal, not a moral claim; (2) it's smuggling in the idea that fictional characters can be wronged, that something can be done *to* them that counts; (3) it's about the player's own character — what killing does to you, which would explain why the game lets you mow down the mooks but punishes you narratively for killing someone with a name.

Push on those three. Which does the practice actually need and which is just decoration? Is it internally consistent — the same design that says 'this one can't die' will happily let you massacre a village of identical models, which seems to imply that harm doesn't matter but death does, or that moral weight tracks narrative prominence rather than anything about the entity itself. And is there anything genuinely philosophically loaded in the fact that we make the irreversible deaths the emotionally weighted ones (autosave, no reload, no takebacks) whilst making everything else trivially reversible with a quicksave? If we took seriously the idea that game characters do matter morally, what concretely would have to change about how these systems are designed — and would that even be coherent, given they've got no inner life to be harmed? Or does the 'mattering' have to be relocated somewhere else entirely, like to the player, or to the fictional world as an object of care?

One more thing: I'd like the line drawn pretty sharply between what the practice *strictly* presupposes and what it merely fits well with, because I suspect most of it is the second one. And tell me where it's just underdetermined, such that several rival theories would licence exactly the same design choices.
