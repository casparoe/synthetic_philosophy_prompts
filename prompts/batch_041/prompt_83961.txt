I convene a final-year seminar on the philosophy of work — twelve students, a term of Weber, of labour law read as moral argument, and of essays that fall due on the very day of our last meeting, which is to say on a day when nobody in the room has had an hour to themselves. I want to close the session by reading a poem aloud, and I want a poem whose form is doing philosophy rather than decorating it. The three regimes we have spent the term separating are these: the Protestant ethic, which outlives the death of its doctrine as a bodily unease in unoccupied time — guilt with no commandment behind it; the statutory short week, which does win hours back but tends to win back the wrong kind, the kind that exists to restore you for Monday rather than the kind that does not know Monday exists; and karoshi, the limiting case, in which a labour-standards office must fix a number of overtime hours above which a death is deemed to have been caused by the job. Our running thesis, which the poem need not endorse but must not flatten, is that no leisure is vacant: read otium and negotium as two columns of one ledger and every free hour is drawn against somebody's unfree one, at some remove. I want the poem to leave a reader unable to say cleanly who has the right to rest.

Approach this as a poet who has read the labour history and as a philosopher who has had to make verse scan; and take the form to be the pantoum, because the pantoum's repetitions are the argument. Each quatrain's second and fourth lines return as the first and third of the next, so a sentence is never merely repeated but recontextualised, and the poem conventionally closes by arriving back at the line it began with. That is exactly what I want done with a line of the order of "I have earned this" — a boast in the ethic's voice, a contractual entitlement in the short week's voice, an epitaph in the report's voice. So: three voices, the refrains passed among them, every return audibly meaning something the previous occurrence did not. One instruction matters more than all the others. The interlocking must hold exactly, line for line, until the karoshi stanza, and there it must break — the line that ought to return does not, and the failure must be legible as a deliberate rupture (a withheld line, a substitution, a hole in the stanza) rather than as a lapse in craft. A form built out of return is the right instrument for showing what does not come back; and after the rupture, if the poem closes at all, its return to the opening line must either mean something new or be visibly impossible to complete without lying. Five to nine quatrains, recitable aloud in under three minutes, rhythmically free but with the repeated lines repeated exactly, since the whole effect depends on my hearing the same words twice and meaning two things by them.

Before you commit to anything, brainstorm a set of candidate refrain-lines and choose among them deliberately; then take a definite position and let the poem's choices carry it — I would rather have a poem that risks an argument than one that holds three positions at arm's length and calls that balance. The words Protestant, thirty-five, and karoshi need not appear, and if they do they must be doing work no paraphrase could do; no epigraphs, no allusions to named theorists, nothing that wants a footnote — it has to land on students who have read Weber and little else. After the poem, in no more than two hundred words, tell me where the poem takes liberties with the ideas (if it treats the ethic as an affect rather than a doctrine, say so; if it treats the karoshi threshold as arbitrary when the legal standard is not, say so) and which of the readings it leans on a specialist would contest. Be committed in your choices and candid about what they cost.
