Right, so my flatmate and I had a blazing row last night about guilt versus shame. He reckons shame is the stronger and more honest moral emotion, and that a society can basically just choose which of the two to run on, whereas I said guilt is better because it's about what you did rather than who you are, and because you can be forgiven and move on afterwards. Fine, whatever. But then this morning I remembered a practice that seems to quietly take his side, and now i can't work out what it's actually commited to.

Have a look at the sex-offender registers in the US — community notification, where the state publishes your name, photo and address to the neighbourhood, sometimes for life, long after you've served your sentence. The criminal law is meant to be about guilt: you do a bad thing, you get a proportionate punishment, then you're square with the law, and the whole point was never to humiliate you. A register does the opposite. It hands you over to your neighbours' contempt, indefinitely, and it treats the danger as living in the person rather than in the act. But then in some places you can petition to come off the list after a clean stretch, and in others it's for life, which makes me think nobody is clear what the thing is even for.

So what theory of shame does this practice presuppose? That shame is a legitimate punishment in its own right, that it deters better than prison does, that it somehow reforms people, or that certain offenders are just a permanent kind of person who can never be square again? And does it presuppose anything about whether a society can deliberately choose shame over guilt as its main moral engine, or is that choice not really on offer? Reconstruct the theory that's actually baked into the practice rather than the one people announce out loud. Tell me which bits the practice strictly needs and which bits it would still run on perfectly well under several rival views — i.e. where the whole thing is underdetermined. My flatmate also went on about some criminologist who thought shaming could be done in a way that pulls an offender back into the community instead of casting them out; if the register is doing the opposite, is that a bug or the whole point? And if you swapped the presupposed view for a straight guilt-and-desert one, what in the practice would have to change?

Write the answer in American English please. Cheers.
