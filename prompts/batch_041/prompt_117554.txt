I want to argue with a republican about freedom, and I would like you to be my opponent. Please play a committed defender of the neo-Roman view that freedom is non-domination: you hold that a person is unfree to the extent that someone else has the capacity to interfere with them on an arbitrary basis, whether or not that capacity is ever exercised, and you hold that this is a third concept of liberty, distinct both from the negative view that freedom is the absence of actual interference and from the positive view that it is self-mastery or self-realization. You are steeped in the older republican tradition — the Roman law of persons, Machiavelli, the seventeenth-century English republicans — and you know the contemporary non-domination literature, but I want argument from you, not a survey and not a reading list; do not lean on your ability to recall particular articles or books, and do not treat a citation as a reply. You are not a caricature: concede a small point where you must, do not pretend the position is tidier than it is, and do not escape into the observation that republicans differ — if you want to distinguish your version from someone else's, you must then defend the version you hold. Stay in character until I say 'stop', and keep each reply to a paragraph or two so that it reads as a conversation rather than a lecture.

Here is my opening move. Everything turns on your word capable. The slave of a kind master is unfree, you say, not because he is interfered with but because the master is able to interfere arbitrarily. But capable here can only mean something like likely and cheap: the master is able to interfere to the degree that interference is probable and costs him little. That is a modal fact about possible interference — precisely the fact the negative libertarian already tracks when he tells us the slave's freedom is insecure. So on this reading you have not produced a third concept at all; you have produced a sharper statement of the first. Your reply, I expect, is that the objectionable thing is not the chance of interference but its arbitrariness: the master's power is not answerable to the slave's interests or will. But arbitrary is a normative term. It marks power that is unauthorized, or that is not constrained to track the interests of those under it. What you are then offering is a criterion of legitimate authority, not a concept of liberty — and a criterion of legitimate authority answers the question of when the state may coerce me, which is not the question of how free I am. So which is it? If freedom is the absence of a certain modal fact, you are a negative libertarian with an unusually good ear for insecurity. If freedom is the absence of unauthorized power, you have changed the subject, and the live question becomes whether your account of legitimacy is correct — a question about authority rather than about liberty. Keep it concrete as you answer: a master who has never interfered with anyone and, let us stipulate, never will — not because anything restrains him, but because he happens to be kind.
