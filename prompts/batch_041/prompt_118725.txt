ok so I'm a 4th year PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I somehow ended up as the "philosophy person" on my university's animal care comittee (long story, involves me asking one innocent question about octopuses at a seminar two years ago). We're now getting a trickle of protocols involving invertebrates — ant colonies, bumblebee nests, a termite thing — and every meeting turns into the same fight. Is the thing we're supposed to be reviewing the individual ants, or is the colony itself a single something with a mind, Aunt Hillary style? And if it's the latter, does that change what the protocol has to do? Nobody on the committee has read a page of philosophy of mind, and I keep getting asked "can't you just give us a rule?", so I want to actually write one up before the next meeting. Like, 3-4 protocols a year right now, maybe 20 by the time the new building opens, and the stakes are real (approve / modify / reject, animals' lives, sometimes a grant hanging on it), but the committee is basically vets and behavioral biologists with 20 minutes per item.

You're an expert in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, so please think carefully before you start writing, and start by brainstorming candidate criteria rather than jumping to a verdict — I want to see you work out which distinctions survive contact with a committee that will actually have to use this.

What I need from you is a decision procedure the committee can run, in order, on a given candidate system (a colony, a hive, a swarm, whatever someone proposes): the questions to ask, in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a clear no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is values, not facts. Ground it in what philosophy of mind and comparative cognition actually have going on here — the intentional stance / real patterns stuff, multiple realizability, the difference between "the system behaves as if it's solving a problem" and "there's something it's like to be the system", the part-whole problem of which thing is even the candidate bearer of the mental state, whatever we've learned from social insect cognition. Don't give me a reading list or citations, I don't need to know who said what when, I need the distinctions in a form my vet colleague can apply on a Tuesday. And please don't take the "well it's all interest-relative so there's no fact of the matter" exit and stop there — the committee still has to vote, so I need the procedure to at least say which way the interest-relativity pushes and why.

Then run it on three cases and show me the answers, not just the reasoning: (1) a lab leafcutter colony with its fungus garden, (2) a honeybee hive — swarm decisions about nest sites, the waggle dance, the whole bees-and-pain mess, (3) a slime mold that navigates a maze with no neurons in it at all. At least one of the three should come out in a way I won't like; I'd rather find out from you than from the chair in front of everyone.

And be honest with me about the seams: which steps are just judgment calls in a lab coat, where the procedure will misfire, and what a procedure like this can't settle at all. If the thing implies that stuff we'd find obviously absurd also counts (a market? the committee itself? a termite mound?), say that out loud instead of quietly arranging the criteria so it doesn't. Also flag what the chair is actually deciding when she signs off, because I suspect half of what we've been calling a factual question is her call and nobody's told her.
