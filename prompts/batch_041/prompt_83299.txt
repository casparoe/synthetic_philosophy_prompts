I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of fifteen years, and in that world the question of inference to the best explanation is treated as settled in a way that has always seemed too quick: Bayes is the whole story, the explanatory virtues are either priors in disguise or heuristics for guessing at likelihoods, and anyone who insists that explanation carries independent evidential weight is politely said to be doing psychology rather than epistemology. The people I read who take explanation seriously do not concede this, and as far as I can tell the two camps have been talking past each other for decades without either side moving an inch. What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict.

Here is the impasse as I understand it. One side holds that inference to the best explanation is an autonomous form of inference with its own norms, irreducible to probabilistic reasoning, and adds that the best of a bad lot of candidate hypotheses need not be likely to be true, which is a problem for any account that starts from priors. A second side holds that any explanatory virtue you can name either encodes a prior or a likelihood, in which case explanationism is a fragment of Bayesianism, or encodes neither, in which case it is a heuristic with no evidential force. A third side holds that the first two are answering different questions, one about the logic of confirmation and one about how inquirers should spend scarce cognitive effort, and that explanation was never meant as a rival to conditionalization. Meanwhile there is a formal strand arguing that explanatoriness is simply evidentially irrelevant, and an opposing strand producing cases where it looks plainly relevant.

My own toy version of the worry is a trilemma. If explanatory virtues are priors, explanationism collapses into Bayesianism; if they are likelihoods, they are evidence and again nothing is added; if they are neither, they are extra-evidential and cannot justify belief. Either the explanationist is a Bayesian who has not noticed, or they are doing something indefensible. I suspect this trilemma is the engine of the stall, and that it is sustained by an ambiguity in what counts as an explanation and by the fact that nobody has stated clearly whether explanation is offered as a rule of inference, a rule of acceptance, or a constraint on the space of hypotheses one begins with. But that is a hypothesis, and I would rather you tell me whether it is right.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Verbal, over the words inference and explanation? Substantive, about the epistemology of priors and the space of candidate hypotheses? Two questions wearing one name, a formal one about confirmation and a practical one about inquiry? Or a genuinely hard problem that simply has not been solved? Say what each side is actually committed to, whether my trilemma is exhaustive or admits an escape, and what would count as progress here, whether a theorem, a distinction, or a change of question. I do not need a survey or a reading list; I want the structure of the disagreement and an honest answer about whether anything could move it.
