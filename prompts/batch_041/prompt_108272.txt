I am a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy working on practical reason, and I have been handed the 90-minute session in our department's spring workshop series on hard choices. The audience is sixteen people — graduate students from philosophy, law, and public policy, plus two faculty — in one seminar room, with no budget beyond index cards, masking tape, and a few plastic trays. I want an activity that makes them feel the force of a specific claim: that when two options are close in value, the familiar three-way verdict of better, worse, or equal can run out, and what is left over is neither ignorance nor a tie. Design the activity itself rather than an explanation of it. Nothing should be named in advance; participants should arrive at the problem through the rules, and I should not say the word for the missing relation until the debrief. No lecture up front and no assigned reading.

The specific trap I want them to fall into is this, stated in my own words so that you can build the rules around it: if A and B are close in value and A+ is a marginal improvement on A, most people will grant both that A+ is better than A and that A+ is still not better than B — but if A and B were equal, then A+ would have to be better than B. That leaves the three standard verdicts exhausted. I want the rules to force that sequence out of them: a public ledger on which each participant files a verdict at each stage; a stage at which the marginal improvement is introduced and each person must say what the improvement did and did not tip; and an auditing step in which a designated clerk, who is forbidden to argue and may only read back the participant's own earlier entries, hands the ledger over and asks for the missing line. Then I want them to try to file the A/B pair into trays marked with the three verdicts they have been using, and to discover that the trays will not take it; leave one unlabeled tray on the side table and let the group decide whether to use it and what to write on it, without prompting from me. The pairs themselves should be described in a few lines each and should differ in kind rather than in amount, so that nothing in the description hands the participants a common scale; I would like at least one pair to be such that no single respect of comparison obviously covers both options, so that the group has to ask what they are even comparing them with respect to. The last move should be a forced commitment: each participant picks one of the two options and writes a sentence defending the choice as correct, not merely as made, and then trades sentences with a neighbor and has to say whether the defense establishes correctness or only reports a preference.

In the debrief I want the moments in the activity mapped onto the pieces of the idea, and I want three diagnoses kept apart: that the options are equal after all and we are simply bad at measuring; that the comparison is indeterminate, so there is a fact of the matter we cannot see; and that the three-way verdict is the wrong frame to begin with. I also want the debrief to raise, without resolving, whether the leftover relation belongs to the values themselves or to our concepts, and to contrast the view that a hard choice is one in which the reason to choose is made by the commitment rather than found beforehand with the view that when the options are on a par both choices are permissible and neither is correct. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect someone to convert the whole exercise into uncertainty about their own preferences, someone else to insist that the leftover relation is just rough equality, and the audit to turn adversarial — and how to handle each of those without rescuing the participants from the problem. Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce and what I will have to supply in the debrief: my guess is that it cannot show that the values themselves lack a common measure, since all the evidence it generates concerns the participants' comparative verdicts rather than the structure of the values, and that it cannot touch the way a hard choice is lived with for years after it is made.
