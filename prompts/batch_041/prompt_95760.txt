I am writing a historical novel whose central figure is a Renaissance condottiere, and in preparing for it I have been reading Machiavelli — though I suspect with the wrong ear. He insists that a prince must above all possess virtù; yet in the same breath he advises that prince to dissemble, to break faith whenever it serves him, and to prefer being feared to being loved. I have always taken virtù to mean virtue in the ordinary sense — courage, uprightness, integrity of character — and if that is what he intends, I cannot see how the conduct he counsels could be a way of cultivating it. He speaks, moreover, as though virtù and fortune were the two powers that decide a prince's fate, which makes it sound less like a moral quality than a technical one. Have I misread him? I ask because the answer governs my whole conception of the man: whether my protagonist is meant to be a good man, or merely a formidable one.
