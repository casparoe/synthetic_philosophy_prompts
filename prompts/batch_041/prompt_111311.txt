A friend of mine who teaches moral philosophy has, over the years, ruled on a series of cases I have put to her, and I should like to know what she will say about one more. She holds that it is quite in order to act on a doctor's word about which of two drugs is safer, or on a sommelier's word about which wine is better, without having judged the matter for herself. She holds that it is not in order for a grown woman of ordinary intelligence to act on a friend's word that a particular joke is cruel, or on a colleague's word that an advertising campaign is dishonest; on such questions, she says, one must see it for oneself. Yet she thinks it perfectly proper that a six-year-old should take her mother's word that hitting her brother is wrong.

Now I want to ask her about a character in my current book: a nurse of full competence and ordinary conscience who, on a night when the ward is so short-staffed that she has seconds rather than minutes to decide, must choose whether to act on a senior doctor's judgment that a family's wishes should be overridden. She has no time to reach her own view; she can act only on his.

What will my friend say the nurse ought to do, and what rule is she in fact running — that testimony is acceptable in non-moral matters always and in moral matters only where the recipient cannot judge for herself at all, that it is acceptable wherever she cannot judge readily, whether from incapacity, inexperience, or want of time, that it turns on expertise rather than on domain, or something else? I should like your best guess, the rival rules that fit her earlier verdicts equally well, and the further case that would force her to choose between them.
