I'm assembling a handout for the second meeting of a graduate seminar on the communitarian critique of liberalism, and I want it to push students past the familiar labels and into the actual structure of the disagreement. Please build me a decision tree — a taxonomy in tree form — that starts from the most elementary commitment a reader might hold and branches, node by node, until it lands on a named position. Each branch node should be a question that genuinely divides the positions beneath it, phrased so that a reader can answer it without already knowing which position the answer commits them to; each leaf should carry the name of the position, or the word unnamed if no one defends it, together with a single sentence naming its most awkward commitment. Build the branches out of the logical space rather than out of a roster of names: first the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then the actual views located in that space.

The distinctions I care about, and I don't mind if the tree adds others: whether the self is prior to its ends or constituted by them; whether political justification must be neutral among conceptions of the good or may proceed from a substantive one; whether the state may promote a particular way of life or civic virtue; whether the primary subject of justice is the individual or a community; and whether neutrality is itself a sectarian value rather than a neutral procedural constraint. Treat these as separable, so that a view can be non-neutral in its grounds of justification while still individualist in its subject, or communitarian in its metaphysics of the self while liberal in its politics; I suspect those crossed combinations are where the interesting cases live, and I would rather the tree expose them than quietly collapse them into the standard camps.

Flag any pair of positions the tree cannot separate, and mark which combinations of answers go unoccupied, with a one-line diagnosis of whether the gap is there because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because no one has bothered to develop it. Where the discussion is likely to turn on a point about which people who know this literature genuinely disagree, say so, rather than presenting one side of it as the settled consensus.

Please organize the response under these headings, in this order: Root question (two or three sentences fixing what the whole tree is asking); The tree (the main body, with each node's dividing question and the positions it feeds); Leaves (each named position with its one-sentence awkward commitment); Empty branches (the unoccupied answer-combinations and your diagnosis of each); Pairs the tree cannot separate; and Settled versus contested (a short closing section separating what is common ground among specialists from what remains in dispute).
