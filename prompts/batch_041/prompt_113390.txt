Two friends of mine, Lena and Omar, spent most of a long dinner arguing about an apology that neither of them had received. A colleague of ours had been turned down for a position she was well qualified for, in a process she believed had been run carelessly, and the person responsible later wrote to her: "I apologize for any frustration this caused. It was never our intention to make you feel undervalued." She read the note aloud to us and said it had made things worse. Lena and Omar have been circling it ever since. I would like an adjudication of their exchange, not a verdict on the note.

Their argument, as best I can reconstruct it:

1. Omar: This is not an apology. To apologize is to concede that one did something wrong and that the other party's reaction was warranted. The note concedes nothing; it regrets the frustration and quietly files the problem under her feelings. What she received was an expression of regret, and regret is available to a person who is certain he did nothing wrong.
2. Lena: "I apologize" is not a report of a feeling; it is the deed itself, the way "I promise" is the promise. Requiring sincerity would make the occurrence of an apology depend on a private fact that no one on the receiving end can inspect, and would mean that one could never be apologized to by anyone one suspected of insincerity. The right thing to say about a cold apology is that it is a poor apology, not that it is not one. A hollow thing still has a shape.
3. Omar: Then Lena should be willing to say the phrasing is irrelevant. But she is not: she would agree that "I apologize for any inconvenience" performs a different act from "I was wrong to do it." If the words fix which act is performed, then the concession of fault is internal to the act — and if a hollow concession can change the act's identity, why can a hollow sentiment not void it?
4. Lena: Because the two are doing different work. Content determines which act is being performed; sincerity determines whether it was performed in good order. An apology that concedes nothing is not a defective apology but a different act, an expression of regret. A concession of fault made without remorse is still a concession, and it does what apologies are for: it alters what the wrongdoer may afterwards deny and what the wronged party may demand. The promise analogy holds — a promise made with no intention of keeping is still a promise, and bad faith is precisely the charge we want to remain available.
5. Omar: The promise analogy cuts the other way. Promising is constituted by a conventional undertaking that binds regardless of feeling, so sincerity was never its point. Apology's point is the acknowledgment of fault to the person wronged, and an acknowledgment the speaker does not believe is not an acknowledgment but an imitation of one. He offers two cases he says Lena cannot handle: a court-ordered apology read aloud by a lawyer, and an apology the recipient flatly refuses to accept.
6. Lena: Coercion and insincerity are different failures. A coerced undertaking does not bind, whereas an insincere one binds and is merely dishonoured. Refusal bears on whether an apology is accepted, not on whether it was made.

Neither would move, and I could not referee them. Please go through the six moves one by one, rating each from clearly mistaken through defensible to clearly right, and justifying each rating. Then say which of the two holds the better general principle, and — what I would most like to understand — which distinctions they are running together that, once separated, would dissolve most of their disagreement. If the exchange can be settled only by a case neither of them raises, supply it and say what it settles.
