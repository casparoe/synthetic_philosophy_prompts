I am designing a single-session workshop for a graduate seminar whose students come from economics, ecology, and computer science, and I want the session organized around a classification instrument rather than a lecture. Please build me a decision tree, or an equivalent taxonomy with an explicit branching structure, that carries a reader from a starting commitment nearly everyone in the room accepts — that uncoordinated competition sometimes leaves every participant worse off than some alternative that is jointly available — to a named position about what these traps are and what, if anything, can be done about them.

The instrument should be diagnostic rather than descriptive. A reader should reach a leaf by answering questions about concrete cases, without knowing in advance where an answer leads, so that the path taken reflects what the reader actually believes rather than which label sounds congenial. Phrase every branch node as a question a reader can answer about a case, and label every branch with the claim that separates it from its siblings.

Build the tree from the logical space: begin with the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, and only then locate the positions in that space. The following distinctions matter for my purpose, and I would like the tree to turn on them; if you think a better cut exists, make it and say what you changed.

1. Whether the harmful outcome is an equilibrium of the one-shot game, in which each agent's best reply to the others is to continue; a ratchet, in which each agent would prefer to stop but cannot because the others have already moved; or a product of selection, in which nobody is choosing and the losers are simply replaced (a runaway, a Malthusian spiral, a Red Queen dynamic).
2. Whether the harm is absolute, in the sense that everyone ends up worse off by their own lights, or positional, in the sense that the total is fixed and the competition only redistributes it.
3. Whether the cost falls on the participants themselves or on third parties who are not at the table.
4. Whether the participants could in principle bind themselves to a different outcome, so that the obstacle is either the absence of an enforceable agreement or the absence of any agreement that would be individually rational to keep.
5. Whether the escalation is driven by a shared metric whose validity decays as it is optimized, so that everyone is optimizing the same proxy and the proxy stops measuring what it was meant to measure.
6. Where a remedy would have to act: on the payoffs (taxes, prices, liability), on the set of players (merger, hegemony, a single global decision-maker), on the information the players have (making the trap common knowledge, monitoring, transparency), or on the players' preferences themselves.

You need not use every one of these as a branch node; if you drop one, say why, and if two of them cannot both be tested by questions a reader could answer, say which you kept.

The tree must contain, as leaves, at least the following positions, and you should add others wherever the logical space has room:

- the tragedy of the commons and its two classic remedies, privatization and state regulation, together with the polycentric or self-governing alternative that denies the dichotomy;
- the security-dilemma or arms-race reading, on which the trap is sustained by mutual fear and could be dissolved by reassurance rather than by enforcement;
- the view that interjurisdictional or interfirm competition is not a trap at all but a discovery procedure that sorts preferences and rewards efficiency, so the "race to the bottom" is a name for something beneficial;
- the empirical claim that races to the bottom rarely happen and that competition more often ratchets standards upward;
- the family of views that calls the whole class of outcomes "Moloch": the outcome is nobody's choice, no participant can unilaterally escape it, and no single lever fixes it, so the honest position is a diagnosis without a remedy. Include, as separate leaves, the version that expects a global coordinator or "singleton" to be the only escape and the version that treats the trap as permanent;
- the selection-theoretic view that such traps are features of evolutionary or market selection and cannot be repaired by changing anyone's incentives, only by changing the environment in which selection occurs.

For every combination of answers your tree permits but for which you cannot name a position, mark the leaf as unnamed and say in one sentence whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it appears to be unexplored. If two positions in your tree cannot be separated by any question a reader could answer, say so explicitly, and explain either what further question would separate them or why none can.

The tree should be usable on at least the following cases, which differ in structure. For each case, indicate the path it takes and the leaves it can reach:

- an audience at a concert in which each person stands to see better, with the result that nobody sees better than before;
- overuse of antibiotics in agriculture or clinical practice, where the cost falls on others and on the future;
- two firms that would each prefer to ship a feature later but each ships early because the other might not wait;
- the evolution of a costly ornament in a species where no individual is choosing;
- a credentialing spiral in which each student must acquire more schooling to hold a position that has not changed;
- competition among jurisdictions to attract a mobile tax base.

Please put the result in a form I can hand out: nodes as questions, branches labeled with the answers, leaves as positions. Where a position has a standard name, use it; where it does not, say unnamed. Aim for roughly ten to fourteen leaves and no more than about six levels of branching. Do not provide a reading list or citations, and do not assume I can look anything up: name the positions and describe them in your own words. Write the whole thing in American English.
