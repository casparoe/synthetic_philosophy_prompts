I have been asked to give the address at my town's bicentennial dinner next month, and I am stuck in a way I did not expect, so I am asking you to write the speech for me. Some context first: I have no academic training of any kind and I am not a historian. I run a small business on the square. A friend lent me an essay by Nietzsche — one of the pieces he called untimely, from the 1870s — about what history is for, and what it costs, and it has changed how I hear every conversation about this dinner.

The essay sorts the ways a person or a community uses its past into three. There is the monumental use: you hold up the great examples of the past so that they pull you toward doing something great yourself. There is the antiquarian use: you love and keep what is old and near — your town's objects, papers, graves — and you are grateful for it. And there is the critical use: you put the past on trial, find it guilty, and break with it. His point is not that one of these is correct. Each is necessary to a healthy life and each has its own disease. The monumental invents heroes and then leaves you living behind their masks. The antiquarian turns you into a caretaker who can no longer let anything end or begin. The critical is the hardest, because the thing being condemned is also what you are made of; he says you cannot step out of it simply by judging it.

Now the dinner. The town is two hundred years old this year. The money that built the square, the church and the first school came from a founding family, and how it came is no mystery to anyone who has looked: the founder acquired the land under the square from a family that had no choice, on paper that was legal and a bargain that was not fair, and his descendants still own that land and the town still pays them for it. Nothing about that ever ended. It is on the town's books. Two things are true at once. The family has been good to this town for two hundred years, and a young woman from that family — decent, generous, never anything but kind to me — is hosting the dinner and paying for it. She has also been gracious about the high school students who spent last spring in the deed books and came back with the story, and who will be sitting together at the back of the hall to hear whether I say it out loud.

Three groups in the room want three different speeches. The bicentennial committee wants the monumental one: they built this with their hands, so can we. The historical society wants the antiquarian one: gratitude, continuity, the depot saved, the families thanked for donating their papers. The students want the critical one: the town should say plainly what was done and who is still paying for it. The committee chair has asked me to end on a high note, which I take to mean, do not end on the deed.

I do not want a speech that splits the difference, and I do not want one that names three modes at a dinner and then says something warm about the past and the future. I want the essay's distinctions to do real work: to tell me which of the room's demands I can meet, which I must refuse, how to refuse them without insulting anyone, and above all where to land at the end. Constraints: no quotations from anyone, since a quotation would lose the room and this has to be my own words from first sentence to last; no apology on behalf of the dead and no prosecution of the living; no singling out my host; and none of the words healing or closure, which the committee uses to mean that a subject should be dropped. Eight minutes, roughly twelve hundred words.

Two things I would like you to settle while you write. First, my own addition, which may be nothing: all three of his ways seem to treat the past as finished — as treasure, as a spur, or as a crime scene. What I keep wanting is a way of speaking about the past as unfinished, as something whose outcome is still being decided by what we do next, which is precisely what the rent on the square makes true in my town and what a high note asks me to forget. Decide whether that is a fourth way or the critical one wearing a friendlier coat, and let the ending of the speech be whatever you decide. Second, there is a stretch of the essay about people who live almost without history at all, and about people who look at the whole business from such a height that nothing in it seems worth getting worked up about. I have read it four times and I cannot tell whether he admires those people or pities them, or whether he thinks the second sort is a cure for the first. I suspect the answer lies in his opening pages, where he seems to measure the health of a person or a people by how much of the past they can take in and digest and still be able to act — but I may have that backwards, and if my reading of him is wrong in a way that matters, write the speech a better reading would produce rather than the one my reading deserves.

What I am asking for is the speech itself, ready to be read aloud.
