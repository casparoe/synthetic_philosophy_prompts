I am a first-year PhD student in economics who has started reading methodology on the side, and I keep encountering references to Milton Friedman's 1953 essay on the methodology of positive economics and to what commentators call the F-twist. I recently read a chapter in a popular book on economic methodology — the author is a well-regarded economic journalist rather than a philosopher — that summarizes the position as follows:

"The essay's central claim, which Samuelson later nicknamed the F-twist, is that a scientific theory should never be judged by whether its assumptions are realistic. What matters is only whether the theory generates accurate predictions. Friedman's favourite illustration is the billiard player: a skilled player behaves as if he had solved the equations of motion governing the balls, even though he has done no such calculation, and the hypothesis that he behaves this way is confirmed by his success at the table. This shows that an assumption can be literally false — the player does not know any physics — and still be scientifically legitimate, because the theory it belongs to predicts well. In fact, Friedman goes further and says that the more unrealistic the assumptions of a theory are, the more significant the theory is, since a good theory abstracts from the mess of reality and keeps only the essential features. So the F-twist is really an instrumentalist doctrine: theories are neither true nor false, they are just tools for prediction, and a theory with false assumptions can be the best theory available. Samuelson's F-twist objection — that if you never test assumptions you can cherry-pick whatever predictions happen to come out right — is generally regarded as having refuted the doctrine, though it survives in practice because economists like simple models. What Friedman was really doing, on this reading, was giving economists permission to use unrealistic assumptions whenever they want."

I have no prior investment in either side of this debate. If anything, I am inclined to think the popularizer is basically right, because the economists I know do talk this way about models. But the summary feels too tidy to me, and I would like to know which parts of it I can trust before I repeat any of it in a seminar.

Please audit the summary sentence by sentence. For each claim, tell me what a philosopher of economics working on this literature would accept, what they would wince at, and what is straightforwardly wrong. I am especially interested in the following: whether the popularizer has correctly stated the relation between testing a theory's assumptions and testing its predictions; whether the billiard-player example is being used the way Friedman used it; whether the "the more unrealistic the better" line is being reported as Friedman's criterion or as something weaker; whether the instrumentalist label is a fair characterization of the essay or a contested reading; and whether the Samuelson objection is best described as a refutation, a constraint, or something else. Finally, tell me whether the popularizer's concluding claim — that the essay amounts to a permission slip for unrealistic assumptions — follows from anything Friedman actually argued, or whether it is a distortion of a narrower methodological point.

Please state how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point in your audit about which you are least sure, explaining what would change your mind.
