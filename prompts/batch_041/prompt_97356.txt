For most of my adult life I took it for granted that people have beliefs and desires, and that saying so was simply describing a fact about them. That confidence has been eroding, and not for the usual philosophical reasons. My father's dementia has progressed to the point where my brother and I disagree about what we are even doing when we describe him. My brother says our father no longer believes that my mother is dead, and that saying he does is a story we tell to keep him, and ourselves, comfortable. I started out angry at this, but the more I have watched myself talk about what Dad believes and wants, the less sure I am that I know what I mean. So I have been keeping a rough account of the attributions I make without thinking, and I cannot get them to line up under a single rule.

Some of them I feel sure about. When I say the dog wants to go out, I take myself to be right, and not merely for convenience. When I say the toddler believes the moon is following her, I take that to be true as well; she really does take the world to be that way. But when the repairman says my thermostat believes the room is cold, or my laptop thinks I have mistyped, I hear that as loose talk and nothing more, even though I am told the thermostat genuinely registers a temperature and acts on the gap between that reading and a set point. Here it gets worse. I have no trouble saying that the company I work for believes it is under attack from a competitor, and I mean that almost literally, even though I could not tell you whose belief it is, since no one person holds it. Yet when a doctor says my nephew's immune system believes the transplanted kidney is an invader, I flinch; that one seems to me a metaphor, even though, as far as I can tell, it is the same kind of distributed, headless process as the company. And there is a case of my own I find harder still. My neighbor is genuinely kind to me and has stood up for my family, and she also supports policies that would make our lives much worse; I want to say she believes she is my friend and believes that people like me are a problem, and that both are true of her at once, which I find unpleasant to hold in my head.

What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of them, that accounts for these verdicts — one that says precisely when attributing a belief or a desire is apt and when it is mere metaphor. I have tried the obvious candidates and each fails somewhere: that the thing must have a mechanism that tracks how things are; that it must have a single mind; that it must be able to be wrong in a way that frustrates its own purposes. Tell me which principle survives the cases, what it says about the ones I have not listed, and, honestly, which of my verdicts it forces me to give up — starting with my father, where I most want to be told I am right and suspect I may not be. Write it as continuous prose, the way you would in a letter; no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
