I'm a graduate student in philosophy, midway through a seminar on the norms of assertion — the question of what a speaker has to satisfy for an assertion to be proper: that what she says is true, that she believes it, that she knows it, that she is justified in it, or something else again. I've noticed that the literature has the shape of one long argument by counterexample: someone proposes a norm, someone else produces a case where the norm returns the wrong verdict, and the norm is patched or abandoned. I want to see that dialectic sent up, and I'd like you to write the piece. Take the role of a philosopher of language and epistemologist who knows this material cold and also has a stand-up's sense of timing, and produce a satirical press release, in the deadpan register of a standards organization announcing a new revision of an official specification, in which a fictional international body — you can call it the International Board for Assertoric Standards, or invent your own — announces version 5.0 of its Standard for Proper Assertion. The conceit is that the Board has spent decades hunting for a single universally binding norm of assertion, and that every earlier revision was forced by a counterexample the previous revision could not survive; narrate that revision history as if it were a record of product recalls and field failures. Aim for 700–900 words, laid out with a headline, a dateline, two or three subheadings, and — if the genre will bear it — a table of conformance tiers.

The humor has to come from the philosophy rather than from invented absurdity, so the piece must handle, correctly and without ever announcing that it is doing so: the case of a speaker who asserts something false but entirely blamelessly; the Moore-paradoxical assertions, in which a speaker asserts that p while disclaiming belief in p, or knowledge of p, in the same breath; the lottery case, in which it seems fine to believe, and awkward to assert, that one's ticket will lose; and the awkwardness of any norm one can only satisfy after the fact — that what one said turned out to be true — which makes conformance retroactive and, arguably, not something a speaker can aim at. Let the Board's members quietly disagree with one another: give the truth-norm faction, the belief-norm faction, the knowledge-norm faction, and the justification-or-warrant faction each a dissenting statement or a footnote, and let someone raise, at least in passing, the pragmatist thought that asserting is just offering to add something to the conversation's common ground, so that a challenge and a retraction are moves in the same game. End with the Board adopting something hedged and context-sensitive that plainly concedes the original ambition, and work in somewhere a rueful admission that the Board's own announcements keep violating whichever norm the current revision endorses.

Before drafting, think through carefully which counterexample actually defeats which norm and why — the logic has to be sound, since the intended reader is a specialist who should laugh rather than wince. Draft it straight: no winking at the reader, no meta-commentary about the satire, no explaining the joke. The ideal reader is someone who has argued about the knowledge norm over coffee and will recognize every case you are quietly staging.
