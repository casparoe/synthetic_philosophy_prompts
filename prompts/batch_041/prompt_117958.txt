I am a high school student in a philosophy elective, and we have just finished a unit on David Benatar's asymmetry argument for antinatalism. Our teacher has asked us to peer-review each other's quiz answers, and I have to assign a grade out of 20 and write comments that justify it. The quiz question and my classmate's answer are below. Could you give me a point-by-point assessment? For each part of the question, please say what the answer gets right, what it gets wrong, and how much each error matters, and then give a final grade out of 20 with a short justification. I need specific comments I can pass on, not just a general impression.

Quiz question (20 points):

Benatar's asymmetry between pleasure and pain is often summarized as two claims: the absence of pain is good even if no one experiences that good, whereas the absence of pleasure is bad only if there is someone who is deprived of it. (a) Using the four-case comparison between an existing sufferer, an existing happy person, a never-existing sufferer, and a never-existing happy person, explain how Benatar derives the conclusion that coming into existence is always a harm. (8 points) (b) State the most common objection to the asymmetry — that it is ad hoc because it combines impersonal and person-affecting value judgments — and explain how Benatar might answer it. (6 points) (c) Does the asymmetry argument depend on the empirical claim that most lives contain more pain than pleasure? Explain. (6 points)

Classmate's answer:

Benatar's asymmetry says that the absence of pain is good even when there is no one to enjoy it, while the absence of pleasure is not bad unless someone exists who is deprived of it. To see how this works, imagine four cases. (1) A person exists and suffers: the pain is bad. (2) A person exists and is happy: the pleasure is good. (3) A person never exists, so there is no pain: this is good, and it is good for the person who would otherwise have suffered. (4) A person never exists, so there is no pleasure: this is not bad, because there is no one who is deprived. Comparing (1) and (3), nonexistence avoids a bad and has a good; comparing (2) and (4), existence has a good and nonexistence has no bad. So nonexistence is always preferable to existence, and coming into existence is always a harm. This is why Benatar says procreation is wrong.

The usual objection is that the asymmetry is rigged: it treats the absence of pain as impersonally good but the absence of pleasure as person-affecting bad. If we are going to say that the absence of pain in case (3) is good even though no one enjoys it, why can't we say that the absence of pleasure in case (4) is bad even though no one is deprived? Benatar's answer is that the two cases are not parallel: pain is bad when it is experienced, and its absence is good whether or not anyone experiences that absence, whereas pleasure is only good when someone experiences it, so its absence is only bad when there is someone who is deprived. He also points out that we do not lament the absence of happy people, but we do lament the presence of suffering people.

Finally, the asymmetry argument does depend on the empirical claim that life contains more pain than pleasure. Benatar supports this with his discussion of cognitive biases, which lead us to overestimate how good our lives are. So the asymmetry and the pessimism argument work together: the asymmetry explains why coming into existence is a harm, and the empirical claim explains why the harm is serious.
