Can you help me think through a puzzle I keep hitting in higher-order theories of consciousness? Skip the usual tour of the taxonomy (higher-order thought vs. inner sense vs. self-representational) — I want the actual conceptual knot. I've been going back and forth on this for weeks and cant get it straight.

Here it is. On the standard story, a first-order state becomes conscious by being the target of a suitable higher-order representation, and the higher-order representation is also what fixes what the experience is like — thats what makes the misrepresentation cases interesting. But now: either the higher-order state gets its content and character from the first-order state it's about, or it has that character on its own and imposes it on the target. If it inherits, then the first-order state is already the thing with the phenomenal character and the higher-order state is just a pointer — and then it's hard to see what work the higher-order part is doing, or why the first-order state couldn't just be conscious on its own. If instead the higher-order state supplies the character, then the first-order state contributes nothing except being pointed at, and you could swap it out for any other state with no difference in what it's like. Which is absurd.

So: is that a real dilemma, or is there a third option? I'd like you to

(a) say whether inheritance can be had without making the first-order state sufficient for consciousness,
(b) say whether collapsing to one state — the state representing itself — actually dodges the problem or just relocates it,
(c) sketch what a version would look like where the higher-order representation is non-conceptual and attention-like, carrying no attributive content of its own, just a monitoring of whatever's going on, and say whether that helps,
and (d) tell me whether the whole thing pushes toward the conclusion that we're never acquainted with the first-order state itself, only with a representation of it — and whether that's a reductio or just the honest consequence.

Don't worry about who said what or which paper this is from; I care about whether the core claim, that consciousness is a matter of being represented by a higher-order state, can survive intact.
