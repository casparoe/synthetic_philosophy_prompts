I'm a third-year PhD student working on metaphysics of time, and I've somehow volunteered to run my department's film night — we're showing Arrival, the Villeneuve one from 2016, the adaptation of Ted Chiang's "Story of Your Life." Everyone reads it through Sapir-Whorf and the usual "ooh, nonlinear time, isn't that deep" angle, and I'm honestly bored of it. I want to do it through Borges instead — his actual views on time, not just the forking-paths image everyone half-remembers.

So take the role of someone who genuinely knows both things cold: the film down to its plot beats, and Borges's metaphysics of time. Before you commit to a reading, brainstorm for yourself what positions Borges actually stakes out about time — the picture in "The Garden of Forking Paths" where every possibility happens in its own branch, the idealist line in "A New Refutation of Time" where he tries to dissolve time altogether the way Berkeley dissolved matter, the circular / eternal-return material, whatever else is load-bearing — and then tell me which of them Arrival genuinely dramatizes and which it quietly contradicts. Don't just list the positions, pick a side.

My hunch, and tell me straight if it's confused: the film looks Borgesian because Louise ends up seeing her whole life at once, but it's really the opposite of Borges. Arrival hands her one fixed timeline she can't alter — she still has the daughter, she still lets it all happen — which is closer to a fatalist block universe than to Borges's forking of all possibilities. And Borges's deeper move, dissolving the self along with time into an idealist haze, is something the film can't afford, because its whole emotional payoff needs Louise to be a continuous person who chooses. So where does the film illuminate Borges and where does it betray him? Point me at specific scenes — the heptapod logograms, the slow reveal with the daughter, the late scene where Louise and Ian talk about whether she'd change anything if she could see her whole life — and say what a careful critic has to concede against my reading.

End with what, if anything, Arrival adds to the Borges debate that argument alone doesn't. And if the pairing is a stretch, or if I'm conflating Borges's forking time with eternalism in a way that makes the whole question ill-posed, say so plainly rather than humoring me — I'd rather you push back than give me a tidy reading that doesn't hold up.
