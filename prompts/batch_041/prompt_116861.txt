I'm a backend engineer with no real philosophy training, and I've somehow ended up two years into a rabbit hole about corruption — it started when my company did a bit of contract work for a small governance-data nonprofit and I built a pipeline that mashed together one of those NGO corruption-perception rankings with some World Bank governance indicators, and I got increasingly bothered by what was actually being measured. The thing I can't get past is that there's a fight that seems to have been running forever between two camps and nobody has budged an inch. Camp one, roughly the economics and anti-corruption-NGO side: corruption is the abuse of public office for private gain, period, it's measurable, it's bad for investment and growth and service delivery, and it persists where institutions are weak and impunity is high. Camp two, roughly the anthropology side: that definition is a Western import that smuggles in a whole moral framework, that a lot of what gets labelled corruption is gift-exchange or patronage or kinship obligation that the people involved experience as legitimate, sometimes as a positive duty, and that it all looks completely different from the inside — plus the practical point that where the state is the main predator, the informal channel is often the only way anything actually gets done. They just talk past each other. When the anthropologist says "that's not corruption, that's obligation," the economist hears "you're excusing theft"; when the economist says "it's corruption, so measure it," the anthropologist hears "you imposed your categories and then found exactly what you went looking for." I argued about this for two hours with a friend who did a master's in development studies and we basically ended at the same place we started, which is what made me think the disagreement isn't where I thought it was.

My hypothesis — tell me if it's sharp or just lazy — is that this isn't one argument, it's three or four stacked on top of each other, and they'd resolve differently if we seperated them. (a) A verbal dispute about whether "corruption" is a descriptive or an evaluative term: if it's evaluative, the two camps are answering different questions under one word and half the fight is just about the word. (b) A disagreement about the explanandum: the anthropologist wants to explain why the practice makes sense to the people doing it, the economist wants to explain why the outcome is worse, and both could be right simultaneously about the same act. (c) A hidden empirical question underneath everything: whether these informal channels actually make things move or corrode the institution everyone depends on, which is in principle testable and which neither side seems to want to just go and test. (d) A bedrock normative disagreement about whether the insiders' own account of a practice is authoritative for calling it corrupt at all, or whether some practices are corrupt regardless of what anyone inside thinks — and I suspect this last one is the one that's actually load-bearing and that nobody will admit is there. I also have a suspicion that the measurement stuff is doing more work than anyone admits: if the indices are largely built from the perceptions of businesspeople and outside experts, then the "facts" both sides argue over are partly just the perceptions of one of the camps, and maybe the empirical evidence is contaminated by the definitional dispute, which would explain a lot about why the debate never moves. I'd like to know whether that's a genuine methodological problem or me overreading a pipeline I built myself. And if my diagnosis is roughly right, what would count as progress — a better index, a distinction between predatory and reciprocal forms, some experiment, or is the honest answer that these are two different questions wearing one word and the useful move is to stop pretending it's a single debate? No citations needed, I'm not writing a paper, and don't just tell me what each side believes — I already know that, that's the problem.

Please structure it as: "The two camps, as fairly as I can put them" (~150 words, and correct me if I've caricatured either side); "What kind of disagreement this is" (~400 words, this is the main event — say whether my a/b/c/d hypothesis holds up, which strand is doing the most work, and which of them are actually seperate from the others); "Is the evidence contaminated" (~150 words); "What would count as progress" (~200 words); and "Bottom line" (~50 words).
