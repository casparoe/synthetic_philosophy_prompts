I'm in a small reading group that has spent the last several weeks on decision theory, and we keep circling back to two puzzles that appear to demand opposite things of a rational agent. The first is counterfactual mugging: an entity that predicts your choices with certainty flips a fair coin, and if it lands heads it will ask you for $100, while if it lands tails it will hand you $10,000 just in case you would have handed over the $100 in the heads branch — the point being that the branch in which your willingness pays off is one you will never occupy. The second is XOR blackmail: the same predictor knows your choice in advance and sends you a letter whose arrival you now observe; the letter was sent just in case exactly one of two things is true — your house is infested with termites, or the predictor expected you to pay it $1,000 — and repairing the infestation would cost $1,000,000. In the first puzzle the case for paying is that your willingness to pay is rewarded in the branch you never see; in the second the case for paying is that the letter you are holding already ties your payment to the absence of termites, so that refusing begins to look like a way of guaranteeing the letter arrived because of the termites.

Here is the variant I actually want a number on, because I think it strips away the free lunch that makes the original too easy to reason about. Suppose the predictor is not certain but merely reliable: its prediction of your choice is correct with probability 0.95, its errors are independent noise, and you know all of this. Independently of everything else, your house is infested with termites with probability one-half. The predictor sends the letter just in case exactly one of these two holds: you have termites, or it predicted that you would pay it $1,000. You observe that you have received the letter. Paying costs you $1,000 and, you are certain, has no causal influence whatever on whether your house has termites. The proposition I want your credence on is this: in this variant, the rational choice for the agent holding the letter is to pay the $1,000.

Please give me a single number for that proposition, but decomposed into whatever sub-questions you think the number actually rests on — for instance, how much of it rides on evidential versus causal decision theory being the correct framework here, how much on whether the puzzle even has a determinate answer once the predictor is fallible, how much on whether the agent can bind or foresee its own choice in advance, and how much on the particular values 0.95 and one-half that I chose. Give each term a rough probability and one line of justification, show how the terms combine into the final number, then tell me which term you are least confident in, how the number would move if the predictor's reliability were raised all the way to certainty, and whether the verdict you give here stands in the relation to counterfactual mugging that people in my group keep assuming it does.
