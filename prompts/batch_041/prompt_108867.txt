I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, working on the epistemology of historical testimony, and I've been collecting cases that seem to me to share a shape for which I can't find a name.

Three debates about whether a story is legend or history. At the mound at Hisarlık, usually identified with Troy, excavators found a fortified city with a destruction layer, and Hittite documents name a western vassal kingdom and a ruler whose names some scholars read as Greek; some conclude that a Greek expedition against that city really happened, while others accept the archaeology and the philology and still deny that either bears on the story. In the Arthurian case, the earliest surviving notices, in ninth- and tenth-century Latin compilations, present Arthur as a battle-leader and nothing more, and historians who want a historical Arthur subtract the later romance and keep the battles. In the Exodus case there is no Egyptian notice at all, and the argument is over whether a small group of people who left servitude in Egypt could lie behind the narrative, or whether the whole thing is an origin story composed for later political purposes.

The move I keep seeing is this: to get from the story to the event, you cross out whatever serves the storyteller — the marvels, the round numbers, the moralizing — and treat what is left as a residue of something that happened, on the grounds that no one would have bothered to invent it. But the residue is then almost by construction the least interesting part of the story, and the procedure has no stopping rule: cross out enough and you arrive at a claim too thin for anything to count against it, while stopping early simply restores the maximalist reading. Worse, the same reasoning appears to run in both directions, so that a detail which flatters the narrator is read as invention and a detail which embarrasses him as memory; what presents itself as an evidential criterion looks more like an inference about a narrator's incentives, converted into a claim about the world.

I know that the criterion of embarrassment in historical-Jesus research is one instance of this, and that arguments from silence are a neighbour, but neither seems to be the general thing, which I suspect is really two separate claims run together: one about transmission, that odd or inconvenient details are the ones that survive, and one about motive, that no one invents the unflattering.

So: does this pattern have an established name and literature, and if not, can you name and define it in a way that marks its edges — how it differs from the criterion of embarrassment and from an argument from silence, and when the inference is legitimate rather than collapsing into the absence of a stopping rule? A label alone won't help me; I need the carving.
