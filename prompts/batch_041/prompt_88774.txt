I am a graduate student in economics working on epistemic foundations, and I keep returning to an argument in our department's theory seminar that nobody seems able to resolve. The dispute concerns the common prior assumption — the assumption, built into most of the game-theoretic models we use, that all agents' beliefs about the world can be derived from a single shared prior over states by conditioning on each agent's private information. Roughly one camp, which I will call the conventionalists, holds that this is a mere modeling convention: because any profile of beliefs can be re-described as one prior together with a suitably rich type space, the assumption carries no empirical or normative content. On their view it is a normalization, not a claim about how people actually think, and the real work always lies in deciding how to decompose a situation into a shared prior plus asymmetric information. The other camp, the substantivists, insists that the assumption does genuine work. It is what underwrites the results we teach in the first week — that agents who share a prior cannot agree to disagree once their posterior beliefs are common knowledge, and that such agents cannot find a mutually acceptable bet, or a speculative trade, on purely informational grounds — and since those results are offered as claims about actual rational agents, the assumption must be a substantive thesis about how rational agents form and revise belief. They then observe that people plainly do agree to disagree and do trade against one another on the strength of disagreement, and treat this as a refutation; the conventionalists reply that such cases merely show the type space was drawn too thinly.

The longer this runs, the more I suspect the two sides are not disagreeing about anything at all — or rather, that they are disagreeing about two different things under a single name. My hypothesis is that the dispute is partly verbal (what the phrase the common prior picks out: a real prior over the actual world, or a purely formal object internal to a type space) and partly a genuine philosophical disagreement that neither side acknowledges, namely whether a given body of evidence determines a unique rational degree of belief, or whether two agents with the same evidence may rationally hold different priors. If that is right, then what the conventionalists call a modeling convention and what the substantivists call a substantive claim are answers to different questions, and the genuine disagreement lives in epistemology rather than in game theory.

I would like you to diagnose this dispute for me. Is my hypothesis correct, or is there a real empirical or formal question doing the work that I am missing? If the disagreement is partly verbal, exactly what is being equivocated on, and how would you disambiguate it — is the phrase the common prior ambiguous, or is it the word belief? If the substantive residue is the uniqueness of a rational prior given evidence, spell out what follows for the common prior assumption: does it simply become an instance of that epistemological debate, and is it defensible on either side of it? Finally, and honestly, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress here — a formal result, a conceptual distinction, a change of question — or whether nothing should be expected to move it.
