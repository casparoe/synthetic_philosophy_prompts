im putting together the opening for a reading group thats doing Nagarjuna this term and i want to start the first session with a short poem instead of a mini-lecture. can you write me one?

heres the shape i want. sixteen lines, four quatrains. one refrain line, and the same line has to turn up in every quatrain, but it walks down one slot each time -- line 1 of the first stanza, line 2 of the second, line 3 of the third, line 4 of the fourth. so the identical sentence keeps getting relocated, and i want the three lines around it each time to make it read differently: as an assertion in the first stanza, as a denial in the second, as somehow both at once in the third, as neither in the fourth. the sentence itself never changes, only where it sits and what its sitting next to. thats the whole trick and i care more about that landing than about the lines being pretty.

the subject is the tetralemma (catuskoti) -- is / isnt / both / neither -- and how Nagarjuna runs a question through all four corners instead of picking one. pick a single live question to run the corners on and stay with it the whole way (does the self exist, is the mind just the brain, is free will real, whatever you think will land for a mixed group of readers), dont hop topics mid-poem. and the ending has to duck the obvious trap: dont let "neither" come out as the winning corner, and dont tack on a fifth stanza to tidy things up, because a fifth corner is precisely what emptiness isnt. the fourth quatrain should feel like the frame giving out under you, not like the answer finally arriving. someone whos read the MMK should be able to tell what youre doing; someone who hasnt should still get a real poem out of it.

after the poem, give me a short note -- not a reading list, i just want to know what you did. tell me which reading of the tetralemma you committed to (the one where all four corners get knocked down, or the one where the corners were never theses in the first place and the whole exercise is therapeutic), and say plainly where the poem takes liberties with Nagarjuna or leans on a contested reading. be decisive about it rather than hedging -- id much rather have a poem with a real interpretation baked into it than a cautious one with none.
