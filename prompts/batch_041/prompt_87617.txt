I have a settled set of judgments about how historians ought to describe the past, and I want to know whether a single principle underlies them. The cases below are stipulated composites rather than reports of particular events, and everything needed to decide them is stated in the case; I am not after a survey of what others have written, and I do not need references.

1. A historian of the twelfth century tells us that a besieging army killed the inhabitants of a town that had already surrendered, and calls what happened a war crime. I think that is the wrong word. I am content to say the killing was wrong, and content to say it was atrocious; I am not content to file it under a legal category that did not exist, as though the commander had broken a rule he could have looked up.

2. A historian of the nineteenth century calls a merchant who bought and sold enslaved people, and grew rich on their labor, a moral monster, and notes that the man's neighbors thought him a credit to the town. I think the description is permissible, and that the neighbors' approval is irrelevant. Whatever vocabulary was on offer, the people he owned did not need it in order to know what was being done to them.

3. A historian of the fourteenth century writes about a manor's GDP, its growth rate, and a recession after the plague. I think this should not be done. Something can be computed, but the category presupposes a bounded economy whose output is measured and compared across time, and importing it settles in advance what an economy is.

4. A historian observes that a given society's language contains no word dividing religious practice from other practice, and concludes that the society had no religion. I think the conclusion is wrong: the absence of the word is not the absence of the thing, and describing their rituals and their gods as religious is no distortion.

5. A historian says that a seventeenth-century anatomist who deferred to Galen was being irrational, since Galen was wrong. I think irrational is the wrong verdict. Given the evidence and the state of the argument, the man's reasoning was defensible; the word measures the distance between him and us, not a defect in him.

6. A biographer of an eighteenth-century man describes his attachments to men as homosexual, glossing this as a claim about desire only and not about identity. I cannot make up my mind whether this is legitimate or whether it quietly installs a modern organization of desire.

7. A historian of medieval Europe refuses the word feudal on the ground that nobody in the period used it. I think the refusal is a mistake: the word is ours, a term of analysis rather than a report of anyone's self-understanding, and giving it up makes the account vaguer, not more faithful.

The principle I have been working with is this: the question is not whether a category is ours, but whether it could have been theirs. If a fully informed and morally serious person of that time and place could have reached the same description, then using our word for it is legitimate; if he could not, the description is an anachronism wearing the costume of an explanation. That test seems to me to get 1, 2, 3, 5, and 6 roughly right, and to fail on 4 and 7, where the past could not have reached the description and yet the description still strikes me as correct.

What I want is a principle, or a small set of them, that does better. If you keep something like the availability test, say precisely what it is a test of, and why it should govern evaluative descriptions and descriptive categories differently if it should. If you discard it, say what replaces it. In either case, state each clause precisely, name the case that forces it, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot keep. I would rather surrender one verdict than buy a principle that saves all seven by adding an epicycle.

Finally, test your principle on cases I have not listed: a historian who calls a nineteenth-century colonial campaign a genocide; one who calls a practice torture although it was lawful where and when it occurred; one who writes about medieval childhood as though the category were timeless; and one who uses nationalism to describe a society organized around dynastic loyalty. Where your principle's verdicts on these strike me as wrong, I would like to know whether you would amend the principle or defend the verdict.
