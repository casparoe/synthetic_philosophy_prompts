Bit of an odd one. I'm the outgoing president of our uni's philosophy society — we meet in a back room of a pub, and this year, mostly by accident, we ended up reading our way through the longtermism stuff: the hinge of history hypothesis, the claim that we might be living at the point where the whole future of civilisation is most up for grabs, that sort of thing. I'm giving the closing speech at our end-of-year dinner next Friday, four or five minutes, and I want it to be honest rather than stirring, which turns out to be much harder than it sounds. The room's a mix: half are members who've read all the same things I have and will notice if I overstate the thesis; our patron is a retired Aristotle scholar who thinks the whole business is a fad with bad metaphysics and isn't shy about saying so; and the other half are parents and partners who've come for the free dinner and have never heard of any of it. So it has to land for people who know where the bodies are buried without losing the ones who just want to know why their daughter spends Tuesday nights in a pub arguing about the year 2200.

The trouble is that the rhetoric the idea seems to invite is exactly the rhetoric I don't think I'm entittled to. There's a weak version — our actions right now have unusually high expected leverage, for structural reasons — and a strong version, that this is *the* decisive moment, a window that's open now and shuts forever. Only the strong version supports the "you are the generation that decides everything" ending, and I'm not confident in it; it seems to smuggle in a picture of history as a thing with a shape that terminates. And even if we are at the hinge, there's the probability business: a friend of mine keeps saying that if you're at the hinge with some probability, all the grandeur gets multiplied by that number, and what's left looks a lot like ordinary decency. He also calls the whole thing secular providence, and I can't fully argue him out of it — partly because my nan was a Methodist lay preacher who believed God had a plan for each of us, and I've started to wonder whether I've just swapped that for a spreadsheet.

Could you write the speech? British English please. What I want is something a room of half-sceptics can't catch me out on — no overclaiming, no pretending we know what the hinge is or that we're standing on it — but that still gives the leavers something to hold, because it's my last night and I'd rather not end on "well, probably nothing matters much anyway". If the honest version genuinely can't carry an ending like that, tell me what you'd do instead.
