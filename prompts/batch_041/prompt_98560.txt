Right, so I'm a novelist — my last book was about a bloke who runs a cricket farm, and the whole thing is really a dramatised version of what I keep calling the scale argument: even if the odds of any single insect being sentient are tiny, there are so many of the buggers that the expected amount of suffering in play is enormous, and that on its own should make us sit up. Next month I've been asked to give a talk at a symposium on insects and welfare and defend that as an actual moral claim rather than a literary mood.

The room's going to be tricky. It's mostly insect neurobiologists — people who've spent their careers on insect brains, who are pretty sceptical there's anyone home, and who get annoyed when a novelist waves the neuroscience away. There's a couple of welfare economists who'll be all over the expected-value maths and will want to bang on about fanaticism, about how tiny probabilities times huge numbers can justify almost anything. And one or two philosophers of mind who'll want to know what the word 'sentience' is even doing in the sentence.

I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a defence. Tell me the eight or so objections I'll actually recieve from these people, phrased the way they'd phrase them, ordered by how much damage they'd do to my case. For each, a short reply I can actually deliver in a couple of minutes, plus a flag on where the reply is thin — so I know what to concede up front and what to hold. And throw in the one question I'm dreading and hoping nobody asks.

For what it's worth, my line is this: I don't claim to know insects suffer. I claim our ignorance should be modelled as a probability rather than treated as a flat 'unknown', and that on almost any sensible prior the expected suffering of reared insects swamps the stuff we already take seriously — so the burden shifts onto whoever wants to wave it away. I also think the novel form is doing real work here and not just decoration, so be ready for them to go at that seperate point too.
