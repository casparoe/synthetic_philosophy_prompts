My two best mates from uni got into a proper arguement at a barbeque last weekend and I've been chewing it over ever since. One of them, Dan, did a masters in conflict studies and now works for an aid NGO; the other, Aisha, is a human-rights lawyer who's spent a fair bit of time in the region. It kicked off when someone mentioned the 2011 Libya intervention. Dan said Libya was the textbook case of humanitarian intervention done right: Gaddafi was about to go door to door in Benghazi, the UN resolution (1973) authorised force to protect civilians, and the intervention stopped a massacre everyone was braced for. Aisha said it wasn't civilian protection at all, it was regime change in disguise -- the resolution authorised protecting civilians, not toppling Gaddafi, and NATO spent months grinding down the regime until the rebels walked into Tripoli, which is regime change by any name. Dan shot back that you can't protect people from a regime that's actively hunting them without taking away that regime's ability to hunt them, so degrading Gaddafi's forces just was civilian protection; the threat and the regime were the same object. Aisha said that's exactly the trap: if protecting civilians always ends up requiring the removal of the regime, then every humanitarian intervention is regime change, and the humanitarian label is a fig leaf for a war over who governs. She added that the people who designed the responsibility-to-protect idea deliberately kept it narrow -- mass atrocities only -- precisely to avoid an Iraq-style slippery slope, but the doctrine can't help sliding, because the state committing the atrocities is usually the very thing you have to neutralise. Dan said that's just the 'it turned out badly' objection in disguise, and that you have to judge the decision on what was known at the time, not with hindsight -- and that 'worse off than under the dictator' quietly assumes the dictator would have stayed and behaved. Aisha said hindsight is the whole point: everyone knew removing Gaddafi would leave a vacuum in a country with no institutions, same lesson as Iraq, so the risks were foreseeable and the intervention is indicted by the very standard Dan wants to use. Dan said the real disagreement is the principle -- she thinks 'humanitarian intervention' is a category error, a noble-sounding name for great-power regime change, whereas he thinks it's a genuine category and Libya was a case where it applied but the execution was botched. Aisha said the category is the problem: once you accept that force may be used to protect a population from its own government, you've made a state's sovereignty conditional on your judgement of its conduct -- and whoever gets to make that judgement isn't doing humanitarianism, they're doing empire with better PR. They both turned to me at the end and I could'nt pick a side; they sounded right at different moments.

Could you adjudicate this move by move? For each of the moves above, say who has the better of it and why, flag anything either of them is quietly assuming or leaving out, and then give me a verdict on the question they actually ended on -- whether 'humanitarian intervention' is a genuine category or a contradiction in terms. Please don't just split the difference to keep the peace; where one of them is clearly right, say so, and where they're talking past each other, say that too. Write your answer in British English.
