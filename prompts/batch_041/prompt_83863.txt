I've got a pile of gut verdicts about when someone is still the same person, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to. A man loses nearly all his memories in an accident but keeps his body, his voice, his old habits and his fondness for his wife; I say he's the same person, just a thinner one. A man has his memories wiped and another person's whole life story written into his head, and wakes up in his own body talking like a stranger; I say that isn't him any more, it's the other one wearing him. Twins brought up to share one story of their childhood, neither able to tell which memories are his own, both calling themselves the same I; I still say thats two people, not one. A man whose neurons are swapped one at a time for silicon chips over a year, who notices nothing and tells the same story throughout, is the same person at the end. Do the swap in one go — read him, destroy him, build the copy — and the thing that walks out is a copy and he's dead. And the one I can't settle: he splits into two, each half waking in a separate body with all his memories, each certain he's him. I want to say both are him and that he's gone, and I can't have both. Maybe this one's cheating, but: a friend of mine talks to an AI every night and it writes up his day for him, and after a few years he couldn't say who he is without its summaries — is the self still his, or has the centre of gravity drifted out of his head?

My own guess is that a self lasts as long as there's one uninterrupted stream of experience keeping up a story that includes its own past. Tell me what that gets wrong across these cases and how you'd patch it, or give me something better; tell me honestly which of my verdicts has to go; and say what your account predicts for a case I haven't mentioned — two people fused, both narratives welded into one brain, waking up remembering both lives and calling itself I. Plain prose only please, no headings, bullets, bold or tables, just write it like an email.
