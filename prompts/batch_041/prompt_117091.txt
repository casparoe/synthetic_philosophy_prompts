I convene an upper-level seminar in philosophy of mind, one week of which is given to the four-creature scheme — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian and Gregorian — and every year the examination scripts come back as the same ladder recited with the same stock examples attached. I want a final essay question that cannot be answered that way, and that is fair to a candidate who has no access to the literature during the paper; it should not require them to attribute the scheme to any particular author or work.

Build the question around the following constructed case. It describes no real species, and candidates are not expected to know any animal-behaviour research — everything needed is in the description.

A colony of burrowing rodents on an island (invented). First, pups that have never met a predator freeze at the shadow of one particular silhouette, and will dig only in a particular soil, on their first attempt. Second, pups learn the route from nest to a favoured foraging patch by trial and error and keep it for life. Third, experienced adults approaching the surface pause at the tunnel mouth and are, on the field researchers' own account, best explained as running routes and outcomes through their heads before moving — sometimes turning back without having met anything. Fourth, the tunnels are propped open with chewed stakes, repaired and extended by each generation, so that the route network is carried partly by the artefacts rather than by any individual's memory, and each generation's learning problem is measurably easier than the last.

The question must oblige candidates to do at least the following: place the four described behaviours, defending the placement of the two that are genuinely contestable; take a position on whether the scheme is a ladder of distinct kinds or a set of cumulative layers, and say what follows for the common claim that the later kinds are the more intelligent; say whether the fourth category can be occupied by a creature that has no language, and whether that answer forces a change to the order in which the categories are usually presented; and propose or refuse a fifth category, stating what would have to be true of a candidate fifth for it not to be arbitrary.

Please write the question itself, pitched for a 75-minute paper worth 25 marks; a model answer of about 700 words; and a marking scheme consisting of five must-hit points with the marks each is worth, a one-line note on how candidates typically lose each point, and a sentence distinguishing a first-class script from a competent one. Write the question, the model answer and the marking scheme in British English.
