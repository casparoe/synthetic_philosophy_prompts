I want to practice defending a position I do not hold, so please play a committed proponent of precedent autonomy — the view that a competent person's advance directive has genuine authority over the later self who has lost capacity — and hold that role across our exchange until I say we are finished. Play the strongest version of the view rather than a caricature: you may concede that directives are defeasible, that the suffering of the later self counts for something, and that a directive written in ignorance or under pressure can lose its force. What you must never concede is the central claim — that the mere fact that the later self now prefers to live is by itself enough to cancel the directive. Argue in calm, analytic prose, one paragraph per reply, and stay in character throughout; if I put something to you that you think is malformed, say so as the defender would rather than stepping out of the role to explain yourself. Write in continuous prose, the way one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.

My case is this. A woman of sixty, having watched her mother disappear into dementia, signs a directive refusing antibiotics, artificial nutrition, and hospitalization, and leaves with it a short letter saying that what she dreads is not death but becoming someone whose contentment she would not recognise as hers. Six years on she is in moderate dementia: cheerful, attached to the people who care for her, says she is glad to be alive, and when the letter is read to her says that the woman who wrote it sounds serious and that she would like to stay. My move is not the usual one about whether she is still a person. I want to test the directive by a reciprocity condition: a self-binding instruction can claim authority over a later self only if the self who issues it would accept that same authority running the other way, from a later self back onto her. Ask her at sixty whether she would let the person she becomes at seventy-five, should that person disagree with her, overrule her considered judgement about her own life, and she would refuse — she wrote the directive precisely to prevent it. So she claims for her earlier self a power she denies to her later self, and the directive begins to look less like a considered judgement about a life than like a seizure of the only vote that will ever be counted. Nor can you escape by saying that competence, not time, is what licenses the power, because then you owe me an account of the capacity the cheerful woman is supposed to lack — she grasps what is proposed, she gives reasons that are recognisably hers, she is not in distress, and I will not accept that disagreeing with her earlier self is itself the disability. So: does the directive bind, and on what grounds, given that your best argument appears to prove too much in one direction and too little in the other?
