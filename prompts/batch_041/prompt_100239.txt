I'm a novelist, about two-thirds through a book whose whole engine is a precommitment contract — near-future setup where you can sign what my characters keep calling a "lock-in," a legally enforceable arrangement where you hand another person the power to act against your own future wishes. It's a plot device, but the more I write the more I realize I actually have opinions about when these things should hold, and I can't get them to line up, which is starting to feel like a me problem rather than a character problem. So here's me being embarrassingly honest about my gut verdicts, and I want to know what principle, if any, is quietly generating them.

Verdicts: (a) a woman in early recovery signs a lock-in giving her brother the right to freeze her accounts and check her into a facility if she uses — I think he should do it, no question, she doesn't get to wriggle out by saying she's changed her mind that week. (b) Same woman, twelve years clean, steady job, wants the thing torn up — I think it should be torn up; the contract did its job and now it's just a cage. (c) Two friends sign a lock-in agreeing neither will let the other quit the band before a set date, with a money penalty — I think that one's faintly ridiculous and shouldn't be enforced, even though structurally it's the same thing as (a). (d) A character signs away, in advance, her right to refuse a specific medical procedure years down the line — I think the later refusal should win, always, she's the one on the table. (e) A country writes something into its constitution so a future majority can't simply undo it — I think that entrenchment should mostly be respected, which seems to flatly contradict (d). My tentative stab at a principle is that a lock-in binds when the later self's wish is itself the symptom the lock-in was meant to treat (addiction, panic, a moment of weakness) and doesn't bind when the later self is just a different, competent person with different values — but (c) breaks that, since the band-quitter's later wish is a symptom of nothing, and (e) breaks it in the other direction, since entrenchment binds even competent later majorities.

Is there one principle that gets all five, or close? And what does it say about the case I keep circling back to — a character who signs a lock-in about her own end-of-life care before early-onset dementia, then, deep in the fog, wants out? If no single principle saves all five, tell me which of my verdicts I should give up and why, because right now I suspect it's one of the two I feel most sure about, which is annoying. Give it to me as a numbered list of exactly five items, no intro and no wrap-up: the first item is the principle stated as precisely as you can manage, and the other four are the upshots — the case it saves, the case it forces me to abandon, what it predicts for the dementia case, and what it predicts for one more case you make up yourself.
