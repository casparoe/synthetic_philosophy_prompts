I recently read a popular book on decision-making that offers the following capsule summary of Herbert Simon's work:

"Simon's great contribution was to show that the rational-agent ideal at the heart of economics is a fiction. Real people do not maximize; they satisfice — they settle for an option that is good enough rather than the best one available. Bounded rationality is the thesis that we are cognitive misers: since thinking is costly, we take mental shortcuts and accept merely acceptable outcomes, which is why markets are full of predictable errors. Simon's claim was descriptive and deflationary — he was not saying that satisficing is a good way to decide, only that it is what we in fact do — and it cleared the ground for behavioral economics and the heuristics-and-biases program, which showed that these shortcuts generate systematic biases."

I found this persuasive, partly because it lines up with everything I have read about behavioral economics. But I have a nagging sense that the summary is doing something to Simon that Simon would not have signed off on, and I cannot put my finger on what. Please audit it.

Go through the summary claim by claim. For each one, tell me what a careful reader of Simon would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong, being explicit about which technical distinctions the summary collapses — for instance, between a claim about the actor's computational capacities and a claim about the structure of the task environment, or between a descriptive reading and a normative one. I am especially interested in whether satisficing is being characterized as a lapse from optimization rather than as something with its own internal logic, and in what the aspiration level in a satisficing model is supposed to be doing. Then tell me whether the summary's concluding implication — that Simon's project and the heuristics-and-biases program are essentially the same enterprise — follows from anything true in the earlier sentences, or whether it is a separate and questionable claim grafted on at the end.

I would also like the response to flag where its own reasoning is most likely to fail, and to say what evidence or argument would change its verdict on the summary — for instance, what one would have to find in Simon's own writing to conclude that the summary is, after all, substantially fair.
