Construct a case in which attributing a belief to an artificial system is predictively indispensable, yet the attributed content cannot be recovered from the system's machinery. The system must be a real, buildable artifact — a trained model, a controller, a robot — with no consciousness and no capacities ordinary engineering could not supply, and its behaviour must be specified closely enough that a reader could in principle write down its state transitions.

Impose three constraints. First, the attribution must be earned rather than stipulated: treating the system as believing a particular content, together with the goals you attribute to it, must yield at least one prediction about its behaviour that no belief-free model built from the same inputs matches, or matches as cheaply; state that prediction and the alternative that misses it. Second, the internal states that carry the behavioural load must fail a sensitivity test you state explicitly — the attributed content should require responsiveness to some external fact that the internal state is invariant to, or the state should be equally well described as tracking a coarser or differently-carved content. Third, keep the scenario to one paragraph of ordinary prose, and introduce at most one term of your own, which you define.

Then, in the analysis: state the attributed belief precisely, including how coarse or fine-grained its content is; give the verdict of an instrumentalist who holds that an attribution is legitimate just in case it is indispensable for prediction, and the verdict of a representationalist who holds that a system believes that p only if some internal state has p as its content, saying exactly where the two part company on these facts; give the design-stance description of the same system and say what it leaves out; name the single feature of the case that does the work and describe what survives its removal; and give the strongest reply available to a representationalist who resists your verdict, noting whether that reply costs them their general position. End by saying which consideration would move you to the opposite verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
