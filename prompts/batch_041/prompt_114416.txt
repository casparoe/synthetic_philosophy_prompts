Everytime my screenwriting group argues about adapting real people we get about three moves in and start repeating ourselves, and I'd like a diagnosis instead of another round. My line is that when you dramatize somebody who's dead — put words in their mouth, invent a scene that makes them look a certain way — you've done something to them, and 'it's only fiction' doesn't get you off the hook, because the audience isn't watching it as fiction. Two of them say the dead are just gone, you can't wrong someone who isn't there, and the thing we actually owe is to the living: the family, the community, the audience's grip on what happened. We've been having this fight for a year and nobody's moved, and one of us keeps saying the dead have no rights, which I think is a slogan doing alot of unearned work. My suspicion is that we're not having one argument. There's a semantics question in there — does a dramatization assert anything, so can it misrepresent the way a history book can? — plus a question about whether posthumous reputation is even the kind of thing that can be an interest of someone who no longer exists, and maybe a third thing that's really about who owns a story, which sounds like a different question wearing the same coat. So which is it: a verbal dispute about 'fiction' and 'owe', a substantive moral disagreement, two questions under one name, or a hidden empirical claim about what audiences actually take from a 'true story'? And whatever the diagnosis is, what would move it — a distinction, a case, some evidence, or nothing at all?
