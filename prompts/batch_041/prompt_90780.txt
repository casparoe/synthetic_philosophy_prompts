I'm opening a two-day workshop on thought experiments in science in a couple weeks and I've left the opening remarks way too late, so: help. Thirty people, half of them working physicists and chemists who use thought experiments constantly and get itchy around philosophy, half philosophers of science who've read all the standard stuff and will be irritated if I act like its new. Ten minutes, spoken, no slides, and please no reading list — everyone in that room already has one and it would just be me showing off.

The question the workshop is about is how a thought experiment gives you knowledge in the first place — whether it's really just an argument wearing a picture, or whether the imagining itself is doing evidential work. My plan is to open by actually running one on the room instead of describing one. The case I want to use: shrink a human being down to about a millimetre tall and put them in a swimming pool. Try to picture it properly — not a tiny person-shaped doll bobbing there, but the actual scenario, a person at that size in water. It falls apart. They'd weigh a billionth as much but their skin would only have a millionth the area, so surface tension and drag would be about a thousand times stronger relative to their weight; they'd be stuck in the water like it was glue, and they'd lose heat about a thousand times faster than they make it, compared to now. The picture keeps collapsing into either a dead frozen speck or a normal person who just happens to be small, which is the incoherent Hollywood version.

Heres the provocation I want to build the whole thing around: maybe the imagining failing *is* the point. Maybe what a thought experiment teaches you rides on the shape of the collapse — the specific way the picture breaks tells you something about the world — rather than on any successful imagining. I don't want to defend that in ten minutes and I definitely don't want to pretend I've settled whether thought experiments are just arguments. I want the room to feel the failure happen to them, live, and then spend two days fighting about what it means.

So write the remarks. They have to bring the scientists along without doing philosophy at them, and not be naive enough to annoy the philosophers, and they have to actually get thirty people to try the imagining and notice it breaking — that's the whole trick and I honestly don't know if it's pull-off-able in a room. End by handing over to the first speaker. And I'd rather it sound like me talking than like a paper.
