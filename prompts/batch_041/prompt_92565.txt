Our office vets emergency public-health measures for compliance with rights law, and the hardest cases are those in which the measure that would save the most lives is also one that confines, conscripts, or requisitions particular people in order to protect everyone else. We resolve these case by case, and I want something we can actually run.

Write me a decision procedure for such proposals: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates in a refusal, and where it hands the decision to a named official because what remains is a question of values rather than of fact. Ground the procedure in the paradox of deontology — the objection that a constraint forbidding violations of rights as a means of minimizing rights violations is self-defeating by its own lights — and have it state explicitly whether it is absolutist or threshold-based; if threshold-based, what sets the threshold other than the sheer size of the numbers. It should say whether it turns on the distinction between harm used as a means and harm accepted as a side-effect, and why. It must also keep separate what each official may do from what we collectively should do, since a single recommendation binds a whole ministry, and it must mark for each measure who is used and who bears the cost.

Run it on three proposals: requisitioning a pharmaceutical firm's patent to make a drug affordable; detaining one person past the statutory limit as leverage to prevent an attack; and understating a risk to the public in order to prevent panic. Include at least one case whose outcome I will not like, say where the procedure will misfire, and say what it cannot decide.
