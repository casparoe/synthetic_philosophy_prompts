I'm putting together a grad seminar on the metaphysics of time for next term, and I keep getting hung up on a stretch of McTaggart that everyone else seems to skate past. Here's the passage, from "The Unreality of Time" (Mind, 1908) — punctuation as printed:

It would, I suppose, be universally admitted that time involves change. A particular thing, indeed, may exist unchanged through any amount of time. But when we ask what we mean by saying that there were different moments of time, or a certain duration of time, through which the thing was the same, we find that we mean that it remained the same while other things were changing. A universe in which nothing whatever changed (including the thoughts of the conscious beings in it) would be a timeless universe.

If, then, a B series without an A series can constitute time, change must be possible without an A series. Let us suppose that the distinction of past, present and future does not apply to reality. Can change apply to reality? What is it that changes?

Could we say that, in a time which formed a B series but not an A series, the change consisted in the fact that an event ceased to be an event, while another event began to be an event? If this were the case, we should certainly have got a change.

But this is impossible. An event can never cease to be an event. It can never get out of any time series in which it once is.

The A series / B series machinery I can teach in my sleep, and the later regress argument is at least a clean piece of clockwork. What I can't get a grip on is this middle move. McTaggart asks whether change is possible without an A series, considers exactly one candidate — events beginning and ceasing to be events — kills it off on the grounds that an event can't get out of a time series it's once in, and then acts as though the matter is settled. But that's not the only account of A-series-free change going: the usual B-theorist line is that change just is a thing having different properties at different times, the poker hot at one date and cold at another, which needs no events popping in and out of being and no privileged present. So is McTaggart's dismissal a real argument against that view, or is he quietly helping himself to something eternalist-flavoured — events as timelessly existing items there to be dated — which a B-theorist would cheerfully grant, and which therefore can't be used to show that the B series alone won't do the work of time? I want to use this as a wedge into the presentism/eternalism fight, so I need to know exactly where the pressure is.

What I'd like from you: walk me through what that passage is actually up to, and tell me how much of it is argument and how much is verbal manoeuvre. Specifically — (1) what is "an event can never cease to be an event" supposed to mean, and what does the sentence commit him to about what events are? (2) Does the argument engage the property-variation account of change at all, or does it slide past it? (3) If it slides past, is there a repair available on his behalf, or does everything end up resting on the A-series regress further down the paper? Lay the steps out so I can see which premise is load-bearing. I don't need a tour of the secondary literature or a reading list; I want the joints visible.

One more thing, and I mean this seriously: seperate what's actually settled here from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this. My hunch is that a few of these points — whether B-theoretic change deserves the name, whether McTaggart's "event" is a technical notion doing illicit work, whether he's entitled to his opening premise about time and change — are places where the confident textbook story is one party's line dressed up as consensus. Flag which is which, and if the reading I'm pushing turns out to be a minority view, say so plainly rather than smoothing it over.
