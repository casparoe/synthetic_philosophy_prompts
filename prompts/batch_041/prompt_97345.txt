I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for a long time, and I keep running into a pattern I can't name. I'd like you to tell me whether it has an established name, and — more importantly — to carve it precisely, because a bare label won't help me.

The pattern turns up in two places I would have sworn had nothing to do with each other.

First case. A man, still sharp, signs an advance directive refusing artificial life support if he becomes severely demented. Years later he is severely demented and, by all appearances, content: he eats, he recognizes his grandchildren, he does not seem to be suffering. His family now has to decide whether to follow the directive. Some say the man who signed it is the real one, and the person in the bed is in no position to release him from his own instruction; others say the person who asked for this is gone and the person who is here wants to live. The striking part is that the first camp describes itself as respecting his autonomy, while using a preference that no longer has a bearer to override a preference that does.

Second case. A woman in her twenties resolves, in writing, never to have children, and tells everyone. At forty she changes her mind and has a child, and nobody — including her — treats the earlier resolution as having any authority. She says she was a different person then, and we agree.

My first pass was: the difference is competence. We honor the earlier self exactly when the later self cannot revoke. But that gets the cases backwards. In the dementia case the later self is the one who cannot contest the instruction, and that is precisely when we are most tempted to honor the earlier one; in the child case the later self is fully competent, and we let her overrule the earlier one without a second thought. So competence is not doing the work.

My second pass was: the earlier self wins when its preference reflects something deeper and more stable than the later self's passing state. But I can't say what makes a preference deep without begging the question, and in the child case the twenty-year-old would have said her resolution was exactly that.

What I keep circling is this. We talk as though the earlier self has authority over the later self — as though a person can bind herself across time, and the binding runs one way, from past to future. But when I look at the cases, the earlier preference seems to function less as an authority and more as a proxy: we let it stand in for the later self's own preference precisely when the later self cannot be asked, or when we distrust the answer. And the moment the later self can answer for herself, the earlier authority evaporates. If that is right, then the whole notion of an earlier self binding a later self may be a fiction we invoke to avoid saying what we are really doing.

I suspect this is near the debates people have about advance directives, about personal identity over time, and about self-binding promises, but I don't know whether there is one name for the structure, or whether what I am pointing at is actually two or three different things wearing a single coat. If there is an established term, I want it, and I want the exact respect in which my description outruns it. If there isn't, I want a coinage with a definition I can test.

Please structure the reply as follows, and do not skip the boundary cases — the boundary cases are the whole point.

Closest existing names (roughly 200 words): the concepts my pattern is nearest to, what each one captures, and the precise respect in which my pattern is not that concept. No citations or reading lists.

The carving (roughly 350 words): a definition of the phenomenon, stated so that it covers both of my cases and excludes at least three near-misses, with a boundary case for each exclusion.

The deciding principle (roughly 250 words): what actually separates the cases where the earlier preference binds from the cases where it does not, if anything does; if nothing does, say so and say what that implies.

The name (roughly 100 words): the established term if there is one, or your coinage if there is not, plus one sentence on why the label earns its keep.
