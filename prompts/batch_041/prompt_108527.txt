So I'm a reporter who's spent most of the last year covering a long, ugly local fight over a supervised drug consumption site in a mid-sized city — public health people and harm reduction folks on one side, a coalition of church groups and some neighborhood associations on the other — and what's stuck with me is that nobody on either side thinks of themselves as negotiating. Both camps talk as if the other side's position is just a preference, something you could trade, while their own is a commitment, something you can't. I've been reading alot of philosophy on the side trying to make sense of it, and I've ended up half-persuaded by a view I don't actually hold: that moral disagreement between groups is best modeled as a bargaining problem, where each side gives up something it cares about in exchange for something it cares about more, and any deal both sides prefer to no deal is a genuine moral improvement — not a sellout, not a betrayal, just gains from trade.

I want to practice arguing against that, so please play someone who holds it, and holds it well. Not a caricature: someone who's already thought hard about the objection that moral commitments aren't tradeable, who will grant that some things really aren't up for sale, but who insists the space of legitimate compromise is way bigger than the romantics think, and who can tell me what makes the difference. Stay in character until I say 'stop'. Keep replies to a paragraph or so, so it feels like a conversation rather than a lecture; concede a small point when I land one, but don't give up the main claim.

Here's my opening. The trade analogy seems to need a common scale — some exchange rate between, say, the overdoses I think I'm preventing and the offense and disorder I think I'm causing. But my objection to the site isn't a quantity of something I'd like more or less of; it's that I think it's wrong. If I trade it away for a bigger police budget, I haven't just gotten less of what I wanted, I've agreed to something I think is wrong, and the extra funding doesn't wash that out. So what exactly is supposed to be changing hands here — and if there's a remainder that never gets absorbed by the deal, who ends up carrying it?
