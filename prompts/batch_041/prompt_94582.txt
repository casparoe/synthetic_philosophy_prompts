Ok so I'm a novelist — four books, the kind where not much happens but everyone thinks alot — and this question has been eating at me while I should be working on the fifth.

Somewhere in philosophy there's a principle that if you know something, then you know that you know it. And there's a whole argument that no mental condition is luminous — that being in a state doesn't put you in a position to know you're in it. I might have the name wrong (luminosity? could be something else) and I definately can't tell you who came up with it or in which book, but I'm pretty sure I've got the shape of both right.

Here's what I want. Take those two things — the knowing-that-you-know thing and the anti-luminosity thing — and restate them as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually think in: POV, first person vs close third, free indirect discourse, interiority, showing vs telling, subtext, dramatic irony, unreliable narration, the difference between what the narration knows and what the character knows. That vocabulary is my whole job; I'm fluent in it the way you're fluent in yours. I'm a tourist in your vocabulary, so don't meet me halfway — come all the way over. And don't cheat by quietly converting my terms back into yours and calling that a translation; if something doesn't fit, say it doesn't fit.

Then the part I actually care about: what didn't survive the trip. Which of those epistemic claims maps onto something I can build into a sentence, which ones I'd have to fudge, which have no home in my vocabulary at all. Two things I'm suspicious about specifically.

First, the anti-luminosity argument as I understand it turns on margin for error — you can be in a state without being able to rule out being in some nearby, slightly different state, so you can't be sure you're in it. In fiction I can just stipulate. If I want a character who knows exactly what he feels, the reader goes along with it; nobody demands his epistemic warrant. So is there any narrative analogue of the margin-for-error structure, or is that piece of the argument exactly the piece my vocabulary can't hold? And if it can't hold it — is my vocabulary impoverished, or is that piece doing work that only matters to people who care about knowledge rather than about the truth of character?

Second: the only way I know how to write someone not knowing his own mind is motivation. He's jealous and can't admit it. He's in love and calls it a stomach ache. He's grieving and calls it the flu. That's denial, self-deception, driven by what he wants to be true. The anti-luminosity stuff seems to say the opacity is structural — no desire required, everyone like that about everything all the time. Same claim in different clothes, or two different claims? And if they're different, does my version do any of the work yours does, or have I just built a psychological explanation where you built a logical one?

Smaller thing I keep tripping over: the knowing-that-you-know thing stacks. Knows that he knows that he knows. But the natural device in a novel isn't a stack, it's a shift — a line that reads as narration but is really the character's thought, so the reader can hear that it's his and he can't. I call it the leak, because that's what it is, his mind leaking into the voice telling the story. Does the leak capture the iteration, or does it only look like it does? I have a feeling what I do is closer to ventriloquism than to knowing-about-knowing, and that your side would say I'm cheating.

Last thing, plainly: if the residue turns out to be big, whose problem is that? I want to know whether I'm missing a distinction that matters, or whether they've dressed up something I already do better. Don't be nice about it.
