You are an expert in the philosophy of medicine and biology, with a specialization in the metaphysics of death and the ethics of organ procurement. I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy preparing a prospectus for a two-year dissertation, and I need your help turning a half-formed idea into a rigorous proposal. Please think carefully and work through the conceptual terrain before you commit to a framing; where a term is ambiguous, say so and disambiguate it rather than papering over it.

My half-formed idea is this. The long-running dispute between the whole-brain criterion and the circulatory criterion is usually presented as a disagreement about biology -- which function, when it ceases, marks the death of the organism. I suspect it is at least as much a disagreement about which of two incompatible jobs the concept of death is being asked to do at once. On one reading, "dead" is a metaphysical or biological status: the organism has ceased to exist as an integrated whole. On another, "dead" is a normative gate: the point at which it becomes permissible to stop treatment, to withdraw support, or to procure organs. Contemporary practice seems to require these two roles to coincide, and I want to know whether that requirement is a discovery about the world or a stipulation that keeps generating contradictions. The recent controversies over protocols that restore circulation to organs in situ after a declaration of circulatory death, and over bodies that are maintained on ventilators for years after a brain-death determination, strike me as places where the fusion of the two roles shows its seams. I am also struck by the fact that every candidate criterion has to fix a threshold somewhere along a continuum, and I cannot tell whether such a threshold is something we discover or something we legislate.

Turn this into a proposal with the following parts. First, state the research question so that it has a determinate answer -- I do not want a question that can be deflected with "it depends on your definition." Second, explain why the existing positions leave the question open, including what the standard biological, metaphysical, and clinical accounts each assume without arguing. Third, set out the method: what I would actually do, month by month, and why conceptual and case-based work is the right approach here rather than empirical or historical research. Fourth, say what a successful result would look like -- what would count as having shown that the two roles can be separated, or that they cannot -- and what would count as the project failing. Fifth, give an honest assessment of scope: is this two years of work, or is it either trivial or a career? Also address one obvious objection: that distinguishing the metaphysical from the normative role of "death" merely relocates the puzzle rather than dissolving it, and say how the project would respond.

Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; refer to positions and arguments by describing them, and I will supply the references myself. Write in a formal register, and begin by brainstorming two or three competing ways to frame the central question before selecting one and building the proposal around it.
