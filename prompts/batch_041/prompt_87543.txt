A growing number of public institutions now route consequential conversations through machine translation with no human interpreter in the loop: immigration officers run preliminary asylum screenings through a translation app, hospitals hand patients discharge instructions produced by software, police read suspects their rights from an automatically generated translation. In each case someone has decided the output is good enough to act on; and in each case the same agency that certifies human interpreters by testing their comprehension accepts the machine's output on the strength of nothing but how it reads. Treat that practice—not any particular vendor's system—as the thing to be explained, and tell me what it presupposes. Does acting on machine output commit the institution to a view on which meaning is a property of strings, transferable across languages by matching them to one another, so that understanding what a sentence means is separable from producing an equivalent sentence in another? Or can the practice be defended by someone who thinks translation just is the production of an equivalent text and requires no grasp of meaning at all, whether the translator is human or machine? Which theories of meaning does the practice quietly rule out, and which does it leave open? I am especially interested in the gap between the everyday case, where a garbled rendering is harmless, and the consequential case, where it can cost someone their liberty or their health: is the institution's confidence here a claim about the technology, or a claim about meaning? Reconstruct the implicit theory of meaning and translation, say whether it is coherent and whether the practice actually requires it, and tell me what would have to change if we adopted a view on which understanding is indispensable to translation.
