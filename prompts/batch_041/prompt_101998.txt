For the past two years I have served as the student recorder on my high school's Standards Committee, the body that hears cases in which a student is accused of breaking a school norm and decides whether to impose a sanction. I keep the minutes, and unofficially I keep a spreadsheet of how each member votes. The member I find most interesting is Jonah, a senior who has become our swing vote: several decisions have turned on him alone. Next week the committee hears a case I expect he will find difficult, and I would like to predict his vote, and, more than that, to understand the rule he has actually been applying.

Our civics teacher once handed out a page distinguishing three ways a culture can be "tight," and I have been testing Jonah's record against them. A norm can be tightly written, meaning it is codified, posted, and announced; tightly consequential, meaning that breaking it would unravel some practice the community actually depends on; or tightly shared, meaning the members themselves hold it, expect one another to follow it, and do the enforcing. I cannot tell which of these Jonah is tracking, because in most of our cases they have moved together.

Here is his record.

1. Phones in the exam room. The prohibition is printed in every exam room and restated before each exam. It protects the integrity of the exam, which everyone relies on, and students will shush one another if someone reaches for a phone. A student was caught with one. Jonah voted to sanction.

2. The seniors' table. Nothing about it is written anywhere, but the front table in the cafeteria has long been understood to be for seniors. A sophomore sat there once. No practice depends on it, students do not defend it, and the administration has never commented on it. Jonah voted not to sanction.

3. The banned jacket. Our dress code prohibits a particular style of jacket, and the prohibition is posted in the main office. Students regard it as arbitrary, no practice depends on it, and no students enforce it. A student wore one to class. Jonah voted not to sanction.

4. The moment of silence at assemblies. Assemblies begin with thirty seconds of silence. This is written nowhere; the student council introduced it years ago, it works only because everyone observes it, and students themselves object when someone talks. A student talked through it. Jonah voted to sanction.

5. The bus queue. Students formed a queue at the bus stop on their own, and it functions only because everyone holds to it; there is no posted rule, and students enforce it themselves. A student cut the line. Jonah voted to sanction.

Next week's case. This term the administration, without consulting the student council, posted and announced a new rule for the main corridor: students must keep to the right and must not stop to talk, on the grounds that congestion there has genuinely been making students late to class. The congestion is real, and the rule addresses it. Students, however, consider the rule pointless; the student council voted against it, and there is no student enforcement at all. In fact, some students walk on the left deliberately. A student was written up for walking on the left while talking with a friend, and that morning no one was actually blocked or late.

I would like you to tell me, first, how Jonah will vote and why. Second, I would like the set of rules that fit everything above, including rules I have not thought of, along with what each one predicts about next week's case. Third, I would like your best guess at the rule he is really running, and your confidence in it. Fourth, tell me which of the five earlier cases is the closest analog to the new one, and be honest about whether the new case straddles two of them rather than matching either. Fifth, tell me which single further case would best tell the surviving rules apart, and what each rule predicts about it. Finally, if the committee follows the reading you favor, what principle is it committing itself to about how much rule-following our school should demand, and what would it be committing itself to under the main alternative?
