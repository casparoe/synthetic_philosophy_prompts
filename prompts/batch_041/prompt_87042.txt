I am a novelist, midway through a book set among a Chinese family that has been in Manchester for three generations, and the central chapter turns on a funeral. I want the rites to be right without the scene reading like an anthropology seminar: the paper money and the paper house going into the flames; the food laid out at the graveside; the wailing that the older women can switch on and off; the coat held up and the name called three times, to bring the soul back before it strays too far; and the family's brisk consent, the moment he was pronounced, to the removal of his corneas. A friend who read the draft made a remark that has lodged in me. She said that the whole performance assumes the dead man is still there — that he can be called, fed, housed and supplied with cash — while the same people, in the same afternoon, speak of him as gone, comfort one another that he is past suffering, and say the rites are for the living; and she suspected they would be embarrassed if anyone put the question to them directly. I would like you to work out what the practice actually commits them to. Does provisioning the dead presuppose that they persist in some sense and can be affected by what we do — and if so, what manner of persistence, and what sort of thing is it that can go hungry or run short of money? Or can the practice get along without any such belief, with the offerings understood as expressions of grief, as a way of continuing a relationship that death has not ended, or as a technique for reshaping the mourners' own feelings? Tell me which parts of the practice strictly presuppose a view of the dead, which rival views it quietly rules out, which merely sit comfortably alongside it, and where the practice is so underdetermined that a dozen incompatible conceptions of death would all license the same funeral.

The second thing that troubles me concerns Zhuangzi, whom I have been reading with more enthusiasm than understanding. What draws me is his refusal of the whole arrangement: the story of his singing and drumming on a tub when his wife died, and his instruction, when his own end came, that his body be left out for the birds and the ants rather than buried with ceremony, on the grounds that he would feed one creature or another either way and that preferring the ants to the birds was mere favouritism. Yet I have a nagging sense that he is not simply the enemy of ceremony my youngest character wants him to be. I have a scene in mind in which that character, who has taken Zhuangzi to heart, stands at the graveside unable to decide whether to wail or to laugh, having concluded both that the rites are a fiction and that refusing them would be a rudeness to a man who is not there to be affronted. So I would like you to take the analysis further. If the family genuinely adopted the Zhuangzian view — that death is one more turn of things, and the dead are past being harmed or helped — what would be left of the funeral? Would the rites survive as pure expression, as a courtesy with no metaphysics behind it, or does the practice depend on the belief in a way that would make it collapse? And is there a coherent position in which one performs the rites wholeheartedly while holding that the dead are beyond need and beyond injury, or does that slide into doing something elaborate for a person one takes not to exist? Please give me your reconstruction in British English, and end by defending whichever reading of the practice you think is best.
