I've spent a while trying to get straight on the factored cognition picture of deliberation, and I think I've basically talked myself into believing it, which makes me nervous. So talk me out of it. What would count against it?

Here's the version I believe. Take any hard question that has to be deliberated about. The bet is that it can be broken into subquestions small enough that the same kind of process can answer each one, and — this is the part I care about — that the breakdown is checkable: you can tell whether an answer to a subquestion is any good without having already solved the whole thing. Then a deliberator just is that tree, and better deliberation means a bigger tree with better leaves, not a different kind of act. The ideal limit is HCH: take a human, swap the human out for something that models that human's values (an inverse-reinforcement-learning sort of step), then let the result be amplified — allowed to consult itself on the subparts — and take the fixed point. On this picture, what should be done is whatever the fixed point says, and the interesting thing about a mind isn't its unity but its decomposition.

What I want from you:

1. What does this predict that a rival picture doesn't — say one where good judgment is holistic, or where the unity of the agent is doing the real work? I want things that are actually testable-ish, not just nicer-sounding.
2. Which objections should move me and which are just grumbling? Sort them from "this should make me drop the view" to "awkward but survivable."
3. What would someone who already holds this view concede counts against them? I don't want only the criticisms that come from outside.
4. The assumptions above — decomposability, checkability, subtasks being easier than the whole — are those empirical claims that could fail, or have they been quietly turned into definitions, so that nothing could count against the picture? And if it's the latter, is that actually a problem for an idealization like this, or is that just what idealizations are? Related: is this even an account of deliberation, or just a claim that deliberation is a tree-shaped computation, in which case what work is the word "deliberation" doing?

The thing that would move me most is a case where deliberation clearly goes right and can't be factored — where the subparts only make sense in light of the whole, or where you can only tell whether a sub-answer is right once you already have the whole answer. If you think such cases exist, give me them concretely; if you think they're all just badly chosen decompositions, say that too. And be honest about whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — if it has, tell me whether that's fatal for this kind of picture or fine. Also, can you seperate out the objections that are really about amplification from the ones that are about factoring in general? I suspect a couple of them get run together. No need for citations, I'm just trying to think this through.
