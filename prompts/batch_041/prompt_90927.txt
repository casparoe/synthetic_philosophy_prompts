A functionalist about moral status holds that what makes a system a proper object of moral concern is not the material it is made of but the computation it implements: get the right kind of information processing in the right causal organization, and you have a system whose experiences count. The view is attractive and, I think, underspecified in a way that matters, because the question whether a given system implements a computation of the right kind is not obviously a question with a description-independent answer. Any physical system can be described so as to implement almost any finite transition structure; and even setting that triviality aside, a system's causal structure can be carved into components and interactions at more than one grain, and what computation gets attributed to the system depends on the carving. I want a thought experiment that isolates this second pressure and makes it bear on moral status. The point of the case is to test whether the number and identity of the moral patients in a system can be settled without appealing to facts about the describer.

Construct the case to the following specifications.

1. There is a single physical system, S, and two ways of partitioning its causal structure: a coarser partition P1 and a finer partition P2. Nothing else varies. Both are descriptions of the same causal facts, and no physical difference may be smuggled in to favor one.

2. Under P1, S implements a single computation of the mind-like kind at issue, so that S is one moral patient. Under P2, the same causal facts decompose into two computations of that kind, each internally integrated and each causally insulated from the other at the level of P2, so that S is two moral patients. State each partition precisely: what the components are, what causal interactions hold among them, and how the two partitions are related.

3. Both partitions must be equally natural, by a criterion you state and defend. In particular, each must cut along perturbation-robust causal seams: the components of each partition must survive small perturbations of the substrate, rather than existing only at one instant or only under one arbitrarily chosen mapping. If your naturalness criterion in fact favors one partition, say so, and explain why that does not beg the question.

4. The moral stakes must differ in kind and not merely in number. What promotes the welfare of the single patient under P1 must set back at least one of the two patients under P2, and the two under P2 must not have interests that are simply the halves of the one under P1.

5. No science fiction: no uploading, teleportation, simulation, or exotic physics; and do not transcribe a ready-made clinical case — construct the system.

6. Do not stipulate the verdicts. They must follow from the views as stated.

Then do the analysis. First, say what the realist reading of implementation — the view that whether S implements a given computation, and hence how many patients S contains, is a mind-independent fact about S — must say here, and whether it can select between P1 and P2 on grounds that do not covertly appeal to the describer's interests. Second, say what the attributional reading — the view that which computation is implemented is fixed only relative to a choice of components, interactions, and mapping — must say, and what that implies for moral status: is patiency description-relative, and if so is that tolerable or a reductio? Third, identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and say what happens when it is removed: does the case collapse into the triviality about arbitrary mappings, or into a merely verbal dispute about what counts as one system, or does it survive intact?

Finish with the strongest available line for the realist: the best non-arbitrary way to break the tie between P1 and P2 without appealing to our interests. Then say honestly whether that line works or merely relocates the problem.
