I'm an undergraduate enrolled in a course on the ethics of deception, and I've run into a passage I can't get straight. In lecture we were given the distinction between lying and merely misleading: a lie is a statement you believe to be false, whereas misleading someone — answering with a true but carefully selected set of facts, letting a false impression stand, exploiting an implicature — need not involve asserting anything you take to be false, and the live question is whether the second is really any less wrong than the first. Then we were assigned a thirteenth-century text that seems to cut across the whole picture. Here is the passage, from Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 110, a. 1, in the translation our course uses:

"Accordingly if these three things concur, namely, falsehood of what is said, the will to tell a falsehood, and finally the intention to deceive, then there is falsehood—materially, since what is said is false, formally, on account of the will to tell an untruth, and effectively, on account of the will to impart a falsehood. However, the essential notion of a lie is taken from formal falsehood, from the fact namely, that a person intends to say what is false... Consequently if one says what is false, thinking it to be true, it is false materially, but not formally, because the falseness is beside the intention of the speaker so that it is not a perfect lie... If, on the other hand, one utters falsehood formally, through having the will to deceive, even if what one says be true, yet inasmuch as this is a voluntary and moral act, it contains falseness essentially and truth accidentally, and attains the specific nature of a lie."

I can follow the vocabulary of matter, form, and effect, but I can't see how the pieces are supposed to fit, and the last sentence appears to say the opposite of what I would have predicted.

Please help me work through it. I'd like answers to these, in order, and I'm not asking for a reading list or citations — I want the argument reconstructed:

1. What exactly is "formal falsehood" doing here, and is the criterion about what the speaker believes to be false, about what he intends to say, or about what he intends to bring about in his hearer?
2. On this account, is the intention to deceive necessary for lying, or only for a "perfect" lie? What turns on the distinction between the species of an act and its "perfection"?
3. Why should a true statement made with the will to deceive count as a lie? What sense can be given to the claim that such an act "contains falseness essentially and truth accidentally," and is this a terminological stipulation or something an argument forces?
4. Where do the modern cases land? Take a literally true but deliberately incomplete answer, and a true sentence chosen because I know you will draw a false conclusion from it. Would he call either a lie? If not, does he have any other resource — the virtue of truth, the notion of dissimulation in the next article, which seems to treat misleading by outward deeds as itself a lie while treating the man who merely refrains from saying what he is as not lying — for condemning it?
5. What about the reverse case: a false statement made to someone I know will not believe me, with no intention of deceiving anyone? Is it a lie for him, and is it a sin?
6. Finally, does this framework support or undermine the claim that misleading is morally better than lying? It looks to me as though it makes the verdict turn on the speaker's will rather than on the truth-value of the words, which is not what the lecture's distinction does. Is he tracking a different question from the one we were given, or is there a way to read him as answering ours?
