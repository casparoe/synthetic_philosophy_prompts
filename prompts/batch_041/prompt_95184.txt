I sit on the critical-care resource allocation committee at the hospital where I work, and over the past few months — through two cases I would rather not have been part of — I have arrived at a position I did not expect to hold: when we can save one patient or several and cannot save them all, the fact that there are more of them should not by itself decide the matter, and the right procedure is a lottery among those who can be saved. I have agreed to present this at a regional meeting on crisis standards of care, and what I want from you is a question-period prep sheet, not a draft of the talk.

The position I will defend has two levels, and I expect the pressure to fall on the joint between them. At the level of the individual decision, I claim that a saving decision is legitimate only if it can be justified to each claimant taken one at a time, with the number of other claimants bracketed out of that justification — being one of five rather than one of two is not a consideration that I, as a claimant, could offer or be moved by. At the level of institutional design I concede that numbers do real work: we should count expected deaths when we decide how many beds to fund, and count lives saved when we audit a protocol after the fact. What I deny is that this second-level counting can be carried back into the first-level choice, and that denial is the part of my view I am least confident I can defend.

The room will be about twelve people. Seven are practising intensivists and emergency physicians who know triage protocols intimately and philosophy hardly at all. Two are clinical ethicists with philosophy training who know this literature well and regard my conclusion as close to indefensible. One is hospital counsel, who will care about whether anything I propose could survive review. One is the family representative on our committee, who has watched these decisions from the receiving end. One is a health economist who reasons in quality-adjusted life-years and will hear my position as a refusal to face arithmetic.

Prepare me for the question period: the six or seven objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that particular objector would phrase it and attributed to them, each with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and an honest note on where that reply is soft or where I should simply concede. Tell me what to concede at the outset and what has to be held. Name the weakest point in my own position, the one I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. And tell me the question I am hoping nobody asks.
