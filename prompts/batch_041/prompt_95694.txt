I'm writing a long feature on Richard Rorty's afterlife in American intellectual life, and the passage below — drafted by a colleague whose reading I generally trust — is meant to introduce neopragmatism to readers who have never opened him. Before it goes to fact-checking I'd like a sentence-by-sentence audit rather than a general verdict, because my suspicion is that it is mostly right in a way that would be hard for a non-specialist to catch.

Rorty's neopragmatism starts from the thought that since Descartes and Locke, philosophy has pictured the mind as a mirror in which the world is represented, and knowledge as the accuracy of the picture. Rorty argues that this picture is incoherent, and that once we drop it we can drop the whole apparatus of correspondence, foundations, and privileged method that goes with it. What remains is justification: inquiry is a social practice in which we justify our beliefs to one another, and 'truth' is simply the honorific we attach to the beliefs our community has decided to keep. He therefore concludes that there are no facts of the matter, only the vocabularies different communities happen to find useful; and since no vocabulary can be shown to fit reality better than any other, the choice among them is a matter of taste or of politics rather than of reason. Politically this becomes the advice that we stop chasing objectivity and settle for solidarity, and that philosophy's job is to keep the conversation going rather than to get anything right. The familiar objection — that the liberal ironist who privatizes her doubts about her own final vocabulary can give no reason for her commitment to avoiding cruelty, and so refutes herself — seems to me decisive.
