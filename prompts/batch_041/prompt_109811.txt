So I'm in this strange reading group — half philosophy grad students, half people from an AI safety org — and two of us have basically stopped speaking to each other over the question of which computations matter morally. Here's the setup. One of them, call him the functionalist, thinks that if a system implements the right kind of computation — the right causal/organizational structure — then whether it's sentient, and so whether it has moral status, is settled by the computation, substrate be damned: silicon, neurons, wetware, doesn't matter, get the organization right and it counts. The other, call her the attribution skeptic, thinks that's close to incoherent, because which computation a physical system implements isn't a fact about the system but something we impose on it: pick your grain of description and the same lump of matter implements anything you like. She says you can't read moral status off a computation, and that the real question is what we're doing when we attribute a computation, or a mind, to something in the first place. They've been going at it for months and its all heat and no light.

What I want from you is the joint statement they could both sign after a proper adversarial collaboration. So give me, first, the claims both can accept: about the framework (what 'implements' would have to mean, what each side is actually assuming), about what's really at stake for moral status, and about what would and wouldn't count as evidence for either side. Then the precise remaining disagreements, each phrased so that the side that holds it would accept the wording — no strawmen on either side. Then the cruxes. I suspect one is whether implementation is objective or interest-relative, and another is whether the grain of description is fixed by the system's causal structure or by us; I'd also like you to work out whether they're really disagreeing about whether the computation has to be *used* by the system or merely *instantiated* in it, because I think that's where half the confusion lives.

One more thing: they keep talking past each other on a concrete case, so make them both commit to it. Take a system that nails every behavioral and functional profile you could want, but is by construction a lookup table — its internal states are just entries indexed by a hash of the input, with no counterfactual structure beyond that (or, if you prefer, a minimal program that regenerates those entries on demand). The functionalist says that's still implementing the computation if the table is fine-grained enough; the skeptic says that's exactly the case that shows implementation-talk doing no work. They both agree the case matters. So write their predictions about it into the document, and tell me which parts of their fight turn out to be verbal — a clash over the words 'computation' or 'sentience' rather than over a fact — and which are genuine. Format: a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table with a column for each crux and a column for each side's prediction on the lookup-table case.
