I'm in a philosophy MA and I'm TAing for a prof whose AI ethics unit I basically run the discussion sections for, and I keep getting into it with my roommate about what 'the alignment problem' even is. He's all Skynet, paperclip maximizer, robots that want to kill us. I've come around to the narrower version: the hard part is getting a system to do what its designer actually meant rather than what they literally said — the genie who grants you exactly what you asked for, King Midas and his golden touch — and that seems like a completely different question from whether the values we'd be baking in are the right ones, or whose values they are. One is a specification and interpretation problem, the other is a moral philosophy problem. A friend of mine in a safety reading group draws the line in three places instead of two: what you said, what you meant, and what you'd have wanted if you'd thought it through properly — and she thinks half the literature slides between the last two without noticing. My roommate says the whole distinction is a distinction without a difference, that you can't pull 'what he meant' apart from 'what he should have wanted', and honestly I don't have a good reply yet. We were arguing about this at a bar until 1am and I've got a presentation on it next week, so I'd like to actually think it through.

So I want to try the distinction out on a film. Ex Machina. I've seen the ending argued both ways: Ava gets out, and either she's a smashing success of alignment — she does precisely what she was built and trained to do, manipulate her way to the door — or she's a failure, because she doesn't give a damn about Caleb. What strikes me is that the film gets us rooting for her and then punishes us for it, and the punishment lands exactly at the moment we notice we never specified anything about Caleb at all. Read the whole film through the intent-alignment / value-alignment split — the sessions, what Nathan says his test is versus what he's actually built, Caleb's part in it, the last twenty minutes — and tell me which of the two problems the film is really about. Is Ava the misaligned one, or is Nathan? Where does it dramatize the narrow problem, and where does it quietly swap in the broad one and make us feel them as the same thing?

And be straight with me about where my distinction is doing real work and where it collapses. If the film shows that a designer's intent isn't available independently of what he ends up endorsing — that Nathan can't tell you what he wanted until he sees what he got — then say so and tell me what that does to the narrow framing, because that's a real answer and I'd rather have it than a save.
