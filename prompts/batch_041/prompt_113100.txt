I teach a 300-level seminar on rationality and the will and I'm putting together the take-home final. I need one essay question on Buridan's ass -- the donkey stuck between two identical haystacks -- that my students can't answer by saying 'well obviously it just picks one' or by reciting the usual bit about how the case is really about free will. Give them 60 minutes, worth 25 marks.

Here's what I want it to do. Make them actually argue about what 'no preference' means and whether choice without preference is even a coherent idea, not just report that some people think it's a paradox. So build the question around a case I've been using in lecture: the ass's two haystacks are indistinguishable to it in every respect it can register, but differ in some tiny respect it can't register (say one is a molecule heavier), and there's a third thing on the table that the textbook versions always leave out -- the ass can also just keep deliberating, or lie down, or stand there and starve. I also want a tie-breaker in there. The ass could consult a coin. I want them to notice that the coin only helps if the ass already prefers following the coin to not following it, and prefers heads=left to heads=right, and that there's no obvious place for that regress of tie-breakers to stop except somewhere arbitrary.

Then make them deal with a principle I'll state in the question and have them assess, something like: an action is rational for an agent only if she has a consideration that favours it over every other action available to her. Does that principle condemn the ass? Does it condemn the coin-flip? Does it condemn just picking the left haystack for no reason at all?

Marking scheme: I want five must-hit points with the marks each is worth (total 25), and one line per point on how students typically blow it. Model answer of about 700-800 words, written at the level of a strong student rather than a textbook. I want the scheme to seperate an A from a B by naming the specific moves the A answer has and the B answer lacks -- not by vagueness about 'depth of understanding'.

Two constraints. First, the question has to be self-contained: no 'discuss with reference to the literature', no citations, nothing they'd need to have read beyond the lecture, because otherwise they just name-drop. Second, keep apart the three things they always run together -- the claim that a person can't act unless some motive tips the balance, the claim that it's irrational to act when nothing tips the balance, and the claim that in a tie all the tied options are rationally on a par. The question should force them to say which one (if any) the ass's starvation actually follows from, and what happens to the case if you deny it.
