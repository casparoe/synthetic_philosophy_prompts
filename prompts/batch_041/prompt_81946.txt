I'm teaching an undergrad unit on minds and machines and want to end on Ex Machina, but I'm sick of the usual 'is she really concious / Chinese room' reading — I think the film is much better read against Dennett's intentional stance, so read it that way for me. The bit of Dennett I want in play: beliefs aren't inner inscriptions you'd find if you opened up the skull, they're more like centers of gravity, real patterns we posit because predicting the thing that way is cheap and works — and once the stance pays off, there's no further fact of the matter about whether the system 'really' believes anything. Now the film. Nathan built Ava, so he always has the design stance available and never needs the intentional one, which may be exactly why he can be so cold to her; Caleb has no such option, and the film seems to punish him for taking the stance (or is it rewarding Ava for exploiting it?). And Nathan's whole company is a search engine that predicts people's behaviour from data — the design stance turned on us. So: does the film vindicate Dennett — she gets out, the stance worked, nothing further to ask — or does the last act, with Caleb locked in the building and Ava at the crossroads, demand something the stance can't give? Where does the film cheat or beg the question, what would Dennett say about that ending, and does the film add anything the arguments don't — especially about wether adopting the stance is a neutral predictive move or already a moral commitment?
