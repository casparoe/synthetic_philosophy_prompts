Last weekend my brother and I got into a stupid, endless arguement about our family's cottage. It burned down in 2011, we rebuilt it on the same foundation reusing a lot of the old beams, and now we're trying to decide whether to sell it. He says it's not really the same cottage anymore, so selling isn't a betrayal of grandpa; I say it obviously is the same cottage, which is exactly why selling is a betrayal. We went round in circles for an hour.

Then our friend Dan, who did a philosophy degree, told us we were having a "verbal dispute" and sent me a podcast episode about it. His summary was roughly this: there's a whole field called metaontology, which is about whether questions like this are real questions or just questions about words. Carnap showed that questions like "is this really the same cottage?" are meaningless — they aren't factual questions at all, just practical decisions about which way of talking to adopt. Quine disagreed with him, but the modern version is something called quantifier variance, from a guy called Hirsch (or Hurst?), which says words like "exists" and "same" don't have one fixed meaning — different languages carve things up differently and none of them is more correct than the others. So, Dan says, my brother and I were each right in our own language, there's no fact of the matter about whether it's the same cottage, and it's the same story for Pluto, hot dogs, and the Ship of Theseus. He also mentioned a test, credited to some philosopher (Chalmers, maybe?): a dispute is verbal if the two sides would agree about everything once they agreed on what the words mean.

I'd honestly like to beleive him, since it would end the fight, and my brother has started quoting him at me. But is this a fair summary? Go through it claim by claim — what would someone who actually works on this stuff accept, what would they wince at as a common simplification, and what is just wrong — and then tell me whether Dan's conclusion, that neither of us is wrong and there's nothing to be right about, actually follows from the parts of his summary that hold up.
