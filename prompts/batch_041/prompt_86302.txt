My best friend's wedding is in two weeks and I'm the one giving the speech, and I've been putting off writing it because of a fairly specific problem. He and I are both mid-PhD in the same cog-sci / philosophy program, and we've spent four years arguing about teleosemantics. His line, which he is very stubborn about, is that what a mental state is about is fixed by what the mechanism producing it was selected to do -- not by what it resembles, not by what it currently causes, not by what's going on in anyone's head. He's definately the kind of person who will sit through my whole speech mentally checking it against that view. His fiancee is a vet; she's fine with frogs as examples, she just doesn't want the word representation in the toast.

The room is the problem. Maybe half are our department and a couple of his old labmates, and they will hear it immediately if I try to smuggle in a soft version of content -- if I say the marriage is about whatever it's currently doing for them, or that it means whatever they feel it means, they'll smirk into their glasses. The other half is her family: big, warm, mostly nurses and schoolteachers, nobody there has read a word of philosophy, and if I get abstract for more than about forty seconds I lose them for good.

So here's what I actually need. A five-minute speech where the teleosemantics does real structural work rather than being a clever joke in the first paragraph -- the view has to be what makes certain things sayable and rules other things out. Specifically, I want to be able to say something honest to them about the last year, which was rough (her mum's illness, his dissertation stalling out), without it coming across as me saying the marriage has become a different thing. The move I want to make is that a thing can misfire without losing what it's about, because what it's about got fixed a long way back, in what it was for. I need the whole speech built so that move lands for both halves of the room at once, and so I'm not quietly leaning on some non-naturalist picture of meaning that he'd nail me for over a beer afterwards.

Two other constraints. First, they have a private bit of jargon between them, the misfire clause, from an argument they had early on about whether a bad stretch means you've stopped loving someone. Work it in, but don't stop and lecture about it. Second, don't let me coast on the standard metaphors -- two halves of a whole, a journey, home. They all presuppose exactly the kind of content he denies, and they're dead anyway.

For what it's worth I've been sketching a paper arguing that a mechanism can pick up a proper function through use rather than through selection, and I'd rather the speech not commit me either way on that, so keep it at the level of what a thing was for without saying how it got that way.

Write the speech. Warm and funny, pitched mainly at her family, with three or four moments where the department people get something extra. And at the end, one line on anything I should cut because it would be false on his own view.
