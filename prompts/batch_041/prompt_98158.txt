I report on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about what it means to call meaning indeterminate. I would like the conceptual core of Quine's thesis on translation explained to me with some rigor, but I want it built around a case I am going to stipulate rather than around the standard field-linguist story, which my readers have already met and can recite.

Suppose two linguists have each spent years with an isolated community and each produces a complete translation manual. Both manuals map every utterance the community ever makes onto an English sentence that is true exactly when the native utterance is, and both predict every act of assent and dissent, including in response to stimuli no one has presented before. Manual A renders a certain recurring native particle as one of our logical connectives; Manual B renders that same particle as a different connective and compensates by translating the surrounding material differently, so that no complete native utterance ever comes out with a different truth value under the two manuals. No behavioral test distinguishes them, and it is not that one is better supported and we merely lack the test.

Here is what I need. First, why Quine holds that the choice between the manuals is neither a matter of one being correct and the other incorrect, nor simply a case of two equally faithful translations of a single underlying meaning. Second, how this differs from the ordinary situation in which evidence underdetermines theory — why Quine thinks the indeterminacy does not shrink as we accumulate more facts about the speakers, their dispositions, and their history. Third, what the apparatus of stimulus meaning and the abandonment of the analytic-synthetic distinction are doing in the argument; I want to see how the pieces connect, not merely what each piece is. Fourth, the objection I find most pressing, which is that the two manuals are not genuine rivals at all, because whatever the manuals deliver just is what we mean by meaning, so nothing has been shown to be indeterminate. Give me that objection at its strongest, then tell me where you come down on it and why.

Finally, for the piece itself, I want a short exercise a reader can perform on herself to feel the problem rather than merely follow it — something that leaves her unable to say which of the two manuals she would be choosing between, and ideally a little unsettled about the question. Keep it to something she can actually do in a minute or two.
