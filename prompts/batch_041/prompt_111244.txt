Anglo-American evidence law draws a striking line between two ways of arguing from a person's past to their present conduct. A litigant is ordinarily forbidden to argue that someone has a character trait — a violent disposition, a dishonest nature, a general carelessness — and that the person therefore acted in keeping with it on the occasion in question. Yet if the litigant can instead establish a habit — a specific, repeated, near-automatic response to a specific situation, such as always taking the same route or always checking the lock — the evidence is admitted to show that the person acted that way this time. The inference has the same shape in both cases: past regularity, therefore present conduct. One form of the inference is barred and the other is welcomed.

I would like to understand what this distinction presupposes about character itself. It appears to commit the law to the view that broad, cross-situational traits are poor predictors of what a person will do, while narrow, situation-bound dispositions are reliable — which is close to what the situationist critique of virtue ethics has argued against the classical picture of robust character. Is that the commitment the practice actually carries? Reconstruct the implicit theory of character that the character/habit line seems to require, and say how much of it is genuinely presupposed rather than merely accommodated. Could the exclusion of character evidence be defended on purely epistemic or normative grounds — that juries would over-weight it, or that it is unfair to try someone for who they are rather than for what they did — with no commitment at all about the predictive power of traits? Which parts of the practice strictly presuppose a situationist psychology, and which are compatible with a robust trait theory?

I am also puzzled by an apparent inconsistency. The same body of law will treat character as a real, ascertainable property of a person whenever character is itself an element of the claim — the honesty of a defamation plaintiff, the fitness of a parent, the competence of a driver — and it will hear evidence of a defendant's character at sentencing. So the law seems at once to deny that character reliably issues in conduct and to affirm that character is a fact a court can find. Is that tension real, or does the practice hold together? And suppose we replaced the presupposed view with a full-blooded, global-trait conception of character: what would have to change in how courts treat the two kinds of evidence, and would the habit category survive at all?
