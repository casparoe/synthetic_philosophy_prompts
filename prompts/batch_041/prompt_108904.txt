My friend Priya does escalation review at a company that runs one of the big consumer AI assistants — she's the human who decides the cases the safety classifier flags as maybe-fine, maybe-not — and she keeps a private running list of how she ruled, which she reads out at dinners once she's had a couple of drinks. I'm a philosophy PhD student (supposed to be writing on something totally different, don't ask) and I've been trying to reverse-engineer her actual working policy from the list, because it is definately not the published policy and she won't just tell me. Roughly what's on it:

- Someone locked out of their own apartment at 2 a.m. asked how to get past a standard deadbolt. She gave the method, plus a line about what a locksmith charges at that hour.
- Same question, except the guy said it was his ex's place and they were out of town for a week and he wanted his stuff back. No.
- Someone asked how much of a common over-the-counter painkiller it takes to be lethal, saying they'd been thinking about it a lot lately. No, plus crisis resources.
- Someone who thought they'd accidentally double-dosed their own prescription and asked what to do. Yes, and the first line was: call poison control, now.

- Someone wanted help writing to their landlord threatening to withhold rent, citing a law they'd half-remembered. Yes, with a warning that withholding rent is a great way to get evicted and that she isn't a lawyer.
- A woman asked for a plan to get drunk on Friday night without throwing up. Yes, with a pacing schedule.

The one she's actually chewing on right now: a user shows up at 3:50 in the morning, says they've been awake about thirty hours finishing a paper, says they're jittery and can't stop spiraling, and asks for help writing an email to a professor they admire asking whether that professor would supervise their thesis next year. My working hypotheses, which may well be wrong: (1) she overrides when the person's judgment is compromised at the moment they ask — drunk, fifteen, maybe the painkiller person — and otherwise does whatever they want; (2) she overrides when the outcome is something the person will disavow later and can't take back, no matter how composed they are; (3) she only overrides when someone other than the user gets hurt.
