I'm a retired respiratory physiologist — thirty-odd years of it, mostly control of breathing, dogs early on and then humans — who got bored in retirement and started reading philosophy about four years ago, and I've somehow been let loose to give a paper at the little choice-and-rationality workshop my old department runs jointly with the economics people. My talk is called something like "Independence is a claim about individuation, not about preference", and the gist is this. Everyone treats the Allais results as a straight clash between what choosers do and what the independence axiom permits, and I want to argue that the clash is really about who gets to decide what counts as the same branch and the same consequence across the two problems. In the standard presentation the experimenter hands you a state space and a consequence space and then the axiom does all the work; but Allais himself was pointing at complementarity — the 89% branch isn't the same object in the two problems, because what it means depends on what happens on the other branches, and if the chooser is entitled to individuate the branches her own way then the axiom has no purchase on her. My own proposal, which is probably naive and which I want to try out on them, is to keep a cardinal notion of the difference a choice makes, so that an act is evaluated by how the differences between the available acts are spread across the branches rather than by the levels of the prizes, and then independence holds only across branches on which that difference is constant. I've been calling it difference-invariance, for want of a better name. If it works, the Allais preference comes out perfectly consistent and the common-ratio results stop looking like an anomaly — and I suppose I should admit that I'm half-hoping somebody shows me it doesn't work.

The audience is what's worrying me. It'll be about twenty people. A solid block of behavioural and experimental economists, plus a couple of the judgement-and-decision people from psychology, who think the whole matter was settled decades ago: independence is empirically dead, the common-consequence and common-ratio results have been replicated forever, there's all the small-stakes calibration business, and they will be mildly irratated that a physiologist is digging it up again. Then two proper decision theorists: one a Savage-style Bayesian who thinks a preference that violates independence is simply incoherent and can be pumped, and one who works on the rank-dependent and risk-weighted sorts of model and will tell me, probably correctly, that everything I want already exists and that I've reinvented it badly. And there's the department's lone Kantian, who will ask what any of this has to do with reasons for action. What I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get from these particular people rather than the textbook ones, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that person would really put it, with a two-minute reply I can actually deliver and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I'm dreading. Please write the whole thing in British English, and please don't give me a reading list or references — I can't look anything up from the podium anyway.
