One of the people in my reading group — we're mostly ex-CS folks who never took a real set theory course — keeps explaining Zorn's lemma with a mountain picture, and I cant tell anymore if the picture is illuminating or if it's doing all the work. The picture: a poset is a mountainside, elements are points on it, x ≤ y means y is at least as high as x, a chain is a monotone path that never goes downhill, an upper bound for a chain is some point at least as high as everything on the path, and "every chain has an upper bound" means every path can be continued upward. So, he says, a maximal element is just a peak, and Zorn is the claim that if you can always keep going up, you have to eventually stop at a peak — obviously true, since a mountain can't rise forever, so the whole song and dance about the axiom of choice is just people being precious about something any hiker already knows. He uses this to argue Zorn is "morally the finite case" and that anyone who thinks choice is doing real work here has been spooked by set theorists. I've got a nagging feeling the picture is smuggling in more than he thinks, but every time I try to say where, I get tangled.

Could you audit it? I want the correspondence table first — what maps onto the poset, the order relation, a chain, an upper bound, a maximal element, the "keep going" step, and the bit about a mountain not being able to rise forever. Then where it breaks: which features of an actual mountainside the metaphor quietly imports into the poset that a poset need not have, and which of those matter for the conclusion he wants versus which are harmless. I'm dimly aware that "maximal" isn't "maximum" and that "you can always keep going" isn't just a thing you can do in a poset, but I don't trust my grasp of why, or of where exactly the choice sneaks in, or whether there's some regime where his picture is actually literally correct and he's just overgeneralizing from it. Then the verdict: does the conclusion he's using it for — Zorn is obvious, choice is a red herring — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the mountain carrying the whole argument? If the picture can be repaired into something that survives, tell me what the better version is.
