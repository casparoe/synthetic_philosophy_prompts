I am a retired experimental scientist, now several years into reading philosophy of mind, and I would like a consistency audit of my current position on the timing of experience before I try to write it up. The case that started me thinking is the color phi phenomenon: two disks are flashed in quick succession at different locations and in different colors; subjects report seeing a single disk move from the first location to the second and change to the second color, with the change apparently occurring before the second disk is flashed. I am also interested in a variant I call the split-probe case, in which the second disk is replaced by a masked color patch and the report is delayed; if subjects still bind the later color to the earlier disk, I am not sure whether that strengthens the Orwellian reading or merely widens the Stalinesque one. Here is where I have landed:

1. Physicalism. Every fact about phenomenal experience is fixed by physical facts about the brain; there are no non-physical phenomenal properties.

2. Scientific realism. When a mature science posits a state or process, there is a fact of the matter whether it occurred, even if it cannot be observed directly.

3. Phenomenal realism about timing. For any subject and any time t, there is a fact of the matter about what that subject was phenomenally experiencing at t, not merely about what they later report or remember.

4. Color phi realism. In the standard color phi case, the subject genuinely experiences the first disk as changing to the second disk's color at the time of the first disk, that is, before the second disk is presented; this is not merely a memory error or a post hoc confabulation.

5. Orwellian and Stalinesque as a substantive empirical distinction. In any given revision case, exactly one of two things is true: either the experience itself is revised after the fact, which I call the Orwellian reading, or the experience was accurate and only the memory or report is revised, which I call the Stalinesque reading. I take this to be an empirical question with a determinate answer.

6. Probe pessimism. Any experimental probe of what was experienced at t must be implemented by neural processes occurring after t, and those processes are themselves subject to the same kind of revision; so no experiment can in principle distinguish an Orwellian from a Stalinesque account of a given case.

7. Temporal order by neural order. The temporal order of a subject's experiences is fixed by the temporal order of the neural events that realize them, not by the order in which the represented contents are reconstructed.

8. Fallibility. Subjects can be systematically wrong about the temporal order of their own experiences, even under optimal attention.

I would like you to audit this set. Which commitments are in genuine tension, which pairs only appear to conflict, and how severe is each genuine tension? Please rank the genuine tensions from most to least severe rather than treating them as equally bad. For each genuine tension, say what has to give, that is, which commitment would need to be weakened, reinterpreted, or abandoned, and what that revision would cost. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and why. Finally, if you had to bet on whether the set is consistent as it stands, say so. End with a final line of the form \answer{...}, where the braces contain the label of the commitment you would revise first, or none if no revision is needed.
