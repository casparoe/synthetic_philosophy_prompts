I have no formal training in philosophy; I write software for a living. I have been trying to understand G. E. M. Anscombe's protest against Oxford's decision to award Harry Truman an honorary degree in 1956, on the ground that he had ordered the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. I would like to know whether my reading of her objection is defensible, because it seems to me that the objection must really have been a historical claim rather than a moral one.

My reasoning runs as follows. Everyone who defends the bombings argues about consequences: whether the bombings shortened the war, how many casualties an invasion of the Japanese home islands would have cost, whether Japan was already attempting to surrender. Those are empirical questions of the kind historians settle from documents. Anscombe's objection, as it is ordinarily reported, was that the bombings were not necessary — that they did not accomplish what they were supposed to accomplish. It follows, I think, that her objection was itself an empirical claim of exactly that kind, and that if the historical verdict went the other way, and the bombings really did save more lives than they cost, her objection would simply collapse. On that reading, calling Truman a murderer amounts to a forceful way of saying that he made a bad call on the evidence available to him.

A colleague of mine who studied philosophy pushed back on this. He offered a hypothetical: a commander who deliberately targets a city full of civilians because it is the only means he can see of ending a war that will otherwise kill ten times as many people. My colleague says that Anscombe's position requires her to call that commander a murderer as well, regardless of the arithmetic, and that this shows the position is monstrous. I suspect he is right about what her position implies, and that is precisely what makes me doubt my own reading of it.

In my own work we distinguish between the objective function that a system is optimizing and the hard constraints imposed on it, and I cannot tell which of those her objection was meant to be — or whether that distinction is even the right one to bring to her.
