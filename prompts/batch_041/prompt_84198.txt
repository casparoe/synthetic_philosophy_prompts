I am a fifth-year PhD student in political theory, finishing a dissertation on moral remainder and the problem of dirty hands, and I have been asked to deliver the eulogy at the memorial service for Owen Ballard, who was mayor of my hometown from 1974 to 1988 and, before graduate school, my first employer. I was a twenty-two-year-old aide in his office in 1983, which means I was sitting outside his door for part of what follows, and that whatever I say in that union hall on Saturday will be heard by people who know more about some of it than I do.

Act as a political theorist with a serious command of the dirty-hands literature and, just as importantly, the craft of occasional rhetoric — someone who has actually written and delivered speeches rather than only written about them. Think carefully before you write, and begin with a short private plan.

The facts, as plainly as I can set them out. In 1982 the city's drinking water was drawn downstream of a plating plant owned by the family that had, for three decades, effectively chosen the mayor. The health department's own sampling showed the contamination that spring. Ballard kept the file closed for eleven months and then used it: he told the owners he would keep the results out of the newspapers and out of the state attorney general's hands if they would fund, and not obstruct, the filtration plant and soil remediation that came online in 1986. That plant and that remediation are, by any measure anyone has taken since, still working; county pediatric lead levels fell by roughly half over the following decade. In the interval, Dolores Ayala's son, then nine, was diagnosed with lead poisoning. Ballard told her the city had no evidence linking the diagnosis to the plant. That was false. She is alive, in her seventies, and will be in the third row.

He never apologized in public. About a year before he died he told me that he would do it again, and that saying so was not the same as saying he had been right — he was insistent on the difference, and I think he was right to be.

Now the difficulty, which is why I am not writing this myself. My dissertation turns on a distinction I believe the standard treatments blur. Walzer's 1973 essay in Philosophy & Public Affairs and Williams's chapter "Politics and Moral Character" (the 1978 piece in Hampshire's volume) both treat the politician's guilt as something he carries while the rest of us observe; neither asks who, if anyone, has the standing to discharge it. I have come to think these cases divide in two. Some wrongs leave a determinate injured person who holds the debt and may forgive it or refuse to, as she chooses. Others dissipate into a public — a lie told to a city, a confidence broken with no one in particular — and there no individual has standing, so no collective absolution is valid, and a room full of mourners has no more authority to grant it than a stranger has to forgive my debts. A single act can do both at once, in the same afternoon. That is what happened here.

This matters because a memorial service is, among other things, a machine for manufacturing absolution, and I do not want to run that machine. I also do not want to deliver a prosecution, or a seminar, and I do not want to be so scrupulous that I fail the room: his widow, his grandchildren, the men and women who worked for him and loved him and are entitled to their grief.

So write the eulogy. Roughly eight minutes spoken, for about two hundred people in a union hall — family, old allies, a few reporters, and a contingent from the environmental justice group that has spent a decade trying to keep the filtration plant from being renamed after him, which the family wants and they do not. The speech itself must carry no citations, no theorists' names, no jargon; the apparatus above has to do its work through the speech's shape rather than its vocabulary. It must name the lie as a lie. It must not treat the remediation as a receipt. It should at some point turn the room's attention toward Ms. Ayala without conscripting her, without asking her to stand, and without presuming to know what she wants. And the room must be able to leave still able to mourn him.

Before the text, give me two things. First, three or four sentences on what this eulogy must do and what it must not be caught doing. Second, a list of the moments where the distinction between the forgivable and the unforgivable debt actually bites in the text — the places where a conventional eulogy would reach for absolution and this one has to refuse it. Then the full text. After the text, give me the single sentence you would defend as the hinge of the whole thing, and why. You may invent the small details a eulogist would know — his manner at the podium, what he was like in the office — but you may not invent facts about the contamination, the deal, or the Ayala family, and you may not put words in her mouth.
