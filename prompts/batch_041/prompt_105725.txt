I write a long-form column on science and ideas for a general-readership monthly, and I am putting together a satirical piece for the back of the book. What I want sent up is a habit of mind I keep colliding with in my reporting: the managerial-therapeutic tendency to treat other people's conduct as something to be understood, adjusted, and worked around rather than answered -- the office culture in which nobody is ever blamed because everybody is being handled. The obvious philosophical home for the joke is P. F. Strawson's essay 'Freedom and Resentment', and I want the satire built out of his actual machinery rather than a vague sense that determinism is spooky. So here is the apparatus I want the jokes to come from. Strawson contrasts the participant stance, in which we take up reactive attitudes toward one another -- resentment, indignation and guilt, but also approval and gratitude, since he puts praise on the same footing as blame -- with the objective attitude, the stance we take toward the very young, the severely disturbed, and those we have decided are simply to be managed or treated. Two of his distinctions need to stay sharp: an excuse keeps us inside the participant stance by showing that the act was not really the agent's, whereas an exemption suspends the stance altogether by showing that the agent is not a full participant in it; and the objective attitude is not merely a mistake, it is a genuine resource, indispensable for understanding people and for dealing with those who cannot be addressed. The exaggeration I want is of a feature Strawson himself concedes -- that we really do use this stance, and that the determinist's picture gives us reason to use it more -- not the pretence that he recommends it.

Please write the piece as an in-universe corporate document: a training handbook issued by the people-and-culture department of a large firm that has resolved to eliminate blame from its operation. Give it a bland title and numbered sections in the style of a compliance manual: a rationale for the change; a decision procedure for determining whether a colleague's conduct is to be answered or merely treated, with excuses routed one way and exemptions the other; a worked case or two; a section on how the firm's recognition programmes must be reformed, which is where the symmetry of praise and blame has to bite, since a policy that keeps the gratitude while dropping the resentment is not stable, and the handbook should reach that conclusion without embarrassment; and a closing section in which it candidly concedes the strongest thing that can be said against it -- that trust, relationships, and the very practice of holding one another to standards seem to depend on the participant stance, and that no one has ever managed to hold the objective attitude toward everyone for long -- and then files that away as a transitional difficulty for next quarter's training. Around 800-900 words, deadpan throughout, with no framing note explaining the joke and no winking at the reader: the humour has to come from the accuracy of the parody, so that a reader who knows Strawson laughs while a reader who has never heard of him still sees precisely what has gone wrong. The handbook should not quote or cite Strawson; its authors would never have heard of him.

After the handbook, append a brief structured summary of the piece as a single JSON object with the following fields: "title" (the handbook's title and the number of sections); "target" (one sentence naming the practice or cast of mind being satirised); "form_parodied" (the genre the piece imitates, in a phrase); "machinery_used" (an array of objects, each with "distinction", naming one Strawsonian distinction the piece actually relies on, and "where_it_works", one line saying which section it does its work in); "reductio_step" (the single step at which the handbook's logic becomes absurd or costly, quoted or closely paraphrased from the piece); "concession" (the strongest objection the handbook allows, in its own words); "plain_reader_takeaway" (one or two sentences on what a reader who has never read Strawson comes away believing); and "irony_risk" (an object with "section", naming the passage most at risk of reading as sincere endorsement rather than parody, and "safeguard", saying what you did to guard against that).
