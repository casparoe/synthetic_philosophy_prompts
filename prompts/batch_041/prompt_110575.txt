I retired from experimental physics a few years ago and have spent the time since reading philosophy, mostly on the question of when a pattern we find in nature deserves to be called real rather than a convenience of description. Dennett's three stances strike me as getting at something, but I cannot get my own verdicts to line up under a single principle, and I would like you to try.

Here is what I think, case by case. The temperature of a gas is real, even though no molecule has a temperature and the quantity only exists once you stop tracking individual trajectories. The critical exponents of a magnet and of a fluid are real, and if anything more real than the microscopic details on which the two differ. The effective mass of an electron in a crystal is real; my colleagues and I treated it as a property of the material, and I still think we were right to. A support level on a stock chart is not real. A chess engine wanting to control the centre is real, since I predict its moves better that way than by reading its code, whereas a termite colony deciding where to put the ventilation shafts is not, even though I can predict the mound's structure by talking as if it had reached a decision. And a gene's interest in being copied is not real; that one I have never believed.

My provisional principle is this: a pattern is real to the degree that it survives re-description. If I change variables, coarse-grain differently, or move to another level of description and the pattern is still there and still predicting, I should believe in it; if it dissolves, it was an artifact of my bookkeeping. In the lab we had a rough version of this rule, and it served well enough for data.

Test that principle against the cases above. Which of my verdicts does it force me to abandon, and if I insist on keeping all of them, is the repaired principle one I could actually state and use, or a patchwork? I would also like its verdict on two cases I have not settled: a market's expectation of inflation, and the boundary of a species in a ring of interbreeding populations.
