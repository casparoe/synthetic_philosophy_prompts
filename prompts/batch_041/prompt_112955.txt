I cover European politics and ideas for a monthly magazine, and I keep meeting the same image from people who want to insist that the European Union cannot afford to stand still: integration is a bicycle, and a bicycle that stops moving falls over. In Brussels it does a great deal of work. It is used to argue that any pause in deepening, whether a slower treaty round, a member state that wants powers back, or an electorate that wants a breather, is never a neutral option but a step towards disintegration, and so to convert the Union's founding commitment to ever-closer union from a political programme into a condition of its survival. I would like that analogy audited rather than admired. Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. First, the mapping: what plays the part of the rider, the pedalling, the speed, the balance, the road and the fall. Second, which parts of that mapping are doing genuine work, in the sense that the target really does have the feature the argument needs. Third, where the mapping breaks, with attention to what a bicycle supplies for free and the Union does not: a destination, a single rider with purposes of their own, the option of dismounting, and the fact that a bicycle is stable at any constant speed and unstable only at zero. Fourth, what the image smuggles in about who is riding, where they are going and whether anyone else agreed to the journey, and how that bears on the two standing complaints, that the Union suffers a democratic deficit and that membership costs states their sovereignty. Fifth, whether the conclusion the image is used to license, that stasis means collapse, survives the audit, and if it does not, what comparison would serve better. Test the whole thing against the awkward case: one rider has now got off, and the bicycle has not fallen over. Please write in British English.
