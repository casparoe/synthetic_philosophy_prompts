For a decision-theory reading group I've been asked to circulate my own position on ambiguity so the others can attack it, and I want a consistency check before I do. Here is what I would defend.

(1) In the two-urn case — one urn with a hundred balls, fifty red and fifty black, and a second urn with a hundred balls, red or black in an unknown proportion — I strictly prefer a bet on red from the first urn to a bet on red from the second, and the same for black. I take this preference to be rational, not a bias to be corrected.

(2) Ambiguity is not risk at one remove: an act whose payoff depends on an unknown composition is not equivalent to a compound lottery over objective chances, so the preference in (1) is not ordinary risk aversion applied to a second-order gamble.

(3) Choice is consequentialist and time-slice by time-slice: at any point in a sequential problem, what I should do depends only on the prospects of the options then available, and my earlier decisions have no authority over my later self beyond whatever they have already caused.

(4) The best explanation of why people avoid the unknown urn is comparative ignorance — what we dislike is being in a position where we know our information is worse than it could be, not the ambiguity itself.

(5) Rational preferences do not shift when the only thing that changes is what is salient to me, rather than the options or the facts.

(6) My degrees of belief are additive probabilities, and conditionalization is the only way they rationally change.

Tell me which of these I can hold together. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and how severe is each one; which pairs only look like a conflict and why; for each genuine tension, what the cheapest concession would be and what it would cost me; and which single change would clean up the most. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad.
