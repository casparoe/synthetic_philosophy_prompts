I write about philosophy and science for a living — I'm the ideas-desk guy — which means my job is basically paraphrasing cleverer people's arguements, and next week a podcast wants me to defend my own views on moral language rather than describe everyone else's. So, dry run with you first. Here's the package, and I'd say all of it out loud:

1. When I say "lying is wrong," I'm not reporting anything. I'm giving voice to a non-belief state — an aversion plus a demand, not a representation.
2. Moral sentences embed fine. "If lying is wrong, then telling my brother to lie is wrong" is a perfectly ordinary conditional, and arguments with moral premises are valid or invalid like any others. So I owe some account of what a moral sentence is doing when it isn't being asserted. I'm told the conditional/embedding bit is called the Frege-Geach problem.
3. Truth is thin and cheap: "it's true that lying is wrong" adds nothing whatsoever to "lying is wrong."
4. When two people disagree about lying, they really disagree — its not two people describing their own feelings past each other.
5. My own patch, which I've been calling the same-content constraint: for a moral argument to be valid, the moral words have to carry the same meaning in the premise and in the conclusion, otherwise modus ponens is just a word game.
6. And I'm no relativist — I'll happily say that torturing a kid for fun is wrong whether or not anyone agrees with me.

Something doesn't sit right here and I can't tell if it's real or just nerves. Which of these actually pull against each other, which pairs only look like they do, and what's the cheapest one to give up — and what does giving it up cost me? Order them by how bad the damage is rather than handing me a list of tensions I could wave away.
