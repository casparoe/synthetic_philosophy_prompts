My grandad died in the spring and left forty-odd years of obsessive local history stacked in the back bedroom: parish register transcripts, photos of buildings that got demolished in the sixties, cassette tapes of old farmers reciting field names nobody uses anymore, and a shoebox of family papers that turns out to include some fairly grim material about his own father's role in running a labour camp during the war. My brother wants to digitise the lot and hand it to the county archive. I want to bin the tapes and the field names and keep only the handful of documents about our great-grandfather, because I dont think I can carry the rest around and I don't think the world needs another shelf of a dead man's hobby. My aunt, who is a retired archivist, says we're both being sentimental and that the whole point of history is to break the past open and see what it was actually made of. It's gotten a bit heated, honestly.

Someone in the family keeps quoting Nietzsche at me, and I finally read the bit about the three ways of doing history — the monumental one, the antiquarian one, the critical one — and now I can't stop trying to score them. So here's what I want: rank those three by how much each would actually help me in this exact situation. Not in general, not for society or for the culture, for me, right now, with the shoebox. Give me an ordered list, a sentence or two of justification for each, and say something about what the ranking is doing — whether the one on top is on top because it's the truest thing to do here or just because it's the one I can live with.

Two things before you start. If you think monumental history simply can't be pointed at a perpetrator — that the mode needs admirable figures and there's nothing to do with a monster except drop him or invert him — tell me, because that's the knot I keep hitting and I suspect it's the real disagreement in the house. And if the premise is wrong — if those three don't exhaust the options, or if the critical mode isn't really something you do so much as something that happens to you — say so plainly instead of giving me the tidy list anyway. I'd much rather be told my question is confused than be humoured.
