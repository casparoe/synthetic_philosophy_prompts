I am a novelist, and I run a small weekend workshop for fiction writers on the architecture of invented societies — the unglamorous machinery of who counts, who decides, and who is simply assumed into the background of a story. I would like you to design a single ninety-minute activity for about fourteen participants, most of them working novelists with no formal training in political philosophy, that makes them feel the boundary problem — the fact that a political community cannot settle the question of its own membership without already presupposing an answer to it — rather than hear it explained.

The setting is a fictional port town, the Harbour Settlement, which has just declared itself independent and must now draft a founding charter. I want the philosophy to live in the rules and the roles, not in anything I say from the front of the room. The participants should walk into the trap themselves.

Please work the following into a runnable design. The founding table seats five factions: the Saltmakers' Guild, which holds the warehouses; the Marsh-dwellers, who own the land the town is built on and resent it; the Shipowners, who control the harbour; the Debtors, an encampment of settlers whose labour built the sea wall and who hold no property; and the Enclave, a body of refugees admitted provisionally a year ago. Give each faction one or two concrete interests that pull against the others, so that coalitions form and dissolve. The charter must be adopted by a four-fifths majority of those seated, and the charter itself must state who is entitled to vote on future amendments. Once adopted, the franchise can be changed by a simple majority of the franchise as it then stands.

Then arrange for the assembly to run headlong into the circularity. After they have adopted their charter, introduce a sixth party — the Seasonal Crews, who work the docks half the year, live under the town's laws while they are present, and turn out to outnumber the founding five combined. Require the assembly to apply its own charter to their admission. The predictable result is that whichever coalition wrote the rule finds a reason to keep it, and the participants discover that their founding document was a description of the balance of power that happened to be phrased as a principle. Let them sit in that for a few minutes before any debrief.

I want the debrief to earn its keep. Map the moments of the activity onto the structure of the problem: the point at which someone first asks whether the Seasonal Crews should be seated, the point at which the rule is invoked to refuse them, and the point at which a participant notices that the same move could have been made against them. Give me the two principles that a designer will reach for in the moment — one that seats everyone whose interests are affected, and one that seats only those who are actually subjected to the community's coercion — and show how the Harbour Settlement's factions let a facilitator distinguish them concretely, because the Company offshore is affected but not subjected, while the Seasonal Crews are subjected but only intermittently affected. I would also like the debrief to give the writers something to take back to their manuscripts: a way of asking, of any invented society, who is missing from the room where its rules were made.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. I expect at least one participant to try to resolve the whole thing by appeal to fairness or natural rights and to grow frustrated when the room will not converge; I want your advice on whether to let that frustration run or to redirect it, and on how to handle the participant who simply refuses to admit the Seasonal Crews and defends the refusal as realism. Include a realistic timeline for the ninety minutes, the materials I need to print, and how the factions should be assigned so that no one feels handed a villain.

Be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce. I suspect it cannot give anyone the experience of being genuinely bound by a decision they had no part in — the participants will always know it is a game and can leave — and I would like you to say plainly where the facilitator has to supply, by argument, what the room cannot feel, and which part of the boundary problem will remain, after the exercise, a matter of taking my word for it.
