I wrote this out for a seminar and now I can't leave it alone. Here's the argument:

(1) Greatness is a matter of degree — of any two possible beings, one is at least as great as the other.
(2) Call a being supreme if nothing greater than it is possible.
(3) Existing necessarily is greater than existing contingently: other things equal, a being that exists in every possible world is greater than one that exists in only some.
(4) So if a supreme being is possible at all, it exists in every possible world. Suppose one exists in world w and not in v; then a being like it in every respect except that it exists in v as well would be greater than it, which contradicts (2). And a being that exists in every possible world exists in this one.
(5) So the whole thing hangs on whether a supreme being is possible.
(6) It is. Go through the greatness-making properties — knowing everything, being able to do anything, being perfectly good, existing necessarily, and so on. Each one on its own is plainly possible; nobody thinks knowing everything is a contradictory property. And none of them rules any other one out; what about knowing everything would stop you from being able to do anything? And if a bunch of properties couldn't all be had by one being, that would have to show up as a clash between some of them, and there isnt one. So they can all be had together, and a supreme being is possible.
(7) Therefore a supreme being exists, necessarily.

My friend's objections. She says (3) is just Kant's old point about existence not being a predicate — but (3) doesn't say existing is a property, it says existing necessarily is greater than existing contingently, and I can't see how Kant's point even touches that. She also ran the same argument with greatest conceivable island and got a greatest conceivable island, and with a being that is all-powerful and all-knowing and perfectly malicious and got that existing necessarily too. She says (6) makes possibility way too cheap, that any bunch of properties that don't obviously clash gets you a necessarily existing thing that has them all, but she can't say where the cheat is either. The island one I can brush off — an island's greatness is bounded by what an island is, so it can't include existing necessarily — but the malicious one is harder, and the general charge bugs me alot.

I'd like to keep (3) and (4) if I can; they feel like the engine of the thing. Which step do I actually give up, why did it look so good, and what does giving it up cost? If the answer is that I give up (6) and now have no argument, tell me what a theist is supposed to do at that point and what they pay for it.
