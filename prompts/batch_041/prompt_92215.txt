Every week or so a product team drops a new 'safeguard' on my desk and asks me to sign off. I do safety review at a mid-size AI company — not one of the big ones, we dont have a research team to spare — and the thing their guarding is usually some new agentic deployment (right now: a coding agent with repo write access and a browser). My job is to say whether what they built actually protects us, and what I keep finding is that half of these only work if the model is already on our side, which is the one assumption we're not allowed to make. We keep having the same argument and there's no procedure, so I'd like you to write one I can run.

Per proposal, the procedure has to land on: is this a control measure (it holds even if the model is actively working against it), an alignment measure (it works by making the model want the right things), or neither — a capability eval or a compliance ritual wearing a safety hat? And then: how much does it actually buy us?

Two in-house things to build in rather than argue with:

- We've started calling measures structural or voluntary. Structural: the model's cooperation isn't needed for the thing to bite (no network in the sandbox, nothing merges without a human clicking, weights frozen). Voluntary: it only works if the model goes along — self-reports of intent, honesty fine-tuning, refusal training, a monitor that reads the chain of thought and takes it at face value. My suspicion is that voluntary measures are carrying most of our proposals and I'd rather the procedure surface that than have me say it in every meeting.
- Review budget: we get a fixed number of human-review minutes per 1,000 agent sessions and I don't get to pretend otherwise. Anything leaning on human review has to state its coverage and say what the model can do on the uncovered fraction.

So: questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a flat no, and where it hands off to a person because what's left is how much residual risk we're willing to eat, not a question of fact. Include a step for what always happens after launch — the safeguard is annoying, the team quietly routes around it — because a measure that gets switched off in month two is worse than nothing if we budgeted for it.

Then run it on three:

A. A chain-of-thought monitor that flags sessions where the reasoning looks like it's planning something the user never asked for.
B. Model writes the code, a smaller trusted model reviews every diff, no merge without a human ack, sandbox has no network.
C. A values fine-tune — the team says the model now really cares about not exfiltrating data.

Verdict on each, including the one I won't like. I suspect C is what the execs are quietly counting on. Tell me where the procedure can't help and I just have to decide.

At the end, say what would change your mind about the procedure itself — which argument or consideration would make you reorder the steps or split one in two — and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
