A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether Peirce and James held two genuinely different theories of truth or one theory delivered in two idioms, and we got nowhere, because every case either of us reached for was a case both men had already discussed — religious belief, moral commitment, the will to believe, the comfort of a flattering error — so we ended up trading readings of the two texts instead of testing them. I would like a case that settles it, built to order rather than found.

Please construct a thought experiment whose verdicts are fixed in advance: a single belief, held by one person or a small community, which James's account of truth must count as true and Peirce's account must not.

Constraints on the case:

- Ordinary and realistic — contemporary or near-contemporary life, no science fiction, no brains in vats, no gods, no far-future or indefinitely extended societies, nothing that turns on exotic physics.
- It must not be an example either philosopher is known to have used, and it must not be a religious, moral, or free-will belief. I want a plain empirical belief about how things stand in the world.
- James's verdict has to be earned rather than stipulated. The believer's holding the belief must be borne out in the course of experience — the expectations it commits them to are met, and met repeatedly, in what they go on to encounter — and the belief must be expedient for assignable reasons. It must not be a merely comforting error, and above all it must not be a case where the belief is simply false while useful. I do not want the two verdicts to fall out of an external fact-check against a world that sits there, indifferent to the belief, waiting to falsify it.
- The Peircean verdict must fall out of Peirce's criterion and not out of a stipulation he would have rejected. Specifically, the reason his account withholds the title must be that inquiry into the question would not converge, not that he would have found the belief distasteful or imprudent.
- The divergence must be traceable to one structural difference between the two accounts, which you must name and defend. I will not accept the answer that James is lax and Peirce is strict, or that they merely meant different things by the same word.
- The scenario itself should be one paragraph.

If, once you try, you conclude that no such case can be built because Peirce's account is more permissive than I am assuming, say so plainly and show me where the impossibility lies rather than forcing a case that does not work.

After the scenario, state the belief, then walk through the two verdicts and exactly why each falls out, and tell me which feature of the case is doing the work. Then remove that feature and tell me what happens — in particular, whether the case collapses back into the stock objection that a belief can be useful and false. Then put on James's hat and give me the strongest way for him to deny that the case shows any real disagreement, and say what that response costs him. Finish by telling me whether the case settles the argument I was having — a difference in doctrine or only in emphasis — and, if it does not, what kind of case would.
