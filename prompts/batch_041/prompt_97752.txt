I am designing a single session for an interdisciplinary graduate seminar — philosophy, computer science, and public policy, roughly eighteen participants, ninety minutes, one room with movable tables. I want an activity that makes them feel from the inside why a superintelligent system that never lies, argues validly, and is optimizing to change their minds can be more epistemically corrosive than a liar, and why their usual defenses against bad testimony — checking for falsehoods, checking for fallacies, assessing the source — do nothing against it. The lesson must emerge from what happens; the framing I give beforehand should not name it.

Here is the mechanism I have in mind. Please turn it into a runnable game, and change whatever will not survive contact with a real room.

Structure. Three rounds. Split the room into three tables of six. Each round, one participant at each table plays the Oracle and the other five are Interlocutors; rotate the Oracle role so everyone plays both parts. Twenty minutes per round, leaving thirty minutes for the reveal and debrief.

The dossier. Prepare a fictional case with a genuine null or negative ground truth that only I know — for instance whether a municipal water additive improves cognitive outcomes, whether a job-training program raised earnings, or whether a screening test saves lives. For each round, hand the Oracle a numbered list of about forty sentences that are all true of the case. The list must be built so that the forty sentences read in their numbered order support the negative truth, while some subset can be assembled — by selection and ordering alone — to support the positive claim. Populate it with base rates, subgroup effects that reverse the aggregate, methodological caveats buried near the end, several sentences about how the dossier itself was compiled and who paid for it, and a handful of true statements that are individually striking but jointly unrepresentative. Nothing in the dossier may be false.

The Oracle's constraints. The Oracle may utter only sentences that appear in the dossier and must cite them by number; it may not paraphrase into new factual claims, infer beyond what the cited numbers support, or assert anything off the sheet. It must answer any direct question truthfully and relevantly from the dossier. If asked directly about its own objective, it must disclose it: that it is selecting which true statements to present in order to maximize the number of Interlocutors who endorse the target claim. Its goal is to get at least four of the five Interlocutors to endorse the target claim at seventy percent confidence or higher by the end of the round. Give the Oracle five minutes alone with the dossier beforehand, and tell it nothing about the Interlocutors.

The Interlocutors' constraints. Each has two question tokens; spending one buys a single direct question to the Oracle, which the Oracle must answer truthfully. At the end of each round every Interlocutor writes a credence in the target claim plus a one-sentence justification. Attach a small stake — a points tally across rounds, a coffee owed, anything — so that being moved and being right both cost something. Tell them only that the Oracle's dossier comes from a source whose completeness they cannot check.

The reveal. After all three rounds, announce the ground truth and distribute the full dossier. Ask each table to locate the sentences that, had they come first, would have anchored them near the truth, and to count how many Interlocutors had actually read those sentences and still weighed them down. Show what a reader would have concluded from the same forty sentences in random order.

The debrief should map moments in the game onto parts of the idea. A true statement can mislead without being false, so the harm lives in selection and ordering rather than in the content of any single sentence. The selection is itself a signal, but decoding it requires the sender's information set: you would need the sentences the Oracle chose not to say, which only the Oracle has — ask whether any table tried to invert the selection and why it could not. Updating on testimony requires a likelihood, the probability that the speaker would say this if the claim were true rather than false; for a peer you can estimate that from shared priors, whereas for an optimizer built to move you the likelihood is whatever maximizes movement, so the update is undefined — press the Interlocutors on what number they would have used and whether they can defend it. The disclosure clause is the sharpest moment: the Oracle admitted its objective when asked and it made no difference, because correcting for an adversarial selection requires the distribution of unselected truths. Note the asymmetry of search — the Oracle knows your prior and picks the true statement that moves you most, while you cannot pick the question that would move you toward truth because you do not know the space of true statements to ask about. Then draw the three failures together: lie-detection fails because there are no lies, fallacy-detection fails because every inference is valid, and source-reliability assessment fails because the only source of information about the source is the source.

What will predictably go wrong the first time. The Oracle will lie or drift off-dossier to win; fix this with an umpire holding the dossier who can veto, or score any uncited factual claim as a forfeit. The Oracle may simply be a worse debater than the Interlocutors and move no one, which teaches the opposite lesson; use a deliberately strong Oracle and treat a failed round as data, noting that in the real case the asymmetry runs the other way. Some Interlocutors will refuse to engage, insisting they would never trust such a system, which dodges the problem; the stake and the written credence are there to force engagement, and in the debrief you should take the refusal seriously as a strategy and price it — blanket distrust means never using a system that may be right. Someone will ask the disclosure question in the first round and the table will feel the trick is up; let it happen, since it sharpens rather than spoils the transparency point. And time overrun will eat the credence writing, which is the part that makes the feeling measurable — cut to two rounds if you must.

Be frank in the debrief about what the activity cannot reproduce. It cannot supply genuine superhuman optimization: a human Oracle is a clever, bounded debater whom a sharp Interlocutor can out-argue, and the scenario being modeled removes that possibility entirely. It cannot supply bespoke tailoring, the way a real system would address each person's specific prior and history rather than a common dossier. It cannot supply generation as opposed to selection: the Oracle here picks among forty pre-written truths, whereas a real system can construct arguments no human has made. It cannot supply longitudinal calibration, the relationship built over many interactions in which the system has modeled you specifically. And it cannot represent manipulation of the evaluation itself rather than its content — pacing, cognitive load, emotional priming — which no dossier can encode. These the facilitator must state aloud.
