Retired lawyer here, I read a lot of history for fun and I keep running into a pattern I can't name. Take any big event — a famine, a crash, a battle that went the wrong way — and you'll get a list of contributing factors, three or four of which are each described as enough on their own to have brought it about. Fine, overdetermined, I know that much. But here's the part that bugs me: everyone still goes on to name *the* cause, and the one they pick isnt the strongest or the most necessary — its whichever factor is unusual (the thing that departed from the normal run of things), or, if the question is how to prevent the thing next time, whichever factor somebody could actually have changed. Same event, different question, different "cause", even though the set of factors is identical and each of them was sufficient. So "the cause" seems to be selected by salience or by modifiability rather than by anything in the causal structure itself. Does this have a name, and is there a decent account of what the selection rule really is? I don't just want the label — I want to know how it differs from plain overdetermination and from what "but-for" means in law, and where the edges of it are (is it one thing or several?). If nothing fits, coin something and define it.

Give it back to me as a JSON object with these fields: "verdict" (one of "established", "partial_match", "no_term"), "established_term" (the accepted name if there is one, else null), "nearest_concepts" (array of objects, each with "name", "where_it_fits", and "where_it_breaks"), "selection_rule" (your best account of what actually drives the choice of "the" cause), "proposed_term" (the name you'd coin, or the established one if it fits), "definition" (one or two sentences), "not_to_be_confused_with" (array of strings, each naming something it's often mixed up with and the one-line difference), and "boundary_cases" (array of short cases that test where the phenomenon starts and stops).
