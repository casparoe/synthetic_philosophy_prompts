I have no academic training in any of this — I left a computer science degree after a year and now work the night desk at a hospital scheduling office — but for the past year or so I've been reading everything I can find about how the systems that are supposed to check other AI systems actually get trained, and I've gotten stuck on one distinction: whether you judge a model by the steps it takes or by the results it produces. The more I read, the more it looks to me as though the field quietly conceded something enormous — that we mostly can't inspect the reasoning, so we grade the answer instead — and then carried on talking as though nothing had been conceded. What nags at me is that every defense of result-grading, if I follow it honestly, seems to eat itself: the thing doing the supervising is itself a model trained on outcomes, so the chain of trust either terminates somewhere nobody can justify or runs on forever. I've tried to explain this to people at work and I keep either sounding like a crank or getting lost halfway through, so I want to see it done properly as comedy, partly because it will force someone to commit to the details and partly because I'll be able to tell from the jokes whether they actually know the material.

So: write me a satirical internal policy handbook of the kind a large AI lab would circulate the week it abolishes step-by-step review of its models' reasoning and replaces it with results-only verification. It should be written entirely in the lab's own voice — bland, self-congratulatory, faintly legalistic, never winking — and it should believe what it says. Number the sections and give the lab a name that sounds like every other lab's. Include an FAQ at the end in which at least two of the questions are ones the policy genuinely cannot answer without contradicting an earlier section, and one footnote that quietly hollows out the section it is attached to. The comedy has to come from getting the technical picture right rather than from invented absurdity: the way grading only outcomes cannot distinguish a lucky answer from a competent one; the fact that a process supervisor is usually trained the same way the supervised model was, so the regress is real; the way a model judged only on results has no incentive to remain legible about how it got them; and the awkward point that whatever you measure once a system is deployed is a measurement of a world the system has already helped to make. Aim for about 800 words, and keep it readable to someone who knows nothing about any of this, while being recognizably about the right things to someone who does.

Then, on a separate page, break character. Tell me which of my suspicions the parody has to distort in order to be funny — that is, what would have to be true about how supervision actually works for the piece to count as a fair exaggeration rather than a cheap shot — and which parts of it you would write differently if you thought process supervision were the stronger position. I'd rather be told where I'm wrong than be agreed with, so say plainly which single consideration would move you, and where your own reasoning in that closing note is most likely to be off.
