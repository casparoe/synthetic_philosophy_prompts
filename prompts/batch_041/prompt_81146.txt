I am a sixth-form student writing an essay for a school philosophy prize, on whether a community can be under a duty to remember its own atrocities, or whether forgetting can sometimes be the more decent course. To keep myself from platitudes I have built the essay around a case of my own invention, and I would like you to argue it through and reach a verdict.

The case: eighty years ago, a town was the site of a massacre of one of its minority communities. Almost no one alive took part or was directly bereaved, and the descendants of the perpetrators are now a majority. Two proposals are before the town. The first is a memorial museum on the site, with the names of the dead read aloud each year. The second, put forward by some of the victims' descendants, is a public garden with no plaque, no ceremony, and the town's testimonies and records sealed in an archive to be opened in a century; they argue that the museum would fix their families permanently in the role of victims and hand their children an inheritance of grievance, whereas the archive honours the dead without conscripting the living. Their opponents reply that a town which declines to remember has already begun a second killing, and that the garden is erasure with better landscaping.

I want a verdict rather than a survey. Two questions I would particularly like you to settle. First, whether the duty people invoke is a duty to do something — to build, to gather, to recite — or merely a duty not to falsify: a standing obligation to keep the record straight, which a silent garden might satisfy perfectly well. If it is only the latter, say what is lost, and whether the loss matters. Second, who holds the duty and to whom it is owed — the dead themselves, their descendants, the present town, or the generations who will inherit whichever version of the past we leave them — and whether the same decision could discharge the duty to one claimant while wronging another. (If the dead cannot be wronged at all, tell me whether that collapses the duty or merely relocates it.) Then take the objection that commemoration is often a substitute for reparation, a museum opened while the stolen land stays stolen: say whether that defeats the case for remembering or only raises its price. End by saying which proposal the town should adopt, and why.
