I am a retired experimental physicist — forty years at the bench, no formal training in philosophy — and over the past three years I have been reading my way, mostly on my own, into decision theory and game theory. A small institute that funds work at the boundary of formal methods and social philosophy has invited me to submit a two-page proposal for a one-year fellowship, and I would like help turning a half-formed idea into that document.

The idea concerns program equilibrium. In the construction I have in mind, each player in a one-shot game submits not a move but a program that is permitted to inspect the other player's program before choosing; the striking upshot is that mutual cooperation in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma can be an equilibrium after all, since each program can make its own move conditional on what the other program does when confronted with it. What holds the whole thing up is that the players read one another's source code completely and for free. In every institution I have actually lived inside — peer review, collaboration agreements, the way a laboratory is audited, the way an arms treaty is verified — nobody reads anyone's source code. What circulates instead are partial, delayed, and expensive representations of what others will do: reports, disclosures, escrowed documents, statistical summaries, reputations, and sometimes nothing at all.

My question, which I would like the proposal to state precisely enough that it has a determinate answer: what becomes of program equilibrium when legibility is imperfect and priced — when reading another agent's policy costs something, and the cost falls on one side or the other — and does the resulting equilibrium structure distinguish the institutions that genuinely sustain cooperation from those that merely describe it? I am genuinely unsure whether the right formalization treats the cost as a cost of reading, a cost of being read, or a cost of making oneself readable, and I would like the proposal to commit to one of these and defend the choice.

Please write the two-page proposal: the question; why the existing treatments leave it open; the method, laid out month by month, and why a formal-conceptual approach rather than an empirical one; what would count as a result — a theorem, a counterexample, a taxonomy — and what would count as the project failing; and an honest assessment of scope, given that I program well enough for small simulations but am not a professional modeler. If it goes well, I would also like the proposal to gesture at whether the difference between the two kinds of institution bears on why we call some of them legitimate and others merely effective. Do not supply citations or a reading list; I will add those myself.
