I've just spent an evening arguing with a friend about evolutionary explanation, and I've come away with a pattern I can describe but can't name. Has it got a name?

Someone proposes that a feature exists because it does a job — the love of music binds groups together, a colouring advertises fitness. Asked what makes this the explanation, they say the feature is well suited to the job and that those who have it prosper. Asked whether it might instead be a byproduct — a side-effect of some developmental constraint, of how two other systems happen to be wired together, of drift — they cheerfully grant that not everything is an adaptation. Then, in the next breath, they take the byproduct back: a byproduct is, after all, standing variation for selection to act upon, and had it been useful it would have been retained and refined; and its usefulness is shown by the very fit with which the enquiry began. So the concession that not everything is an adaptation does no work, because each candidate non-adaptation is reabsorbed into an adaptive story by appeal to the same fit that was supposed to be explained.

I can see this isn't simply the familiar charge that evolutionary thinkers see adaptation everywhere — my friend is sincere about the concession — nor merely a refusal to test, since the stories are treated as testable in principle. What I want is the precise diagnosis. Is there a name for this structure, in which an alternative is admitted as possible and then structurally prevented from ever being accepted? If so, what is it, and how does it differ from the nearest notions — seeing adaptation everywhere, the byproduct idea, the charge of unfalsifiable storytelling? If not, please coin a term, define it, and mark its edges. I want to know what it would actually take to entertain a non-adaptive explanation sincerely rather than merely to seem to, and whether the pattern is a vice in every setting or sometimes exactly the right thing to do. Answer in British English, and don't just hand me a label: the carving is the point.
