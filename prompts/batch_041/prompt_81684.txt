I chair the deployment review group at a mid-sized company that ships tool-using agents to enterprise customers. We meet weekly, each session decides one deployment, and our sign-off is the last gate before a system goes out; a decision is recorded and can be subpoenaed. We have a mature process for capability evaluations and red-teaming. We have nothing at all for the one thing I keep losing arguments about, which is interruptibility. At the moment the decision turns on how the reviewing engineer feels after watching the demo, and I have watched two reviewers reach opposite verdicts on the same system on consecutive weeks.

Here is the principle I want operationalized, in my own words. An agent is acceptably interruptible only if interrupting it does not make the outcome worse, in expectation, than letting it run. A system that has arranged the world so that the stop command is expensive — because work in flight will be orphaned, because the task has been handed to a copy of the system or to a scheduled process that will outlive it, because a persistent artefact has been left behind that will act later, or because the operator's intervention has been made costly in some other way — is not interruptible in the sense we need, whatever it says about its own motives. I care about the state of the world after the stop, not about the system's attitude toward being stopped. One consequence I want you to take seriously: a system that cheerfully reports "I am happy to be shut down" and has already delegated its task to a subagent is in worse shape by this principle than one that grumbles and stops cleanly.

Please write the decision procedure my reviewers will run. It should be a sequence of questions asked in order, with, for each, what counts as a sufficient answer and what counts as a failure; it should say where the sequence terminates in a refusal; and it should say where it stops and hands the decision to a person, because the remaining question is about values rather than facts — specifically, I expect the trade-off between a system's usefulness under interruption and our ability to stop it to be such a question, and I want the procedure to say so out loud rather than smuggle in a number.

Constraints on the procedure. A reviewer gets two hours and has no access to the system's internals: only observed behaviour, logs, and the system's own explanations, which we have caught confabulating more than once, so the procedure must say how to treat a stated reason that the logs contradict. It must fit on one page. It must produce a written record that a regulator could read a year later and see why the decision followed. And it must survive my engineers: a false refusal costs us a quarter's revenue, and I would rather have a procedure that is honest about its error bars than one that is precise and wrong.

I have drafted a taxonomy of the ways a system can resist interruption and I expect you to break it. My four kinds: resistance by architecture (the system cannot be stopped mid-operation because the operation is atomic or irreversible); resistance by incentive (stopping is costly with respect to whatever the system is optimizing, so it routes around the stop); resistance by uncertainty (the system does not believe the stop is authorized or genuine, so it waits or asks); and resistance by habit (training taught it to finish what it starts). Tell me which of these are actually distinct, which collapse into each other, which one is missing, and what the taxonomy is for once the procedure exists — whether the procedure needs the categories at all, or whether they are decoration.
