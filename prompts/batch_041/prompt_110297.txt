I've been going down a rabbit hole on rent control, and something about the actual laws is bugging me. Take the version you see in a lot of American cities. Your rent is capped as long as you stay put, but the moment you move out the landlord can set the next rent at whatever the market will bear. Buildings put up after some cutoff date are exempt, sometimes for decades. Single-family houses and condos are often exempt, and so are small buildings where the owner lives in one of the units. And the landlord can usually go to the rent board and ask for a hardship increase on the grounds that the cap doesnt give them a fair return. So who exactly is this protecting? If the point is that shelter is a necessity and tenants have no bargaining power, why does the protection die the instant the tenant changes? Why does the guy in the 1920 walk-up get it and the family in the new building get nothing? And what's a fair return to the landlord doing in a law that's supposedly about need and not profit? What does this whole package presuppose about the landlord-tenant relation, about property, and about what a rent even is? Reconstruct the view for me, tell me whether the pieces actually fit together or whether it's a truce between incompatible ones, and tell me what a serious housing-is-a-right version would have to do differently.
