Suppose Benjamin Franklin could be brought forward to the present and asked to evaluate a contemporary moral-practice platform called The Thirteenth Virtue. The platform is explicitly modeled on his own moral-perfection regimen. Participants keep a daily ledger with a row for each of the thirteen virtues. But the scoring is not a simple mark of success or failure. Before each day begins, a participant must enter a probabilistic forecast for each virtue: the probability that they will act in accordance with that virtue that day. At the end of the day, the platform scores each forecast with a proper scoring rule (for example, a Brier-type penalty for the squared distance between the forecast and the outcome), so that a participant earns points for accurate self-prediction rather than for virtuous conduct. The participant's overall moral perfection score is the sum of these calibration scores across the thirteen virtues, normalized to 100. The platform also includes a humility component: it displays a suggested forecast generated by a hidden model of the participant's character, and it awards humility points to the extent that the participant adopts that suggestion over their own initial forecast. The designers claim Franklin would approve because he, too, kept a ledger and aimed at perfection.

Predict what Franklin would say about this platform. Do not merely summarize his regimen; use it to generate a concrete verdict. In your answer, address the following: (1) reconstruct the central aims and methods of his actual moral-perfection project, as you understand them; (2) give his likely overall verdict, separating what he would endorse from what he would reject; (3) analyze at least three specific features of the platform—such as the proper scoring rule, the humility component, the hidden model, the normalization to 100, or the identification of perfection with calibration—and explain how his own commitments would lead him to assess each; (4) describe the version of the platform, if any, that he would recommend; and (5) mark the places where you are extrapolating beyond anything he explicitly considered. Avoid anachronism: do not attribute to him technical concepts he could not have had, but do consider what his analogous concerns would have been. Write your answer in American English.
