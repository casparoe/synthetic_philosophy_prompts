I'm putting together the final for my MA seminar on moral uncertainty and the moral status of invertebrates — it's a wierd little course, half the students signed up for the animal ethics and half because they heard we'd do expected-value stuff — and I've been stuck on the essay question for two weeks now. The problem is that every question I try gets answered with the same three paragraphs about the scale argument: insects are staggeringly numerous, we're unsure whether they're sentient, so expected value says their interests dominate, done. I want a question where that move is the *opening* of the answer rather than the whole thing, and where the student has to actually get their hands dirty about why anyone would distrust it.

So here's the shape I've landed on. I've drafted a hypothetical position — not a real paper, I'll say so on the exam sheet so nobody tries to look it up — which I'm calling the Scale Principle: for any policy, sum over all taxa (number of individuals affected) × (credence that a typical member of that taxon is sentient) × (expected welfare change per individual), and choose the policy that maximizes the sum. Applied to pesticide regulation, with something on the order of 10^18 insects alive at any moment (the exact figure doesn't matter for the question, I just need the order of magnitude) and a credence of even one in a million that a typical insect is sentient, the expected number of insect lives at stake swamps any human effect you could name. What I actually want to test is this: a student could object that this is just the mugging dressed up — the stranger who offers you an absurd payoff if you do him a small favour, resting on a probability he seems to have plucked out of the air. I want the question to force them to decide whether that objection lands, and if it lands, *where* exactly it lands. Is the problem the tiny probability, the enormous number, the fact that the probability is stipulated rather than measured, or something else entirely? And then, because this is a final and not a conversation, I want them to say what a repair would look like and what the repair would cost — if you put a floor under the probabilities you're willing to act on, they have to name another argument they'd be breaking along with it.

Could you write the exam question itself (closed book, 50 minutes, one essay), a model answer of around 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit moves, each with its mark allocation and a one-line note on how students typically botch that move? The A/B line I care about most is this: an A answer has to locate the disagreement at the level of what makes a credence *usable* in an expected-value calculation, not at the level of big numbers being spooky. Also please keep the model answer citation-free — they can't bring anything in anyway, and I don't want them memorizing names instead of arguments.
