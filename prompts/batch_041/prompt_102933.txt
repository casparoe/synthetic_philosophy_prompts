Ok so I run research at a small policy shop that puts out "what history teaches" briefs, and I keep getting into fights with my own writers about what we're allowed to say caused something. We mostly do financial crises and state breakdowns, and our running example is a mid-size regional bank that went under in 2011 after a fraud, a funding shock, and a regulator who was slow by about six weeks — and our writers keep claiming each of those three was, by itself, sufficient to kill the bank, which is either true and a nightmare for our sentences or false and we should stop saying it.

Before I read one more thing about causation in history, give me the desiderata. What does any theory of "X caused Y" in historical explanation actually have to deliver? I want:

- the cases it has to get right — two genuinely independent sufficient causes; a cause that only did its work because another cause had already happened; the trigger versus the background condition; the case where you remove any one of the causes and the outcome still happens; the trivial contributing cause that nobody would ever name in a sentence; and the case where a cause was necessary but not sufficient and another was sufficient but not necessary
- the distinctions it can't collapse — cause vs condition vs background; necessary vs sufficient vs contributing; "caused it" vs "made it more likely"; and the difference between picking a cause to tell a story and identifying one for counterfactual purposes
- whatever it can't contradict, including that the counterfactual can only ever be run in our heads, that historians have to select or they can't finish a sentence, and that we can't rerun the bank.
