I am a judge, and four times a year I preside over the citizenship ceremony in my courthouse. I have been giving more or less the same eight minutes of remarks before the oath for six years, and I want to rewrite them from scratch. Please write the new version.

Here is the occasion, so that you can pitch it properly. A hundred and fifty or so candidates are seated in rows, with their families behind them, a handful of officials to one side and a photographer at the back. The order never varies: I speak first, then everyone stands and takes the oath of allegiance together, out loud and in unison, and then we hand out the certificates. Many of the candidates will be following me through a headset or through a relative's whispered translation, so the sentences have to survive being half-heard; anything that depends on catching the first half of a clause is lost.

What I want the remarks to be about is what the oath actually is, and I would like that thought to shape the whole speech rather than decorate its edges. When they say it, they will not be telling us how they feel; they will be doing something, making a status that did not exist a moment earlier, and it takes hold only because the rest of us, the officials and the families and the country behind them, treat those words as making it. Two things follow, and I want the speech to be honest about both. The first is that the act works whether or not the person speaking it means it warmly: someone who takes the oath with a divided, tired or calculating heart is a citizen from that moment, and the defect is in the speaker's relation to the act rather than in the act. I would rather say that plainly and gently than pretend that a hundred and fifty people are all feeling the same thing at the same instant. The second is that if the words need our recognition in order to work, then the ceremony lays something on us as well as on them, and I want that said out loud without its turning into a threat or a sermon.

I have been reading Austin, and the distinction I keep returning to is between a speech act that misfires, because it is the wrong person or the wrong procedure and so never comes off at all, and one that comes off perfectly well and is nonetheless hollow, because the speaker does not mean it. I believe the oath is the second kind, and that this is the interesting thing about it rather than an embarrassment. But I do not want a lecture on Austin. No names, no jargon, no theory vocabulary: if anyone in the room can tell that I have been reading a philosopher, I have failed.

Two things I cannot say, and I would like the shape of the remarks to come from not saying them. I cannot promise these people that the country will turn out to deserve what they are undertaking; that is a promise I have no power to keep, which would be precisely the hollowness I am describing, and I would be doing the very thing I am warning them against. And I cannot pretend that this is the first oath some of them have taken, since some come from places where an oath to the state was extracted rather than given, and they know better than I do how cheap a promise can be made to sound. That should be acknowledged in a sentence or two as a fact about the room, not as a remark about their old countries and not as an invitation to feel anything in particular about it.

There is one thing I have never been able to settle, and I would like the remarks to leave it unsettled rather than quietly resolve it: the oath is recited in unison, so the "I" in it is spoken by a hundred and fifty people at the same moment, and I have never known whether that is a hundred and fifty separate acts or one act with a great many mouths. Do not explain the puzzle; just do not write anything that assumes an answer to it.

About eight hundred words. It will be read aloud from a single sheet, so give me continuous prose: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no numbered lists, no tables. And do not quote the text of the oath itself; the oath belongs to the moment after I sit down, and I do not want its wording in my mouth.
