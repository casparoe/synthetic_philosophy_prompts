Im a 4th-year PhD student in philosophy, and this term I'm TAing an upper-level seminar on theories of well-being (basically hedonism and its critics). The prof has dumped the final-exam essay question on me and wants me to hand in the question plus a model answer and a marking scheme, so that the four of us grading can actually agree with each other. Exam is closed-book, 90 minutes, this is the only question on the paper, worth 25 marks, so it has to carry alot of weight.

The problem: every year, the heterogeneity question ('what do pleasures have in common?') gets answered by a recitation -- Bentham thought pushpin was as good as poetry, Mill said higher pleasures, Feldman said it's all attitudinal -- and then they stop, having never once said why hedonism needs an answer. So I want a question they can't get through that way, and specific enough that a good student has to actually make moves rather than list positions.

Here's what I want the question to force:

- A precise statement of what the heterogeneity problem is, and why it's a problem for hedonism as a theory of well-being in particular -- i.e., what work the shared property has to do (for aggregating, for ranking, for explaining why pleasure is the good). Not just 'pleasures are diverse'.

- An assessment of two responses: (1) a single-feeling view, on which pleasures share an intrinsic hedonic quality; and (2) a no-common-core / family-resemblance view, on which 'pleasure' names a loose cluster and hedonism survives by going pluralist or disjunctive about the good.

- Both of those have to be run against two cases I'll put in the stem. Case A: tepid water -- the very same sensation is pleasant when your hands are cold and unpleasant when you're overheated. Case B, which I've invented for the exam and which I'm hoping isn't already a stock example: someone is miserable for the whole of a long, cold hike, complains the entire time, and afterwards reports that it was the best day of the year and wouldn't trade it for anything. Students have to say whether that was a pleasure at the time at all, and what each of the two responses is forced to say about it. I want them to notice that a no-common-core view handles Case B cheaply and pays for it somewhere else, while the single-feeling view has to either go relational (hedonic tone as a matter of how the state strikes the subject in her circumstances) or deny that the hike was pleasant.

Marking scheme out of 25, with a clear line between an A and a B: name the specific moves a top answer has to make, and the specific mistakes that should cost marks. Things I'd want in there: not collapsing the metaphysical question (what do pleasures share) into the axiological one (what makes pleasure good); noticing that Case A only threatens an intrinsic-quality view if you assume hedonic tone is intrinsic to the sensation; not letting them get away with 'the two views are just different levels of description'; and something about whether hedonism can go disjunctive without ceasing to be one theory of value -- if the answer to 'what's good' is 'sensory pleasure or attitudinal pleasure or...', has the view actually earned anything? Add moves I've missed, I'm not confident I've got the list right.

No citations anywhere in the question or the model answer, please -- it's closed book, they can't look anything up, so don't make any part of it depend on knowing who said what.

Model answer around 700-800 words, written the way a strong student would write it rather than the way a textbook would, and make it contestable in places -- I'd much rather it take a side and defend it than sit on the fence. At the end, tell me how confident you are in the model answer and in the scheme, and flag the single point you're least sure about. The last line of the whole thing should be \answer{...} with a short phrase inside the braces naming the one move that most cleanly separates an A from a B.
