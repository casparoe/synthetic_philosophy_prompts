I'm in a reading group on chance and credence and I got stuck with the write-up nobody wanted, which is: what would any adequate account of the deference relation between credence and objective chance actually have to deliver? So give me the desiderata, not the doctrine. I don't want "the Principal Principle, obviously" -- I want the list of things a theory of this link has to get right, the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it can't blur (say, between a chance being unknown and a chance being inadmissible, or between chance as a feature of the world and chance as a summary of evidence), the constraints it can't violate, with the case behind each requirement. Mark which ones are non-negotiable and which are just house taste, tell me which single requirement does the most to rule candidates out, and -- this is the part I actually want -- flag any pair of requirements you think can't be satisfied together. My own suspicion is that whatever makes a chance objective enough to be worth deferring to is exactly what lets your evidence undermine it, so that the thing you're supposed to defer to can be something you've already got evidence against, but I'd like to know if thats a genuine tension or just me being tired at 1am.

Here's the case I keep chewing on and I want it treated as a test, not a footnote. A friend works on weather forecasting; the operational "probability of rain" comes out of an ensemble of deterministic simulations run from slightly perturbed initial conditions, so the number is basically "how many of my runs said rain." She calls it the chance of rain. I can't tell whether that's a chance I'm meant to defer to the way I defer to a coin's bias, or just a very good summary of somebody's uncertainty, and it feels like the answer should fall out of the list rather than the other way around. Same for a calibrated actuarial table, or a casino's house edge. If the honest answer is that a theory of deference simply has no jurisdiction here because there's no objective chance in a deterministic system, fine -- say so, but then draw the boundary for me, because a list that leaves the jurisdiction unspecified is only half a list.

And be blunt if I've set this up badly. It's possible "objective chance" is a term of art different people use for incompatible things -- the thing that makes frequencies stable, the thing a good physical theory outputs, the thing an ideal agent would know -- in which case the right answer isn't one unified desiderata list, it's several, one per notion, and I'd rather hear that than get a tidy list that papers over the split. Where it's genuinely contested, don't hedge into mush: give me your own verdict and say how confident you are in it.
