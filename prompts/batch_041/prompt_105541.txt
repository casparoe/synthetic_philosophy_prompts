I'm putting together the joint statement for an adversarial collaboration on money pumps and diachronic rationality, and I'd like you to draft it. The two camps have been circling each other for years without converging. On one side, the exploitability camp: they think money-pump arguments show that cyclic or otherwise incoherent preference sets are irrational, and that this is basically the whole point of a pump. On the other side, the resolute-choice camp: they think a pump at most shows you can be exploited under certain setups, and that rationality doesn't require your choices across time to add up to a single coherent ranking. Both are serious positions, neither is a strawman, so write it so that both of them could actually put their names to it.

Here's the concrete case I want the document to be about, because the disagreement gets sharper on it than on the textbook three-good cycle. Call her Rae. She has three options -- three jobs, say, differing in pay, hours and commute -- and her preferences run in a cycle: she prefers B to A, C to B, and A to C, so no ranking of the three fits her pairwise preferences. A pump is available: each adjacent swap costs her a $5 fee, and she can be walked A to B to C back to A, landing at A minus $15, and then walked again. Now, three wrinkles I want treated seperately rather than lumped together. (i) The fast pump: all three swaps are offered in one sitting and she can see the whole sequence coming. (ii) The slow pump: she gets one offer a day, in an order she can't anticipate, and never sees the sequence as a sequence. (iii) The dispersed pump: the three swaps are offered by three different people, none of whom knows about the others, so there's no single exploiter and no conspiracy.

What I want the document to settle, or at least to seperate cleanly: does the pump show that Rae's preferences are irrational, or only that she's exploitable under certain information conditions? Does the sophistication reply -- she foresees the pump and simply refuses the first trade -- defeat the argument, or does refusing just relocate the cost, since she has to forgo an option she prefers? Is exploitability by an ideally informed trader even the right criterion of rationality at all, or is that the real crux hiding under the other one? And does the dispersed pump do any independent work, or is it just the fast pump with extra steps?

Structure it as a numbered agreed-claims section -- what both camps can sign about the case, about what a pump is, about what it does and doesn't establish -- followed by a disagreements table, where each camp's view is stated in wording that camp would actually accept for itself. Then the cruxes. I suspect one is whether exploitability is a rationality criterion and another is whether the cost of self-commitment or refusal counts against the preferences or against the situation, but tell me if I've got the cruxes wrong. For each crux, give the case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. And if any part of this fight is really just about words -- say, about what each side is using "irrational" to mean -- say which part and why. Don't hand me a verdict on who wins; I want the thing both of them could sign.
