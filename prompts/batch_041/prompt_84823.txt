A university's registrar's office has, after a faculty vote, adopted a dialetheist policy: certain contradictions in the academic regulations are now treated as true. Draft the deadpan FAQ page the office posts for students and advisers — numbered questions and answers, roughly 600 to 700 words, in the flat, defensive register of an actual registrar's office: cross-references to other sections, a list of forms to bring, an offer of a fifteen-minute appointment, a note that the policy was reviewed by the Faculty Senate.

The comedy has to come from the logic being handled correctly. The office must distinguish true contradictions from claims that are merely neither true nor false; must never suggest that every contradiction is true; must have a substantive answer to the student who infers that if the regulations are contradictory then anything follows, and that answer should be the paraconsistent one — the failure of explosion, the failure of disjunctive syllogism — rather than a joke about paperwork; and must explain, without itself becoming inconsistent, why the office has not turned into a trivialist institution in which every statement is true and no ruling can be contested. Work the doctrine into ordinary registrar business: prerequisites, late-withdrawal deadlines, grade appeals, tuition refunds. Include at least one case that is both upheld and dismissed on appeal, and at least one student question that exposes a genuine cost of the policy. The office should never concede an error and should never wink at the reader. Do not cite outside literature or name logicians; the office may cite its own invented memos, committees, and reports freely.
