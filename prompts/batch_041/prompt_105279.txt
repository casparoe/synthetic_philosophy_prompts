I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on consciousness and the self, and I'm assembling the take-home final. I want a single essay question on split-brain cases and the unity of consciousness that cannot be answered by reciting the familiar "one mind or two?" debate or by summarizing the standard commissurotomy findings; the students have already read that material and will simply regurgitate it. To force them onto fresher ground, I'd like the question anchored to a hypothetical case I've been turning over with a colleague. The patient — call her R. — has undergone a complete commissurotomy, but she also receives an experimental "callosal bridge," a prosthetic channel between her hemispheres that genuinely transmits information, though poorly: the signals are delayed, the bandwidth is low, and when the delay is deliberately exploited the two hemispheres can be made to disagree on simple perceptual and motor tasks. R. herself is unshaken: she reports feeling like one person, describes a single continuous stream of experience, and is distressed by any suggestion that she might be two. What I want the students to confront is not the crude headcount but a subtler question: what would have to be true of R.'s experience for the phrase "a single stream of experience" to be literally correct, and whether the unity of consciousness should be treated as an all-or-nothing property or as something that admits of degrees. They should have to say what work the notions of a "stream" and a "single subject" are doing, and to face the possibility that a system can issue unified behavior and a unified self-report while failing to be unified in whatever sense actually matters.

Please draft the exam question as it would appear on the paper, a model answer of about 700–800 words, and a marking scheme worth 25 marks built around five must-hit points, giving each point's mark value and a one-line note on how candidates typically get that point wrong. I'd like the marking scheme to separate an A from a B answer by naming the specific moves a top answer must make — for instance, keeping the unity of consciousness distinct from the unity of agency, treating R.'s self-report as evidence to be explained rather than as a verdict, and taking a defensible position on whether partial connectivity yields partial unity — and by naming the specific confusions that should cost marks. Assume a 90-minute exam and that students have the reading list but no internet access.
