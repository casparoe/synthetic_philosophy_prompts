I'm a novelist — three books out, the last one set in 1870s Yorkshire, and I'm about three years into a fourth that keeps stalling, and I've finally worked out that the stalling isn't a plot problem, it's a philosophy problem. There's a scene I keep writing and deleting where one of my characters does something my 2020s self finds frankly monstrous, but inside her world it's completely unremarkable, no one blinks, and I can't decide whether my discomfort is a bug in the draft or the entire point of the book. So I've been reading around the whole presentism versus anti-presentism thing in the philosophy of history, the argument about whether historians can ever escape the standards of their own time, and honestly I think everyone in that debate is treating presentism as if it's one single thing, and it isn't. My half-formed hunch is that there are at least two axes tangled up together: the standards we need just to make a past agent intelligible at all — the categories of motive and reason we have to presuppose before we can even describe someone as acting rather than merely happening — and the standards we use to grade them, to approve or condemn. And maybe a third, the standards of what even counts as a satisfying explanation of why someone did what they did. I've been calling it the two-clocks problem, because I suspect you can't read them on your clock without losing them, and you can't read them on theirs without losing the ability to say anything true about them. What I can't tell is whether the intelligibility axis is genuinely inescapable while the grading axis is at least partly escapable, or whether it's the other way round, or whether "escapable" is even the right word for either.
