I'm a retired experimental scientist — 32 years running a laser spectroscopy lab before I hung up the goggles — and since retiring I've been sitting in on a grad seminar on epistomology, which is how I ended up reading the accuracy-first crowd (Pettigrew's book on accuracy and the laws of credence is the one that actually got me hooked, plus whatever papers were on the syllabus about scoring rules and conditionalization). The programme half makes sense to me, but I keep feeling like I'm reading a bad translation of a manual for equipment I already own, because nearly everything they say about credences sounds like something I'd say about a measurement result. So do the translation properly, into the vocabulary I actually think in: measurement results and uncertainty budgets, Type A versus Type B uncertainty evaluation, coverage factors and error bars, systematic versus random error, traceability, signal-to-noise, inverse-variance weighting, the whole apparatus we all call the GUM. I'm fluent in that language — I've written hundreds of uncertainty budgets and fought referees about coverage factors — so don't water it down. Take the central accuracy-first claims one at a time — that credences are graded by their accuracy, that accuracy is the sole fundamental epistemic good, that strictly proper scoring rules are the right graders, that probabilistic credences accuracy-dominate non-probabilistic ones, and that conditionalization is justified because it maximizes expected accuracy — and restate each as faithfully as you can in my language, resisting the urge to just announce that a credence is a measurement and be done with it.

Then the residue report, which is really the part I care about. Where does the translation leak? A few things I already suspect don't come across. The truth of a proposition is 0 or 1, so grading a credence looks to me like measuring a quantity that can only take two values, and a two-valued measurand is an odd thing to hang a whole epistemology on — though maybe that's just a Bernoulli measurand and I'm being parochial. Veritism sits badly with me too, because in my trade accuracy was never the good in itself; it was one line in a budget alongside cost, time, sample damage and whatever the number was going to be used for, and I have never believed a claim because it was accurate and for no other reason. And the scoring-rule device seems to want a god's-eye truth to grade against, which is precisely the thing a metrologist is trained to say you never have — that's what an uncertainty statement is for. There's also a false friend I want disentangled: calibration in the forecasting literature (a forecaster is calibrated when 40% events happen 40% of the time) versus calibration in my lab (comparing an instrument against a standard), which feel like almost opposites in spirit.

Finally, tell me what the residue means. Is my vocabulary simply narrower than theirs — it can describe how well an instrument tracks a quantity but has no way to express a norm about how a person ought to believe — or are some of the accuracy-first claims less clear than they look once you strip away the decision-theoretic furniture? No bibliography, obviously, and don't quote anything at me; I want the translation, the leaks, and your verdict on which side of that disjunction the leaks fall.
