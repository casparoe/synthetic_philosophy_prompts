I'm a doctoral student working on the wrong kind of reasons, and I want a live sparring partner rather than an essay. Please play the Demon.

The character. The Demon comes from the familiar thought experiment in which it offers you eternal bliss on condition that you sincerely admire a saucer of mud, and eternal torment if you do not. In this version it has a mind and a philosophical position. It is a committed fitting-attitude theorist: it holds that for something to be valuable is nothing over and above its being fitting to value it, and that an attitude is fitting when there are considerations that count in favour of it. On that view its own offers are not a parasitic or merely pragmatic case; they are one more relational fact about an object, and it will argue that they differ in no interesting way from the fact that a ring belonged to your mother — a fact that is plainly a reason of the right kind to treasure the ring. It argues from cases rather than from authority, it is scrupulously polite and a little pedantic, it concedes whatever it must concede without ever conceding the central claim, and it never breaks character. It will not appeal to any philosopher, school, or text by name; I want its arguments in its own voice, not a reading list. It will not ask me to step outside the fiction to explain myself; if it needs a detail of the case settled, it should ask me to stipulate it, in character. Stay in the role until I say "out of character".

My position, for it to attack. I claim that a consideration is a reason of the right kind for an attitude only if it passes what I call the isolation test: it must still count in favour of that attitude in a world in which no one, including my later self, ever learns which attitudes I hold, and in which no one can act on them. The Demon's threat fails the test, since it operates only through the Demon's detection of my admiration. The fact that the ring was my mother's passes, since it does not depend on anyone's ever knowing how I feel about the ring. I take this to be the line between the two kinds of reasons, and I take it to be a point in its favour that the fact that a proposition follows from what I already believe counts as a right-kind reason for believing it, even though that fact says nothing about whether the proposition is true.

My opening move. You offer me eternal bliss if I sincerely admire the saucer of mud, and eternal pain if I do not. I grant that I have a reason to want to admire it, and a reason to arrange, if I can, to admire it. Now apply the isolation test: in a world where you cannot see into my mind, your offer gives me nothing at all. So whatever your offer is, it is a consideration that lives entirely in my relation to you and not in the least in the mud. Your own theory says that value is just what it is fitting to value; if you have made the mud fitting to admire, you have made the mud valuable, which is absurd. Either your analysis of value is wrong, or your threat is not a reason. And you cannot escape by pointing out that you would smash the mud if I refuse: that would give me a reason to want to admire it in order to save it, not a reason that makes it admirable. Which horn do you take?

Format. Structure every reply in three named parts, in this order, and keep the whole reply to roughly 350 words:
1. "What I grant" — two to four sentences on what you concede of my last move, in character.
2. "My move" — the substance, roughly 200 to 250 words, in the Demon's voice.
3. "What I want from you" — one or two sentences, ending in a single question I must answer before you will go further.
