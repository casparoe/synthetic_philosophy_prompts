I retired some years ago from a career in an experimental science, and I have spent much of my reading time since on moral philosophy and on the historiography of the Holocaust. I have arrived at the point where I would like the dispute over perpetrator conduct handled the way I was trained to handle a contested causal claim: as a decomposition with weights attached, to be probed by counterfactual perturbation, rather than as a contest between rival theses. The dispute in question is the familiar one between the situational reading associated with Browning, on which men of unremarkable background and unremarkable convictions could be brought to shoot unarmed civilians by the pressures of the position they occupied, and the cultural reading associated with Goldhagen, on which the killing was the work of a hatred already prepared in the surrounding society and needing only the opportunity. Having read on both sides, I cannot say which carries the greater explanatory burden, and I have come to suspect that the question is badly posed when it is put as a contest between them.

What I would like from you is a weighted decomposition, not an essay. The case to be explained is the conduct of a battalion of middle-aged reservists, drawn from a single German city, assigned in occupied Poland to the shooting of Jewish civilians. Below are nine candidate factors. Assign each a weight between 0 and 1, to two decimal places, with the weights summing to 1; give each factor a plausible range as well as a point value, since I would rather see the uncertainty than have it concealed; and sort the nine into four tiers of importance, labelled central, substantial, minor, and negligible.

1. Authority and legality: the order arrived through a state apparatus whose right to command was not seriously in question, so that declining would have registered as insubordination rather than as conscientious refusal.

2. Peer accountability: the men acted in the view of comrades whose good opinion they needed, and the salient contrast, as they experienced it, was not between killing and not killing but between standing with the others and letting them down.

3. Pre-existing hostility toward the victims: an animus supplied by the surrounding culture, at a strength sufficient to make the killing appear right, or at least not a wrong that required an excuse.

4. Cumulative brutalization: prior exposure to violence in the same or an adjacent theatre, so that killing had lost its novelty and much of its physiological cost.

5. Division of labour: the work parcelled out so that most men executed a single step of an operation whose overall shape they were never obliged to hold in view.

6. The social price of declining: the disposition of the men to read whatever latitude they were given as a test of loyalty rather than as a genuine permission to withdraw, so that the binding constraint on participation was social rather than disciplinary.

7. Career and material interest: the ordinary preference not to damage one's standing with superiors, one's pay, or one's prospects of being posted somewhere worse.

8. The ratchet: the unit's own past conduct setting the baseline for the next act, so that no single decision ever presented itself as the decision to become a killer.

9. The after-hours life of the unit: drinking, talk, and shared recollection that converted the killing from a private atrocity into a common and discussable activity.

Along with the weights, tiers, and ranges, I want the following.

(a) One sentence per factor stating the consideration that moves it up or down.

(b) A classification of each factor as enabling, licensing, or motivating: as making the killing physically possible, as making it permissible in the men's own eyes, or as making it likely. Then say which of the three kinds carries the greatest aggregate weight on your figures.

(c) The single factor whose removal would most reduce the outcome, together with a judgement on whether that removal is historically coherent, that is, whether the factor could have been absent while the others were as they were.

(d) The pairs of factors that are most substitutable, where deleting one would merely transfer weight to the other rather than alter the outcome.

(e) What would have to be true for the ranking to flip to the other side of the Browning–Goldhagen dispute, and what kind of evidence would settle the matter.

(f) An explicit note on where the additive picture misleads, because the factors plainly interact, and which interaction you would most want to test.

Finally, recompute the weights under three modifications, reporting only the weights that change and one line of explanation for each. First, three or four of the men's comrades have already declined to take part and have suffered nothing worse than transfer to other duties, so that the price of declining is visibly small. Second, the men arrive with no culturally supplied hatred of the victims beyond a diffuse and unremarkable prejudice. Third, the same killing is carried out by crews who never see the people they kill.

Close with a sentence naming the weight you would most expect to be wrong and in which direction, and a sentence on what your figures imply about how much of ordinary decency should be credited to persons and how much to circumstances.
