I'm putting together a little interview series for a reading group I run on decision theory and existential risk, and next month's guest is a philosopher who's been arguing that the right response to acausal threats — the kind where some agent you can't causally affect, or who can't causally affect you, tries to extract concessions by predicting your decision — is to adopt what she calls a surrogate goal: a goal you don't actually care about, but which you commit to so that threats against your real goals lose their leverage. She thinks this is different from ordinary precommitment because the threatener's leverage comes from prediction rather than causation, and she's got this line that surrogate goals are the acausal analogue of burning your bridges. I want to press her on the details rather than let her give the standard spiel. Can you draft eight to ten questions? I'd like at least a couple that get at whether adopting a surrogate goal is itself a causal act that reintroduces the very causal channel she says isn't there; one on how a surrogate goal is supposed to be stable under reflection and self-modification, since if she can later change her mind the threatener can predict that; one on whether the threatener, being smart, can just predict her adoption of the surrogate goal and route around it; and one on how this differs from the acausal trade literature, where agents cooperate rather than threaten.

For each main question, give me a likely answer she'd give and a follow-up that makes that answer harder to use. She's definately good at saying that's a different problem, so I want the follow-ups to close that escape hatch. Also maybe one question about whether surrogate goals could be used defensively by a weaker party against a stronger one, or whether they only work when the two sides are roughly matched in predictive power. And I'm curious whether she thinks a surrogate goal has to be something the agent actually comes to care about, or whether it can be a purely strategic fiction that the agent never internalizes — because if it's the latter, I don't see how it's supposed to fool a predictor that's modeling her algorithm rather than her surface behavior.
