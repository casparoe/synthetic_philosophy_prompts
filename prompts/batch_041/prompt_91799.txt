I'm a retired chemist — 32 years of running instruments and putting error bars on things — and I've spent the last couple of years reading philosophy of probability, which I assumed would be the easy part and is definately not. There's a dispute I can't get past, and what I want from you is a diagnosis of it rather than a verdict on it.

The dispute is whether a rational person's degree of belief in something has to be a single number, or whether it can be a spread — say, that the next coin comes up heads, somewhere between 0.3 and 0.7, with nothing inside that range singled out. One side says no, you must have a point: if you're rational then you have some definite degree of belief, and if you claim to have a range you're either being coy about the number or you're really reporting two things, a lower bound and an upper bound, while having no view at all in between — which isn't a state of belief so much as the absence of one. The other side says the range just is the honest state: the evidence often supports a whole family of numbers, picking one out of the air would be arbitrary, and the range isn't ignorance but a real doxastic state with its own logic and its own rules for how it should change when you learn things. There's a case I keep seeing mentioned where learning something that looks irrelavent to the question at hand makes your range get wider instead of narrower, which strikes me as either a deep problem or a curiosity, and I honestly can't tell which.

What gets me is that the papers don't converge. The point camp runs arguments about what an interval commits you to in betting scenarios, and about what an interval-holder can know about her own credences, and concludes that she's either incoherent or not really an agent with opinions at all. The range camp replies that those arguments assume what's in dispute — that a belief has to be measurable by what you'd bet — or that they show you need to be able to act, not that you need to be a point. Then the point camp says that if you're not a point you can't even say what you'd take, and around we go.

My hypothesis is that this isn't about probability at all but about what a mental state has to be to count as a belief — whether a belief is a definite thing that shows itself in choices, or whether it's more like a set of possibilities you haven't ruled out. But I can see two other readings. Maybe the two sides are answering different questions under one heading — one about what the evidence supports, one about how an agent should act — with a single word, credence, doing double duty. Or maybe there's a real formal question buried in it: some theorem about whether an agent can have everything each side wants at once.

So tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, using what each side actually says rather than a survey of the field. If it's verbal, say which word is doing the damage. If it's about which cases count as data, say which cases. If it's bedrock, say so and tell me why no argument should be expected to move it. And then: what would count as progress here? A distinction, a theorem, a decision to stop asking one of the two questions — or nothing, and we should stop waiting for the literature to converge?
