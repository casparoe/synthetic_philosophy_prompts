I am a second-year philosophy student who has been asked to run one of the tutorial sections for the first-year module on mind and meaning next week, and the topic I have been handed is Russell's theory of definite descriptions. The students will have had a single lecture on it already, so they will have heard the phrase 'quantificational analysis' and will probably have written down the expansion into clauses, but I am told that most of them cannot say what problem the theory was built to solve. The convenor has asked me to run the section so that the theory looks like an answer to a puzzle rather than a formula to memorise. I have fifty minutes, a whiteboard, about twenty students, several of whom are taking the module only to satisfy a distribution requirement and none of whom have done any formal logic; there is no set reading and I have been told explicitly not to assign one. I am taking a third-year philosophy of language course myself this term, so I am comfortable with the material and do not need it explained to me. What I want from you is not an explanation of the theory but a plan for the explanation: the order in which the components have to be introduced, and, for each step, what earlier step it depends on, so that the motivation stays visible at every point instead of being saved for the end.

For the carrying example I had wanted to avoid the standard King of France case, which some of them will already have met and which makes the whole thing look like a party trick about fiction. The example I would prefer to build on is an ordinary accusation from a group chat: someone writes that the person who took their charger from the library should return it, when in fact it is not clear that any single person took it, or even that anyone did. I am drawn to it because the sentence is plainly meaningful and seems to be making an accusation, and yet nothing satisfies the description, and I want the students to feel that tension before I hand them Russell's way out. But I am not confident the example can carry the entire arc, and I suspect it will fail me once we reach negation and the ambiguity of 'the person who took the charger did not return it'. Tell me whether to keep it, replace it, or keep it for the opening and switch to something else later, and what exactly the carrying example must have and must lack if it is to do the job.

I would also like the misconceptions folded into the plan rather than listed separately: I am fairly sure they will read the theory as a claim about what speakers mean rather than about truth conditions, that they will conclude a failed description makes a sentence meaningless rather than false, and that they will treat 'the' as a disguised name; for each, I want to know exactly where in the sequence I should head it off, and why that point and not an earlier or later one. There is one further question I keep being asked and do not know where to put: we routinely use descriptions like 'the table' when there are several tables in the room, so uniqueness appears to fail yet the description works perfectly well. Should I raise this, and if so where, or will it derail the section? Give me the full sequence, a single closing question that will show me whether the idea has landed rather than been parroted back, and a short list of what I should openly set aside at this level and say so out loud.
