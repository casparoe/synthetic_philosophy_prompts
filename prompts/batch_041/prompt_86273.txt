I host a small decision-theory podcast, and next weeks guest is a philosopher whos spent years arguing that the absent-minded driver problem has been misdiagnosed. Quick reminder of the setup in case it's drifted: you're driving home, there are two intersections that look identical and you can't tell which one you're at, and the amnesia is total — you dont even remember having made any decision. You want to get off at the second one. Exit at the first, payoff 0. Exit at the second, payoff 4. Blow past the second and stay on the road, payoff 1. The standard answer everyone gives is that you should exit with probability one third at each intersection (equivalently continue with two thirds), which gets 4/3 in expectation. Her line is that the number isnt the interesting part — the puzzle only looks like a paradox if you assume the driver is choosing an *act* at the node he happens to be at. Ask what he should do *here* and you get a recommendation he can't carry out (continue now, because you'll exit at the next one — except the next one is indistinguishable from this one, so there's no doing that). Her fix is to model the driver as choosing a *disposition*, or policy, once: continue with probability two thirds. Then the answer falls out with no new decision theory required; causal decision theory plus a proper account of what's actually being chosen is enough. She also likes to say the sharpest fact about the puzzle is that node-by-node reasoning makes the driver do worse than simply never exiting at all (never exiting gets you 1; the unimplementable plan nets you 0), and that no other puzzle puts the failure of that reasoning quite so starkly.

Draft me eight to ten questions that make her work rather than restate the thesis. I need at least one question each on:

- what 'adopting a policy' could even mean for an agent who by construction cannot remember adopting anything. Is the policy a state of the driver at the intersection, or a fact about how the driver is built? If it's the latter, in what sense is it *his* choice rather than a design feature we're evaluating?
- whether the randomization is a rational choice or merely a hedge, and what happens if the driver is a deterministic device that can't randomize at all — then the best implementable policy is never exit, payoff 1, so does the 4/3 answer make rationality hinge on a physical contingency about coin-flipping?
- whether her policy move is just the pick-the-best-whole-mapping-from-situations-to-actions view (the updateless / functional decision theory family) wearing a new name, and if it isn't, what work the word policy does that whole mapping doesn't.
- who the agent is: are the two intersections one chooser or two? And does the puzzle only go through if the driver cares about the trip as a whole rather than node by node — if you make the payoffs local to each intersection, does her diagnosis survive or does the puzzle just dissolve?
- the unimplementability point. She says the naive recommendation can't be carried out; isn't that true of basically every imperfect-recall problem? If so, what's the general principle she's actually after, and can she state it without it collapsing into just be sophisticated about your future selves?

For each of those, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so I'm not stuck if she takes the obvious exit. End the sequence with one question about what would count against her diagnosis — what variant, formal result, or intuition would make her stop thinking act-versus-policy is the right way to carve the puzzle. No what-drew-you-to-philosophy filler, and dont have me ask her who said what in the literature; I can't check that live and it isn't the point.
