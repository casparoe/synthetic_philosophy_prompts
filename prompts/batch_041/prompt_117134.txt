"People say Malthus predicted that population would outrun the food supply, and that since then the population has grown enormously and food production has grown even faster, so he was wrong. Others say he was right but early. Using what philosophy of science says about testing, falsification and the rescue of theories, explain when it is legitimate to say a theory was 'right but early' instead of refuted, and say what a theory would have to do to earn that treatment. Use Malthus as your worked example."

And here's the answer, from a retired schoolteacher who's been coming for a year:

"Malthus's argument has two parts. Population, when it isn't held down, grows geometrically, while food supply can at best grow arithmetically, so unless something keeps numbers down — famine, disease, war, which he called the positive checks, or restraint and delayed marriage, which he called the preventive checks — people get pushed back down to the level the food can support. So the prediction is that any improvement in living standards gets eaten up by extra people.

On the face of it that failed. Since he wrote, the population has multiplied several times over and food production has grown faster than population, at least in most places. But I don't think that refutes the theory, and the reason is Duhem's point, usually called the Duhem-Quine thesis: you never test a theory on its own, you test it together with a bundle of auxiliary assumptions — about farming techniques, about what counts as food, about trade, about what people do with extra income. When the prediction fails, the failure tells you that something in the bundle is wrong, not which thing. In Malthus's case the auxiliaries that broke were the ones about agricultural technology, which he couldn't have anticipated (synthetic fertiliser, mechanised farming), and about fertility, because he didn't expect people in rich countries to choose to have fewer children. So the core claim — that population is limited by the means of subsistence — is untouched, and the 'right but early' verdict is legitimate here.

Popper's idea of falsifiability is what makes this hard to police. Popper said a theory is scientific only if it forbids something, and that the way to tell science from pseudo-science is that a scientific theory sticks its neck out. Malthus's theory does stick its neck out — it says that if you improve the food supply, population will rise and swallow the gain — so it isn't in the same boat as astrology. What makes the rescue easy is that Malthus never put a date on it. A theory that says 'eventually' can always be saved, because the counter-evidence is always from the wrong period. Popper would say that an unfalsifiable claim of this sort is not just bad science but meaningless — it doesn't say anything about the world at all.

The interesting question is what separates a legitimate rescue from a cheat. Popper's later work on research programmes is helpful here: a research programme is progressive when the modification predicts something new that we can then go and check, and degenerating when it only explains away the awkward fact after the fact. So the test for the 'right but early' move is whether it adds content. If a Malthusian just says 'wait', that's degenerating. If they say the pressure will show up first as food prices rising relative to wages and then as a fall in fertility, that's a real claim and we can go and look.

So my answer is that Malthus wasn't wrong, he was early, and the reason we can say that is that his auxiliaries failed rather than his core, plus the fact that his core still generates testable claims today."
