In the early 1960s, the Seattle Artificial Kidney Center faced a severe shortage of dialysis machines. To decide which patients would receive treatment, it established a lay committee—soon dubbed the God Committee—that ranked candidates using criteria such as age, sex, marital status, number of dependents, occupation, past performance, future potential, and community service. The committee's defenders described it as making necessary rationing decisions; its critics accused it of playing God and reducing human worth to social utility.

Analyze this episode through the lens of Schlick's view of the meaning of life. Schlick argues that the meaning of life is not a goal or purpose that life serves, but is found in the activity of living itself—paradigmatically in play and in youthful creative activity, where the activity is its own end and not a means to something beyond it. He thus rejects the idea that life's meaning is a matter of what a life contributes to some external end.

First, theory illuminates case: What conception of life's meaning is implicit in the committee's selection criteria? Is it a goal-directed, means-end conception—one that treats a life as meaningful insofar as it produces certain social goods? How does Schlick's view challenge that conception? If Schlick is right, does the committee's procedure become not merely morally questionable but conceptually confused—an attempt to measure something that is not a measurable quantity? What would Schlick say the committee should have done, if anything, given that some rationing decision had to be made?

Second, case tests theory: Does the episode confirm Schlick's view, or does it expose a limitation? For example, does Schlick's emphasis on play and creative activity leave him unable to say anything about tragic choices among lives, or does it imply that such choices are always a category mistake? Can the committee's criteria be reinterpreted in Schlickian terms—say, as an attempt to maximize the opportunity for play and creative activity—or does that misread Schlick's insistence that meaning is internal to the activity? Finally, consider whether the episode reveals a tension in Schlick's view: if meaning is found in the present activity, how can we compare lives at all, and what does that imply for any institution that must allocate scarce life-saving resources?
