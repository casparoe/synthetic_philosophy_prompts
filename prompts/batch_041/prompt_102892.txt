Ok so I've been going down a rabbit hole on this "imprecise probabilities" stuff and I think I've ended up with a view of my own, but I want to know how exposed it is. Here's my view: whenever someone says their credence in something is vague, or "somewhere between 0.3 and 0.6," that's never a real feature of their mind — it's just shorthand for a whole pile of ordinary precise credences they haven't bothered to seperate out, one per background assumption. So "imprecise credence" is bookkeeping, not its own kind of mental state. If I'm right, there should be no case where a person with one fixed body of evidence is rationally required to keep a spread of credences going rather than just pick a single number.

What would count against this? I want the arguments and cases that should actually move me, sorted by how hard they'd hit — especially any case where a person seems to genuinely have no fact of the matter about their own degree of belief, or where squashing it down to one number makes them do something obviously dumb that the spread view avoids. I keep seeing "dilation" mentioned (I think it's where knowing something extra makes you *more* unsure of the thing you cared about?) and I don't fully get it, so walk me through whether that's a problem for me or not. Betting and Dutch-book stuff too — I don't know if those cut for me or against me. And be honest with me: has my view just defined the problem away so that nothing could ever count against it? If so, say so, and say whether that's actually bad for a view like this. Also tell me which of the imprecise-probability people would themselves admit some of this counts against them, not just what the critics on the outside say.
