I'm on the grant panel of a small foundation and we've got about 1.8 million dollars to spread over three years, earmarked for conceptual and theoretical work on consciousness rather than new experiments. The framing we've landed on is basically Dennett's heterophenomenology: take what subjects say about their experience as the data, treat their reports as the utterances of a theorist's fiction rather than as transparent windows onto an inner show, and then build and test whichever intentional-stance story best predicts them. Most of the panel is happy with that as a slogan and then we immediately start disagreeing about what it actually commits us to, which is why I'm asking you rather than the panel.

So: what are the open questions here that most deserve the money, and in what order? Two constraints that I think should really bite. First, we want work that survives the no-report paradigms, the cases where you get the neural signatures people usually treat as markers of consciousness while the subject isn't reporting anything at all. If heterophenomenology is constitutively report-based, I want to know whether that's a feature or a bug, and which questions that forces to the front. Second, we care about whether the method scales to systems that can't fill in a questionnaire: animals, infants, and, yes, LLMs that will cheerfully narrate an inner life on request. I'd rather fund the questions that, if answered, would let us say something about those cases then the ones that just tidy up the original framing.

Please defend the ordering. For each question: why it's upstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers rather than neuroscientists, and what would move it up or down the list over the next couple of years. Don't give me a reading list or citations, I know the literature well enough and I want your reasoning anyway. And write it as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
