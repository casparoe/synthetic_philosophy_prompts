I'm preparing the opening session of a graduate seminar on Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and rather than summarize the three ways it says we can relate to the past, I want to read a poem that enacts them. Write me one, roughly twenty-five to thirty-five lines, in four movements. The first three should each take up a single concrete episode from the past — choose it yourself, something small and specific enough to be seen rather than a period or a civilization — and handle it the way the essay says monumental, antiquarian, and critical history respectively handle the past: the first reaching for the exemplary and the great, the second revering and preserving what is near, inherited, and its own, the third judging, breaking up, and condemning. The same episode, or at least the same object or scene, should recur in all three, altered by the stance taken toward it, so that the reader watches one piece of the past handled three ways rather than three different pasts. Each movement should carry its mode's characteristic temptation as well as its promise: the flattening of the singular into the merely exemplary, the reverence that turns the past into something that can no longer be lived with, the judgment that forgets it is passing sentence from inside the very inheritance it condemns. The fourth movement should not resolve these but stage what the essay leaves open — that life may need the capacity to forget and to transform the past rather than only to remember, judge, or preserve it — and it should be honest that this too may be one more stance rather than an escape from stances. Someone who knows the essay should recognize each mode without its ever being named: do not use the words monumental, antiquarian, or critical, and do not title or label the movements; let the verse do the distinguishing. Give me only the poem, continuous verse from first line to last, with no headings, section titles, bold, bullets, or prose commentary — the way I would read it aloud to open the class.
