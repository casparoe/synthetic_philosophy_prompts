I trained as an economist and now spend most of my time in philosophy of science, and before a workshop I want to write down where I stand on how models should be assessed so that people can push on it. Here is the package. First, Friedman's F-twist, roughly as he states it: the realism of a theory's assumptions is irrelevant to its assessment, and the theory is to be judged by the accuracy of its predictions. Second, a stronger corollary I am attracted to: the more wildly unrealistic the assumptions, the more likely the theory is to have latched onto something important, so falsity in the assumptions is not something to apologise for. Third, a model earns its keep only if it isolates the causally relevant factors in the target system. Fourth, idealisation and approximation are different things, and only the former is licensed by the first two commitments: an assumption that distorts by exaggeration is acceptable, whereas one that is merely an inaccurate estimate of a real quantity is a defect. Fifth, a verdict I am attached to: the zero-transaction-cost assumption in some models is harmless, while the representative-agent assumption is not, even though both are plainly false. Sixth, when I put a model to work making a welfare claim rather than a prediction, I do not think the falsity of its assumptions can be waved away. I suspect that not all of this hangs together. Tell me which commitments genuinely pull against each other and which only appear to, order the real tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what that would cost me. Please answer in continuous prose: no headings, no lists, no bold, just something I can read straight through.
