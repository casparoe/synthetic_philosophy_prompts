Longtime lurker on the rationalist blogs, first time posting. I've got a pile of gut verdicts about "what someone actually meant" when they say a thing, and I cant tell if there's a real principle behind them or if I'm just pattern-matching. Enough sequences absorbed to know I should report the gut before theorizing, so here it is:

1. Someone says "can you reach the top shelf?" and I hand them the jar. They weren't asking about my arm length. They meant: get me that.
2. "Some of the applications came in late." I hear: not all of them did.
3. A rec letter that says the student "shows up on time and takes legible notes." I hear: this student is bad and the writer knows it. Every word is praise; that's the joke.
4. "They had a kid and got married" — kid first. "They got married and had a kid" — wedding first. But if I'm just listing two unrelated facts, the same words carry no order at all.
5. "It'll take about three hours" — ballpark. "It takes three hours to get there" — I kind of mean exactly three, or at least that's the number to plan around. Not sure that's the same phenomenon as #2.
6. "Some of them passed — in fact, all of them did." The not-all reading evaporates, fine.
7. Logic class: "some numbers are prime." No not-all anywhere, just: at least one. Hiring meeting: "some of the candidates can code." Absolutely not-all. Same word, same cooperative speaker, different room.
8. Weird one: a support chatbot writes "your refund has been processed." Do I get to say it *meant* the refund is done, or did it just emit that? I want to say it meant it, which seems like trouble for any story that ties meaning to intentions.

#7 is the one I can't square. If the not-all in #2 comes from the word, why does it vanish in the logic class but survive in the hiring meeting? If it comes from the context, why does #3 feel like the writer *meant* the insult rather than merely hinted at it?

Tentative principle I'd offer: a speaker means whatever a reasonable hearer would infer on the assumption that the speaker is being relevant and helpful. But that predicts not-all everywhere a stronger alternative exists, and #7 says that's wrong. So either "relevant" needs sharpening or I need to split "what was said" from "what was meant" somewhere.

Can you give me the actual principle (or a short list of candidates), tell me which of my verdicts has to go, and say what the survivors predict for two cases I didn't list — someone who says "I believe the meeting is at three" when they're the one who booked the room, and the chatbot?
