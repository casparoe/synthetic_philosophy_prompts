Having spent the better part of four decades as an experimental physicist, and the three years since my retirement working my way through philosophy — first epistemology, then the philosophy of mind, and lately the philosophy of concepts — I have been invited by a small humanities institute to submit a short research proposal, of roughly two and a half thousand words, in support of a one-semester visiting fellowship. I have a half-formed project and would like your help turning it into a proper proposal.

Please begin by adopting the role of a philosopher of concepts and categorisation who is also at home in the computational modelling of perception and of letterforms — the tradition of Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project at Indiana, which set out to model the generation of stylistically uniform gridfonts. Think carefully before you commit to a framing. Open with a brief brainstorming section, in a few bullet points, in which you set out the two or three ways my question could be posed and the trade-offs among them; only after that write the proposal proper. I do not want a bibliography or a literature review by citation — I shall supply the references myself — though you may of course refer to positions and arguments by name.

My half-formed idea is this. The Letter Spirit problem — what makes every 'a' an 'a'? — looks like a question about categories, and it is usually answered by appealing to some structural essence or prototype of the letter. But the letter 'a' does not have a single topology. It occurs in a single-storey form, essentially a closed bowl with a stem, and in a double-storey form, a lower bowl surmounted by a small counter and an arch; the same bifurcation afflicts 'g'. So whatever 'a'-ness consists in, it cannot be a single structural skeleton, since two things that differ in the number of enclosed regions can both be unmistakably 'a'. The letter also shades off into neighbouring shapes — the Greek alpha, the rotated or mirrored form, the degenerate scrawl — without any obvious threshold. I suspect the categories cannot be defined independently of the styles in which they are realised, and that "the essence of the letter" is really a family of invariants under transformations that preserve the style. As a physicist I am tempted by an analogy: that category membership is what remains fixed as one varies the stylistic parameters, in the way that physical quantities are defined as invariants under a gauge transformation. I do not trust this analogy and I want it tested rather than adopted.

Turn this into a proposal with a precisely stated question — I suggest something like whether the identity of a letterform category is specifiable as a set of structural invariants holding across all styles, or whether category membership depends constitutively on the style system in which a form is embedded — together with why the existing positions leave it open, the method, set out month by month for a single semester (I can program, so feel free to include the construction of a small generative model or parameter space alongside the conceptual analysis), what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing, and an honest assessment of what would make it too large for the time available. Please also say, in a short closing paragraph, whether the gauge-invariance analogy illuminates the problem or merely trades on a false parallel, since I would rather abandon it early than build on it.
