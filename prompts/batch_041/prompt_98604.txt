Trying to get my own view straight before a seminar and I want a consistency check rather than a summary, so here's the package. (1) Lewis's analysis of convention is basically right: a convention is an arbitrary regularity in behaviour that persists because everyone expects the others to conform and prefers to conform given that they do. (2) Conventions are the right model for social norms generally, including the ones that feel moral — promise-keeping, queueing, which side of the road we drive on. (3) Those norms are the product of evolutionary processes — imitation, reinforcement, trial and error in a population over time — with no designer and no one ever having worked out the reasoning. (4) What makes something a convention rather than just a good idea is that the alternative was equally available; the arbitrariness is the essential bit, not a side note.

Where does this actually come apart? I've got a nagging sense that (1) and (3) can't both be the whole story, since Lewis seems to need people to hold fairly rich mutual expectations of each other and evolution doesn't obviously hand you those, but I can't tell if that's a real problem or just a gap I could paper over. And I half-suspect (2) and (4) are fighting, because the norms that feel least arbitrary — the ones people will die for — are exactly the ones I'm most tempted to say are more than conventions. There might also be something wrong with (2) on its own: a convention is something you follow because others do, but a moral norm is supposed to grip you even when defecting would pay.

Don't just reassure me that it all hangs together. If something has to go, say which, and rank the tensions by how bad they are rather than listing them as equally bad — and tell me which ones only look like tensions. If I've misstated Lewis anywhere, push back on that before anything else.
