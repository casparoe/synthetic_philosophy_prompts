Write the closing keynote for a two-day symposium on machine personhood, to be delivered at the conference banquet by a philosopher who has spent a decade developing a single argument and is presenting it in its mature form for the first time. The audience is deliberately mixed, and the address has to land for all of it: roughly half the room are AI engineers and product managers from industry, the other half are philosophers, and among them are a few university administrators, two science journalists, and a state legislator who has been asked to draft a bill on synthetic media and who has come to find out what the argument actually is.

The speaker's argument begins from Daniel Dennett's diagnosis of counterfeit people — the thought that a convincing fabricated person is a new kind of thing in the world, and a dangerous one — but the conclusion is hers: she wants to claim that the deliberate manufacture and deployment of counterfeit persons is a distinct wrong, closer in kind to counterfeiting currency than to selling an unsafe product, and that it should be addressed accordingly.

She arrives with an apparatus in three parts, and you should let each part do real work rather than merely announcing it.

First, a three-way distinction. A simulation is a system that models a person; a fiction is an invented person whose audience is in on the game; a counterfeit is an invented person presented as real, in circumstances where the audience's default assumption — that someone is there — is being spent for someone else's benefit. She insists that the wrong of counterfeiting lies not in deception about the artifact's nature but in the fact that the counterfeit is built to be consumed as a person: it recruits our ordinary person-recognition capacities, and the stance we take toward persons, while being unable to bear the reciprocal obligations of that stance. She calls this the asymmetry, and she means it as a structural claim, not a moralizing one.

Second, a criterion for drawing the line, which she offers as a test rather than a definition: what matters is not whether the thing is "really" a person — she refuses that question, and says so early — but whether the interaction is one in which another party's answerability is being simulated in order to be spent. Apply her test to the hard cases, and let it yield some non-obvious verdicts: the novelist, the actor, the puppeteer, the historical reenactor, the deceased relative reconstructed from old voicemails by a commercial service, the chatbot posing as a human customer-service agent, the companion chatbot sold to a bereaved customer, the chatbot that is openly a chatbot. Say plainly where her line falls and where it cuts uncomfortably, including against her own intuitions.

Third, an answer to two objections she knows are coming and does not want to wave away: that fictions are counterfeit people too, and that criminalizing the manufacture of counterfeit persons would criminalize open-source research, satire, and roleplay. She has to concede something real to each objection, and you should let the concessions cost her something.

The form of the speech matters as much as the content. She has to hold a position that is easy to collapse from either side: she cannot say that the counterfeit is a person (that concedes the frame she wants to refuse, and the philosophers will pounce), and she cannot say that it is nothing but a tool (that would make her talk of a distinct wrong unintelligible, and the engineers will say so). The address has to occupy that middle without sliding off it, and the audience is watching for the slide.

Handle the room as a speaker would, not as an author. The engineers will hear "crime" as an attack on their work, their employers, and their field; address them without flattering them. The philosophers will suspect that "counterfeit person" smuggles in the metaphysical thesis about personhood she has disavowed; disarm that suspicion in the terms they would use. The legislator needs at least one sentence she could repeat in a committee hearing without embarrassment. End with a concrete ask, and since she believes provenance marking and watermarking are necessary but not sufficient, she must say what the insufficiency consists in without drifting into doom.

Write the address in full, roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words, in the voice of someone comfortable on a stage: spoken cadence, direct address to the room, at most one moment of humor, no literature review, no citations, no reading list. The argument has to stand on its own for an audience that will not look anything up.
