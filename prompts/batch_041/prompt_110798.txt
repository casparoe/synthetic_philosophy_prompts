Write a pantoum spoken by a counterpart, addressed in the second person to the one person whose counterpart he is.

The view the poem has to work with is this. On a concretist account of modality, the other possible worlds are not abstract representations or bookkeeping devices but universes as concrete as this one, and actuality is an indexical on all fours with here and now: every world is actual to its own inhabitants, and none is actual simpliciter. It follows that what it is for something to be possible for a person is not for there to be a world in which that person does it — a person is confined to one world — but for there to be a world containing someone sufficiently like him in the respects that matter: a counterpart, bound to him by resemblance and not by identity. The counterpart who wins the election is therefore not the man who lost it. He is someone like him, and that is the whole of the connection.

I want a poem that pays the price of that view rather than softening it. Constraints:

1. Form. A pantoum of four to six quatrains. In a pantoum the second and fourth lines of each quatrain return as the first and third lines of the next, and the closing quatrain takes up the first quatrain's third and first lines, so that the poem's last line is also its first. The recurrence has to do work rather than decorate: the same words come back in new company and so become different utterances, which is exactly what needs saying about the counterpart relation — resemblance without identity — and about the indexicality of "actual", whose reference moves with the speaker while the word does not.

2. Voice. The counterpart speaks; the addressee is a single person, addressed as "you", never a class or a crowd. He must not claim to be that person, and he must not be allowed to shrink into a copy, a portrait, a photograph, or a mere possibility of him either. He is as concrete as the one he addresses and his world is as real, so whatever pathos the poem has must come from separateness and not from unreality. This is a poem for a view on which he is nobody's shadow: keep spectral imagery away from him.

3. Refrain. One recurring line should turn on an indexical — actual, here, now, I — and the shift in what it picks out should become visible to a reader as the poem proceeds, without anyone in the poem explaining it.

4. The stanza that hurts. One quatrain must turn on the case in which the counterpart does the thing the addressee did not, or the reverse, and it must be plain from that stanza that nothing crosses between them: no transfer, no causal contact, no shared deed, no secret identity. Do not console the addressee at the end by making the two "really one" — that is the dodge the poem exists to refuse.

5. Closing. Since the form returns the opening line at the end, use that return to land the thought that "actual" in his mouth and "actual" in mine pick out different worlds, and that no one is standing anywhere from which one of the two is the real one.

6. Register. No naming of the theory or its proponents inside the poem, and none of the vocabulary of possible-worlds talk — no "possible world", "counterpart", "modal", "concrete". No lecturing, no argument in verse, no title, no epigraph, no notes. Lines of roughly speech length, rhyme only where it costs nothing. A reader who holds the view should find the poem right; a reader who has never heard of it should find it a good poem first and only afterwards work out what it was about.
