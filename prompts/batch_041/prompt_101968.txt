I want your credence in one specific metaphysical claim, together with the reasoning that generates it, term by term.

The claim: there is no possible world whose complete inventory consists of exactly two objects that are qualitatively identical in every respect. This is the two-sphere case Max Black constructed against the Principle of Identity of Indiscernibles: a world containing nothing but two iron spheres of the same size, mass, temperature, and surface composition, arranged symmetrically, with nothing else anywhere and no asymmetry in the world's laws. State your credence that such a world is impossible (equivalently, your credence that the Principle, so read, is a necessary truth).

Three stipulations, so that the question has a determinate answer rather than sliding between readings. First, read qualitative difference in the demanding sense: a difference counts only if it is grounded in pure, non-relational properties, where a property is pure only if its specification makes no reference to any particular individual. So neither the property of being identical to sphere A nor the property of standing in some relation to the other sphere counts as a qualitative difference. Second, let the world be static, and let its law be itself invariant under the interchange of the two spheres. Third, do not let the answer hinge on whether we call the spheres substances, bundles, or bare particulars; take those as competing hypotheses to be scored, not as presuppositions.

Now decompose. I expect at least these terms, though you should add whatever else you think belongs: (1) whether the Principle is a necessary truth at all, or only a contingent regularity, a regulative constraint on our concepts, or a truth about how we individuate rather than about how things are; (2) whether bare particulars, thisnesses, or haecceities are coherent, and if coherent, whether any exist; (3) whether the two-sphere scenario is genuinely conceivable in the sense that matters, and whether conceivability is any guide to possibility here; (4) whether relational structure, or the symmetry of the whole world, could ground a real difference between the spheres even when every relational description of each is the mirror image of the other; (5) whether the Principle of Sufficient Reason, or a general demand that identity facts be grounded in something, does independent work beyond the terms above; (6) whether the permutation symmetry of identical particles in quantum mechanics tells for or against the possibility of such a world, or is simply beside the point because it concerns states rather than individuals. Assign each a rough probability, say how they combine into your final number, and tell me which term your number is most sensitive to.

One wrinkle of my own, since I do not want the stock example recited back to me: suppose the undetectability of any difference between the spheres is guaranteed by the world's laws rather than merely by our ignorance. No possible observer inside that world could ever have grounds for asserting that this sphere rather than that one is the one that did something. My question is whether that nomically guaranteed undetectability makes the supposed difference unreal, or merely makes it undetectable. If your answer is that it makes it unreal, say whether the argument is verificationist in a way you would accept elsewhere, and if your answer is that it makes it merely undetectable, say what the difference is supposed to consist in given that nothing in the world can register it.

Finally, tell me what would move you. Which argument or consideration, if it turned out to be correct, would carry your credence across the midpoint in the other direction, and where in the chain above is your reasoning most likely to be wrong? I would rather have a decomposition with its weakest joint clearly marked than a confident verdict.
