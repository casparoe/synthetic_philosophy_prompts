I am a doctoral student in political economy and I moderate a small reading group that has spent the past several months on what we have all taken to calling trap arguments: cases in which a bad outcome persists because each party is responding sensibly to the others, so that no one involved either wants the outcome or could unilaterally avoid it. Two of the members have now been arguing past each other for long enough that the rest of us have quietly stopped attending. The first, call him A, is what I would describe as a trap realist. He holds that the multipolar-trap idea names a distinctive and systematically under-appreciated structural category, one that collective-action analysis, public-choice economics, and principal-agent stories all miss, and that the vocabulary of an impersonal force — the invisible hand's malevolent twin, a god who demands sacrifice from everyone and rewards no one — is doing genuine explanatory work rather than supplying atmosphere. The second, call her B, is a trap reductionist. She holds that every case offered as a multipolar trap is really one of three familiar things: a collective-action problem already fully captured by a game with stated payoffs, a bargaining failure with a fix available to someone, or an outcome that some party actually intended and is quietly profiting from. On her view the trap vocabulary adds nothing except a feeling of inevitability, and feelings of inevitability are precisely what a good analysis should be dissolving.

I want the two of them to produce the document they should have written a year ago, and I want to sign off on it before the next meeting. It should open with a numbered list of agreed claims: what a multipolar trap is, what the term does and does not commit its user to, which candidate cases both of them accept as at least initially plausible instances, and what both concede about the limits of the idea. It should then set out the residual disagreements as a table, one row per question, with columns for the question, A's formulation of his own view, B's formulation of her own view, the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of them — the case, argument, or evidence both agree would bear on that crux, and each side's prediction about it recorded in advance. Every disagreement statement has to be written in wording its own proponent would accept as fair, and every prediction has to be definite and in principle falsifiable; neither of them is allowed to escape into "it depends," and I do not want a verdict on who is right.

Three pieces of scaffolding from our own discussions should go into the document, since the argument keeps restarting because they never fix these. First, a trap-depth scale they should both define identically and then apply: depth 0, at least one party can exit or opt out unilaterally at negligible cost; depth 1, no unilateral exit, but the present parties can reach and enforce a binding agreement among themselves cheaply; depth 2, no cheap agreement among the present parties, but some external agent — a regulator, a hegemon, a court, a standard-setter — could impose the fix; depth 3, no exit, no self-enforcing agreement, no available external enforcer, and no credible commitment by anyone, so that the outcome is invariant across every feasible move available to every party. A's central claim is that genuine depth-3 traps exist and are common; B's is that depth 3 is empty, and that every apparent occupant of it collapses to depth 2 or lower once you look hard enough for the side payment or the enforcer. Second, a distinction between thin and thick traps: a trap is thin if the parties' preference orderings are fixed and the situation is fully characterized by a payoff matrix, and thick if the competition itself reshapes the parties' preferences or their conception of what counts as winning, so that the counterfactual question "would you all prefer to stop?" has no well-defined answer. Third, a distinction between exit-shaped and voice-shaped traps, where the only available move is to leave the game versus where the only available move is to play it harder. They should then use these to classify the cases they have been fighting about — overfishing, the race to develop and deploy frontier systems, the arms-race dynamics of military procurement, regulatory competition among jurisdictions, the publish-or-perish complex in the sciences, and the escalation dynamics of outrage-driven media — and say exactly where their classifications diverge and why. Where a disagreement turns on an empirical fact that neither of them can check from the armchair, they should say so explicitly, state what they would need to know, and write conditional predictions rather than guessing. Finally, the document should close with a short section identifying which of the remaining disputes are merely verbal — I suspect at least part of the quarrel over whether the trap vocabulary earns its keep is a disagreement about the usefulness of a label rather than about causal structure — and which are substantive, with a sentence of justification for each classification. The document should be self-contained: no citations, no attributions to authors, no reading list.
