I just jumped ship from a pure-maths PhD into an alignment team, and the project I've landed on is all about AI safety via debate — the setup where two models argue it out and a weaker judge, maybe a human or a small model, just picks whichever side it finds more convincing. I went in pretty gung-ho, but I keep tripping over the same worry, the "obfuscated arguments" one: a dishonest debater doesn't have to lie outright, it can just hand the judge a correct-sounding argument that's so tangled the judge can't actually check it, and then the judge can't tell the honest debater's rebuttal apart from a bluff. Half my team treats this as an engineering detail — tighten the protocol, add cross-examination, give the judge more rounds and a bigger budget, and the honest side can always drag the flaw into the open. The other half reckons that's wishful thinking, and that debate's whole selling point — that checking a claim is easier than finding it in the first place — quietly stops holding once the debaters are smarter than the judge.

Anyway, we've been argueing in circles for months and my lead has finally asked me to write the doc our two factions should have produced ages ago instead of sniping at each other. So please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the "protocol optimist" and the "complexity pessimist" about obfuscated arguments in debate. I want the claims both sides can honestly sign — about what debate is even meant to guarantee, what we're assuming the judge can do, and what counts as an "obfuscated" argument in the first place; then the precise disagreement, with each side's position written in wording that side would accept; then the cruxes, and for each crux a concrete test, argument, or toy case that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If you want a concrete case to hang things on, something like this might work: a judge on a fixed token budget, a dishonest debater who can pick among a pile of equally tangled candidate arguments, and an honest debater who has to rebut whichever one actually gets offered — but use your own if you find a better one. And if part of the row turns out to be verbal — say, both sides meaning different things by "the honest debater wins" — call that out and say which part it is. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table.
