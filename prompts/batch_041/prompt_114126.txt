On Saturday evening I have to give the toast at my museum's annual patrons' gala, and I would like you to write it for me. I am the director of a small regional museum; the gala this year doubles as the reopening of our Dutch and Flemish gallery, and the awkward fact hanging over the evening is that six weeks ago our conservation lab re-attributed the panel that has been the centerpiece of that gallery for forty years. It is not, as the catalogue has always said, a seventeenth-century original; the oak dendrochronology, the ground layer, and the pigment analysis all point to a forger working in the late 1920s, whose hand has since been identified in three other collections. The panel is still hanging — we have relabeled it rather than store it — but the family that gave it to us in 1971 will be at the gala, several of our largest donors will be at the gala, two art historians who know exactly what our lab found will be at the gala, and a reporter from the regional paper has been invited. I cannot be dishonest, I cannot insult the donors, and I cannot simply declare that the whole question is a matter of taste and move on, because that would be a lie of omission in the other direction.

What has made this worse is a long argument I had on Sunday with a friend of mine, a philosopher who works on the metaphysics of art and who spent most of the afternoon trying to convince me that my problem is not a problem at all. Her position, as nearly as I can state it, is that whatever value a painting has must be discoverable by a competent viewer in what that viewer can actually see — and if a forgery is indistinguishable from an original at every level of scrutiny, then any preference for the original has to be explained by snobbery, by investment, or by the mere pleasure of owning something scarce. She then pressed what she took to be the killing point. The forger had to solve the harder problem: the original painter simply painted the way he painted, whereas the forger had to reconstruct a dead man's manner from photographs and fragments and get every crackle and every craquelure right, under the constant threat of exposure. So if we say the original is valuable because of the achievement it embodies, we are committed to saying the forgery embodies the greater achievement — and nobody believes that, which shows, she said, that the achievement story is a rationalization. When I objected that we judge a painting against the standards of the tradition it was made in, so that our panel judged as a 1920s work is simply a lesser thing than a 1660s work would have been, she said that was circular: it assumes the conclusion that the date matters. And when I said that at least the wall label had changed, she said the label is the only thing that changed, which means the value was never in the object at all. I think she is wrong somewhere, but I could not say where, and I have to stand up in front of these people on Saturday.

Please write the toast. It should run four to five minutes as delivered, it should sound like a director thanking patrons rather than a lecture, and the philosophical idea has to do real structural work in it rather than being decoration: the toast has to locate the value of the original somewhere other than the flat look of the surface, and it has to meet the achievement objection on its own terms rather than dodging it — while staying intelligible to donors who have never read any philosophy and would be put off by jargon. It must never claim that the forgery is worthless as a painting and never imply that the family who gave it to us were fools. I would like the argument to be carried largely by what the toast chooses to dwell on and what it conspicuously declines to say.
