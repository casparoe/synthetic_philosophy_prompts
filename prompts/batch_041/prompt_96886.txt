Here's the controversy I want you to pick a side on. The standard textbook move is to say Zeno is basically solved by calculus: the distances 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ... converge, the limit of the partial sums is 1, so Achilles catches the tortoise, end of story. But a stubborn critic can push back that this only shows the *distances* add up to a finite total; it doesn't show how you actually *finish* infinitely many distinct sub-tasks, and arguably it just smuggles in the assumption that a continuum of instants can be traversed, which is the very thing in dispute. So the question: does the mathematical treatment of Zeno's paradoxes genuinely solve them, or does it merely re-describe the assumption that motion is possible? Weigh the strongest versions of both sides, then actually come down somewhere. I don't want a mushy "both sides have a point" ending — reach a verdict and defend it.

Some things I specifically want you to bring into the argument, because I think this is where it gets interesting:

Is that actually enough to answer Zeno, or does it dodge the question? Does it handle the arrow paradox — the worry that at any single instant the arrow is just sitting there, so where does the motion live?
- Supertasks: whether "finishing infinitely many tasks" is a coherent thing to do at all, and whether it matters if the tasks are all of the same kind or of shrinking duration.
