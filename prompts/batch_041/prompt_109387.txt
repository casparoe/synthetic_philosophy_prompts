I'm putting together a problem set for a graduate seminar whose first weeks are given over to analogy and concept formation, and I'm tired of the letter-string puzzles that everyone in the room has already seen. I'd like you to invent a fresh microdomain and, inside it, build me one analogy problem designed to specification — not an illustration of conceptual slippage, but a case engineered so that a particular re-description is forced, with the verdicts falling out of the design rather than being stipulated.

The microdomain should be small, closed, and fully explicit: a finite vocabulary of primitive items, two or three stated relations among them, and nothing else — something a reader can hold in mind after a paragraph of description, and not the alphabet, not base-ten numerals, and not any of the usual toy domains. The case itself is a single analogy problem: a source pair and a target, with a candidate answer. Four specifications. First, the problem must support two readings — a surface reading that applies the relation the source pair wears on its sleeve, and a slipped reading that requires re-describing which item plays which role — and the two must yield different answers. Second, the slipped reading must be licensed by a specific feature of the source pair itself (a correspondence, a grouping, an ordering that the surface reading leaves idle), not by appeals to elegance or intuition; you must name that feature. Third, the surface reading must be a genuinely good rule — one that would have served well in the microdomain's other, unstated problems — so that the case exhibits a cost to fluidity rather than merely a failure of imagination on the literalist's part. Fourth, the microdomain must be able to ratchet: identify one further re-description that the slipped answer would make available in the next problem, and say what new role it creates there.

Then do the accounting. Lay out the source pair, the target, both answers, and the reading that yields each; state precisely which feature of the source pair licenses the slippage; and say what becomes of the case if that feature is deleted — whether the slipped reading collapses into the surface reading or into a third reading altogether. Close with the strongest reply available to someone who insists that the surface answer is simply the correct one, and say what that reply would have to concede. Keep everything internal to the microdomain — no appeal to outside knowledge — and don't build the case on the internal machinery of any particular published analogy program; it has to stand on its own for readers who know only the general idea of conceptual slippage.
