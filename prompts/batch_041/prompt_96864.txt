I am a software engineer by trade — compilers and verification tooling, mostly — with no formal training in philosophy; whatever I know about logic I have picked up from writing proofs in Agda and Coq and from reading around the subject in the evenings. Next month I have been asked to lead a session at a philosophy of mathematics reading group that meets at the university near me, and the brief I was given is deliberately provocative: I am to defend the claim that intuitionistic logic, rather than classical logic, is the logic in which mathematics ought to be done, and that classical reasoning is at best a bookkeeping convenience — legitimate when it merely abbreviates an argument that could be carried out constructively, illegitimate when it is used to establish existence claims that nobody could ever exhibit. My plan for the twenty minutes is roughly this: a proof is a construction; a classical existence proof supplies no construction; therefore what it supplies is not a proof of existence in the sense in which mathematicians have always cared about existence. I intend to lean on the halting problem as an analogy, to argue that for many undecided statements there is simply no fact of the matter yet, and to concede early that the double-negation translation lets classical mathematicians embed their work inside a constructive system. The audience is the difficulty. It is mostly working mathematicians — two analysts, a number theorist and a combinatorialist — whose instinct is that excluded middle is so obviously true of mathematical objects that anyone who denies it has changed the subject, together with two logicians who know the proof theory far better than I do and one philosopher of mathematics who is, I am told, a quiet Dummettian. I am the only person in the room who cannot read a sequent calculus derivation without writing it out in full.

Please prepare me for the questions. I want the objections this particular audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they would do to my position, each phrased the way that person would phrase it — the analysts' objection will not sound like the logicians' — with a short reply I could deliver in about two minutes and a note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me which points I should concede at the outset rather than defend, and which concessions would cost me the thesis altogether. Flag anything that is a genuine problem for the argument rather than a debating trick, and say plainly if any of the objections shows that my formulation, rather than constructivism itself, is what is at fault. Finally, include the one question I am hoping nobody asks — the one that, if it comes, I shall have no answer for.

Two further things would help. I am tempted to draw an analogy between excluded middle and the closed-world assumption in database query evaluation, and to describe classical logic as an optimisation that is only sound when every predicate is assumed to terminate; tell me whether that framing will win me the analysts or lose them, and whether it misrepresents the position I am defending. And if the combinatorialist asks why any of this matters to him, I should like to have something better to say than that foundations are intrinsically interesting.
