Ok, so I've been teaching the regress argument for fifteen years and there's a move I keep running into that I've never found a name for, and it's starting to bug me.

Every fall, when we get to foundationalism/coherentism/infinitism, some student says somthing like: why does my belief that there's a cup on the desk need a reason? Nobody's challenged it. If you want me to doubt it, you give me a reason. I used to treat that as a misunderstanding and steer back to the standard menu. But I keep meeting the same move in places where it doesn't look like a misunderstanding. A colleague in the law faculty: a judge can be asked to justify a ruling, but nobody has to justify the rule against hearsay itself every time a witness opens their mouth, and if you want a ruling reconsidered you have to file something, on time, with grounds. A friend who does stats consulting: you don't have to defend every default setting in a model, only the ones a reviewer actually flags, and the reviewer has to say what they think is wrong. I run my own grading the same way -- a claim in an essay needs no defence unless I circle it, and when I circle it I owe the student a reason.

The pattern, as best I can put it: the regress doesn't get stopped, it never gets going, because the demand for a reason is conditional on something happening on the other person's side. Not a basic belief (nothing is exempt by its nature), not a loop, not an infinite chain that's supposed to be fine. A condition on when the demand is live at all. And its making me nervous for a reason I want priced: if the stopping point is 'no one has made a proper challenge yet', then what counts as a proper challenge starts to look like something the parties negotiate, and a stubborn enough person can shield anything.

Two questions. (1) Does this have a name -- the family, or the specific version where the condition sits on the challenger's side? I know there's a cluster of views that sound adjacent (entitlements, hinges, defaults, whatever the contextualists are up to) but I've never been able to tell whether any of those is this move or four different moves. (2) If it has no name, coin one, but the name isn't what I'm after. What I want is the carving: the difference between 'this belief needs no support', 'this belief needs no support from you, here, now', and 'what you're asking for isn't the kind of thing that can be answered'. Is the third even the same phenomenon or just a cousin? And does the move actually terminate anything, or does it just push the regress back one step -- who justifies the challenge? If the honest answer is that it terminates only relative to a conversation, fine, but tell me what that costs, and whether the view can be stated without the stopping point coming out arbitrary.
