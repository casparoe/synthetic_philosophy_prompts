I am a philosopher of biology preparing the final session of a graduate seminar on the concept of function, and I have grown impatient with the way the field stages the dispute between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function — as a pair of monologues, each addressed to its own choir. I want my students to read what a genuine adversarial collaboration between the two camps would have produced. Please draft that document: the joint statement that a committed etiological theorist and a committed causal-role theorist could both sign after working through the cases below in good faith.

By the etiological position I mean, roughly, the view that ascribing a function to a trait is saying something about its history — that the function of a trait is the effect for which it was selected, or more broadly the effect that explains why the trait is present or maintained. By the causal-role position I mean the view that a trait's function is the contribution it makes to some capacity of the containing system, picked out without essential reference to how the trait came to be. I offer these glosses only to fix the parties. Refine them as the document requires. You need not name individual authors, cite any literature, or appeal to any particular paper; the statement must stand on its own for readers who have nothing in front of them but the statement itself.

Please have the two parties work through these four cases, which I have deliberately chosen so that neither camp gets an easy run:

Case A. In a recently described population of blind, cave-dwelling crustaceans, an appendage homologous to a feeding structure in surface relatives has been modified and now detects water-borne vibrations, which the animals use to avoid predators. The population appears to have been founded by a handful of individuals a few thousand years ago, and the evidence suggests the modification may have spread by drift rather than selection; whether selection is currently acting on the appendage's size is contested and, at present, unresolved.

Case B. A structure in a related lineage is a clear homologue of a structure that performs a well-understood role in its surface relatives, but in this lineage the structure is diminutive and no measurable contribution to any capacity has been detected. There is no evidence of selection maintaining it and no evidence of selection eliminating it.

Case C. An organ that was, on the best phylogenetic reconstruction, originally selected for buoyancy control now serves primarily in the production of sound used in mating displays, and its buoyancy contribution is marginal. The historical and the current roles have come apart.

Case D. In a diseased organism, an organ that ordinarily makes a specific contribution to a systemic capacity no longer makes it, and cannot be made to make it again. Nothing about the organ's structure or lineage has changed.

The document should contain the following, in this order:

1. A short preamble, of two or three sentences, stating the question both parties agree they are answering, and identifying whatever they can jointly affirm about what ascriptions of function are for — whether they are explanatory rather than merely descriptive, for instance — without prejudging the substance of their disagreement.

2. A numbered list of claims both parties accept, about functions in general and about the cases above, phrased so that neither party is conceding anything its own commitments forbid.

3. A disagreements table, with one row per live disagreement and columns for: the issue; the formulation of each side's position that that side would accept as fair to it; the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side; a case, observation, or argument that both sides agree bears on the crux; and each side's prediction about it, recorded in advance.

4. For each of the four cases, the verdict the two parties can reach jointly, and, where they split, the split stated in wording each side would accept for itself.

5. A section identifying any disagreements the collaboration concluded were merely verbal — disagreements about which word to use rather than about what is the case — with reasons for that verdict. If you conclude that no part of the dispute is verbal, say so and defend it.

6. For each side, what that side would count as evidence or argument against its own position, stated in terms its own adherent would accept.

Three constraints on the drafting. First, do not make either party concede more than its commitments force; where you find that a side's view cannot be stated without making it sound obviously false, report that as a finding of the collaboration rather than disguising it. Second, do not smuggle in a third position, and do not declare a winner; the point of the exercise is a text both parties would actually put their names to. Third, the cruxes must be real: for each one, it should be clear why settling it would move at least one party, and the shared evidence you cite must be something both parties would agree is relevant before they know how it comes out.
