I'm a software engineer at a small company and I have no formal background in philosophy — the closest I've come is a few blog posts and an argument in a group chat. My team is building an internal assistant that answers hard analytical questions for our operations group: whether a vendor's published security claims are plausible given the architecture they describe, which of two migration plans has the lower expected downtime, whether a particular contract clause actually covers a scenario someone is worried about. We cannot call out to a stronger model. The same mid-size model does everything, and it is not strong — it is fine on one crisp question and starts improvising the moment a question has several moving parts. A colleague described a picture he'd read about somewhere and could no longer find: instead of asking the model the hard question, you ask it to split the question into sub-questions and answer each sub-question with a fresh copy of itself, each of those copies being free to split its own sub-question further, the claim being that this can in principle get you much further than any single answer, because at every step the work actually being done is small. He could not remember where it came from or what it was called, and I don't care about the provenance — I want that picture turned into a procedure my team can run on every incoming question.

So please write the procedure. It should be an ordered set of checks a competent engineer can run in about twenty minutes without knowing any theory, and it should end in one of three states: answer directly, answer by decomposition with the decomposition attached, or escalate to a human. I need it to say what makes a split admissible — I assume each sub-question has to be strictly easier than the parent by some criterion we can actually apply, and the sub-questions have to be jointly sufficient once a stated composition rule is applied, but tell me if that's wrong or incomplete. I need it to say when to stop splitting and just answer, since the recursion has to bottom out somewhere and our model is weaker than the reasoner in the picture my colleague described. I need it to try to break its own split before trusting it: which sub-question, if the model quietly gets it wrong, poisons the whole answer without anything downstream looking off, and how do we notice? I am also worried about a case I don't have a name for — the model that checks a sub-answer is the same model that produced it, so agreement between the two is weak evidence and a blind spot that the model has and we don't will survive the check; say what, if anything, a procedure can do about that with only one model available. Two homegrown devices of mine are in the draft and I'd like you to either fix them or throw them out: a 'difficulty ledger' in which each node carries an honest estimate of how hard it is relative to what the model can do in one shot, with a split permitted only if the ledger strictly decreases going down; and a 'trust budget' capping how much of a final answer may rest on the model's own self-assessment. Mark clearly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say what each step of the record should contain so the whole thing is auditable after the fact.

Then run it on three questions, showing the procedure's output at each step, not just the conclusion. First, a question that is mostly a pile of independent facts with a small calculation at the end — something like whether a vendor's stated key-rotation interval is consistent with the rest of their published design — where I expect the procedure to say yes and to say why. Second, a question the team badly wants to automate but where I suspect the honest answer is no: whether a proof or a formal argument someone handed us is actually valid. Third, a case where every sub-question is easy and the hard part is the composition — for instance deciding which of two strategies is better when all the relevant facts are already on the table — where splitting may just relocate the difficulty rather than reduce it. At least one of the three should come out against what the team wants; don't soften it. Finish with what this procedure cannot do, where it will predictably misfire, and what a human has to supply that the procedure never can — including whether the underlying picture is even true, which I understand the procedure itself can't tell me.
