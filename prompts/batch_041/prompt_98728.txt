Ok so I keep a running file of translation calls I've made and I'm trying to figure out what principle, if any, they add up to. I translate mostly short poems and constrained little things — I got the bug from that Hofstadter book where he spends an absurd number of pages on one tiny French poem, though I couldn't tell you his conclusions, I just caught the disease. Everything below is a text I wrote myself or one I'm free to mess with, so don't go hunting for editions, I don't need citations, I need a principle.

Here's the pile. My verdicts are gut verdicts, not theory.

A. A palindrome that actually says something — a small scene, not word salad. I rendered it into German and got a flawless palindrome that says something entirely different and sort of random. I call that a failure.

B. An acrostic: the first letters of the lines spell the name of the person it's for. In my version the acrostic is perfectly preserved, same name, but the lines now say something blandly different from the source. I call that a success.

C. A limerick whose whole punch is a homophone pun. The target language has no homophone for it. Two drafts: one keeps the meter and rhyme and swaps in a different joke that fits the same beat, one drops the rhyme and just glosses the original pun as plainly as it can. I think the first is a decent translation and the second is clearly worse.

D. A haiku. One English draft hits the syllable pattern exactly but the image is mush and the season word is gone. Another runs a syllable or two long, keeps the image sharp, keeps the season word. I think the second is better and the first is a failure. Which sits badly next to C, where I was happy to spend the sense to buy the form.

E. A sestina: six end-words rotating through a fixed permutation. My version keeps the permutation but with six completely different words that don't correspond to the source's at all. Success, I think — the pattern is the form.

F. A lipogram that avoids one particular common letter. In the target language a different letter is the one you'd have to strain to avoid, so I avoid that one instead. Success. The constraint is "write without leaning on the obvious letter," the specific letter is incidental.

G. A nonsense counting rhyme for kids — the words mean nothing, the rhythm and the tune are everything. I wrote brand new nonsense with the same beat and the same singable shape. Success.

H. A sonnet with a real turn at the ninth line. My draft keeps the rhyme scheme letter-perfect but flattens the turn into just more of the same. Failure, and I'd rather have a draft that keeps the turn and breaks the rhyme.

I. A poem written to a strict syllable count that no reader would ever count. My version adds or drops a syllable here and there to keep the sense clean. Success, nobody could tell.

J. The one I keep going back and forth on: a riddle-poem whose answer is a pun that only exists in the source language. I invented a new riddle, new answer, same theme and same shape. Is that a translation, a "version," or a new poem that stole a suit? I genuinely don't know, and I don't trust my own answer.

My tentative principle, and I already suspect it's either circular or wrong: a translation succeeds when it preserves the constraint the source's readers would have noticed and cared about — call that the load-bearing constraint — and everything else is decoration you're allowed to spend to buy it. My worry is that this just says "preserve what matters," which is no principle at all until you tell me how to spot what matters without peeking at my verdicts.

Then give me its verdicts on cases I haven't listed, at least two of them, invented by you, including something structurally awkward (a constraint that's cheap in one language and brutal in another, or a form that has no counterpart at all). And settle J if you can.
