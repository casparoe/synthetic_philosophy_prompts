A friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about whether it can ever be in order to say that one historical atrocity was worse than another, and the argument came to rest on Slaughterhouse-Five, which we had both just reread. He treats the Tralfamadorian refrain that follows every death in the book, no matter who has died, as Vonnegut's settled view: a refusal to put suffering on any scale at all. I think the novel is itself an act of comparison, written to make Americans reckon with Dresden by setting it against what they thought they already knew about the camps. I am teaching it in the spring and would rather not stand in front of a class with a position I cannot defend, so please give me a reading that does justice to both of us. Two distinctions seem to me to be doing the work, and I want to know which one the novel actually trades on. Is the objection to comparing atrocities metaphysical — there is no common measure, so the question has no answer — or expressive — the comparison may be perfectly true and still an insult to the dead? And when we say that one atrocity was worse, worse in what respect: a larger toll, a larger share of a people destroyed, a different kind of wrong? The legal definition of genocide turns on an intent to destroy a group as a group rather than on the number killed, so the law itself ranks by kind and not by scale, and I want to know whether the novel would accept that ranking or find it obscene. Tell me where the book resists my reading — if the Tralfamadorian calm is a symptom of Billy's damage rather than a doctrine the novel endorses, the anti-comparison view may be the target rather than the moral — and close by saying what the novel contributes to this argument that a philosophical essay could not.
