Write an essay assessing a proposed answer to the special composition question — the question of when it is that some things compose a further thing. The proposal to assess is this: a plurality of things compose something just in case they are jointly integrated into a single causal-functional process, that is, roughly, just in case they interact in a way that sustains some common capacity that none of them sustains on its own. Call this the causal-integration principle. It is offered as a middle path: it is meant to avoid the two extreme answers according to which some things always compose something (unrestricted composition) and according to which some things never do (mereological nihilism), and it is meant to improve on the restricted answers that appeal to contact, fastening, or cohesion.

Your essay should reach and defend a verdict on whether this principle is a satisfactory answer. In working toward that verdict, you should do at least the following.

First, situate the principle. Explain how it is supposed to differ from the extremes and from the more familiar restricted answers, including the answer that composition occurs only where the parts constitute a life or organism, and the answer that composition is simply a brute fact that holds where it holds.

Second, press the strongest objections. In particular, consider whether any principle of this kind escapes the dilemma that it must be either vague where the underlying facts are not, or arbitrary, or else quietly collapses into one of the extremes. Consider also the argument from vagueness, which runs roughly as follows: if composition is restricted, there must be a sharp boundary between cases where it occurs and cases where it does not; but there is no such sharp boundary; therefore composition is not restricted. Say whether this argument bears on the causal-integration principle, and whether the principle can be restated so as to survive it.

Third, test the principle against cases chosen to pull in opposite directions: a single organism, a colony of ants, a coral reef, a slime mold, a tumor, a river, a star, and a crowd or a corporation. For each, state what the principle implies and whether that implication is a point in its favor or against it.

Finally, address whether the verdict you reach turns on a substantive fact about what exists, or instead on how we choose to use the word thing — and if the latter, whether that makes the question less interesting than it first appeared.

Reach a conclusion and justify it. Do not end on a non-committal note; if your verdict is qualified, state the qualification precisely and defend it.
