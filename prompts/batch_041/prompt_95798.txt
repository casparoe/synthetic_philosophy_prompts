I am preparing a graduate seminar on sentience as a matter of degree, from insects to thermostats, and I want to avoid the familiar question of where to draw the line. I have been working with a view I call lifetime-sum gradualism: an entity's moral status is fixed by the total quantity of valenced experience it accrues over its existence, regardless of how that experience is distributed across time or across its parts. Please give me an example of a conceptual issue that this view faces. Construct a concrete case—ideally involving an entity whose sentience is intermittent, or whose sentience is realized only in disconnected episodes, or whose total valenced experience matches another's but is distributed differently—and explain the difficulty the case exposes. I am especially interested in cases near the thermostat end of the spectrum, where the posited experience is minimal and fragmentary. Do not simply restate the marginal-case problem. In your response, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on sentience and moral status, and do not present a contested position as if it were the consensus.
