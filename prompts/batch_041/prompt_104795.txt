I chair the grant committee of a small foundation that has committed roughly ten researcher-years over three years to the conceptual and institutional design of sortition-based governance. The money is for philosophy, political theory, and institutional design only — none of it for experiments, surveys, field trials, or anything empirical. We are not commissioning a literature survey; we need a defended agenda for where those researcher-years should go. Assume the response has no ability to look anything up, so do not supply citations, reading lists, or the names of specific works.

Our working prototype, which we expect most of the funded work to bear on, is deliberately non-standard, and I want the agenda to be responsive to its quirks rather than to sortition in the abstract. Picture a Citizens' Review Chamber: about 120 members drawn by stratified random sampling from the adult population, serving staggered two-year terms that are not renewable. Its authority is negative only — it may repeal or suspend existing statutes and regulations by simple majority, but it may not introduce, amend, or draft new legislation. Any repeal it passes must be reconsidered by the ordinary legislature within one year, and the legislature can reinstate the measure only by supermajority. Members deliberate for a fixed number of days and may summon a fixed budget of expert witnesses. Before the vote, every member must file a written rationale, and the rationales are published anonymously afterward. Each of these features is our guess rather than a conclusion, and we are willing to abandon any of them if the conceptual work shows it to be a mistake.

Which open questions in this area most deserve our money, and in what order? I care less about the list than about the reasoning: for each question, why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for theorists with no empirical budget, and what we would have to learn over the next couple of years for it to move up or down the ranking. Weight neglectedness as well — most existing thinking assumes either elected legislatures or assemblies with full law-making power, so tell us where the mismatch between that and a repeal-only chamber leaves us unserved, and where the standard answers transfer cleanly and the neglect is illusory. Make the criteria explicit (importance, tractability, neglectedness, how many other questions depend on the answer), say how you trade them off against one another, and state what evidence or argument would reorder your ranking.

Take a definite position: make the choices and defend them rather than laying out the options and leaving the ordering to me. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
