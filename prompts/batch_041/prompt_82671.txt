I am preparing an application for a nine-month writing fellowship at an interdisciplinary institute, and I need a proposal of about 1,200 words. The project is a critical study of the contrast between cranes and skyhooks, and of the related charge of greedy reductionism, as these are used to sort legitimate reductive explanations from ones that smuggle in an unexplained miracle. Both pieces of vocabulary come from Dennett's Darwin's Dangerous Idea; the book glosses the greedy reductionist as one who tries to explain apparent design without cranes, that is, by skipping the intermediate building blocks rather than by arriving at an unwelcome conclusion. I want the proposal organized around a claim about that contrast rather than around a survey of the debate, and I would rather not have a bibliography: I will supply the references myself, so refer to arguments and positions by description, not to page numbers or titles I would have to check.

Here is the half-formed idea the proposal should develop. The contrast is normally drawn by provenance: a crane is itself something that had to be built, a skyhook is not. But provenance is a diachronic fact about where a capacity came from, whereas the explanatory demand the distinction is meant to police is synchronic: what produces or underwrites this capacity here and now. Nothing guarantees that the two line up. A posit can be a crane in the causal history and still function as a skyhook in the explanation actually offered, because its own construction is never accounted for and the explanatory work is done by a label. I suspect the reverse case also occurs, where something is called a skyhook merely because it has not yet been derived, and the accusation serves as a promissory note in the other direction. The second strand concerns greedy reductionism. Dennett's own gloss makes greediness a matter of missing intermediate steps, which is a structural property of an explanation; in practice, though, the accusation is nearly always made on the basis of the conclusion rather than the gap, which makes it circular, since whether the reduction was greedy then depends on whether it succeeded, which is the disputed question. I want to know whether a criterion turning on the shape of an explanation, rather than on our verdict about its conclusion, can be had.

Please turn this into a proposal. It should state the guiding question so that it has a determinate answer; explain why the existing positions leave it open, without drifting into a literature review; describe the method concretely, including what I would actually be doing month by month and why working through a stock of recurring accusations is a better route than adjudicating one contested case in detail; say what a successful outcome would look like, what would count as the project failing, and which considerations would decide between the possible conclusions; and give a realistic scope for nine months, including what I should cut first if it proves too large. The review panel will include biologists and psychologists, so define the terms in passing rather than assuming the vocabulary. The proposal should also address one foreseeable objection: that this is really a question for the sociology of science about when a reductive explanation gets accepted, rather than a philosophical question, and say why the project is philosophical anyway. Open with a working title and a one-sentence summary of the project.
