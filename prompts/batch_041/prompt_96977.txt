The position I've talked myself into, and want you to try to break:

Aumann's agreement theorem says that if two agents share a prior, and it's common knowledge between them what their posterior probabilities are (and that they're both just conditioning), then they can't agree to disagree — the posteriors have to match. Fine. Now the strong line I want to take: this means that 'rational, persistent disagreement between epistemic peers' is an empty category. Whenever two people who seem equally informed and equally thoughtful just keep disagreeing, one of the theorem's antecedents must be failing — they don't really share a prior, or their disagreement about each other's credences isn't common knowledge, or one of them isn't updating the way a Bayesian does. So the whole conciliationism-versus-steadfastness fight over 'peer disagreement' is a fight over a case that can't happen. It's a bit like Arrow's theorem, except instead of showing that fair voting is impossible it shows that honest peer disagreement is impossible.

The objection I keep running into is that the antecedent basically never holds for real people — common knowledge of each other's credences is a crazy idealization — so the theorem is vacuous.
