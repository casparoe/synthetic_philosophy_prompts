I recently moved from a pure mathematics programme into a laboratory that builds unconventional computing hardware, and the move has turned an old question into a practical one: to write up what our device does, I have to commit to a view about the status of the Church–Turing thesis. A disagreement at our last group meeting brought the issue to a head, and I would like it adjudicated move by move rather than settled in general terms. The two parties were senior colleagues; I report the exchange as neutrally as I can.

A began: the Church–Turing thesis is not an empirical hypothesis about the world at all. It is a definition, or at most an explication, of what we mean by effective computability, and it fixes the meaning of the term. No physical discovery could refute it: if someone exhibited a physical process computing a function that no Turing machine computes, that would not falsify the thesis; it would only show that the function is computable in some broader sense, while the thesis would remain true of effective computability as defined.

B replied: that makes the thesis safe by making it empty. Read plainly, the thesis asserts something substantive — that every function computable by any finite procedure, including any process a finite physical device could realize, is computable by a Turing machine. That is a claim about the world, and it is falsifiable in principle: a device that decided the halting problem for arbitrary inputs would refute it. Calling the thesis a definition is a verbal manoeuvre that preserves the words while surrendering the claim.

A answered: the manoeuvre is not merely verbal. Deciding the halting problem for arbitrary inputs is not a well-defined physical task until you say what counts as deciding, and any such account presupposes the very notion of an effective procedure that the thesis concerns. Moreover, every real device operates on finite inputs and so can never exhibit the unbounded behaviour the thesis is about. The thesis concerns an idealized notion of computation; it is not the sort of thing a laboratory can refute.

B then proposed a distinction: separate the mathematical thesis from the physical one. The mathematical thesis — that the various formal explications of effective computability (Turing machines, lambda-definable functions, the recursive functions) coincide — is a family of theorems, and its status is mathematical. The physical thesis — that every function computable by a physically realizable process is Turing-computable — is a separate and genuinely empirical conjecture about the world, and it is the one a device could refute, for instance by exploiting an infinitely long computation or a spacetime region in which an observer can read out the result of a computation that never halts in finite proper time. Conflating the two, B said, is what lets A make the empirical claim vanish.

A conceded the distinction but not its moral. The convergence of the formal explications, A said, is not a proof of the kind B wants; it is a confluence of independent analyses that is strong evidence but not a derivation, and the physical thesis is not testable as B suggests, because every proposal of that kind either smuggles in an idealization no actual device realizes or yields an answer that cannot be effectively checked. A device whose output cannot be verified has not computed anything in the relevant sense.

B's last word: requiring that the output be checkable proves too much, since verification is itself a computation, so the requirement assumes the thesis. And the mathematical thesis is not merely an inductive convergence; the standard explications can be proved to coincide once one has a prior analysis of what an effective procedure is. The only genuinely contestable step is that prior analysis — which is precisely why the thesis is not a theorem about an antecedently clear notion.

Neither would give ground, and the dispute now bears directly on how I should describe the computational power of our hardware. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it is right, wrong, or right only in part, and why. Then say what both parties are missing, what the epistemic status of the thesis and of its physical variant actually is — definition, theorem, or empirical conjecture — and what follows for how I should characterize what our device computes.
