Im putting together a handout for a reading group thats half sociologists and half philosophers, and the week's topic is whether generations are real kinds — whether "Gen Z" and "Boomer" name anything, or are just buckets we pour birth years into. I want a decision tree that takes a reader from a few concrete cases to a named position: 8-10 branch nodes, no more than about 12 leaves.

The distinctions I definitely want on the tree (add others if they earn their place):

- whether the label tracks anything over and above age and the year you're asking in. Worth building in that in any single cross-section age, period and cohort are linearly dependent — birth year is just survey year minus age — so separating a cohort effect from a period effect isn't an empirical question you can settle from data alone, it needs a substantive assumption to break the tie.
- whether a generation has to be self-aware to be real, i.e. whether people must recognise themselves in the label and act on it, or whether it can be a kind none of its members ever notice.
- whether the kind is supposed to have an essence or is only a probabilistic cluster that predicts tendencies without predicting any individual (the "no true Millennial" complaint).
- whether the label is doing explanatory work or doing political work — explaining by cohort what used to be explained by class, say.
- whether applying the label changes the people it's applied to, so that the kind becomes real by being used.
- whether the kind would survive the label being abolished. Money stops being money if nobody treats it as money; tigers don't stop being tigers.

Every node should be answerable by someone who doesn't know the positions — concrete cases and concrete claims, never "are you a realist about cohorts?". Cases I want used: the December 1996 vs January 1997 boundary; a person who resembles nothing in their cohort's stereotype; a counterfactual that swaps the conditions someone grew up under; whether five-year buckets would be more real or less than twenty-year ones.

Then put the positions at the leaves, named where there's a name for them and marked unnamed with a descriptive label where there isn't. Tell me which answer-combinations nobody occupies, and for each say whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexamined. Flag any two leaves the tree can't actually separate rather than papering over it — I'd rather the tree confess.

Write the tree itself, not prose about the tree.
