I'm a doctoral candidate in the philosophy of social science, preparing a seminar session on explanation in the historiography of the Holocaust — specifically the dispute over whether the men who did the killing are better explained by situational dynamics or by prior culture and character. I want a dialogue I can hand to students as a model of what a genuinely unresolved disagreement looks like, rather than a survey of positions. Please write that dialogue.

Take the role of a philosopher of history who has thought hard about the epistemology of explanation: what it takes for a factor to do explanatory work, when a hypothesis is underdetermined by the evidence that is supposed to support it, and how to tell whether a variable is pulling its weight or is simply being read off the outcome. Before you write anything, work the problem through in your own reasoning — what a situationist explanation of the perpetrators actually claims, what a culture-and-character explanation actually claims, where the two genuinely conflict rather than merely coexisting, and which of their disagreements is empirical and which is conceptual. Then produce only the dialogue.

Two interlocutors, both highly competent, neither a strawman. The first is a situationist: he holds that the perpetrators' behavior is explained by authority, group pressure, routinization, escalating commitment, and the division of labor, and he denies that individual character or an antecedent culture of eliminationist belief earns a place in the explanation. The second holds the culture-and-character view: the situation supplies permission, not direction, and the content of what the men did — against whom, in what spirit — has to come from evaluative commitments they brought with them.

The five exchanges should each do real work, in roughly this order:

1. The permission/direction gap. The situationist explains why restraints came off. Does that also explain why the action taken was this action, aimed at these victims, rather than some other atrocity or none at all?
2. The construal dilemma. Either the situation is specified objectively — in which case structurally similar situations ought to produce similar behavior, and they plainly don't — or it is specified as the agent construed it, in which case the construal appears to smuggle back in exactly the dispositions and background beliefs the situationist set out to eliminate. Have each speaker press the other on this.
3. The refusal cases. In the same units, under the same orders, inside the same culture, some men refused or asked to be relieved while others carried out the killings without apparent coercion. What does each side have to say about them, and does either side need those men to have been more virtuous than the rest?
4. The collapse of the culture thesis into its rival. If culture is doing the work, it begins to look like character writ large — but cultures don't act, individuals do, and individuals inside one culture diverge. Does the culture thesis survive, or does it merely relocate the problem?
5. Self-exposure. Ask each speaker to state what evidence or argument would count against his own position, then end the exchange at the point where the disagreement is sharpest and unresolved — no synthesis, no hint that the two views can be had together.

Format: exactly five numbered exchanges and nothing else — no title, no preamble, no stage directions, no closing paragraph. Each numbered item is one exchange: the situationist speaks, then the culture-and-character proponent replies, each speech a few sentences long and responsive to the actual last point made rather than to a position he has attributed to the other. Both should argue from the phenomena under discussion, not from names or authorities.
