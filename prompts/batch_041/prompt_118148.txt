Ok so there's a move I keep running into in arguments about populism and I want to know how much weight it can actually bear. The move goes: the will of the people is one will, the way a person's will is one will. Lots of us, but a single thing that exists, can be known, and can be represented. (Some versions dress it up as a body with a head, and the head is where the willing supposedly happens.) Once you've got that, a leader or a movement can go around the courts, the upper house, the press -- those are just parts, and the whole is what counts.

Audit the analogy for me. Start with the correspondence table: what's playing the role of the single mind, the intention, the body that carries it out, the moment of decision, and the thing that's supposed to make it one will rather than a heap of them. Then the break points, and I'd definately rather you go structural than just tell me that people disagree.

A person's will is dated and it's one thing at a time, so what happens when the analogy has to stretch across a people that changes its mind, or a people that includes people not born yet? The analogy seems to make a change of course look like apostasy or betrayal -- that feels like something it's generating rather than discovering. Same with responsibility: one will makes you the author of what your body does, so the people ends up the author of acts no individual chose.

Here's the part I actually care about. A person's will is something you can be right or wrong about, and the analogy needs that, otherwise 'this is the will of the people' is just whoever said it loudest. But the claim is self-certifying -- there's no procedure that could show the claimant got it wrong. Defect of the target, or of the analogy? Related: a person can weigh her desires against each other, but votes don't register how much anyone wants anything, and on connected questions (guilty, liable, punish) a group can land on an inconsistent position even when every voter is consistent. Does the analogy assume a unity the aggregation machinery can't deliver?

So does the conclusion survive -- the leader may bypass the institutions because the whole speaks through him -- or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if you had to swap it out, would something like the market-price analogy do better: nobody sets the price, everybody contributes, and it's supposed to be wiser than any participant? What would that one have to get right to be any less misleading?
