The literature on the norm of assertion has largely been organized around a single question — which condition, truth or belief or justification or knowledge or certainty, an assertion must satisfy — and this framing tends to reduce the debate to a contest between one-line slogans. Set that framing aside and explain the structure of the problem instead.

Write a self-contained explanation for a reader who has not encountered the debate and cannot look anything up. It should cover four things.

First, distinguish two roles that a condition on assertion might play: licensing the assertion (settling whether one may assert that p at all) and underwriting reliance (settling whether one's audience may take p on one's word, and whether one must withdraw the assertion when they may not). Explain why a condition that is plausible in one role can be implausible in the other, and give a case in which the two roles pull apart.

Second, take the two cases that any candidate condition must survive: the assertion that is confidently held and well evidenced but false, and the assertion that is true but not believed by the speaker — a spokesperson relaying a report she trusts but does not herself believe, a liar, an actor reciting a line. For each candidate condition, say whether these are assertions at all, whether they are defective, and who, if anyone, is wronged. Be explicit about which case is genuinely difficult for which condition and which only appears to be.

Third, consider a reformulation on which the fundamental norm is not a licensing condition on the speaker but a norm governing an invitation: to assert that p is to invite one's audience to rely on one's word for p, and the norm states when that invitation is in order. Explain what this reformulation changes. Does it make the knowledge condition more attractive or less? Can it explain whether an audience that relies on a false but warranted assertion has been wronged by the speaker, or merely disappointed?

Fourth, identify at least one substantive disagreement — about blame, about retraction, about the ethics of reliance, or about what it is to say something — that is resolved differently depending on which condition is correct, and explain why the resolution depends on it.

The aim is an explanation that a reader could follow without prior exposure to the debate, so describe positions by their content rather than surveying authors or works.
