I am a historian finishing a chapter on eighteenth-century provincial justice, built as a composite portrait from magistrates' notebooks rather than from any single archive, and I keep getting stuck on a problem that presents itself as a matter of method but seems to be something deeper: whether I can judge the people I write about without either anachronism or abdication. A colleague and I have been arguing about one sentence in my draft, in which I write that a magistrate's order to have a servant flogged for insolence was unjust. She thinks the sentence smuggles a present-day verdict into the past tense; I think refusing to write it would itself be a verdict, only a quieter one. I would like you to write a dialogue between the two of us, two equally competent historians, neither of us a caricature, in which we try to settle, or at least to locate precisely, our disagreement. Let her be the strongest possible defender of the view that the historian's task is to reconstruct the normative world of the past on its own terms and that our own moral vocabulary is a contaminant; let me be the strongest possible defender of the view that the historian cannot and should not pretend to a neutrality no one actually achieves, and that the standards of our own time are not merely an obstacle but part of what makes the past legible as past. Neither of us should win by being made to say something silly, and neither should be permitted to retreat into a slogan when pressed.

In the dialogue, please have us press each other on the following, and let the argument actually develop rather than cycle. Is the standards of their own time even a single, stable thing, given that the magistrate's own contemporaries, the servant, the parish, the assizes, may well have disagreed among themselves about whether the flogging was just? Does understanding an action require suspending judgment on it, or is judgment part of what understanding consists in? Is the choice of what to narrate and what to leave silent already an evaluation that no methodological scruple can remove? Is there a principled difference between explaining an action and excusing it, or between condemning a person and condemning a practice? Let one of us propose a specific device, a two-column chapter, say, in which the past is narrated in its own terms in the main text while present-day assessment is quarantined in a parallel commentary, and let the other show concretely why the device either collapses or costs more than it saves. Have us test every claim against the single sentence about the flogging rather than against abstractions, and if one of us concedes a point, let the concession genuinely alter the shape of the argument rather than serving as a polite pause before the same point is restated.

You may invent archival detail as needed, but do not appeal to the authority of named theorists, and do not give me a reading list, citations, or footnotes; I want the argument carried by the two of us rather than by borrowed authority. End not with a forced consensus but with the sharpest possible statement of what we still disagree about and why the disagreement may be irresolvable; if you think one side has the better of it, show that in the dialectic rather than announcing it. Aim for roughly fifteen hundred words, and let the prose sound like two people who know the material well and are fond of each other, stuck in a seminar room after everyone else has gone home.
