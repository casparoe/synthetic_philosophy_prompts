For my school's mathematics and philosophy club I have been asked to give a short presentation on the possibility that the basic axioms of mathematics are inconsistent, and I would like to hand out a decision tree that sorts my classmates by the position they actually hold, rather than a list of labels for them to memorise. Please write that handout.

Begin the tree at something the whole room can accept: that if we allow the principle that every predicate determines a set, a contradiction follows (Russell's paradox), and that no one has an absolute proof that the usual axioms of arithmetic are consistent, so the thought that our mathematics might be inconsistent is a live one rather than a curiosity. Every internal node should be a question my classmates can answer without already knowing which position it leads to; no node should simply ask whether one is a paraconsistent logician, and each node should divide the remaining answers over a single genuine disagreement.

I want the tree organised around where the inconsistency is located, because that is the distinction I find hardest to keep straight. The nodes should separate at least these four issues: first, whether the fault lies in our axioms and definitions, which should be restricted or revised, or in the logic we reason with, which should be revised instead; second, whether an inconsistent theory is worthless or can be inconsistent and yet non-trivial and still do real mathematical work; third, whether consistency is a requirement a theory must satisfy or merely a convenience; and fourth, whether a true contradiction would be a feature of the world, of our concepts and language, or only of our formalisation of something that is itself perfectly consistent.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the positions located within that space. Each leaf should be a named position with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I would like the following to appear somewhere: the classical response that keeps ordinary logic and restricts the comprehension principle; the intuitionist response that blames classical reasoning about totalities; the paraconsistent response that keeps the comprehension principle and changes the logic; the dialetheist claim that some contradictions are true; the view that an inconsistent arithmetic can still be a working theory; the formalist or conventionalist response that the demand for consistency is a rule we invented; and the structuralist response that recasts the question as whether a certain structure is possible. If I have overlooked an obvious position, please add it.

Two features matter most to me. First, note any combinations of answers that no position occupies and say briefly whether they are incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Second, flag any pairs of positions the tree cannot separate, instead of inventing an extra question that would divide them.

Seven to nine questions and eight to eleven leaves should be about right, and the questions should be answerable by someone who has taken algebra and geometry but no logic. Please write the whole thing without citations, reading lists or references to particular books, papers or authors, since I cannot check any of them and the handout has to stand on its own.
