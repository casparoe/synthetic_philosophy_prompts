ok so I'm in this seminar on free will and moral responsibility and we spent like two weeks on Strawson's Basic Argument — the one that goes: nothing can be the cause of itself (causa sui), being genuinely morally responsible for your actions would require being the cause of yourself in certain crucial respects, so nobody is ever genuinely morally responsible for anything. The version we read doesn't even need determinism to go through, because indeterminism just swaps out "caused by your past" for "random," and randomness isn't you either.

I sort of believe it and it's honestly kind of ruining me. So here's what I want: what would count against it? Not the usual "but then we couldn't punish anyone" hand-wringing — I want the actual defeaters, and I want them sorted by how much they'd actually move someone who's already on board, from "this kills the argument" down to "annoying but survivable."

A few things I keep circling that I'd love you to work into it:

One, the argument seems to prove way too much — it doesn't just take out responsibility for my bad choices, it takes out everything, my being kind, my being the kind of person who even cares about this. Strawson seems to just accept that, like, fine, that's the truth, and I can't tell if that's the argument being admirably bullet-biting or if it's a sign the argument has quietly assumed a notion of responsibility nobody was ever defending. Which is it? And would a defender concede the "too much" point or wave it off?

Two, one of the readings made a distinction I keep chewing on between being the *author* of your character and merely being *answerable* for it. Does the Basic Argument only work if responsibility has to be authorship? If someone can give a decent account where answerability is the whole game, is that a real defeater or just a change of subject?

Three, is there any live way to be "self-made" that isn't viciously circular? Like, sure, I didn't choose my initial character, but I can revise it over time — is the standard reply that the revision is still determined by how I already was, so the regress just restarts, and does that reply actually work or is it just asserted?

And then the meta-question I actually care about: has this argument been insulated into unfalsifiability? Every objection I think of seems to get absorbed ("that's still not you," "that just relocates the regress"). Is that a real strength or a warning sign? And what's the honest verdict — does the argument risk anything at all, or does it win by never sticking its neck out?

Personally the thing I'd find most persuasive is some case where I'm clearly responsible for something even though nothing about how I came to be that way was up to me — but I don't know if that's an intuition pump or an actual argument, so tell me straight.
