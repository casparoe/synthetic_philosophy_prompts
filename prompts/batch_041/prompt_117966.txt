My uncle died in the ICU last year and I've been chewing on it ever since, because the way it was handled still seems weird to me. He'd had a massive stroke and was on a ventilator, and after a couple of days the doctors ran a whole battery of tests -- shining lights in his eyes, checking for gag and cough reflexes, taking him off the vent briefly to see if he'd try to breathe on his own -- and then told us he was dead. But his chest was still going up and down, he was warm, his heart was beating, and they kept everything running for another several hours while they sorted out the organ donation paperwork. It took me a while to even understand that "he's dead" and "his heart is beating" were both supposed to be true at once. Anyway, a nurse friend of mine then told me about the other kind of donation, where instead of all that, the family agrees to withdraw the breathing tube, and once the heart stops the surgical team waits a few minutes -- just a few -- and then takes the organs. So in one room we call someone dead while their heart is still pumping, and in the next room we call someone dead only after their heart has quit for a few minutes, and it seems to me that someone declared dead the second way would very likely have failed the first way's tests, and vice versa. On top of that I gather the whole system rests on some rule that organs can't be taken until the donor is already dead, which everyone treats as non-negotiable even though nobody I've asked can tell me exactly what it's protecting.

What I want from you is the implicit theory here, not a summary of the debate. So: what view of death does this practice actually presuppose -- that death is the irreversible loss of some particular function, that it's an event or a process, that it's something we discover versus something we declare? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out, and are the two pathways (the ventilator one and the withdrawal one) presupposing the same thing or quietly different things that happen to be spelled out in the same paperwork? Be careful to separate what the practice strictly *requires* from what it merely happens to fit nicely with -- I suspect a few different theories of death could license all of it, and I want to know where it's underdetermined. And then tell me what would actually have to change -- in the protocols, in who decides, in what we say to families -- if we swapped the underlying view for one of the alternatives, say a straightforwardly circulatory definition, or a definition that cares only about consciousness and not about the brainstem, or the idea that death is really a social and legal status we confer rather than a biological fact we detect. If the practice turns out to be internally inconsistent, say where, and tell me whether the inconsistency is doing any work or is just an artifact of how the rules grew up separately.
