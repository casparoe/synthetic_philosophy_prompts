I'm in a second-year seminar on theories of well-being and we've hit a view the reading calls tranquilism — roughly, the claim that what makes a life go well is only the absence of craving and the absence of suffering, and that everything else (pleasure, getting what you want, achievement, relationships) either drops out or gets absorbed into those two absences. I find it weirdly compelling and also like it might quietly collapse into something silly, and my seminar-mates are no help because half of them just say it's Buddhism and the other half just say it's hedonism with extra steps. Could you help me actually think it through?

I'd like you to act like a very careful philosopher of well-being — someone fluent in the analytic hedonism/desire-satisfaction debates and also in the Buddhist material about craving and dukkha, but who doesn't treat either as the obviously correct frame. Then write a dialogue between two grad students on a long train ride home from a conference: one a committed tranquilist, the other a hedonist who thinks pleasure itself (not merely the absence of pain) is doing real work in a good life. They should be equally sharp — I really don't want the hedonist set up as a strawman, and I don't want the tranquilist to just win by definitional fiat. Have the hedonist press the tranquilist on at least these three things: first, if the two absences are all that matter, why isn't a dreamless coma, or being permanently and contentedly sedated with zero craving and zero suffering, the best life available — and what does the tranquilist say is missing there, if anything? Second, doesn't deliberately pursuing craving-lessness just reinstate craving in a new form, a craving for tranquility, so the view is self-undermining as a practical project? Third, is the absence of craving really a separate good at all, or is it just the absence of suffering relabeled — i.e., can there even be craving without suffering, like the pleasant anticipation of something you're about to get? And the tranquilist should get to push back properly, including on the hedonist's own soft spots (the standard worries about a life of intense pleasure that's a total mess, or about pleasure that you'd rather not have had).

Before you write the dialogue, take a few sentences to brainstorm how each character would actually argue and where you think the real crux of the disagreement is, then write it out. Aim for maybe 6-10 exchanges, let them interrupt each other and concede small points, and make sure each reply actually engages the previous one rather than talking past it. It'd be nice if they're both a little tired and hungry by the end — it's been a long trip — but definately don't let the scenery do the philosophical work.
