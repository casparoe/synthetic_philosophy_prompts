I am taking a graduate seminar on the historical Jesus, and I have been trying to sort out my own judgments about the criteria of authenticity without simply reciting the textbook list. The trouble is that when I apply the criteria to particular cases, my verdicts do not line up with any single criterion, and I suspect the real pattern is something like a cost-of-invention principle. I would like you to state the principle that best captures the following verdicts, to test it against the cases, and to say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save.

Here are my verdicts. First, a short aphorism about the kingdom's arrival is attested in two independent early sources, but it closely parallels a pre-Christian Jewish text; I judge it authentic. Second, a saying that would have embarrassed the early church because it shows Jesus ignorant of the timing of the end is attested only once, in a late source; I judge it authentic. Third, a saying that is dissimilar to both early Judaism and early Christian teaching appears only once in a source with a strong theological agenda; I judge it not authentic. Fourth, a saying is multiply attested, but the sources are literarily dependent on one another; I judge it not established. Fifth, a saying coheres beautifully with sayings I already accept, but it is attested once and has no embarrassing features; I judge it authentic with moderate confidence. Sixth, a saying is embarrassing to the early church and multiply attested, but it also fits a general Jewish apocalyptic expectation and could have been produced by a Jewish Christian prophet; I judge it authentic. Seventh, a saying is not embarrassing and not multiply attested, but its vocabulary and imagery are so idiosyncratic that a community invention seems unlikely; I judge it authentic. Eighth, a saying is theologically convenient, fits later Christology, and is attested in a late source; I judge it not authentic. Ninth, a saying is embarrassing to the early church, but the embarrassment is of a kind that a later community might have invented to make Jesus more human; I judge it not authentic. Tenth, a saying is attested in two sources, but one is a later source that likely knew the other, and the saying is also theologically convenient; I judge it not authentic.

A colleague has proposed what she calls the Cost-of-Invention Test: a saying is authentic to the extent that the hypothesis that Jesus said it explains its presence in the tradition better than the hypothesis that it was invented, where embarrassment, independent multiple attestation, and idiosyncrasy lower the cost of the Jesus hypothesis, while theological convenience, source dependence, and the availability of a plausible community author raise the cost of the invention hypothesis. Please tell me whether that principle captures all ten verdicts, or whether a simpler principle would capture most of them at the cost of overturning one or two. If you propose a repaired version, state precisely what it says, identify the case that motivates each clause, and give its verdict on a case I have not listed: a saying that is embarrassing, multiply attested, and idiosyncratic, but also theologically convenient in a way that later Christians would have welcomed. Finally, I would like to know whether a principle of this sort could ever settle who the historical Jesus was, or whether it can only rank sayings by plausibility while leaving the larger portrait underdetermined.
