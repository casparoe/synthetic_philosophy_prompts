I am in a reading group on decision theory, and a friend has put an argument to me that I cannot shake. I think its conclusion is false, but I cannot find the bad step, and I would rather keep the position it attacks than give it up. Here is the argument.

Set-up. You begin with wealth 1 and are offered a sequence of independent even-money bets. Each round you may stake any fraction f of your current wealth on a coin that comes up in your favour with probability p > 1/2; if it does, you win the stake, and if it does not, you lose it. Let W_n be your wealth after n rounds.

(1) A betting strategy is a rule specifying how much to stake as a function of the history of play so far.

(2) For any strategy, define its growth rate as the limit of (1/n) log W_n as n tends to infinity, where that limit exists.

(3) The growth rate is maximised by staking the constant fraction f* = 2p − 1 every round, and no other strategy achieves a larger one.

(4) So any strategy that stakes otherwise has a smaller growth rate, and its wealth falls behind this one with probability tending to 1 as n grows; it is eventually overtaken and, from some point onward, stays behind.

(5) To choose any other strategy is therefore to choose to be permanently poorer, with near-certainty, for ever. (My friend glosses this as the time-average versus ensemble-average point: an individual lives one path, not an ensemble of paths, so it is the pathwise growth rate that governs her fate.)

(6) Hence a rational agent stakes f* = 2p − 1 every round. This is the thesis of 'geometric rationality': rationality consists in maximising the geometric growth rate of one's resources.

(7) Now suppose instead that the game is offered for a fixed, finite number of rounds N, after which you will never bet again. The growth rate in (2) is an infinite-horizon quantity. For finite N there is no such thing as the growth rate, only the distribution of W_N.

(8) For every finite N, staking more than the Kelly fraction — indeed staking the whole of your wealth — yields strictly greater expected wealth W_N than the Kelly fraction does. (Expected wealth after N rounds is (1 + f(2p − 1))^N, which increases with f.)

(9) So an agent who knows the game will last exactly N rounds, and who plays Kelly, is knowingly choosing an option with lower expected wealth than one available to her, and doing so on the strength of a long-run property that, by hypothesis, will never be realised.

(10) If you reply that she should play Kelly anyway because she cares about the growth of her wealth rather than its expectation, you have stopped arguing and started stipulating a preference; the long-run theorem that was meant to justify the logarithm has dropped out of the picture.

(11) Therefore geometric rationality is either inapplicable to the case at hand or unjustified, and it cannot be the fundamental theory of rational choice.
