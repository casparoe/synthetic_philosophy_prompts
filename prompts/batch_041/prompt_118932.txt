I moved to Berlin a few months ago for a postdoc (my partner got a job here, long story) and the contrast with the lab I came from in the States has been messing with my head. At my old place our SSNs lived on a shared Google Sheet and nobody blinked. Here there's an actual human being, the data protection officer, whose job appears to be telling the institute things it is not allowed to know about us, and my new colleagues talk about the census fight back in the eighties the way people back home talk about the Boston Tea Party. Meanwhile my roommate is from Hangzhou and thinks we're all insane — she explained how her parents' neighborhood gets scored, and the part that got me wasn't the scoring, it was the shrug. So I've been trying to figure out what I actually believe about hiding from the state, and my gut seems to have a shape I can't put into words.

Here's what my gut says, for whatever it's worth. Fake birthday at a clothing store's loyalty program: fine. VPN so my ISP can't see what I read: fine. Pseudonym on a public forum: fine. Lying about my address on a rental application: not fine, though I might do it. Refusing to answer a census question, as opposed to lying on it: fine. Giving the government my real address when I move: fine, obviously, that's just how the thing works. Telling my employer my address: fine. Telling my employer my party registration: not fine, and not their business. My bank reporting my income to the tax office: fine. My bank selling my spending patterns to advertisers: not fine. A cop seeing my ID at a checkpoint: fine. A camera reading every plate on my street: not fine, and I honestly can't tell whether that's a real distinction or just a feeling. Me posting my location all day: fine, it's mine. A journalist publishing my address because I wrote an op-ed: not fine. And the two that break whatever pattern I have: I think my undocumented neighbor is fully entitled to give the police a fake name, and I also think it would be fine for a parent to lie about their address to get a kid into a better school, even though both of those are evading something the state has a decent reason to want to know.

The best I've got is a slogan a sociologist friend keeps repeating, that the line isn't public vs. private but whether the data is used to count you or to steer you — so lying to a loyalty program or an ISP or a camera network is fine because they're steering me, while lying to a tax office or a census is different because that's counting. But then my neighbor case has to go, and so does the school-district one, and the German DPO thing starts looking like she's protecting me from steering rather than from counting, which I don't think is what she'd say she's doing. Is there a principle that actually gets all of these, or close? State it precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up, and say what it predicts for the cases I didn't list: a national DNA database for people who've been arrested but not convicted, my institute's new badge system that logs when I'm in the building (my German colleagues are appalled, I genuinely don't care), an insurer that wants my fitness-tracker history, and a supermarket that lets you in only if it recognizes your face. And if there's no single principle, tell me which two I'd need and where they collide. Answer in American English, please — I've started writing "organisation" and it's freaking me out.
