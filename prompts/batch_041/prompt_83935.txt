A colleague of mine has been defending, in seminars and now in print, a claim about moral foundations theory that I want to be sure I have properly assessed before I answer him. His argument runs through an analogy that moral psychologists have made something of a slogan: moral intuitions are like tastes, the moral foundations — harm and care, fairness and reciprocity, loyalty to the group, respect for authority, and purity or sanctity, with liberty sometimes counted as a sixth — are like the receptors on the tongue, and moral systems are like cuisines. Receptors differ in their sensitivity from person to person and from culture to culture, and cuisines differ accordingly; no cuisine is correct. From this he concludes that the psychology of left and right settles the question I am supposed to be teaching. The foundations may explain why the two sides talk past one another, he says, but they tell us nothing about who is right, because the disagreement is between palates.

What I want from you is an audit of the analogy, not an exposition of the theory. Set out the correspondence he is relying on — receptor, palate, cuisine, cook, nutrition, and whatever else the picture needs — then tell me where the mapping breaks and, crucially, which of the disanalogies bear on the conclusion he draws and which are merely decorative. I have two suspicions of my own that I should like tested rather than assumed. First, that taste receptors are evolved detectors of matters of consequence to the organism, so that the analogy may be quietly importing a standard of correctness which it then denies. Second, that taste is the least learned and least culture-soaked of the senses, whereas the phenomena the foundations are invoked to explain — the shifting weight that different political cultures place on harm, fairness, loyalty, authority and sanctity — are precisely the ones that vary with culture. I should be grateful for disanalogies I have not thought of, especially any that cut in his favour.

Then tell me what the analogy can bear. Is there a reading on which it supports a modest realism — foundations as detectors of genuine goods, cuisines as assessable for adequacy even where tastes are not — and if so, how much of his conclusion survives? Is there any route from the psychology of left and right to a normative conclusion that does not depend on the analogy at all, and does it tell us anything about who is right? If a better analogy is available for the same work, give me that too, with the same audit. Write in British English, and please argue rather than cite: I want no reading list.
