I've become pretty convinced by the interventionist picture of causation -- roughly, that 'X caused Y' is to be analysed in terms of what would happen to Y under an ideal intervention on X, and that counterfactual dependence is always relative to some chosen set of variables or a model. I like that it makes causation manipulatable and model-relative rather than a single mind-independent relation. What I want is the case against it: what observations, cases, or formal results should actually move me, ranked by how decisive they'd be. In particular, which of these do interventionists themselves concede counts against them -- the variable-choice problem, absences and omissions, transitivity, trumping-style cases, proportionality -- as opposed to what only critics say? And be honest about whether the view has been qualified into something that can't go wrong: if any awkward case can be swallowed by fiddling with the variable set, is that a virtue or a fatal concession? Keep separate what people who actually work on this agree on from what's genuinely contested, and don't sell me a contested reading as the consensus. I'd especially like to know which single thing, if it held up, should make me abandon the interventionist account rather than patch it. Please write in British English.
