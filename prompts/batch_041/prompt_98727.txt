I am putting together a dissertation prospectus (about 2,500 words, for a committee that will want a determinate question and a feasible plan) on the inefficacy objection to individual obligations in collective-harm cases — carbon emissions, meat consumption, voting — and I would like you to turn a half-formed idea of mine into a proposal I can defend.

The idea is that the entire debate operates at the wrong grain. It treats making a difference as a yes-or-no property that an individual act either has or lacks, and then argues about whether obligations can survive its absence. I think what actually varies across the disputed cases is the shape of an act's counterfactual contribution profile: how the total harm would have differed across the nearby ways the world could have gone, had I acted or not. I want to distinguish at least four shapes. Flat profiles, where nothing changes in any nearby counterfactual. Sloped profiles, where the magnitude of the harm shifts, sometimes too slightly to perceive. Cliff profiles, where the act moves the world across a threshold, so that a handful of acts more or fewer tips the outcome. And endogenous profiles, where whether my act matters depends on how others respond to it, so that the profile is not a fixed fact about my act but a function of social response that my own commitments may partly shape. My suspicion is that the canonical cases — the burger, the short-haul flight, the vote in a large election — are habitually filed as flat when they are really sloped with an imperceptible slope, and that flat and imperceptibly-sloped cases behave quite differently morally even when the expected-harm figures coincide.

Please write the proposal. It should contain: a statement of the question precise enough to have a determinate answer (I am inclined to the form, is there a pair of acts with identical expected marginal harm but different contribution profiles, such that we are obliged to perform one and not the other?); an account of why the existing answers leave this open, framed in terms of positions and arguments rather than authors, since I will supply the citations myself; the method, in stages I could actually execute over four months, including the operational test I intend to use — scaling one's contribution up and down by a factor and asking whether the harm changes — and how to keep the four-way taxonomy from collapsing into expected-value reasoning in fancier dress; a case for why a conceptual taxonomy does the work here rather than appeal to empirical psychology or to a general theory of collective obligation; a separate treatment of redundancy, which I take to be a distinct phenomenon from inefficacy and one the debate tends to conflate with it; a paragraph on why the question is worth settling, in terms of what it changes about how individual responsibility is described; what a completed result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and a candid assessment of scope, including which part of this is most likely to prove too large for the time available. Do not give me a reading list or references.
