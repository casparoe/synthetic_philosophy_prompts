I teach a seminar on minds and machines, and the Gödelian anti-mechanist argument — Lucas's line and Penrose's version of it — stalls every single year, and it's stalled in the literature for sixty-odd years. Everyone knows the moves: the anti-mechanist says we can see the truth of the Gödel sentence for any system we might be, so we aren't that system; the mechanist says that only works against a fixed system and we aren't fixed, or that whatever idealization lets you "see" the consistency of your own system is one the machine gets too. Nobody budges, and the discussion section always dies in the same place. I've started to suspect the disagreement isnt really about Gödel at all but about what kind of thing mathematical "seeing" is meant to be — a warranted state versus an output — so the two sides are idealizing the human mathematician differently and never arguing about which idealization is right. Is that the right diagnosis? And here's a wrinkle I want you to take seriously rather than wave at: once you let either side help themselves to reflection principles — principles that assert the soundness of a system from outside it — or to the thought that mathematical knowledge is open-ended and grows by adopting new axioms, the whole setup seems to shift, and I can't tell whether that dissolves the puzzle or just relocates it. So tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is — a formal one that some theorem could settle, a disagreement about idealization, or a verbal one about "see" and "know" — and given your diagnosis, what would count as progress, or whether we should expect none.
