I am a retired developmental biologist — thirty years on the cleavage and early patterning of mammalian embryos — who has spent the past several years teaching himself philosophy, and I have reached the stage where I want to think with the arguments rather than merely admire them. I would like you to construct a thought experiment for me.

The subject is origin essentialism: the thesis, commonly credited to Kripke, that a person's biological origin is essential to them — that I could not have come into being from a different sperm and egg, or from a different zygote, than the ones that in fact produced me, because any being so produced would have been someone else. The natural rival holds that origin is contingent: that what makes me me is something over and above the particular gametes that gave rise to me, so that the very same individual might have been assembled from a different pair. I want a case that forces these two views into open conflict and makes them deliver opposite verdicts about one concrete individual.

Here is the constraint that matters most, and it is why I am putting the question to someone with philosophical training rather than simply to an old colleague. I do not want a case built out of stipulated possible worlds — the familiar suppose-there-were-a-world-in-which-a-different-sperm-reached-the-egg variety, which seems to me to do all its work by fiat. I want the case to grow out of phenomena that actually occur in early human development, so that the metaphysics must answer to the biology rather than the other way round. Two such phenomena strike me as underexploited. The first is monozygotic twinning: a single zygote divides into two cell masses, each of which becomes a person, so that two individuals trace back to one origin. The second is chimerism: two zygotes, or two early embryos, merge into a single individual, so that one person traces back to two origins. Use either or both, but only as they actually occur — no invented biology, no science fiction.

The case is for a small reading group of amateurs, and its purpose is to test whether origin essentialism, which was formulated with tidy one-sperm-one-egg examples in view, can survive contact with the untidy facts of early development. That purpose fixes what would count as cheating: a case that announces its own verdict fails, however elegant it looks.

Specifications.

1. One concrete case, described in no more than roughly 250 words, using only what actually happens in mammalian reproduction.

2. The case must not stipulate its conclusion. Do not write a scenario that simply declares that some person could or could not have been otherwise; whatever verdicts emerge must fall out of the two theories as applied to the facts.

3. Single out one individual in the case and put to it one sharp question about its modal profile — something of the form, could this person have existed had the early embryo taken the other branch?

4. Then show your work. State what origin essentialism must say about the question and why; state what the rival view must say and why; and make the conflict explicit. If, on the case as you have built it, the two views do not in fact conflict, say so, and either rebuild the case or tell me the construction cannot be done.

5. Identify the one feature of the case that does the work — the feature whose removal would collapse the disagreement — then actually remove it, in a sentence or two, and show the collapse.

6. Say how a defender of whichever view comes off worse would try to defuse the case, giving the most credible repair rather than a strawman.

7. No citations, no reading lists, no appeals to what a particular author is said to have argued. I want the argument, not the scholarship.

Finally, and I mean this without irony: if the whole project is misconceived — if origin essentialism and its rival cannot be made to disagree over a case of this kind without smuggling in an assumption that settles the matter, or if the embryology I am pointing you toward turns out to be irrelevant to the metaphysics — tell me so plainly rather than producing a case that merely appears to work. I would far rather be told that my question is confused than be handed a tidy answer to the wrong question.
