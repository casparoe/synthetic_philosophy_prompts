So I'm a novelist — two books out, a third one stalled for going on two years — and I've been circling a thing about time that I can't seem to write my way out of. A friend who teaches physics finally sat me down with Gödel's work on the rotating universe, the cosmological solution to Einstein's equations where the whole cosmos spins and, if you fly far enough in the right direction, you can loop back and meet yourself at the launch pad. What hooked me wasn't the time travel. It was that Gödel seemed to think that solution tells us something about time itself — that if the laws permit a world like that, then time in the everyday sense, the sense where things genuinely pass, can't be part of the real furniture of the world; the passage of time is in some sense an illusion or an ideal that the mind adds. I have a half-baked suspicion there's a gap between "this strange world is possible" and "time is unreal," but every time I try to say where the gap is I either just restate his conclusion in worse words or start hand-waving about "becoming," and I honestly can't tell if I'm the one who's confused or if the argument is doing something I haven't spotted yet.

I'm applying for a small cross-disciplinary fellowship — they fund a handful of writers each year to do a year of "research toward a project" — and they want a real proposal, about two pages. Can you turn this into one for me? I want the question stated so it has a definite answer, something in the neighborhood of: does Gödel's argument actually establish that the passage of time is unreal, or does it only establish that one particular conception of passage can't survive in a certain kind of spacetime? — plus a clear account of why the readings people usually give it (the lazy ones, "relativity kills the present," "Gödel proved time is an illusion") don't settle that question either way. Then the method: what I'd actually do, week by week, as somebody who isn't a physicist but can read very carefully — reconstructing the argument in my own words, pulling out the hidden premise about what would have to be true for time to count as "real," and running it against the obvious objections to see what survives. I definately want to know what would count as the project succeeding versus failing — if I end up just paraphrasing his conclusion back at him, I've failed, and I'd rather see that coming. And be straight with me about scope: is this two pages of thinking or is it a book? Please don't hand me a reading list or citations, I'll track down the texts myself and alot of them I've already got on the shelf.
