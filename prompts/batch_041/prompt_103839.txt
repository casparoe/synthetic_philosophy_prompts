I spent 32 years as a field geologist and for the last three I've been hanging around the philosophy department's decision-theory reading group, and they've now let me run a session. It's about 18 undergrads, 75 minutes, one room, no computers needed.

What I want is a game where they actually feel escalation of commitment — the way you keep pouring money into a thing because of what you've already sunk into it — but not the cartoon version. Here's the fight I keep having with a colleague: he says the dollar-auction setup is just a sunk-cost fallacy in action, I say that once your'e standing there the next bid can be perfectly rational and the trap is in the structure of the game, not in the players' heads. I want the activity to make them feel both readings at once, so that mid-game they genuinely can't tell which one they're in.

So: rules, materials, a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money (pennies are fine), and one or two switches I can flip partway through — letting them collude, or buy an early exit, or have a third party subsidize one side — so we can watch what changes when only the structure changes. Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the two readings and points to the moments where they make different predictions.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (my fear is they just refuse to play, or one loud kid dominates and everyone else checks out), how to handle it, and be honest about the one thing a game like this can't show them. My guess is it can only show what people do, not what's actually rational — but tell me if that's wrong. Keep it practical, I'm not a theorist.
