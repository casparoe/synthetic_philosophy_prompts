I am a high school student in a philosophy club, and I have to write a paper that defends a position while being honest about its weaknesses. The position I have chosen is the identity of indiscernibles: that no two distinct things could be exactly alike in every respect, so a world containing two objects that differ only by being two rather than one is not merely unlikely but impossible. I find the standard illustration vivid -- a universe whose entire contents are two iron spheres of the same diameter, colour, and temperature, some fixed distance apart -- but I suspect that the illustration and the principle are doing less work than they appear to, and I would like you to tell me what would count against the principle rather than what is usually said in its favour.

In particular, I want to know:

1. Whether the two-spheres universe is a genuine counterexample, or whether it is dissolved by counting relational features as respects in which things can differ. If the spheres differ because one is a certain distance from the other, does the principle still say anything, or has it become true by definition? And if we restrict it to features that do not mention any particular individual, is the restricted version defensible, or does it fail exactly where the unrestricted version is trivial?

2. What to make of the following idea, which I have come across and which I would like explained rather than assumed: two things might share every feature that applies to either of them on its own, while each stands in a relation to the other that it does not stand in to itself -- for instance, each sphere is one metre from the other and not one metre from itself. Does that count as differing in a respect, and so save the principle, or is it a way of conceding that two things can be exactly alike while still being two?

3. Whether physics bears on any of this. If there are physical cases of particles that share every feature we can attribute to one of them individually but stand in some such relation to one another, does that refute the principle, support it, or neither? Please explain the physics in plain terms rather than assuming I have studied it.

4. Whether I should be defending the principle as a necessary truth at all, or whether a weaker claim about how things actually are would preserve whatever is interesting about it.

Rank the considerations from those that would be decisive against my position down to those that would be merely awkward, and for each say whether someone who holds the principle would concede it as a real cost or dismiss it as a misunderstanding. If the honest conclusion is that the principle survives only by being qualified until it says almost nothing, please say so plainly; I would rather learn that now than defend something hollow. Lead with that bottom line in a single sentence, then give the ranked considerations. Please keep the whole response under 600 words.
