John Stuart Mill could use the word 'liberal' without having to say which tradition he meant, and the term has since split in ways he could not have foreseen. In present-day American usage it denotes a broadly social-democratic, redistributive left; in much of continental Europe it still denotes a classical, market-oriented, limited-government position, often as a term of abuse rather than a badge; and the British Liberal tradition sits uncomfortably between the two. Seat Mill between a self-described American liberal and a continental European liberal of the market-oriented kind, and ask which, if either, is his heir. I want less a verdict than a reconstruction of the principles he would actually bring to the question — his account of liberty, his stance on state action and the harm principle, his conception of individuality — together with a diagnosis of where the exchange would stall: is the disagreement substantive, or does it turn on two parties loading opposite economic content into a single word? And say, finally, whether Mill's own position gives either usage a better claim to the name.
