I'm a postdoc in science and technology studies, and in about three weeks I'm giving a work-in-progress talk at a small interdisciplinary workshop on technology and politics. My paper argues that the familiar question of whether artifacts have politics is badly posed, because it asks about artifacts in the abstract when the real variable is what I call the enforcement regime. My claim is that an artifact is politically inert so long as its constraints operate through friction, inconvenience, or cost, and becomes genuinely political only when some party can invoke a sanction to make its sorting stick. I draw a distinction I lean on heavily between two kinds of design: sorting gates, which divide a population into those who pass and those who don't, and ambient defaults, which quietly set the conditions under which everyone operates without ever excluding anyone by name. My running examples are the turnstile and the CAPTCHA for the first category, and the default privacy setting for the second. The punchline is that only the sorting gates can carry politics in my sense, which means the standard literature that treats design itself as inherently political is conflating two different things: the politics baked into a design, and the politics a design merely makes available to whoever happens to operate it. I expect this to land as a provocation, and I want to be ready for the blowback.

The audience is genuinely mixed and I already have a rough sense of what each faction will say. There are several STS people who treat the proposition that artifacts have politics as settled and will hear my enforcement-regime move as a backdoor to the neutralist view they thought they had buried for good; two analytic philosophers of technology who will want necessary and sufficient conditions and will press hard on what exactly counts as politics in the first place; a couple of engineers from a human-computer interaction lab who suspect the whole thing is sociology-of-knowledge hand-waving and will want to know what, concretely, changes in how they build things on Monday; and one historian of technology who reliably asks what my account explains that a plain narrative of who built what, for whom, and against whom does not. Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. I want the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room rather than a generic list of possible criticisms, ordered by how much damage each would do to the paper, each phrased the way the person who holds it would actually phrase it, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front, what to hold firm on, and include the one question I am hoping nobody in the room thinks to ask. Please write in American English.
