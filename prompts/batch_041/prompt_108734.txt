Wittgenstein's private language argument is meant to show that a language whose words refer to purely private sensations is impossible. The target is the idea that I could invent a name for a sensation that only I can feel, and use it as a kind of private label. His argument is that this can't work because nobody else can check whether I'm using the word correctly — there's no public criterion of correctness, so there's no fact of the matter about whether I've got it right. The famous beetle-in-a-box bit makes the same point: imagine everyone has a box with something in it and nobody can look into anyone else's box, so when we talk about the beetle we're really only ever talking about our own box, which means the beetle itself drops out of consideration — and that shows that talk about sensations is really just talk about behaviour. So the upshot is that Wittgenstein was basically a behaviourist about the mind: he thought inner experiences don't really exist, or at least that we can't meaningfully talk about them, and that the whole notion of a private inner life is a philosophical illusion.
