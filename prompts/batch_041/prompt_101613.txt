I'm a master's student in public policy who spent two summers working at a small irrigation cooperative, and I keep running into a pattern that I have no name for and that none of the labels I've been handed quite seem to fit. Here is the shape of it. A shared resource — water, a pasture, a fishery, a shared database, a pool of overtime shifts — is owned by no one and has no formal rule governing its use, yet it stays intact for years, not because people are virtuous but because use is *legible*: everyone can see who is taking what. I hold back not out of principle but because being seen to overdraw would cost me more, in standing and in future cooperation, than the extra I would gain. Now suppose something changes that touches nobody's preferences and nobody's numbers: the resource starts being drawn through an intermediary, or usage gets aggregated into statistics, or the group grows past the point where you know the other users, or a privacy-protecting reform hides individual consumption. Taking becomes hard to attribute. The resource collapses. The users are no more numerous, no greedier, and no worse off in any material sense; all that has changed is that individual use has become illegible. Two further things strike me, and the usual story doesn't capture either. First, the loss of legibility is often something people *chose*, because it arrived bundled with something genuinely good — privacy, convenience, scale — so the collapse is the price of a benefit rather than a failure of character. Second, the obvious remedy — metering, auditing, surveillance to restore legibility — carries its own costs, so there is a real trade-off rather than a clean fix.

So my question is whether there is an established name for this specific dependence of a shared resource on the legibility of individual use, and for the collapse that follows when legibility is lost. I want to be told clearly what it is *not*. It is not, I think, the plain tragedy of the commons, because there the problem is open access and the driver is the number of users or the absence of any norm, whereas here a working norm exists and the only thing that changes is observability. It is not the free-rider problem, because the people here are not riding free — they are still contributing; they have merely stopped being *seen* to contribute, and the collapse runs through reputation rather than through each person's private calculus. It is not just a matter of enforcement, because no rule was ever enforced; the norm held by visibility, not by sanction. And I suspect it is also not the same as the literature on how communities successfully govern shared resources, because that literature seems to treat legibility as one design feature among several rather than as the load-bearing one. If a single established term does cover this, tell me which, and where my description overshoots or undershoots it; if nothing does, coin a term and define it, and tell me where its edges are — whether a norm sustained by mutual visibility is the same phenomenon as one sustained by an external monitor, and whether the collapse still counts if legibility was lost but a new norm grew back in its place.

Please structure your answer as follows: (1) **Restatement** — the phenomenon in your own words in a few sentences, so I can check that you have got it; (2) **The nearest existing concepts** — the two or three closest terms or bodies of work, each in a short paragraph, naming the specific respect in which each fails to fit; (3) **Verdict** — either the single best-fitting term or, if none fits, a coinage with a definition, in one or two paragraphs; and (4) **Boundary cases** — a short list of cases that would test the definition.
