I run grounds and pest control for a mid-sized school district — roughly thirty sites, mostly playing fields, hedgerows, and ornamental plantings — and from April through September I field two or three insecticide proposals a week, from the athletic department, from parents worried about ticks or wasps, from our landscaper, from the county health office. I have a budget, a spray license, and about twenty minutes per decision; anything to be applied while children are on site needs the superintendent's signature. I have read a good deal of the rationalist-adjacent literature on wild animal suffering, the replaceability argument, and dust-specks-style debates about aggregation, and none of it has given me anything I can actually run at seven in the morning.

Please write me a decision procedure — a checklist in prose, the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates in a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than entomology — for deciding whether to approve a proposed application. It has to preserve the moral structure the philosophical literature actually supports while dropping what a staffer without a philosophy background cannot use, and it has to be candid about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Two things I want it to take seriously rather than wave at. First, the substitution problem: an application that kills a pest population need not reduce total insect mortality at all, because broad-spectrum sprays typically hit predators and parasitoids harder than the pest, so the pest rebounds, and because whatever replaces it is also alive and also sentient-or-not by whatever standard you have adopted. Second, the fact that the procedure's answer to which invertebrates count — whether a threshold is drawn at some level of neural complexity, or whether the default is precautionary, treating any plausible capacity for pain as counting — is a choice and not an empirical finding, and I want that marked as such in the procedure rather than smuggled into the facts column.

Then run it on three proposals: a broad-spectrum pyrethroid spray across the stadium field to knock down an ant problem before a tournament; perimeter treatment of a hedgerow and the adjacent classroom wing for ticks, where the county has flagged a Lyme risk; and a request to destroy a wasp nest over a kindergarten entrance that I would rather leave alone. At least one of the three should come out against the answer I would obviously prefer, and you should say so explicitly. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which decisions it will keep getting wrong, and what would have to turn out to be true about insect sentience for the whole exercise to have been pointless.
