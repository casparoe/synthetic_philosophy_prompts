I sit on the clinical ethics committee at the hospital where I work, and we have deadlocked three times this year on cases in which honoring one patient's refusal would foreseeably cost several others their lives. The majority keeps invoking something like "you may not use her as a means," and I keep wanting to ask what the rule is for. I have come to think that we are not really disagreeing about the cases so much as making different responses to a deeper puzzle, and I would like a decision tree that makes those responses visible.

The puzzle, which I have seen called the paradox of deontology, is this: a deontological constraint forbids me to harm someone even when doing so would prevent more harms of the same kind, yet the most obvious rationale for having such constraints — that they protect individuals from being sacrificed for others — appears to favor violating them in precisely those cases. The constraint seems to be justified by something that also justifies breaking it.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Branch each node on a commitment that genuinely separates the responses, and phrase every node as a question someone could answer without already knowing where it leads; where a node needs a case, draw it from clinical or military practice rather than from the usual runaway trolley. I want the tree to separate, at minimum: responses that keep the constraints and reject the minimizing rationale; responses that keep the rationale and revise the constraints, either by making them finer-grained or by giving them a threshold; responses that revise the theory of reasons instead, so that the minimizing rationale was never the operative one; responses that conclude only that agents need room not to maximize, without any constraints; responses that ground constraints in the agent's own agency, so that the constraint concerns what I do rather than what happens; and responses that accept the inconsistency rather than dissolving it. The tree should also distinguish the puzzle of why one would have such constraints at all from the puzzle of why one would keep them when breaking one would minimize breaches, since some views answer one and not the other.

Each leaf should carry the name of the position where one exists and 'unnamed' where it does not, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Then tell me which combinations my branches permit that nobody actually occupies, and for each whether it is empty because incoherent, because unattractive, or because merely unexplored. Flag any two positions the tree cannot separate. Aim for eight to twelve leaves.
