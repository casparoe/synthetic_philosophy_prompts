My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about Ian Hacking and whether psychiatric categories can be real, and I ended up defending a position I hadn't fully worked out. I've since written it up as a short essay, and I'd like your comments on it. Here is the essay, working title "Two Loops, Not One":

Ian Hacking's idea of looping effects is usually stated in one direction: a classification is applied to people, those people come to understand themselves through it, they change, and the classification is then revised to fit the changed people. The standard moral is that psychiatric categories are unstable in a way that natural-kind categories are not, and that this instability is evidence that the categories are made rather than discovered.

I think this conflates two distinct loops, and separating them changes the moral. The first loop runs from label to person: being classified as X makes some people act more like the stereotype of X. Call this conforming. It is real and genuinely troubling for a diagnostic manual, because the manual's own success in spreading a label can inflate the prevalence it measures. The second loop runs from person to label: the people classified as X change in ways the original criteria did not anticipate, and clinicians respond by revising the criteria. Call this drift. This is what happens when a diagnosis is rewritten because patients no longer fit the old description.

These are different phenomena with different implications. Conforming makes a category less valid, because the label is partly producing its own extension. Drift does not by itself make a category less valid; it is what any category does when the world it describes changes. The confusion matters because the anti-realist argument needs conforming, but the vivid historical examples are usually drift. Drift is also not unique to human kinds. When astronomers found objects that fit the old definition of a planet but obviously did not belong with the others, they did not conclude that "planet" was a fiction; they rewrote the criteria. That is drift, and it happened to a category of rocks and gas.

What is distinctive about human kinds, then, is not that the criteria drift but that the drift is mediated by the classified people's own self-understanding: they are not merely measured, they read the measurements. I would call this bidirectional uptake, and I would argue it is the thing worth theorizing, not looping effects in general. If that is right, the interesting question for psychiatry is not whether its categories are real but which loops are conforming and which are drift, and whether the self-understanding-mediation makes a category a better or a worse instrument.

I'd like comments of two kinds. First, on the substance: is the conforming/drift distinction doing real work, or is it a redescription of a single phenomenon with a nicer name? Does the planet analogy earn its keep, or does it trade on an obvious disanalogy — that planets do not read their own classification? And where does the essay overreach; what is the strongest objection I have not anticipated? Second, on the presentation: what would you cut, and what needs to be said more carefully, if this were to be read by someone who knows Hacking's work well?
