My editor wants me to do something funny for the ideas column for once, so here's the pitch I'm working from: a satirical prospectus for a hedge fund called Pascal Capital, marketing a product whose entire selling point is that it pays out infinite utility if the right God exists and nothing otherwise — a dominant strategy, they claim, whatever your priors. Two pages, deadpan, laid out like a real fund doc (investment thesis, risks, fees, the fine print). The jokes have to come from the actual decision theory rather than generic religion-bashing: infinite expected value swamping every probability; the many-gods problem showing up as a portfolio problem, i.e. which deity are we even buying exposure to and how do you hedge; the awkward fact that the payout requires belief and you can't wire-transfer yourself into it, so there's a 'cultivation period' where the client stupefies himself with rituals; the objection that this argument proves exactly as much for every rival faith and therefore nothing at all; and a seperate risk-disclosure section that has to own up to the mugger variant, the guy who offers you infinite torture unless you hand over your wallet. No winking. 600-700 words. I'd like to not get roasted by my philosopher friends, so end with a short editor's note, three or four bullets, on where I've taken liberties with the argument — what I've exaggerated, what I've quietly given away on Pascal's behalf, which bits lean on a contested reading of him — and what would actually change my mind about the piece: what argument or fact would show I've aimed the satire at the wrong target, and where the whole thing is most likely to fall apart.
