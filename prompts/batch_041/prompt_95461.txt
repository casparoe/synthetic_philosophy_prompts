I run a fortnightly reading group for graduate students in economics and philosophy who have each met Aumann's agreement theorem once and remember the punchline. The recurring irritation is that the theorem gets used as a conversation-stopper: whenever two of us disagree about something quantitative, someone observes that if we were both rational Bayesians with a shared prior whose credences had become common knowledge, we could not disagree, so at least one of us must be failing at rationality. I want to replace that move with a proper diagnostic instrument.

Please write me a decision tree, in prose, that begins from the situation the theorem is supposed to cover and ends at a named position on where the pressure to agree actually breaks. Fix the starting point as follows: two people assign different credences to the same proposition; each has publicly stated their credence; each knows the other has heard and understood the statement; and they still disagree. The root of the tree is what follows from that, and the first several branch nodes should be questions about this and similar concrete situations, phrased so that a reader can answer them without knowing which position they lead to. No node may simply ask whether the reader accepts the common prior assumption.

Build the tree from the space of assumptions first, not from a list of names: a shared prior; common knowledge of each other's credences and of each other's rationality; logical omniscience, or at least the ability in principle to check each other's reasoning; both parties wanting to get the proposition right rather than to hold a convenient belief; and the presupposition that the two are assigning credence to one and the same proposition over one and the same space of possibilities. Denying each of these should generate a family of positions; the leaves are where the actual views sit, labelled descriptively rather than by author name, since we will not be looking anything up, and each leaf should carry one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

The following cases must each do work somewhere in the tree, and it should be clear which node each is there to settle:
- two people who share a prior, one of whom has strictly more information than the other, and who still disagree — is this a counterexample to the theorem, or a sign that the theorem is being misread?
- two mathematicians who disagree about an unproved conjecture, each convinced the other's argument contains an error that neither can locate;
- two people who disagree about whether a policy worked, where one of them does not know that a subgroup exists which the other is talking about, or is using a different vocabulary for the same subject matter;
- two people whose disagreement is privately resolvable but who each have reasons not to disclose what would resolve it, so that the public standoff persists.

I want eight to ten leaves. Note any combination of answers that no position occupies, and say briefly whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate and say what further question would be needed to pull them apart. Close with a paragraph on what the tree implies for the move I started with — when, if ever, it is legitimate to tell a colleague that the theorem obliges them to agree with you.
