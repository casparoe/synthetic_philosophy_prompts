I'm a retired hydrologist — 35 years of groundwater modeling, mostly the Ogallala and a couple of basins overseas — and since I retired I've been reading philosophy of science on my own, Popper, Kuhn, Lakatos, whatever the library has. I've hit a pattern I don't have a word for, and I'd like you to put on your philosopher-of-science hat and help me figure out whether it already has a name or whether it needs one.

Here's the thing. Somebody makes a forecast with a date baked into it: we'll hit peak oil by the mid-2000s, that aquifer will be dry by 2020, the machines will outthink us by 2045. The date comes and goes and nothing happens. But the people who made the forecast don't drop it. They say it was right but early — what it really identified was a trend, or a tendency, and the timing was never the point. Then comes the part that really bugs me: the non-event gets recruited as evidence that it's still coming. It's building. Calm before the storm. The reason it hasn't happened yet is that we keep patching things up. So the same failure does double duty — it doesn't count against the theory, and it can count for it. I've also noticed that the "patch" explanation cuts both ways: if we stave off the crash, that's proof the pressure was real.

I can see this is adjacent to things I've read about. It's obviously a rescue of some kind, and I know about ceteris paribus clauses and about tacking on auxiliary hypotheses. But what I'm after seems narrower, and specifically about *time* — the way a forecast can be quietly restated so that its deadline stops being part of its content, with nobody admitting the forecast changed. Is there a term of art for that? I gather there's a whole genre of "Malthus was right, just early" arguments, so maybe somebody has named this. If there isn't a term, don't just hand me a clever label — I want the carving: what the thing actually is, what it isn't, and where its edges are. Is it just an ordinary ad hoc rescue with a clock attached? Is it the same as what Lakatos said about degenerating research programs, or is that too coarse? And how is it different from a theory that was simply vague about timing from the start — because that seems like a different sin to me and I want to know if the professionals agree.

Think it through before you answer; I'd rather have slow and careful than fast. And tell me where your own reasoning is weakest and what would change your mind — if there's a decent argument that this pattern is epistemically fine, or that the line I'm drawing between "right but early" and "vague all along" can't be held, I want to hear it, because I dont trust my own gut on this one. Plain American English, please, and no reading list — I can't keep up with it anyway.
