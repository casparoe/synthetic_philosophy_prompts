I am a professor of philosophy, and next month I shall present a paper at a small closed workshop on Kantian ethics. The audience will consist mostly of Kant scholars and historians of ethics, including two committed Kantians who work on the formula of humanity, a virtue ethicist who is sceptical of rule-centred readings, and a colleague whose expertise is in the metaphysics of agency; they know the primary texts intimately and will not be impressed by appeals to the spirit rather than the letter of Kant. My thesis is that Kant’s prohibition on lying is best understood as a constraint on the maxim of communicative address rather than on the act-type of asserting a falsehood, and that this reading can make room for a narrow permission to deceive the murderer at the door without collapsing the duty of veracity into a consequentialist calculation. On my reading, the duty is owed to humanity in the person of the interlocutor, but only to someone who has not forfeited what I call communicative standing; the murderer, by using the question as an instrument of his ends, forfeits that standing, so the falsehood is not a violation of the duty of veracity but a refusal to let that duty be instrumentalised. I was arguing about this with a colleague in the senior common room last week, and she said the reading was either trivial or heterodox; I want to know which objections will make that charge stick.

Please draft a Q&A prep sheet for me. I do not need citations, a reading list, or a survey of the secondary literature; I need to know what this audience will actually ask. Give me the eight or so objections they are most likely to raise, ordered by how much damage they would do to my position, each phrased in their vocabulary — maxims and universalisability, the distinction between duties of right and duties of virtue, the formula of humanity, the difference between lying and merely not telling the truth, the problem of unintended consequences, the worry that I am quietly converting a duty of right into a counsel of prudence, and so on — with a two-minute reply for each and a candid note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and which single objection I should prepare to meet rather than hope they miss. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and if you judge that the thesis cannot survive the strongest objection in the list, say so plainly rather than softening it into a talking point.
