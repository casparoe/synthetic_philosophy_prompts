I'm in a seminar on the ethics of deception and I keep getting stuck on lying vs. misleading — like whether saying something true *in order* to make someone believe something false is really any better than just lying — and my prof said the cleanest way in is to look at what institutions actually do rather than at intuitions. So here's the practice I want you to dig into. In court, a witness is sworn to tell not just the truth but the whole truth and nothing but the truth, which sure sounds like it forbids leaving the jury with a false picture. But the crime of perjury only punishes statements the witness doesn't believe to be true, and there's a famous unanimous Supreme Court case from the early 70s (Bronston) where a film producer was asked under oath whether he'd ever had a Swiss bank account, answered that the company had had one in Zurich for about six months, and left everyone with the impression that he personally had never had one — when he'd in fact had a personal account in Geneva the whole time. The Court said that wasn't perjury: the answer was literally true, and the remedy for a misleading-but-true answer is that the examiner asks a better follow-up question, not that the witness gets prosecuted. It even resurfaced as a defense during the Clinton impeachment. But then in securities law and consumer protection, a statement that's technically true can absolutely be actionable if it omits something material and leaves a false impression — a half-truth can get you sued. Same conduct, "misleading but not false," protected in one setting and punished in the other.

So what I want: reconstruct the theory of communication this patchwork presupposes. Does it presuppose that there's a real moral and legal line between what a statement *says* and what it merely *gets you to believe* — i.e., that only the semantic content of an utterance is the sort of thing that can be wrongful — or does it presuppose something else entirely, like that misleading is fine when the listener has a fair chance to protect themselves and not fine when there's an information asymmetry plus a duty to disclose? Which rival views does it quietly rule out? Are the parts even consistent with eachother — the oath seems to demand the whole truth while the statute only demands non-falsity, so is that a contradiction or can the oath be read as purely ceremonial/hortatory? And I want you to seperate what the practice strictly *requires* from what it merely happens to fit well with: could a rule-consequentialist, a Kantian about the duty of veracity, and a virtue-ethicist about honesty all sign onto the very same legal rules, for different reasons? If so, say exactly where the practice is underdetermined.

The practical reason I care: a friend of mine in compliance is drafting an internal rule for our company that sorts every employee statement into "false" and "misleading" and makes only the first one fireable — she says she's just copying the line the law already draws. Is that actually the line the law draws, or is she flattening a mess into a tidy binary? And here's my own half-baked hypothesis that I want you to either back or kill: that the lying/misleading distinction is doing almost none of the real work in these rules, and the actual variable is the *structure of the encounter* — adversarial questioning where the other side gets to re-ask, versus a one-shot disclosure to someone who can't interrogate you. If that's right, tell me what would have to change in courtroom practice if we took the oath literally and made misleading testimony punishable the way a misleading prospectus is; if it's wrong, tell me what the lying/misleading distinction is actually tracking that the adversarial-structure story can't capture.
