Ok this is going to get a bit personal but I need to work it out somewhere. About a year ago someone did something pretty bad to me, I won't go into it, and I've spent way to much time thinking about blame since then. And I've landed on a view, which I think is basically Strawson's thing about reactive attitudes (the Freedom and Resentment one): that being morally responsible just is being a fitting target of the reactive attitudes -- resentment, indignation, guilt, gratitude, that whole family. Not that those feelings are evidence of responsibility, or a symptom of it, but that they're the whole of it. There's nothing else there for responsibility to be.

I'm definately fairly convinced by it, which is exactly why I want to know what it's exposed to. So: what would count against it? Not a survey of every objection ever made, I want the ones that should actually move me, ranked by how decisive they'd be, and I want to know which of them a committed defender would themselves admit count against them versus which ones they'd just wave off as a misunderstanding of the view.

Things that have been bugging me, in no particlar order:

- I was on an SSRI for a few months last year and the resentment basically evaporated, but my judgement that what he did was wrong didn't budge at all. Same facts, same beliefs, way less anger. So was he less responsible in June than in March? Because it kind of sounds like my view makes responsibility dose-dependent, which can't be right.

- I can also flip into the detached clinical mode about him where I feel nothing about it, and honestly that mode doesn't feel like a distortion or a lie. If the participant stance is where the truth lives, why does the objective stance feel just as honest?

- I've started thinking there might be two different things people call resentment. One is a kind of demand -- you owe me an account of yourself -- and the other is just a wound, that hurt and I'm not over it. The demand one seems to track responsibility, the wound one seems to track damage, and I can have either without the other. If that distinction holds up, does it break the view or just complicate it? And which of the two is supposed to be doing the work?

- I get genuinely angry at my car when it won't start, and at my grandfather who's been dead for years, and I'd be embarrassed to say either of them is responsible for anything. So is my anger there not really a reactive attitude, or is the view committed to something absurd?

- The reverse case: people who forgive instantly, or who seem constitutionally incapable of holding a grudge, or traditions that treat resentment as something you train out of yourself. Are they not holding anyone responsible? Does the view make a certain emotional temperament a precondition of a moral status?

- And groups. I feel real indignation at companies and governments and I'd say they're responsible for things, but there's no single mind there to be the target of anything. What does the view do with collective responsibility?

The thing I'm most worried about is that it's unfalsifiable. Any time someone says 'actually I don't think he's responsible', I can say 'then you're just not in the participant stance', and any time someone feels resentment I can say 'see, responsibility'. Is that a bug, or is that actually the whole point -- that responsibility isn't the kind of thing evidence decides at all? And if it is the point, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or is that just what a view about a practice has to look like? Be honest on that one, I don't want a rescue.

And if I'm wrong, if responsibility is something over and above what the attitudes track, what am I missing -- what does the rival view predict or explain that mine can't? Don't end on a both-sides note. If you think the view survives all of this, say so, and say why the costs are worth paying.
