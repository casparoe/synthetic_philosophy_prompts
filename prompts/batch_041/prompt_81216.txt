The exercise is worth 20 points: 8 for the typings, 6 for matching each case to a reply, 6 for the verdicts and defense. The three-way distinction in the question is the professor's own coinage for the course, so please grade against the definitions printed on the sheet rather than against anything else.

Here's the question: "Lecture 9 distinguished three ways a single act can stand to a collectively produced harm. A drip contribution adds an imperceptible increment to a stock; the harm is the accumulation, and no increment on its own is noticeable. A flip contribution is the one that crosses a threshold and triggers the harm, though any of many acts could have been the one that crossed it; the harm is the crossing. An echo contribution would have made a difference only in a world where others had not already acted; in the actual world it changes nothing, because the harm is already fixed. (a) Assign each of the following to the type that best fits it — personal flying, eating meat, voting in a national election — and flag any case that seems to fit more than one type. (b) For each case, name which of the three replies to the inefficacy objection from lecture (expected-difference, universalizability, participation) best fits the case as you have typed it, and say why. (c) On the basis of your typings, does the inefficacy objection show that the individual act is permissible? Defend your verdict for each case. Roughly 500 words; no outside sources."

And here's what the student handed in: "By the lecture's taxonomy, personal flying is a drip contribution. My seat adds a few hundred kilograms of CO2 to a stock that is already enormous, and the increment is imperceptible to anyone it might harm, so it does no harm to anyone; the harm is in the accumulation and each individual increment is lost inside it. I would also flag that flying is an echo contribution, since an echo contribution is one that makes no difference and the climate harm is happening either way. Voting is a flip contribution, because the outcome flips on the count and any single ballot could in principle be the one that crosses the line. The probability that mine is the crossing ballot is about one in several million in a national election, so the expected difference my vote makes is effectively zero. The expected-difference reply, which is really the universalizability reply written in probabilities, since both ask me to consider a world in which my act is replicated, says that I should weigh that vanishing difference against what it costs me to vote, and on that weighing voting comes out permissible but not required; I would add that voting has expressive value, which the reply has no room for. Meat is the hardest case and I would type it as a flip contribution: a purchase can be the one that crosses a producer's threshold for ordering another batch of animals, so my purchase is the cause of that animal's death. The participation reply is strongest here: even when my act makes no difference on its own, I am part of the collective that does the harm, and since the collective harm is just the sum of the individual acts, I am responsible for my share of it — roughly one two-thousandth of a cow. So the inefficacy objection fails for meat and for climate, but it succeeds for voting, since voting is not a stock being added to but a single decision that my act almost certainly does not decide."
