For some weeks now I have been following a thread on a forum I have read for years, in which an interlocutor defends the thesis that the sciences divide the world along joints that are genuinely there. His argument leans on the familiar image of the skilled butcher, who separates a carcass along its natural seams instead of hacking through the meat at random; the thought is that our classifications earn their keep exactly to the extent that they follow divisions already present in the material. From this he extracts a fairly strong conclusion: that the question whether some category is a natural kind — a biological species, a psychiatric diagnosis, a colour category, or a cluster that a machine-learning classifier has turned up in unlabelled data — always has a determinate answer, discoverable by empirical means, and that the injunction to keep the map faithful to the territory is a live methodological constraint rather than a platitude.

I am drawn to the conclusion but cannot tell whether the butcher is doing any work in the argument or merely decorating it. I should like an audit of the analogy rather than an endorsement or a dismissal of it. Give me the correspondence explicitly, in a table if that helps: what plays the role of the butcher, the carcass, the joints and the knife, and whether anything in the butcher's situation is left unmapped. Then say where the mapping breaks down, and — this is the part I care about — which of those breakdowns matter for the conclusion he wants to draw and which are merely incidental. Note also what the image quietly imports from the butcher's situation into the scientific one, and whether any of that import is doing hidden work. If the conclusion survives the audit, say so and say why; if it survives only in a weakened form, state that form precisely and tell me what has been lost. And if the butcher is a poor guide here, propose a replacement analogy, being explicit about what it buys us that the original did not.
