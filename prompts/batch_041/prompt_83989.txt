Our town code requires every new house to be set back at least twenty-five feet from the sidewalk, and no one can tell me why. I sat in on the planning board last week, when a developer asked to build closer to the street, and two members argued for twenty minutes straight.

A said: you don't tear down a fence until you know why it was put up. This rule has outlasted everyone who wrote it, which means it survived a century of people who had reasons to remove it, and that survival is itself evidence that it does something. He called that the presumption of prescription — the accumulated judgment of generations, which no one of us can reconstruct from scratch.

B said: persistence proves nothing, because rules disappear only when someone makes the effort to remove them. No one is assigned to audit the code and repeal takes a supermajority, so a useless rule can persist forever, and so can a rule that is merely a by-product of something else. She called this the repeal asymmetry: keeping is free, removing is costly, so we should expect dead rules to accumulate.

A said: but a rule that actually hurt people would provoke a repeal campaign. Harmed people organize.

B said: only when the harm is concentrated and the harmed have standing. Here the benefit is concentrated — incumbent owners' property values — while the cost is diffuse, spread across everyone who will ever pay more for housing, so no campaign would arise even if the rule were a pure loss. And the rule may never have been put up by anyone at all; it may be a convention that emerged, in which case there is no fence-builder to find and your test cannot be run. Her fix was a sunset clause: every rule lapses after twenty years unless the board re-enacts it.

A said: that just means the reformers win by default, since the people who could defend the rule are long dead — and a rule no one can defend is not the same as a rule no one needs.

Who is right about what? Rate each of the five moves, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a short justification for each, and say whether the sunset clause helps or defeats the point. Take a definite position — including which of them is closer to right overall — rather than surveying the considerations.
