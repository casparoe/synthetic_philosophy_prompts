Last night a friend and I argued for two hours about Kavka's toxin puzzle and ended in a variant neither of us had seen. In the original, an eccentric billionaire puts before you a vial of toxin that would make you painfully ill for a day but do no lasting harm, and offers to deposit a million dollars in your account tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink it tomorrow afternoon; the money arrives hours before you would drink, and you remain free to change your mind, so that drinking then buys you nothing and costs you a day of illness. The received claim is that a rational agent simply cannot form the intention.

My friend's twist is this: before midnight you may hand over a bond to a commitment service that will forfeit the bond to a cause you find repugnant unless you drink the toxin by the appointed hour. I want to make this the single essay question on the final for my upper-level seminar on practical reason.

Please write the question, answerable in about ninety minutes, together with a model answer of roughly 600 words and a marking scheme worth twenty marks that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves a top answer must contain. I want the scheme to require, at minimum: the condition the bond must satisfy for the intention to become sincere, stated in terms of the bond and the toxin's disutility; an account of what this variant shows about where the puzzle's force actually comes from; and the strongest reply open to a billionaire who anticipates the manoeuvre. Assume students know the standard literature on the puzzle but have never encountered this variant, and do not let the question be answerable by reciting that literature. No citations or reading lists.
