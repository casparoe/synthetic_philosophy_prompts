I've somehow ended up writing the discussion note for my reading group's Keynes fortnight, and it's the "what did he think a good life actually was" bit that's doing my head in. The easy version is that there are two Keyneses. The young one, in that memoir he wrote about the religion of his undergraduate set in Cambridge, is a thoroughgoing Moorean: what's good in itself is certain states of mind — the enjoyment of beautiful things and of personal affection, chiefly our own — and he's quite candid that this left him and his friends with no real interest in rules or in consequences in the ordinary sense, and that they thought nothing else mattered at all. The older one, in the essay where he squints a hundred years ahead and predicts that the economic problem of subsistence will be solved, so that we'll be at a loss about what to do with the freedom, seems to be after something different: learning to live well once you no longer have to earn your bread, plus a fair amount of contempt for the love of money as a possession.

What I can't settle is whether that's two theories or one. A mate in the group insists the later Keynes has quietly turned into a perfectionist — that the art of life is about activity and skill, about doing things well, whereas the Moorean goods are just pleasant states you could be dropped into by a pill or a nice garden, and Moore's view has no way of telling the difference, so it isn't really the same view at all. I'm not convinced; the later essay still reads to me like it's recommending states of mind, just different ones. Could you compare the two conceptions of the good life for me, say which of the two is the more defensible, and come down on whether my mate is right that they're seperate theories rather than one and the same? British English please, and no reading list — I've already got the texts.
