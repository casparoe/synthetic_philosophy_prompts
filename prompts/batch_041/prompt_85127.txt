I am preparing a proposal for a six-month, single-investigator project (the funder wants roughly 3,000 words plus a workplan, and the reviewers are philosophers of science, so no hand-holding is required). The topic is determinism and predictability, and my complaint is that the question is usually posed badly. The standard move is a pair of slogans: determinism does not entail predictability, because of sensitive dependence on initial conditions; and indeterminism does not entail unpredictability, because chances can be sharp and well calibrated. I think both slogans are true and that the whole surrounding discussion trades on a conflation, and I want to turn that hunch into a funded proposal.

My half-formed idea has three parts.

First, a taxonomy. I want to separate at least three things people mean when they call a system unpredictable: (a) its future state is not fixed by its present state, the ontic reading; (b) its future state cannot be computed from its present state by any procedure at all, given unlimited time and exact input, or cannot be computed from the finite-precision input an actual procedure would receive; and (c) it cannot be forecast by any agent with bounded resources to within a stated tolerance over a stated horizon, which is the reading that does the work in weather, epidemiology, and policy. My suspicion is that (c) is the only reading that earns its keep and that it is not a property of a system at all but of a system together with four choices: the target variable, the tolerance, the horizon, and the resource bound. If that is right, then talk about the predictability of a system, unrelativised, is close to empty. I also suspect that the Laplacean-demon intuition pump is what makes (b) look like the fundamental reading, and that the demon is quietly smuggling in (a).

Second, the part I actually want to be judged on. Once predictability is relativised that way, does determinism retain any constitutive role? I see two live answers and do not know which is right. Answer A: determinism still matters, because it alone fixes the limiting case; under determinism the set of futures consistent with the present can be driven to a point as precision and resources increase, and under indeterminism it cannot. Answer B: determinism matters not at all, because there are deterministic systems whose behaviour is unpredictable in every resource-bounded sense one could care about, and indeterministic systems as predictable as one likes, so the two notions are orthogonal and the residual appeal of A is an artefact of idealisation. I want a proposal that can decide between A and B or show that the dispute is verbal, and I want it to be honest that verbal is a live verdict.

Third, cases. I would rather not lean on the billiard-ball and butterfly-effect chestnuts. I am considering: a discrete dynamical system whose next state can only be obtained by running it, so that the rule is a perfect predictor in principle and a useless one in practice; a chaotic map used to generate a keystream, where unpredictability is a security property rather than an epistemic lack; a stochastic model with a well-calibrated predictive distribution, as a test of whether indeterminism ever buys predictability; and quantum measurement, where I want to resist letting indeterminism do work it does not need to do.

What I need from you is the proposal itself: the question stated so that it has a determinate answer; why the existing positions, namely the line that chaos reconciles determinism with unpredictability, the line that determinism is simply irrelevant to forecasting, and the Laplacean line, leave that question open; the method, spelled out month by month, with a justification for conceptual analysis plus small toy models rather than a survey of the physics literature; what a successful outcome would look like and what would count as failure, with the real risk named, which I take to be that the taxonomy turns out to be stipulative and A versus B turns out to be terminological; the scope that genuinely fits six months; and a short closing paragraph on why the result would matter to how the free-will debate states its premises and to how forecast-based policy claims are hedged. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; refer to positions and arguments by description and I will attach the references myself. Write it as a proposal, not an essay: the aim is to show that the work is worth doing and can be done, not to defend a particular answer.
