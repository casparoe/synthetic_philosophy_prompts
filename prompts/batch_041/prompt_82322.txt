I lead a small strategic-foresight unit in a foreign ministry — four analysts, no satellites, no intercepts, only open sources and whatever structure we impose on them. Our product is a set of long-range assessments: ten- to twenty-five-year outlooks on regions, chokepoints, resource basins, and alliance structures, roughly twenty a year, each of which carries the unit's name and goes to the minister. Their readers live in the news cycle; our job is to live outside it, and we are not doing it well.

The failure is consistent. We over-weight events. A coup, a sudden death, a default, a signed treaty, a new missile — these are what gets recorded, discussed, and remembered. The slow variables that will actually determine the twenty-five-year picture — aquifer depletion, the age structure of a labor force, the erodibility of a delta, the debt-service profile of a state, the physical geometry of a chokepoint, the sunk costs locked into an energy system — receive a paragraph, if that.

I want you to convert Fernand Braudel's program — the longue durée, and his rough division of historical time into structures, conjunctures, and events — into a procedure my analysts can run. Treat it as an operational instrument, not an essay. Specifically:

First, an ordered set of questions to put to any proposed development — a claimed trend, a forecast, a single dramatic event, a piece of received wisdom — that sorts it into one of the three tiers and assigns it weight in a long-range assessment. State what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, and what to do when the answers conflict. The middle tier must do real work: conjunctures are the medium-run rhythms — price, credit, and demographic cycles — and the procedure has to distinguish them from both the near-permanent structures beneath and the noise above, rather than treating the three tiers as a rhetorical ladder.

Second, a rule for the hard cases: a fact that is a structure for one question and an event for another; a genuine discontinuity, where the slow structure itself breaks rather than merely being punctuated; and an event that is in fact a hinge, where a regime falls and the structure really is reset. Explain how the procedure is supposed to tell a hinge from noise without the benefit of hindsight, and say plainly if it cannot.

Third, a guard against the opposite failure. Taken seriously, the longue durée tempts us into assessments in which geography is treated as destiny, nothing anyone does matters, and every outcome was foreordained. The procedure needs a step that keeps agency and contingency in view without collapsing back into event-chasing.

Fourth, the points where judgment cannot be eliminated. Where the checklist cannot decide — where the analyst is making a values call, a taste call, or a bet about which structure is load-bearing — mark that in the procedure rather than concealing it behind a score.

Then run the procedure end to end on two cases, showing each step and the output:

(a) A dramatic event: the unexpected death of a long-serving autocrat in a petro-state, followed by a contested succession. The minister will want an assessment within the week.

(b) A slow variable: a steady decline in the water table beneath a major agricultural region that supplies a third of the country's calories.

Include one worked case in which the procedure tells us to give less weight to something we are attached to, or to spend effort where the minister will find it boring. Close with an honest account of what the procedure cannot do: the questions for which it will generate confident nonsense, and the signals that we are using the word structure as an excuse for fatalism or as a shield against having to make a call.
