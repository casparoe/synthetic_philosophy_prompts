I have spent the better part of a decade on the clinical-policy side of medicine — the part that decides which conditions get recognized, coded, and paid for — and I keep running into a pattern for which I have no name. I suspect it is not quite the disease-versus-normality debate I half-remember from an undergraduate course, and I would like to know whether it already has a home somewhere in the philosophy of medicine or whether I have to invent one.

The pattern is this. Whether a bodily state gets called a disease seems to depend on two things that are not features of the body at all.

The first is whether anything can be done about it. Two people can carry the identical underlying dysfunction — the same measurable deviation from the species norm, the same enzyme deficit, the same lesion on the same scan — and if one of them happens to have the version for which we possess a drug or an operation, we call hers a disease, while his is filed as a variation, a trait, or simply how some people are built. The state is the same; the label follows the remedy. Let a treatment appear, and the very people who were described as different become patients, without any change in their bodies.

The second is whether the state frustrates the person's own aims. A woman whose shoulder dislocates every time she trains for a marathon is, in the clinic's eyes, ill; a woman with the identical shoulder who has arranged her life so that it never dislocates, and who does not experience it as a problem, is not really a case at all. Same joint, same deviation, opposite verdict — and the verdict tracks what she cares about rather than what is wrong with her.

Neither of these is simply a matter of how common the state is; the statistics do not shift between the two people in either example. And neither seems to be captured by the bare thought that disease is a value-laden term, because values are not the only thing doing the work here: the availability of an intervention and the fit between the state and the person's plans are pulling on the label independently. I can see that all of this is adjacent to the familiar quarrel over whether disease is a biological or an evaluative notion, but I do not think either side of that quarrel names the thing I am pointing at.

So: is there an established concept, or at least a recognized name, for the phenomenon in which the disease label tracks (a) whether the state is remediable and (b) whether it thwarts the particular person's aims, rather than tracking anything intrinsic to the state? If such a concept exists, I want to know precisely how it is meant to differ from the ordinary distinction between a dysfunction and an illness, from the general claim that medicine's categories are socially constructed, and from the older argument about statistical versus evaluative abnormality — which of these is nearest, and where each of them fails to fit. If no such concept exists, then name it and give me a definition with edges: what else would fall under it, what superficially similar case would not, and where the pattern breaks down. Are there conditions whose status genuinely does not budge when a treatment appears or when the patient's aims change, and what is different about them?

I care about the carving far more than the label. A term with no boundaries will not help me.
