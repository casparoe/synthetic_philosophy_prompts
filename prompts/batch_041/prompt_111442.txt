I'm in a seminar on logical empiricism and we've spent the last two weeks on Neurath's picture-language project — the museum work in Red Vienna, the pictograms Gerd Arntz drew, the whole thing that got renamed Isotype once Neurath was in exile. What I can't stop chewing on is the negation problem. The pictograms are wonderful at showing that a lot of people have a lot of something: ten little figures, three of them red, the housing blocks going up year by year. But how do you draw a not? A conditional? The difference between all and some? My roommate does graphic design and insists you can just cross something out or draw a red X over it, which I think dodges the question, because a crossed-out figure is still a picture of something. As I understand it Neurath basically conceded that the picture language can't stand on its own and needs a stripped-down word language riding alongside it, which is either a tiny concession or the whole ballgame depending on your mood.

So here's what I want for the last session: a poem I can read out loud, 16 to 24 lines, no rhyme required, built as two columns or two alternating voices. One voice can only do pictures — count things, show them, stack them, line them up. The other voice supplies the connectives and negations and if-thens. Let the form carry the argument: the picture voice should keep reaching for negation and keep failing in a way that's interesting rather than just cute, the word voice should keep bailing it out, and by the end I want the reader to feel the dependence running the other direction too — that the word voice has nothing left to check itself against once the picture voice goes quiet. Don't let it turn into a lecture about Neurath; someone who's never heard of him should still get a poem out of it, and someone who's read him should recognize the actual problem rather than a name-drop. If the museum and the housing blocks can show up as concrete images without becoming decorative wallpaper, even better.
