A reading group I'm in has been stuck for months on the criteria of authenticity—embarrassment, dissimilarity, multiple attestation, and the rest—and I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another round of argument. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement isn't really about the criteria at all but about what 'authentic' attaches to: individual sayings assessed one at a time, versus a whole reconstructed figure that only emerges from the best global account—the two sides answering different questions under one word. What pushes me toward this is that each criterion looks like a likelihood ratio, purporting to say how much more probable a saying is if Jesus said it than if the community invented it; but a likelihood ratio can't tell us what to believe without a prior over what a first-century Galilean would plausibly have said, and that prior is just what's in dispute, so the criteria seem to presuppose the reconstruction they are meant to support. The critics press this, the practitioners concede it, and then both carry on as before. Is my hypothesis right, or is something else doing the work—a hidden disagreement about whether the sources can bear any inferential weight at all, or about what would count as evidence for someone known only through texts written decades later? Diagnose it concretely, and say what, if anything, would move the dispute forward: a distinction, a formal constraint on the criteria, or a change in the question.
