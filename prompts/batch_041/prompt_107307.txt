I'm in a small reading group that has spent two sessions on the Gödelian arguments against mechanism — the family of arguments that say the incompleteness theorems show a human mathematician can outrun any formal system offered as a model of her, because she can survey the system, see that its Gödel sentence is true, and thereby know a truth the system cannot prove. Most of us felt the standard replies — the appeal to tacit consistency assumptions, the charge that the argument idealizes the mathematician — had blunted the original, so one of us tried to sharpen it into a reflexive version. I now cannot tell whether it is a genuine strengthening or a conjuring trick, and it bothers me that I cannot say exactly where the trick would be.

Here is the argument as he wrote it. (1) Let S be any consistent formal system, adequate for arithmetic, that a mechanist might propose as capturing my mathematical reasoning. (2) By Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, if S is consistent then its Gödel sentence is true and unprovable in S. (3) I can carry out the reasoning in (2): for any such S, I see that the Gödel sentence is true, and I take my own reasoning to be consistent. (4) So there is a sentence that I can recognize as true and that S cannot prove. (5) Since S was arbitrary, no consistent formal system captures my mathematical reasoning, and therefore my reasoning is not the operation of a machine. (6) Now take S to be the system that models my own reasoning. By (4), I can recognize the Gödel sentence of that system, which is equivalent to the statement that the system is consistent — so I can recognize my own consistency. (7) By Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, no consistent system can prove its own consistency, and so cannot be a standpoint from which its own consistency is recognized. (8) Hence the system modeling my reasoning is either inconsistent — in which case it proves everything and recognizes nothing, so the appeal in (3) to my seeing the truth is worthless — or it is not a system I can survey, and the argument never gets off the ground.

I cannot accept (5) as it stands, and I certainly cannot accept (8); the whole point of the anti-mechanist argument was supposed to be that I am a consistent, truth-tracking reasoner. One of us suggested that the fix is to say the mind is a succession of systems, each proving the consistency of its predecessor, and that the argument must be extended to rule that out as well, but I could not follow whether that helps or hurts. What I want is a diagnosis: which numbered step should I reject, why did it look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost the anti-mechanist? I would rather keep (2) and (3) if I can, since (3) feels like the heart of the case, but I am prepared to learn that it is the one that has to go.
