Okay, so I'm building a handout for the final week of my ethics-of-markets seminar, and I keep going around in circles trying to lay out the commodification/repugnant-markets debate in a way that doesn't just collapse into "Sandel says some things shouldn't be for sale, libertarians say everything can be." The problem with every taxonomy I've seen is that it sorts people by the label they like rather than by what they actually think about cases, so students just pick a team and stop thinking.

What I want from you: a decision tree (a taxonomy is fine too if the material really wants that shape) that takes someone from a starting commitment about a case to a named position on whether some goods should be off the market. Six to nine internal nodes, six to nine leaves. Every internal node should be a concrete question a student could answer *before* knowing where it leads -- no "do you believe in corrupting goods?" nodes. Each leaf should carry the name of the position (or "unnamed" if nobody actually defends it) plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. Flag any two leaves your tree can't really separate, and if there are combinations of answers that nobody occupies, say so and give me your best guess at why (incoherent? just unattractive? nobody's bothered?).

I need these views to show up as leaves: (a) the "markets without limits" line that says there's no such thing as a good that's intrinsically off-market; (b) the corruption-of-meaning view that selling a good changes what it is; (c) the view that the real objection to kidney sales and surrogacy is background injustice and exploitation, not commodification as such; (d) a voluntarist/consent-only view; (e) the "conventionalist" view that repugnance is just a social fact that drifts over time. If you can squeeze in a view that says the institution, not the individual transaction, is what does the dirty work, please do.

Cases that must appear somewhere in the branches: kidney sales, hurricane price gouging, vote selling, paying a stranger to be your friend, commercial surrogacy, and selling your spot in a queue.

Here's the angle I actually care about, because it's what I've been pushing in lecture and it's not the standard framing. I want the tree to cut along three distinctions:
1. Does the objection target the *transfer itself* or the *existence of the market*? (Loads of students say "commodification" when they mean "this particular deal is coerced.")
2. My "reversibility test": imagine the transaction reversed, with the money flowing the other way -- does the objection flip sign? If it does, you're probably tracking a distributive complaint dressed up as a commodification complaint. I think this one does a lot of work and I want a node for it.
3. Whether the good is being used as a means by the *parties*, versus being turned into a means by the *institution* that surrounds them.

Keep it pitched at smart undergrads who've read a bit but not specialists. Casual, no jargon for its own sake. And at the very end, after the tree, give me a one-sentence summary of which single distinction you think does the most sorting work -- last line, formatted exactly as: Summary: <your sentence>.
