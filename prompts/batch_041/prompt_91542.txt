In my graduate seminar in philosophy of economics on Keynes's theory of probability, a colleague has taken to invoking the slogan that probability is a branch of logic. I have now heard it do several different jobs at once: to insist that probability judgments are objective rather than psychological, to reject the frequency theory, and to argue that practical decisions under uncertainty cannot be modeled as expected-utility maximization over subjective degrees of belief. I would like a conceptual map I can circulate to students. Lay out the distinct precise theses that the slogan could be expressing, from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical. For each, indicate whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or false; and explain what it would commit one to regarding the relation between probability and deductive logic, the possibility of non-numerical or merely comparative probabilities, the distinction between the probability of a proposition and the weight of the evidence for it, and the status of subjective degrees of belief. Then say which of these readings would actually support the uses to which my colleague puts the slogan, especially the use of it to dismiss subjective Bayesianism and to insist that Keynesian uncertainty is not merely low probability. I do not want a bibliography or citations; I want the menu of readings and your verdicts on them.
