I'm a novelist, so this is going to come out sideways, but bear with me. I'm about 200 pages into a book and I've hit a scene I can't write until I settle something. My protagonist, Dana, is a hypocrite, but not the cartoon kind: for two years she's been on her brother's case about how he handles their mother's money — he's vague, he's late with the care-home invoices, he treats the whole thing as an afterthought — and she does the same thing herself, only worse, and genuinely cant see it. The scene is in a hospital car park, he finally says some version of you have no right to say this to me, and I need that to land as a real objection and not just as a move in a fight. Trouble is I dont actually know what the objection is. My writing group and I keep using the word standing and talking past each other: one of them says standing isn't a moral matter at all, it's social, like how you can only say you're welcome if you actually helped; another says no, what's wrong with Dana is that she's setting herself above him and that's the whole of it; a third says it's a red herring and the only question is weather what she says about the invoices is true. My own hunch is that there's a difference between having no right to say it to him and having no right to think less of him, and that those can come apart — but I might just be inventing a distinction because it's convenient for my scene, and I'd rather find out now.

So write me the desiderata: what does any adequate theory of standing to blame have to deliver before I should believe a word of it? Concretely — the cases it has to get right (the plain hypocrite; the reformed one who blames you for exactly what she used to do; the one who does the thing too, just less than you; the one who's right but has no business in your life at all; the one who is impeccable on the matter but is only saying it to hurt you; the one who'd have done exactly what you did if he'd ever been given the chance; and whatever else you think is load-bearing); the distinctions it can't be allowed to collapse (lacking standing vs. being wrong about the facts; blame as something you say vs. blame as something you feel; standing vs. what you're permitted to feel; forfeiting standing vs. never having had it; the brother's standing vs. a stranger's); and the ordinary facts about how blaming actually goes that it can't contradict. For each requirement, give me the case or the consideration doing the work — I don't want a list of abstractions. Then tell me which requirements you think are real and which are one camp's preference dressed up as a datum, and if some subset of them can't be satisfied together, say which and why, because that's the part I most want. If you had to put money on the single requirement that eliminates the most candidate theories, which one is it? Bullets are fine, a page or so. And please actually take a position — a list I can disagree with beats a survey, and if my speech/attitude distinction is a fiction I cooked up for the sake of my scene, say so and tell me what to do with the car park instead.
