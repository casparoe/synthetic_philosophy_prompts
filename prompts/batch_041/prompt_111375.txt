I have been reading the Zhuangzi in translation, on and off, for the better part of a year, and the part I keep returning to is what it says about death — not a doctrine exactly, more a settled mood. There is the story of him drumming on a tub and singing when his wife dies, on the grounds that to wail for her would be to refuse the change she has already gone through; there is the skull that answers his questions in a dream and tells him that among the dead there are no rulers and no servants and no turning of the seasons, and that it would not trade its condition for a return to human trouble; there are the disciples he teases from his deathbed for wanting to bury him handsomely, since the birds of the air and the ants of the earth will get him either way; and there is the butterfly dream, which leaves him unable to say which side of the line he is really on. What strikes me is that all of this is consolation without memory. The dead do not need to be remembered, and the living are told to stop treating the change as a catastrophe.

Now I have watched Pixar's Coco three times, twice with my niece, and I have come to think it is the exact inversion of all that, and perhaps the hardest case to put to it. I know the real holiday does not work the way the film says, but the film's invented rule is the interesting part: a soul in the Land of the Dead fades out for good once nobody among the living remembers them, so that death has a second stage, and that second stage is run on memory; the dead can cross over on the Day of the Dead only if someone at home has laid out their picture. I would like you to read the film through the Zhuangzi on death, scene by scene where it counts — Héctor going faint because the last person who remembers him is forgetting, his friend going out altogether when no one is left who knew him, the family's ban on music that grew out of a grievance against a man who left, and above all the song at the center of the film, which Ernesto de la Cruz turns into a bid for undying fame while Héctor wrote it as a lullaby for his daughter.

Tell me where the Zhuangzi genuinely illuminates the movie: is the film's horror of being forgotten a confusion that exists only on the side of the living, and is Ernesto's hunger for posthumous glory precisely the thing the book would laugh at? And then tell me where the film pushes back, or exposes something the text leaves out, because the dead in Coco are still people with needs, paperwork, and debts to the living, and the film's consolation is that love and ritual can keep them going — which is exactly what Zhuangzi tells his friends not to bother with. I would also like an honest verdict on whether the film's world is even one in which his calm is available, since its dead are not folded back into any great process but simply stop, and on whether that makes the film the wiser work or the more frightened one. Finish by saying what Coco adds to this question that the Zhuangzi cannot, and what the Zhuangzi sees that the film is too kind to admit. No quotations needed — I am not writing a paper, I just want the argument, and it would help if you named the two or three scenes I should go back to with this in mind.
