I want to practise defending the common prior assumption against someone who actually knows what they're talking about, so let's do it as a proper back-and-forth. Play a committed defender of the common prior — the Aumann-style line, the one that says genuine rational disagreement between people who share the same information and know it about each other is impossible. Make him sharp: he should know the objections better than I do, concede the small stuff cheerfully (yes it's an idealisation, no it's not a psychological claim, yes it fails descriptively) and never give up the main line, which is that persistent disagreement after full information-sharing is either a difference in information or a failure of rationality, no third option. He's allowed to stop me and ask me to define my terms if I'm being sloppy — a real defender would. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two so it reads like a conversation, stay in character until I say stop, and lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning — no throat-clearing, no restating my question back at me.

My opening move: I don't buy it, and here's the case that I think actually breaks it. Two forecasters get the same bridge inspection reports, same photos, same sensor logs, no private information worth the name. One puts the ten-year failure risk at 5%, the other at 30%. They swap everything they have, twice, and at the end they still say 5% and 30%. You'll tell me they can't both be rational — fine, that's the theorem, I'm not disputing the maths. But where did the priors come from? Mine came out of a dad who was a structural engineer and too many evenings reading about stress corrosion; his came out of being an institutional optimist who thinks inspection regimes basically work. Neither of us can point to a mistake in the other's updating, and neither of us can point to a private signal the other didn't get. So the common prior isn't something we can be criticised for failing to have — it's just what you'd expect from two people with different histories. The theorem says that if you assume away the interesting case, the interesting case goes away. Why is 'rational' supposed to require me to have had his childhood?
