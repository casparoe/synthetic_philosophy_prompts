I'm midway through a term paper for a philosophy of language seminar and would value your comments on the draft before I revise it. The paper starts from the felicity conditions Austin attaches to performative utterances and argues that what is usually treated as a single requirement—uptake—is really two requirements that come apart in instructive cases. The first, which I call recognitional uptake, is that the audience must register both what was said and the force it was meant to carry. The second, ratificatory uptake, is that some hearer must occupy a role that lets the utterance take hold, the way a bet needs someone willing to take the other side or an appointment needs a person who can be appointed. My claim is that different act-types are sensitive to these two requirements to different degrees. Promises and apologies, I argue, need only recognitional uptake: an apology that is heard but refused is still an apology, and a promise made to someone who never learns of it is still a promise, if a morally inert one. Permissions and appointments, by contrast, need ratificatory uptake, which is why a permission granted to no one, or an appointment made to someone who cannot accept it, seems to fall to the ground. I try to show that this split does more explanatory work than the bare notion of felicity and that it predicts which hybrid cases go which way.

The second half tests the distinction against a case I have not seen discussed much: performatives addressed to machines. If I tell a voice assistant that I promise to call my mother tomorrow, type an apology to a customer-service chatbot, or file a resignation through an automated HR portal, has anything actually been performed? I want to say that the promise and the resignation can still succeed, because their force depends on my future conduct and on institutional roles that third parties can fill, whereas apologizing to a chatbot and forgiving it are hollow, since there is no one at the other end whose recognition or standing the act requires. But I suspect this verdict may be smuggling in a speaker-centric theory of meaning, and that a stricter reading of Austin would say the machine cases fail for want of any uptake at all, which would undercut the tidy two-part story. I would welcome your comments, in particular on four things: whether the recognitional/ratificatory distinction marks a real theoretical cut or merely relabels Austin's contrast between misfires and abuses; what the strongest counterexample to my asymmetries is, since I worry most about forgiveness and about promises to the dead; whether the machine cases genuinely support the distinction or quietly refute it; and, separately, whether the argument as summarized reads as interesting or as a distinction without a difference. Please don't bother with citations or further reading—I would rather you press on the argument itself and tell me where it overreaches or where I have equivocated on what it means for an act to take effect.
