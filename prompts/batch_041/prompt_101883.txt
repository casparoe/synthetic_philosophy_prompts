So I've been leaning on an AI assistant a lot for work lately — it drafts my emails, helps me think through stuff — and it's gotten to the point where I catch myself saying it "wants" me to phrase a request a certain way, or that it's "confused" about what I meant, or that it's "avoiding" a question. My coworker, who did a philosophy minor and never lets anyone forget it, told me the way to think about all this is Dennett's "intentional stance," and here's how he summarized it for me:

"Dennett's whole point is that we should treat things like AI systems as if they had beliefs and desires even though they obviously don't — it's a useful fiction, the same way you treat a chess program as if it wants to take your queen. And since taking the stance is something you choose to do, not something that's true or false, Dennett thinks there's no fact of the matter about whether an AI really believes anything. So for LLMs it's fine to ascribe beliefs as a prediction shortcut, but you're always just projecting — the 'beliefs' are whatever we find it useful to say it has, and a defender of the stance wouldn't claim anything more than that."

That sounds plausible to me, but it also feels wierdly convenient, like it wants to have it both ways — everything is real if it's useful and also nothing is real. Is this a fair summary of Dennett? Go claim by claim: what's accurate, what's a standard simplification people make, and what he'd actually push back on. And I especially want to know whether the last bit follows — that a defender of the stance wouldn't claim any more than "useful to ascribe" — or whether that's exactly the move Dennett is trying to avoid. I ask because I keep attributing thoughts and intentions to this thing, and I genuinely can't tell if I'm making a category error or doing something more defensible.
