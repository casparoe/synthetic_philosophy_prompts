I'm a phd student in philosophy, mostly Western, and I'm running a reading group on the pramāṇa debate for people who are fine epistemologists but know nothing about Indian philosophy. Instead of lecturing I want to open with a sorting quiz: eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that tells people which view they actually hold.

Cases I want in there: the rope taken for a snake at dusk (what was the wrong cognition even about?); a forester describes a gavaya to you and later you recognise one in the forest; you look at a table and take yourself to know there's no pot on it; you hear a claim from someone you've never met and have no track record for; two of your sources conflict and one has to be demoted. Then add two or three cases the textbooks don't have — something involving a translation app, something involving a dog recognising a smell — so nobody can answer straight from memory.

Positions the key should land people on: everything reduces to perception plus inference (Cārvāka-ish or Buddhist-ish, don't sweat the fine print); the Nyāya four, where testimony and upamāna are sui generis and a cognition's validity is established from outside, say by successful practice; the Mīmāṃsā six, where validity is intrinsic and you need a defeater to kill it; the five-pramāṇa version that drops non-apprehension; and for the Westerners, Humean reductionism versus Reidian anti-reductionism about testimony.

No question should just ask 'is testimony a pramāṇa?'. Add one question on whether the pramāṇas are separate sources or kinds of warrant that can overlap on the same fact. And at the end, tell me which pairs the quiz can't separate — my guess is that a reductive answer about testimony looks identical to a reductive answer about absence, but you tell me, and say whether any answer combination is one nobody actually occupies.
