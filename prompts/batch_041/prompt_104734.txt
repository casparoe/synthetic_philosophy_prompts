I run a reading group in science and technology studies, and two of our regulars have been deadlocked for a whole term over whether artifacts can have politics. One of them defends the thesis that certain designs embody and enforce a particular social order, so that the politics resides in the thing itself rather than merely in its use — citing the familiar cases of the low parkway overpasses said to have kept buses off a public beach, the mechanized tomato harvester that helped displace small growers, and the pneumatic molding machines that deskilled foundrymen — and argues that when a design admits of only one workable social arrangement, calling it neutral is a dodge. The other holds the neutral-instrument view: that an artifact is in itself indifferent to the social order, that the same device can serve very different politics depending on who deploys it, within what institutions, and under whose control, and that the inherent-politics reading smuggles social intent into what is at bottom a physical constraint, an economy of manufacture, or a professional standard. They have now agreed to attempt a genuine adversarial collaboration, and they have asked me to draft the document they would both put their names to. (I will say up front that I suspect one of their cruxes is whether politics should be predicated of the finished artifact at all or only of the design process and its uptake, and another is whether the demonstrable flexibility of a technology in use actually defeats a claim of inherent politics or merely fails to address it.)

Please write that joint statement. It should begin with a numbered section of agreed claims — what both sides will concede about what it means for a design to be political, about the burden of proof each accepts, about the difference between a technology that merely makes some arrangement easier and one that makes it mandatory, and about what would count as evidence either way — followed by a disagreements table that states, for each live disagreement, a formulation of each side's position that its own proponents would accept as fair, and identifies the crux on which it turns. For each crux, record a concrete case that both sides agree bears on it, together with each side's advance prediction about how that case should come out. I want at least two cases in the mix: one of the classic examples above, and one fresh case that I am inventing for the purpose, namely a municipal system of adaptive traffic signals that detect approaching buses and extend their green phase while making private cars wait — a design that plainly advantages one mode of transport, was justified by its engineers purely on grounds of throughput efficiency, and could in principle be reprogrammed to favor cars instead. A good statement will say whether the two sides diverge on the invented case in the same way they diverge on the classic ones, and if their disagreement turns out to be partly verbal — a quarrel about how broadly the word politics is being stretched — it should say which part and why, rather than pretending the whole dispute is substantive. Please keep both participants equally competent and neither a strawman; the value of the document lies in formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted, and in separating the disagreements that are real from those that were only ever about words.
