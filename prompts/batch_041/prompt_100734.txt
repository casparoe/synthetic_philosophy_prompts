My younger brother was held for nine days this spring under an emergency psychiatric provision after a suicide attempt, and the episode has left my family arguing about what the state was entitled to do and what the whole thing even was. To get my bearings I have been reading a well-regarded popular history of psychiatry, and its treatment of Thomas Szasz struck me as both vivid and suspicious. This is the gist of what it says:

"Szasz's famous thesis, set out in The Myth of Mental Illness, was that mental illness does not exist. Medicine deals with diseases of the body, and since no lesion, pathogen, or other physical cause has ever been found for the conditions we call schizophrenia, depression, and the like, there is nothing for the word illness to name. What we are really dealing with is a set of behaviours that society finds deviant or inconvenient, and a diagnosis is simply a respectable way of labelling and controlling people who break the rules. It follows that Szasz opposed psychiatry altogether: no one should be treated by a psychiatrist, take psychiatric drugs, or be admitted to a psychiatric hospital, and the institution should be dismantled. Later critics, Laing and the anti-psychiatry movement above all, vindicated his instincts, and the standard modern reply that these conditions are brain diseases misses the point entirely, since Szasz's argument was a moral one about freedom rather than an empirical claim about the brain."

I would like to know how much of this I can trust, because the practical question underneath it matters to me. If Szasz is right in anything like the sense the book describes, then what was done to my brother was not treatment but imprisonment with a medical vocabulary, and I should be saying so loudly; if the book has mangled him, I may be drawing a political conclusion from a caricature. Please go through the passage claim by claim. Tell me which sentences a sympathetic reader of Szasz would accept as accurate, which are common simplifications that lose something important, which he would have rejected outright, and whether the argument as he actually made it supports the conclusion the book draws about psychiatry as a whole.

I am particularly unsure about four things. First, whether the claim that mental illness is a myth is the empirical claim that no physical cause has been found, or something more conceptual that would survive the discovery of one; the book seems to treat it as the former and then, in its final sentence, as something else entirely. Second, whether his objection to involuntary treatment is the same as an objection to psychiatry as such; the book runs these together, and it makes a large difference to what I should think about the medication my brother was offered and now takes voluntarily. Third, whether it is fair to fold him into the anti-psychiatry movement alongside Laing, and what is lost by doing so. Fourth, the book never mentions his insistence that responsibility is never compromised by what is called mental illness, and I suspect that complicates the picture in a direction the book does not anticipate. Where the book is wrong, please say what the more defensible version of the view would be, and whether that version still has anything uncomfortable to say about my brother's detention.
