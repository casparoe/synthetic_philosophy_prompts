I'm a retired analytical chemist — 35 years of spectroscopy, then retirement, and I've spent the last two of them reading philosophy, mostly logic and philosophy of maths. A popular book on paradoxes has a chapter on dialetheism I don't trust, and I'd like to know how much of it to believe. The author's version, as best I can reproduce it:

"Dialetheism is the view that contradictions can be true. Its best-known defender is Graham Priest, who argues that the Liar sentence ('this sentence is false') is both true and false, so the law of non-contradiction fails. Paraconsistent logic is the logic the dialetheists use: since classical logic lets a contradiction entail anything whatever, they have to give up the principle of explosion, and once you've done that you're committed to everything being true and nothing being ruled out, which is why the position has almost no takers. It is essentially Hegel's dialectic with truth tables. A dialetheist also can't tell you you're wrong when you contradict him, since for him both sides of a contradiction can be true — which makes you wonder what the argument was ever about. Priest's own line is that we should stop treating non-contradiction as a law of thought and just be careful which contradictions we tolerate."

In my old line of work a theory that rules nothing out is worthless, so the explosion objection is the one that appeals to me — but I have a nagging feeling it's aimed at the wrong target. Go through the paragraph sentence by sentence: what would a logician who actually works on this stuff accept, what would they wince at, and what is just wrong? And tell me which of the author's punchlines survive once the true bits are separated out. I've heard paraconsistent logic is a whole family of logics used by plenty of people who arn't dialetheists at all, which makes me suspect the chapter is doing some lumping — if my framing is confused, or the question is badly posed, say so plainly rather than humoring me. I'd rather find out I've asked the wrong question than get a smooth answer to it.
