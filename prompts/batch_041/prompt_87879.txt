My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the French 35-hour week — she had just come back from a year in Lyon and insisted it was a moral failure dressed up as economic policy, while I maintained it was simply the lump-of-labor fallacy in legislative form and had nothing to do with morality at all. We got nowhere, and I would like you to settle what we were actually disagreeing about — not with a verdict on the economics, but with a case analysis. Take the reduction of the legal work week to thirty-five hours: the policy itself, its stated rationale of sharing work in order to reduce unemployment, the carve-outs for executives and the compensatory days off (the RTT) it produced, and its partial rollback in the years since. Read all of that through Weber's account of the Protestant work ethic. Do the mapping properly — name the actors (the state, the unions, the employers, the workers themselves), say what each was actually after, and say what the ethic implies they were up against.

Then test a suspicion of mine. I think the policy never built a durable moral constituency because it was defended as a way of redistributing jobs — leisure as recuperation, time off whose point is to restore you for more work — rather than as a claim about leisure as an end in itself, time whose value is internal to it. If that is right, the work ethic should have predicted the failure, since it can stomach rest but not idleness. I want you to show me where the lens breaks anyway. Does Weber's ethic say anything at all about the length of the working week, or is it a claim about the meaning of work that leaves hours undetermined? Is the resistance explained by the ethic, by plain self-interest, or by something the ethic has no name for?

And as a stress test, set the case beside karoshi — death from overwork, which Japan recognizes as both a legal and a social category — where the same ethic, if it is doing real explanatory work, ought to be running unchecked. Tell me whether that contrast confirms the Weberian picture, qualifies it, or exposes that we have been using "the work ethic" for two different things: a specifically Protestant anxiety about assurance, and a far broader, confession-neutral moralization of time. Be disciplined about the mapping, and say plainly where the theory falls silent rather than forcing it to speak.
