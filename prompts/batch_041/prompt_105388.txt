The Cascadia Effective Giving Project has just posted its annual grantmaking methodology page. Its opening paragraph concedes that, because the causal consequences of any funded intervention ramify without limit into the future, no intervention can be assigned a determinate expected value; the remaining seven sections then rank thirty-two charities by cost per expected moral impact unit. Write that page. Keep it deadpan and institutional — no winking narrator, no author who notices the contradiction — and let the comedy come from getting cluelessness right: the series of consequences that never terminates, the tiny probabilities of astronomically large effects that swamp the sober ones, the gap between a risk you can quantify and an ignorance you cannot, and the Project's homegrown devices for proceeding anyway (a cluelessness-adjustment factor, an appendix that estimates effects out to the tenth order and a footnote granting that the tenth order is not the last, a sensitivity analysis over priors nobody holds). Include one worked example — a bed-net distribution whose headline number is set not by the children it saves but by some tenth-order consequence — and invent the charities rather than citing real ones. About 650 words, and legible enough that a reader who has never heard the term can still tell what is being laughed at.
