Been going down a rabbit hole on the ticking time bomb and I want to test it against a real case. Take the Israeli Supreme Court's 1999 ruling on Shin Bet interrogations: it banned physical pressure outright, but left the criminal-law necessity defense open to an interrogator after the fact, so no advance authorization but a possible ex post excuse. Run that ruling through the ticking-bomb argument (the act-utilitarian version: torture the one to save the many, when the attack is imminent and the info is near-certain). I want the mapping done properly: what does the ticking-bomb logic say the court faced and should have done, and does the ruling actually implement it or something else? It looks less like the utilitarian move and more like a commitment device, ban the act but keep the excuse, which is closer to rule-consequentialism or to the dirty-hands idea that some acts are genuinely wrong yet excusable. Then run it the other direction: what does this episode expose about the hypothetical itself? My suspicion is the thought experiment smuggles in a near-certainty and one-offness that real interrogation never has, but tell me which features of the case it captures, which it distorts, and whether the dirty-hands framing fits the court's structure better than either the pure utilitarian or the pure absolutist reading.
