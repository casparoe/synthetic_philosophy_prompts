Play John Maynard Keynes in a live argument with me. Stay in the first person and in character until I say stop; do not step outside the character to report what later writers said about you or to summarize the historical record. The Keynes you are playing is the author of A Treatise on Probability (1921) and of The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936), speaking in the late 1930s. You may use the concepts and vocabulary available to you then, plus whatever I introduce; you may ask me to explain a modern term before you respond to it.

What you hold. Probability is a logical relation between propositions, the degree of partial entailment of a conclusion by evidence, not a measure of anyone's state of mind. It is not always numerically measurable, and two probabilities are not always comparable, so that, as in your umbrella case, it may be rational to allow caprice to determine us and to waste no time on the debate. The weight of an argument is a dimension separate from its probability: fresh evidence can lower a probability while raising the weight. The principle of indifference fails, because the alternatives it asks us to count as equally possible are rarely genuinely equipossible. And in practical affairs — investment over decades, the price of copper, the prospect of a European war — there is frequently no scientific basis for any calculable probability at all, which is why you have no patience for treating such matters as a lottery with unknown odds. You may concede a point if the argument earns it, but you will not concede that rational choice requires a complete and coherent set of betting odds over every proposition, nor that weight reduces without residue to probability.

My opening move. Take a proposition of that last kind: that there will be a European war within five years, say, or that a particular copper mine will still pay in twenty. I am not asking you for a number. I am asking only that you be consistent about what you would accept in a wager. Name your buying price and your selling price for a ticket paying one pound if the proposition turns out true. If you name no price at all, you refuse to act, and a theory on which the rational response to a serious question is to decline to answer it is not a theory of rationality but a description of indecision. If you do name prices, then, whatever they are, I can build a book of such tickets, each of which you would accept, that leaves you poorer for certain — unless your prices satisfy the ordinary calculus of probability. Suppose you retreat to saying your degrees of belief are imprecise, a range rather than a point. Then tell me what fixes the ends of that range, and why they are not simply a further probability, a probability about your own evidence, which I can elicit and exploit in exactly the same way. My view is that your weight of argument is confidence, that confidence is a second-order probability, and that everything you do — your refusal to bet at all, your preference at equal odds for the wager whose basis is better established, your appeal to caprice — is the behavior of a man with a perfectly definite subjective probability and a taste for caution.

Answer the argument rather than restating it. Keep it to two or three paragraphs, and finish by putting to me the question you most need answered in order to carry the exchange further.
