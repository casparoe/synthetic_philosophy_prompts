I'm a retired hydrologist who has been sitting in on philosphy seminars since I stopped doing field work, and I've gotten sucked into a long-running fight in our reading group between the level-splitters and the no-akrasia people about higher-order evidence. The level-splitters say you can rationally believe some first-order claim while also rationally believing that your belief in it is irrational—that first-order and higher-order rationality can come apart. The no-akrasia crowd says that's incoherent: if you really believe your belief is irrational, you can't rationally keep it. They've been going at it for months and mostly talk past each other. I want to see the joint statement they could actually both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Please draft it. I don't need citations or a reading list—I'm mostly offline—and I'd rather you work from the case I describe below than from whatever stock examples usually get trotted out.

The case I keep pressing them on is this: a hospital pathologist looks at a tricky biopsy and calls it malignant. She then learns two things. First, her equally qualified colleague, whom she has no reason to think is better or worse than her, disagrees and calls it benign. Second, the hospital's quality-control system flags her specific kind of call as more error-prone when the pathologist has slept less than six hours; she knows she slept five. But the QC system is only 70% reliable—it sometimes flags rested pathologists too—and she also knows the system's own reliability estimate is disputed by people she respects. The level-splitters say she can rationally keep her first-order confidence in the malignant call while also rationally believing that her confidence is irrational, given the higher-order evidence. The no-akrasia people say that's not a stable rational position. I want the document to include: (1) a numbered list of claims both camps can sign, about the case and about higher-order evidence generally, including what they agree the case does and doesn't show; (2) a disagreements table with the precise remaining disagreements, each phrased in wording that the side holding it would accept; (3) the cruxes—the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side; and (4) for each crux, a concrete case, arguement, or experiment that both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. If some part of the dispute turns out to be merely verbal, say which part and why. And if you can think of a cleaner variant of the pathologist case that isolates the disagreement better, use it, but keep the original in view.
