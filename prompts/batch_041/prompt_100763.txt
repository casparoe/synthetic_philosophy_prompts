I run the rules-and-definitions side of things at a mid-size benefits agency -- my team writes the eligibility criteria, the glossary, the data dictionary, and the logic the caseworkers' system actually executes. We've grown a couple thousand definitions over the years and they refer to each other, and last month we hit one that broke the loader in a funny way. We had a term, 'reviewable case', defined as any case not listed in the schedule of non-reviewable cases, and the schedule was itself built by reference to the reviewable ones, and the thing started flipping: every case came out reviewable exactly when it came out not reviewable, depending on the order the loader walked it. My boss called it Russell's paradox with a lanyard. I only half understand the fix, which I think is something like: you're not allowed to define a set by a rule that quantifies over everything, only by carving a subset out of a collection you already have. Thats the whole of what I know and it's not enough, because the team keeps writing new definitions every quarter and I can't tell which ones are the dangerous ones.

So what I want from you is a procedure I can hand a junior analyst -- not a logician, someone who took one discrete math class five years ago -- that takes a batch of definitions and sorts them into: safe, safe-but-rewrite-for-the-loader's-sake, and genuinely this-will-flip. A list of checks in order, each with a pass or fail and a plain-English reason, plus a clear point where the analyst stops and escalates to me. It has to run in about twenty minutes on a batch of thirty definitions, because that's the throughput I need before every quarterly release, and both kinds of error hurt: an inconsistent rulebook is an audit finding, and needless rewrites make my team hate me.

Ground it in what set theory actually settled on after the paradox -- the shift from letting a rule define a set to only letting it pick out members of a set you already have, and the thought that self-reference is only vicious when it isn't grounded. One of my colleagues also mentioned, vaguely, that you can sometimes just assign levels or ranks to the terms so that a term at one level is only allowed to talk about things at a lower level, and that if you can do the assignment the definition can't bite you. Is that the right test, and can it be turned into something a junior can actually run on paper? I don't need axiom names, I need the distinction those axioms were doing.

Then run it on five of our real definitions and show your work:

1. 'Eligible dependent': a child under 19 of an eligible claimant, or a child under 19 of an eligible dependent. This is our standard recursive one, we've shipped it for a decade, and I'll be annoyed if you tell me to rewrite it -- but tell me anyway if you think so.

2. 'Outstanding balance': the total amount owed minus the total amount paid, where total paid includes credits applied against outstanding balances.

3. 'Non-standard rule': any rule in the rulebook that is not among the standard rules. This is the shape that bit us.

4. 'Flagship case': the case with the highest priority score in the caseload, where the priority score of a case is computed by a formula that includes, for each case, how many other cases in the caseload have a lower score. I honestly don't know if this one is ok and it worries me.

5. 'Undefined term': any term used anywhere in the dictionary that has no entry in the dictionary.

Finally, be honest about the limits. If there's a class of definition that passes every check you give me but still misbehaves once the loader runs it, say so and say what to watch for. And if any of your checks will give a verdict that a working rules writer would find pedantic and pointless, tell me which ones and why I should ignore you on those.
