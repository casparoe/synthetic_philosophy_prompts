I'm teaching a unit on democratic theory next term and I keep running into a puzzle that I've never managed to resolve properly, so I want you to just work it out for me from scratch.

Here's the practice. In Switzerland, if you want to change the federal constitution — either because parliament proposes an amendment or because a popular initiative gets the signatures — your amendment has to pass whats called a double majority: a majority of the votes cast nationwide, and also a majority of the cantons. So you can win a clear majority of the actual human beings who voted and still lose, because you only carried a minority of the cantons. (I think it works out to 23 cantonal votes, since a handful of the cantons count as half for this purpose, and the cantons range from Zürich at over a million people down to a little one of about 16,000. Don't quote me on the exact figures, I'm going from memory.) But for an ordinary federal statute, if the opponents force an optional referendum, it's just the national popular majority and nothing else. Same country, same ballot, same day, two different rules for how much a vote is worth: on the statute every citizen is exactly equal, on the constitutional amendment a voter in the tiny canton is worth a few dozen Zürich voters.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory of legitimacy this practice presupposes. Not the history, I don't care what the framers intended — the philosophy.

(a) Does the cantonal majority presuppose that cantons are themselves subjects of justice, corporate persons whose interests count in their own right over and above the interests of the individuals living in them? Or can you get the same rule out of a purely individualist premise, as a device for protecting geographically concentrated minorities from being outvoted? If the latter, spell out exactly what the individualist premise has to be, because I don't see how each person's interests count equally generates each canton's vote counts equally.

(b) If it does commit you to corporate persons, is that coherent next to the rest of the system, which is otherwise flatly one-person-one-vote individualist? Which part is doing the real work — is the double majority a genuine commitment or a historical bargain wearing a principle as a costume? Separate for me what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with.

(c) Where is it underdetermined? Which rival theories of representation or of political equality would each license exactly this rule while disagreeing about nearly everything else?

(d) And here's the part I actually care about. A grad student of mine suggested we add a third majority: any measure that shifts costs onto people who don't exist yet would also have to clear a majority of age cohorts, so no single generation can outvote the others. Is that a natural extension of the double majority or does it break it? What would the cantonal rule have to presuppose for the generational one to be more of the same, and what would it have to presuppose for the generational one to be a different animal entirely?

No citations needed, I know there's a literature, I just want the reconstruction — a thousand words or so. And if my description of the practice is off, fix the description first, I'd rather you reason from a correct one than a wierd caricature.
