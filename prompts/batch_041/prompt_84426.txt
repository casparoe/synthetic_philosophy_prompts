Exam question: "Consider the auction sometimes called the dollar auction. A one-dollar bill is auctioned to a room of bidders. Bids are open and rise in five-cent increments. When bidding stops, the highest bidder pays the amount bid and receives the dollar; the second-highest bidder also pays the amount bid and receives nothing. (a) Explain why bidding in this auction tends to escalate well past the value of the prize. (b) What, if anything, does the outcome show about the theory of rational choice? (c) Propose one change to how such decisions are made that would reduce the escalation, and say why it works. Answer in no more than 600 words."

Student's answer: "(a) The structure of the game explains the escalation. Suppose I am the second-highest bidder at ninety-five cents and someone else has just bid a dollar. If I stop here, I pay ninety-five cents and get nothing. If I bid a dollar five, I pay a dollar five and receive a dollar — a loss of five cents — provided the other bidder stops. Faced with a certain loss of ninety-five cents and a possible loss of five, I bid. The other bidder now faces the mirror image of that choice and reasons the same way, and the two of us can go on past two dollars, past five dollars, and further. The house collects the top two bids, so the auctioneer is ahead whenever the two bids together exceed a dollar, which happens early and only improves for the auctioneer as the bidding climbs. Nobody in the room intends this; each step is locally sensible.

The escalation is a case of commitment: the bidders stay in because of what they have already put in. Abandoning the contest means admitting that the earlier bids were wasted, and it is the ninety-five cents, not the five cents now at stake, that keeps the second bidder in the game. This is the same mechanism at work when a firm keeps funding a project that has already consumed ten million dollars and is clearly failing: the ten million, not the merits of the project, is what keeps it alive.

(b) What the auction shows is that rationality at each step does not add up to a rational outcome. Every bid in the escalation can be defended on its own terms, and the pair of bidders is ruined. So the theory of rational choice does not give determinate advice here: it tells each player to take the locally best move, and the locally best move is the one that produces the disaster.

(c) The practical fix is procedural: the person who decides whether to raise the bid should not be the person who made the earlier bids. A fresh decision-maker has nothing invested and will weigh the next bid on its merits, whereas a manager who inherited the decision will stop where the original bidder kept going."
