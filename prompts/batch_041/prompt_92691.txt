My friend Dan and I argued for about two hours after a dinner party last night about 'in vino veritas' and I need someone to lay it out for me, because I'm pretty sure he was using it to cheat.

His position: drinking with someone is the best possible way to find out what they're really like, and he brought in Plato to prove it. His version of the Laws goes like this. Plato designs this whole institution where a drinking party is supposed to have a sober commander over it — someone wise who stays dry, because a drunken commander of drunkards is asking for trouble — and the whole point of the drinking party is that wine functions as a *test*. The drinker starts blurting things out with no shame and no filter, so whatever is actually inside him comes out, and Plato says this is a cheap and fairly harmless way to test a man of nasty temper, way cheaper than doing business with him and finding out the hard way. Dan also says Plato wanted the over-forties to drink, because wine was given to us as a medicine against the crabbiness of old age, it makes the soul's temper soft and ductile like iron worked in a fire, and it loosens the old men up enough that they'll actually sing in front of other people instead of dying of embarrassment. There was also something about Spartans and Carthaginians forbidding it to soldiers on campaign, but dont quote me on that part. Dan's conclusion: Plato is on the side of the proverb, 'in vino veritas' is real ancient tech for getting at the truth about a person, and if I want to know what he honestly thinks of me, I should just get him drunk.

My position is that he smuggled in his conclusion. Wine making a man *shameless* is not the same as wine making him *truthful* — a guy with no filter is not a guy with no errors, he's just a guy with no filter. And I suspect what Plato actually cares about is the drinking party as a training ground, a place to practice being moderate in the presence of the very thing that makes you immoderate, which is nearly the opposite of an epistemically useful event. And 'veritas' — the truth about *what*? Whether he likes me, or what kind of person he is? Those are definately different claims and I think Dan needs them to be one claim.

So what I want from you is a menu, not a verdict. Lay out the seperate things 'in vino veritas' could actually be asserting, from the boring to the wild — drunk people are more candid; drunk people can't sustain a lie; wine reveals character rather than facts; wine gives access to a kind of truth unavailable sober; the uninhibited self is the real self; what you say drunk is what you 'really' think — and for each one tell me whether it's a platitude, a real substantive claim, or just false. Then tell me which of them Dan's Plato material actually supports and which it undercuts, and whether any reading of the proverb would license his move from 'get him drunk' to 'you'll learn what he thinks of you'. I'm going off memory for all the Laws stuff so correct me if he or I mangled it.
