I sit on an interdisciplinary reading group on famine and development — economists, historians, and two of us from philosophy, meeting weekly in a seminar room with terrible coffee — and after last week's session two members stayed behind to keep arguing, with me as the only other person still in the room. I'd like you to sort out their exchange, because I genuinely couldn't tell who had the better of it. Nadia, an economist, opened by saying that whatever else Sen accomplished, his central move was to take the vocabulary of natural disaster off the table for famines. Her version of the point: the Bengal famine of 1943 is the paradigm case, because aggregate food availability in the province was not catastrophically below normal, and the deaths came instead from a collapse in what people could actually command — wartime inflation, the pull of demand from Calcutta and the military, the loss of rice imports when Burma fell, and, on her telling, the colonial government's decision to clear boats and rice stocks out of the coastal districts to deny them to a possible Japanese landing, which wrecked the livelihoods of exactly the people who then starved. Calling such an event natural is a category error, she said, and the same error is at work when the Irish catastrophe is remembered as a potato blight or the Chinese famine of the late 1950s as a run of bad weather. Farid, a historian, said he accepted the mechanism but thought the conclusion was extracting something illicit from it: entitlement failure is a description of how the deaths happened, not an assignment of blame, and there can be an entitlement failure for which no one is culpable — if a harvest collapse is severe enough, there may be no redistribution that feeds everyone. He added that the Irish case actually complicates her story rather than confirming it, since the British state intervened on a large scale with relief works and then soup kitchens, and the live historical argument is about whether that intervention was adequate, badly designed, or ideologically blinkered — an argument about sufficiency and culpability, not about whether the event was natural.

Nadia's reply was that he should simply state the test: a famine is political when some feasible alternative set of decisions by the relevant authorities would have prevented most of the deaths, and by that test Ireland, Bengal, and the Great Leap Forward all come out political, with no need to show that anyone wanted anyone to die. Farid answered that the test proves too much, since there is almost always some counterfactual set of decisions that would have saved lives — including decisions by officials who had no way of knowing what was coming, and decisions that would have required dismantling the entire political system. If feasible means logically possible, he said, then every famine is a crime and the category tells us nothing; if it means feasible given the information and constraints of the time, then you need a principled account of how much ignorance and how much ideological commitment excuse a government, and that is the whole dispute rather than a way of settling it. Nadia came back with the Great Leap Forward as the clean case: there, she said, the shortage was itself made — procurement quotas set beyond what the harvests could bear, labor pulled off the fields, a reporting system that rewarded local officials for claiming impossible yields, and a refusal at the top to acknowledge what was happening. That is not a state failing to prevent a famine, she said; it is a state producing one. Farid said that move only relocates the question to knowledge and intention — did the leadership intend the deaths, and did it know they were coming? Political crime, he said, is a legal and moral phrase, and it imports a mental element; if what she means is policy-induced, she should say that, and then the interesting question becomes the graded one about how much causal responsibility, exercised with how much knowledge, under what constraints. He added that even granting that the state manufactured the shortage, the official account that blamed the period on natural calamities is itself a political act of naming, which suggests the naming question and the causal question have to be kept apart. At that point they turned to me, and I said I would think about it and never did.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move rather than crowning an overall winner: for each step, say whether the move is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and briefly give your reason. I would also like your own view on the question they kept circling — whether political crime should be defined by causal responsibility, by knowledge and intention, or by whether the political system gave the victims any way to punish those who let them die — and I would like you to say what both of them are missing.
