So I'm a first-year PhD student and I've somehow gotten obsessed with Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument — I read Anarchy, State, and Utopia over the summer after losing an argument to my brother-in-law about taxes (long story, don't ask), and now I can't stop chewing on it. Next month I have to give a 20-minute WIP to our political theory workshop and I want to try out a reading I haven't seen spelled out the way I want it: that the Chamberlain example isn't really a reductio of patterned principles as such. On my reading what it actually shows is narrower — that a pattern can't be *maintained* through free transactions without interference — and the heavy lifting is being done by an unstated premise, namely that the people doing the trading are already entitled to what they trade. If that premise is carrying the argument, then it doesn't touch someone who accepts a pattern but denies entitlement to pre-tax holdings; it just relocates the fight to acquisition. So I want to pitch it as a consistency challenge to certain patterned views rather than a knockdown, and I want to tack on a related puzzle: if a group of self-owning people voluntarily and unanimously adopt a rule that re-flattens holdings every year (a pattern they keep re-choosing), does Nozick's liberty-based objection even get off the ground, or does it prove too much by forbidding them from binding themselves?

The audience is going to be rough in a specific way. It's mostly Rawlsians and luck egalitarians, a couple of people who genuinely like Nozick and will defend him harder than he'd defend himself, and one person who does formal social choice and thinks all of this is loose talk. I don't want the generic libertarian-vs-egalitarian objections — I can handle those. I want the objections *this* room will actually throw, phrased the way they'd phrase them, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where my reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front before they smell blood, which objection is the one that could actually sink the talk, and — this is the part I really need — the one question I'm hoping nobody asks, plus the least-bad way to answer it if someone does. Also flag anywhere my reading is secretly just a dressed-up version of the boring standard objection, because the formal social choice person will definately say that and I want to be ready for it.
