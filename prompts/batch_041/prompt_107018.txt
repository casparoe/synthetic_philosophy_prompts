I'm a retired atmospheric chemist — 34 years in the field, mostly aerosols — and since I packed it in I've been auditing a second-year metaphysics of mathematics module at my local university. The instructor, who is far too trusting, has asked me to draft one of the final exam questions. I want to do something on Benacerraf's dilemma that won't let students just recite the two horns like a catechism. The thing that always bugged me when I was reading about it is that the semantic horn and the epistemic horn get run together in summaries, and then students think any old anti-platonist move kills both. So the question should force them to separate the two: the problem of picking out which objects the numbers are, if they're objects at all, versus the problem of how we could ever come to know anything about them. And I want them to test a specific heterodox view, which I'll describe here so they can't just google a label: suppose we treat numerals not as referring expressions at all, but still say that sentences like 2+2=4 are true — true in a non-objectual, practice-licensed sense, the way we might say a move in a game is legal rather than true of some object. Call it the no-object truth-apt view for the purposes of the exam. Does that view actually escape either horn, or does it just relocate one of them? The question should make them say, with a concrete arithmetical example, what the no-object view predicts about truth and about apparent reference, and whether it can handle a case where we seem to quantify over numbers (e.g. there is a prime between 10 and 20) without smuggling objects back in.

Please write: (1) the exam question itself, worded so a good student can see it's asking for argument, not summary; (2) a model answer of roughly 700–800 words that a strong student could produce in 90 minutes; and (3) a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves an A answer must make and the specific mistakes that should cap a student at a B or lower. In the marking scheme I'd like you to distinguish, for each horn, between 'solves it', 'avoids it by changing the subject', and 'fails it', because I suspect that's where the real discrimination is. Also, if you think the no-object view is incoherent, say so in the model answer rather than pretending it's a live option — I'd rather the question have a real crux than a fake one. I definately don't want a question where the marking scheme just rewards students for mentioning platonism, nominalism, and structuralism in the right order.
