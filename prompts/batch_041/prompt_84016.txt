Act as a philosopher of cognitive science who works on concept learning and inductive inference, and think carefully before answering — begin by brainstorming the full space of possible objections, then sort them.

Here is the position I hold, about the capacity that Bongard problems exercise. Inferring the intended concept from a handful of positive and negative instances is not a distinct or special faculty; it is Bayesian, or minimum-description-length, inference under a prior that favours simple and natural hypotheses. The gap between human and machine solvers is a gap in search and in the quality of the prior, not a difference in kind: any system with a good enough prior and a good enough search would converge on the human answer, and the residual differences are engineering that should shrink as both improve.

I want the case against this, not a survey of it.

- I keep running into what I think of as trap problems: instance sets that admit a simple, perfectly fitting rule which human solvers reliably reject, and sets whose intended rule really is the simple one, which human solvers reliably over-interpret. Work out what such cases would have to look like in order to count against my view rather than for it, and how a defender would try to absorb them.
- Consider concepts whose only faithful statement seems ostensive — the same as this one, rotated — or whose content is fixed by reference to the example set itself. Can any prior over intrinsic descriptions reach these? If not, is that a refutation or only a restriction of scope?
- Say what my view predicts that its rivals do not, and what would falsify it. Be honest about whether the escape hatch of the prior just not being good enough yet makes the position unfalsifiable, and whether that is fatal or merely a cost.

Rank the defeaters from decisive to merely awkward, say which ones I should concede as counting against me, and separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material — do not present a contested claim as the consensus. I would find most persuasive a case where the human answer is not merely different from the simple one but correct in a way that no ranking over descriptions can capture.
