I'm writing the final for my upper-level seminar on science and religion in early modern Europe, and I want one essay question on Newton that can't be answered by saying "he was a deeply religious man who also did physics." Here's the angle I want to push: my students always split into two lazy camps — the theology is just decoration for a pious age, or the theology is the hidden key that unlocks the whole Principia. I want a question that makes them place Newton's God precisely: which parts of the system actually need him and which don't. Specifically, get them to sort (i) absolute space and time, (ii) the active principles and the cause of gravity, and (iii) whether the cosmos needs divine upkeep, and to say for each whether the theology is load-bearing or detachable. Use the Leibniz-Clarke exchange as the pressure point, but don't let them just recap it — they should say what Leibniz's objection actually threatens and whether Newton or Clarke has a decent reply. The General Scholium is the obvious place to anchor it but I don't want them quoting it like scripture.

So: write the exam question itself, then a model answer of about 650 words, then a marking scheme. For the scheme, give five or six must-hit moves with marks totalling 25, and for each one a one-line note on the mistake that usually costs students the marks — e.g. reading his sensorium remark as flat-footed literalism, or assuming absolute space stands or falls with the theology. It's a 60-minute question. No citations needed, they won't have anything but their own heads. Also try to seperate the B-grade answer from the A-grade one in the scheme, not just list content.
