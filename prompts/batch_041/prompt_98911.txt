In many U.S. jurisdictions, a capital defendant's eligibility for the death penalty turns on whether his IQ falls below a fixed cutoff, typically around 70, and a similar fixed cutoff governs eligibility for special-education and disability services. The underlying scores are continuous, each carries a standard error, and a single point can separate life from death, yet the institutions that use the scores treat the line as real rather than merely administrative. What conception of vagueness does this practice presuppose? Does it commit the law to there being a determinate fact of the matter about who is and is not intellectually disabled, or can it be defended as a conventional line drawn for rule-of-law reasons such as predictability, equal treatment, and restraint on official discretion? The practice both draws a bright line and, through the standard error of measurement, concedes a band of uncertainty around it; are those two commitments consistent, or does one quietly undermine the other? Tell me which rival accounts of vagueness the practice rules out and where it remains underdetermined among them, and what would have to change if the single cutoff were replaced by a graded or multi-factor determination.
