I retired three years ago from a career in experimental physics — decades of designing apparatus, chasing systematic errors, and learning the hard way that a number is only as good as the assumptions baked into it — and I have spent the time since trying to teach myself philosophy, mostly by arguing with friends who are far more literate in it than I am. The other evening, at a small film-and-ideas group I belong to, two of the more philosophically inclined members got into a genuinely interesting fight about why we seek out sad films, and I have not been able to settle it in my own head. The setup: our host had chosen a well-made tragedy for the evening, and afterward he admitted that he deliberately returns to this film every few years even though it leaves him genuinely miserable — not tearful-but-pleasant, he insisted, but actually pained, heavy, low for a day — and that he would not trade the experience away. Call him A. B immediately objected that this just shows the misery is a means to some payoff — the cathartic relief, the pleasure of a well-crafted arc, the glow of having felt deeply — and that if you could get that same payoff without the ache you would obviously take it. A pushed back: he said that is simply false to his experience, because if you rewrote the film's ending into a happy one that delivered the identical relief and glow, he would refuse it — he wants *that* story, the sad one, and would say the sorrow is not a tax paid on the way to the good part but part of the good part itself. B granted the point but claimed it proves the opposite of what A wants: since A obviously does not wish the tragedy to have actually happened to anyone, what A values must be the sorrow *as a safe, make-believe exercise*, the emotion minus its real-world demands, and so the film's unreality is doing the real work. A shot back that if it were merely about safely exercising an emotion, any emotion would serve equally well, and B would then have to explain why the film must be *sad* rather than merely thrilling; the specific sadness, A said, seems to be valued as the *fitting* response to what the story depicts, so the value lies in the appropriateness of the feeling rather than in its pleasantness or safety. B conceded that was a real contender but suspected it smuggled in a notion of "fittingness" that does no explanatory work, since plenty of fitting emotions (grief at a real funeral) are things we would pay to avoid. Neither would yield, and I noticed that each of them had quietly ruled out the textbook moves I half-remembered — the idea that the sadness is not really sadness, or that it is simply outweighed by pleasure — so the usual summaries did not help me referee.

Could you adjudicate this exchange move by move — A's "the sorrow is part of the good part," B's "then it is the safety that matters," A's "then any emotion would do, and it must be the fitting response," and B's "fittingness is doing no work" — rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification, and then telling me which of the two, if either, has hold of the deeper point, and what both of them are leaving out? I would particularly like to know whether the appeal to "fittingness" is a real explanation or a redescription, and whether the fact that A would not want the events to be real genuinely favors B's safety view or can be accommodated by A. Please keep your entire reply to no more than 700 words, and lead with a one-sentence verdict before the move-by-move analysis.
