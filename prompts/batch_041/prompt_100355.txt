I'm a high school student finishing an independent study on how archives shape what can be said about the past, and I would like to close it with a poem instead of another essay. Please take the role of a poet who also knows archival theory, think carefully before drafting, and brainstorm images and voices first.

Write a pantoum of five quatrains — twenty lines, with the interlocking repetitions kept strict, including the final loop back to the opening line. Its subject is the institutional record-keeping of ordinary people: parish registers, censuses, plantation ledgers, workhouse rolls, ships' manifests. The poem should turn on four distinct ways a silence gets made: a life never written down at all; a life written down only in a category that erases it, as a tally, a price, a case number, or a headcount; a record that survives but is never retrieved; and a silence produced in the very act of writing, by whatever the form had no column for. I want the form to do the argument: each time a line returns it should mean something different, so that by the last stanza the same words have become a different kind of statement, and the closing repetition should feel less like resolution than like a file being reopened. Let the speaker move among three positions — the person keeping the record, the person kept in it, and the document itself speaking — and keep the tone plain rather than ornate. Please avoid the old line about victors writing history, and do not quote or attribute anything to real documents; the ledgers and registers can be fictional composites. Someone who studies archives should be able to identify all four silences at work, not just hear a poem about paperwork.
