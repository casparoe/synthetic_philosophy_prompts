Write an essay that reaches and defends a verdict on the following question: if a self is a pattern — a self-sustaining loop of representations that can be copied, with loss, into other systems — does a person's persistence in other minds make their death less bad for them? Take seriously the pattern view of the self, of the kind Douglas Hofstadter has defended, on which a person is realized not only in their own brain but, to a degree, in the brains of those who have come to know them well.

Assume, for the sake of argument, that the residual realization of a person can be measured: the fraction of their characteristic patterns still being run, at reduced fidelity, by living minds. A philosopher proposes that the harm of a death is proportional to the loss of residual realization rather than to the end of the organism — so someone whose mind has been deeply absorbed by others dies only in part, while someone who dies unknown and unabsorbed dies wholly.

Weigh the strongest case for and against this proposal. Against it, consider at least that a pattern running in someone else's head is not a subject occupying the dead person's point of view; that the proposal makes how bad your death is depend on other people's memories rather than on anything about you, yielding the result that the widely imitated die lesser deaths than hermits; and that it may simply confuse what is valuable about a person (their influence on the world) with the person themselves. For it, consider at least that if personal identity comes in degrees, so may death, and that the intuition that death is a cliff is the thing that needs defending; and that much of what we grieve is precisely the disappearance of someone from other people's minds.

Reach a verdict — accept the proposal, reject it, or accept a modified version — and defend it against the strongest objection you have raised. Do not conclude that the truth lies somewhere in the middle.
