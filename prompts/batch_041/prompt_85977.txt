Meanwhile I've been reading about the Happy the elephant case, and I want to see if you can help me run the case through Kant's machinery. Quick refresher on the facts since you can't look anything up: Happy was an Asian elephant at the Bronx Zoo for decades, the first elephant (I think) to pass the mirror self-recognition test, but she spent years alone after her companions died. The Nonhuman Rights Project filed a habeas corpus petition on her behalf in New York, arguing she has a common-law right to bodily liberty and should be moved to a sanctuary. The trial court found she was highly intelligent, self-aware, and emotionally complex, but said precedent barred treating her as a legal person. In 2022 the New York Court of Appeals ruled 5-2 against her. The majority basically said habeas is for humans and that extending it to animals would destabilize things, leaving it to the legislature. The main dissent, from Wilson, said habeas has historically been used by judges to push the law forward in cases involving enslaved people, women, and children, and that the real question was whether an elephant's detention can be so cruel and so contrary to what an elephant is that the writ should be available.

Who counts as what on Kant's picture -- is Happy a person, a thing, something else? What would Kant say the court should actually do, and does his indirect-duty view cut for or against the majority? I'm especially interested in whether the mirror self-recognition evidence does any damage to Kant's empirical assumptions about animal self-consciousness, or whether his view is robust to it. And then flip it: what does the case expose about the limits of the indirect-duty framework -- what does it fail to capture about Happy's own solitude, her suffering, or the dissent's cruelty-to-an-elephant framing? Be disciplined about the mapping, say where Kant is just silent rather than pretending he has an answer, and don't give me a bibliography -- I want the argument, not the reading list.
