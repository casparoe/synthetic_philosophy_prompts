My department's annual dinner is next month and I've somehow been landed with the after-dinner speech, and I want to spend it on Gödel's rotating universe. Setup you should know: the dinner is joint with the relativity group, and it's also the first event of a new centre for philosophy of physics, so there'll be a few donors and a dean in the room alongside people who genuinely know their way around a metric. Six minutes, so call it eight hundred words, written to be spoken. That gives you two halves to land for — the relativists, who'll wince if I say anything sloppy about closed timelike curves, and everyone else, who'll wince if I say anything at all about closed timelike curves.

What I want the speech built on is the argument Gödel ran in 1949, the same year he published that rotating-dust solution to the field equations — matter swirling, the whole thing stationary rather than expanding, and if you travel out far enough you can follow a path that brings you back into your own past. The time-travel bit is the part everyone has heard, and I don't want the speech to be about it. I want it about what he did with it: he argued that in a world like that there's no objective lapse of time, no fact of the matter about which events are happening 'now', and that since the equations permit such a world, relativity doesn't force time to be the flowing thing we take it to be — which he read as a point in favour of something in the neighbourhood of the idealist/Kantian line on time. I may be mangling that; if I am, put the right version in rather than tiptoeing around mine. The thing I actually want the room to feel is the *shape* of the inference, which I think is stranger and more interesting than the conclusion: a universe that isn't ours is being used to say something about our concepts. Is that legitimate, or is it a trick? Philosophers of physics do this constantly and physicists find it suspicious, and the speech should be honest about that — I'd much rather the audience leave with the puzzle than with a verdict, so don't oversell the conclusion. Since it's also the centre's launch, it'd be good if the speech quietly showed why this kind of question is worth caring about, without ever sounding like a pitch. Two more things. Somewhere in there I want the fact that we've actually looked and the universe doesn't appear to be rotating — there are observational bounds, from the microwave background I think, and I don't remember the numbers, so don't make one up, just don't leave the audience thinking Gödel's universe is a live candidate for our own. And I'd like the speech to end by arriving back at its own opening, since the whole subject is a world where the future leads back into the past — but lightly, I don't want it to feel like a magic trick. If it runs long, cut physics, not the puzzle. Don't put words in Gödel's or Einstein's mouth — paraphrase, don't quote. And no bibliography, no "for further reading", nothing I'm supposed to go look up; I'm giving this on Friday.

Start with the speech itself — no preamble, no notes to me about what you did or why.
