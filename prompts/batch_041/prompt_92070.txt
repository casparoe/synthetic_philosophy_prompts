I'm a third-year student doing a joint phil/maths degree, and I've somehow been voluntered to run next week's session of our GEB reading group — 16 people signed up, 75 minutes, a seminar room with four of those small whiteboards, a stack of A4, and phones going in a box at the door. We've just done the MIU chapter: start from MI, four rules (add U to anything ending in I; double whatever follows the M; replace III with U; delete UU), try to reach MU, and you can't — the point of the chapter being the difference between working inside a system and stepping outside it. I don't want to re-lecture material they've already read. I want them to grind on it themselves, hit the wall, and feel the jump out as *their* move rather than mine. And then, this is the bit I care about most, I want to burn the people who think they've already got the point. Because what everyone takes away from that chapter is basically a spell — MU, impossible, count the I's, they're never a multiple of 3 — and I want a session where that spell stops working. So: an activity for 16 people in four groups of four, 75 minutes, no computers, all paper and whiteboards.

Here's the shape I'm imagining, but redesign it if you see something better. Round one: each group gets the four rules and the target MU, and I say nothing except go. Round two: instead of me revealing anything, the groups have to produce an argument that MU is *unreachable* — and it has to survive an attack from a designated devil's advocate in each group whose whole job is to break it (the advocate is not allowed to say "you're wrong", only to produce a specific string that shows the argument doesn't do what it claims, or explain why they can't). Each group gets two "oracle tokens" that buy one yes/no question to me about the system, which I'm hoping models the jump as a costly, deliberate, optional move rather than a rule you can just apply; I'd like you to write the three or four questions groups will most likely ask and a safe, non-spoiling answer to each, because a facilitator improvising that is how the whole thing leaks. Round three, the sting: a new sealed envelope with the *same four rules* but a different target — I was going to use MUIU instead of MU, on the theory that what people remember is the verdict rather than the reason, but I don't actually know whether MUIU is reachable and I refuse to build a session on a guess, so check it and swap in something better if it's too easy. The people who "know" that this family is a dead end should get quietly wrecked. If you can also build a third system where the obstruction is a different *kind* of thing — not a count of letters, but a parity, an ordering, some quantity that can never go up — that would be the ideal ending, because then nobody can win by remembering the trick; but only if you can actually verify it, and don't bluff one at me.

What I need back: the activity as I'd run it (materials, roles, a minute-by-minute timeline with hard stops, the exact wording I hand out or say, and where the sticky notes and the big "claims" sheet on the wall come in), the debrief that maps what happened onto the object-level/meta-level distinction without ever having said the words "jumping out of the system" beforehand, and — separately, marked as not for students — your answer key: the derivation for the round-three target written out step by step with the rule used at each step, and the invariant argument for round two in a form I can check against what the groups produce, so I can tell a correct impossibility proof from a group that just got bored. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: I already suspect that most groups will spend twenty minutes flailing, that an impossibility claim from the loudest person will get accepted without the advocate doing their job, that one guy who's read the whole book twice will try to spoil round one, and that I'll blow the debrief because I'll still be adjudicating round three. And be honest about the part the activity can't do: my worry is that this teaches "there's always a clever invariant waiting to be found", which I'm fairly sure is false in the cases that actually matter, and that a 75-minute game can't reproduce the fact that outside toy systems nobody hands you the invariant or tells you whether one exists. If the session as I've sketched it can't do what I want — or if the whole twin-target idea doesn't work the way I think it does — say so plainly instead of dressing it up; I'd rather redesign now than find out in front of sixteen people.
