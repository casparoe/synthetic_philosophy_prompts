A colleague in a seminar on collective decision-making handed me the following argument, and I cannot tell whether it is a reductio of the argument or of its subject matter. I would like a diagnosis: which step should be rejected, why that step looked acceptable, and what rejecting it costs.

The setting. A three-person committee must decide whether to make a job offer. The members agree that an offer is warranted exactly when two sub-questions both come out a certain way — call them P (the candidate's research is strong) and Q (the candidate's teaching is strong) — with the offer, R, to be made just in case P and Q. Each member holds a fully consistent and complete set of judgments:

- Member 1: P yes, Q yes, R yes.
- Member 2: P yes, Q no, R no.
- Member 3: P no, Q yes, R no.

The committee resolves each question by majority vote. Then P carries (members 1 and 2), Q carries (members 1 and 3), and R fails (members 2 and 3). So the committee affirms P and Q and denies the very proposition it agrees they entail. So far, the familiar discursive dilemma.

The argument.

(1) The committee's judgments are its own, not merely a tally of its members' opinions.

(2) A body of judgments is coherent only if it is consistent and closed under the entailments its holder accepts.

(3) A procedure respects the members just in case the committee's judgment on each proposition tracks the majority of members' judgments on that proposition.

(4) Any procedure that respects the members in the sense of (3) will, on some profile of individually coherent members, yield an incoherent committee. Call this the formal result.

(5) So respect for members and coherence cannot both be demanded; one of them must give way.

(6) The same structure recurs within a single person over time. Suppose the evidence bearing on P, Q and R reaches me in three installments, and on each occasion I form the judgment best supported by the evidence so far, accepting throughout the same connection among the three propositions. Treat my three successive selves as three "members." If a procedure that respects each member's judgment can yield incoherence, and I am not permitted to be incoherent, then either I must disregard some of my own considered judgments or I am condemned to incoherence.

(7) But a single person can be perfectly coherent — I simply revise. So (6) is absurd, and the whole argument, including (4), must be unsound.

If I must choose, I would rather keep (1) and (2) than (7). I suspect the trouble lies in the step to (6), but I cannot say precisely where or what it would cost to fix.
