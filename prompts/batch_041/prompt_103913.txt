I am assembling materials for an aesthetics reading group on the paradox of tragedy — the apparent inconsistency between the fact that a work makes us sad and the fact that we seek it out and are glad we did. I do not want a survey of the literature. I want a diagnostic tree: a classification instrument that begins from a few commitments a reader can actually introspect about and leads, by a series of answerable questions, to a named position on why the sadness of sad art is worth having.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a roster of authors. Begin with this fixed skeleton, which is my own way of dividing the terrain, and let the tree's first branch sort readers by which of these relations they think holds between the negative affect (call it N) and the enjoyment (call it E): (i) identity — E is not a further state but N itself under a favorable description, so "enjoying" names the mode rather than a second feeling; (ii) production — N causes E, as in views on which the negative affect is converted, discharged, or otherwise transmuted; (iii) precondition — N is required for E without being part of it, because it supplies the stakes, the contrast, or the occasion that E needs; (iv) aggregation — E is independent of N and simply outweighs it, so the paradox is arithmetic; (v) dissolution — there is no N of the problematic kind, either because the affect is not a real emotion or because it is not intrinsically unpleasant.

Then let the lower branches separate readers by further questions, each phrased as something a reader could answer without knowing where it leads: whether the emotion is felt toward real or make-believe states of affairs; whether its unpleasantness is intrinsic or inherited from its object; whether the value of the episode is located in the emotion, in a cognition (insight, understanding, a correct appraisal of the world), in the artifact's formal achievement, or in the activity of engaging with it; and whether the relevant enjoyment is first-order or is a second-order response to one's own being moved.

Required leaves, at minimum: the discharge or catharsis view, the conversion view, the quasi-emotion view, the meta-emotion or second-order view, and the aggregation view. Attach the standard label to each leaf where one exists and mark the leaf unnamed where none does.

Every branch must be labeled with the question that separates it from its siblings, and every leaf must carry one sentence naming its most awkward commitment. Note any combinations of answers that no position occupies, and diagnose briefly whether the slot is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot distinguish, rather than letting the questions pretend to separate them.

Keep it to seven to nine leaves. Use these cases at least once each, since they are the ones my group keeps arguing about: a tragedy enjoyed more on a second viewing, when suspense is gone; a sad piece of music whose lyrics are in a language the listener does not understand; a documentary about real suffering that one finds artistically magnificent and morally uncomfortable; a tragedy whose events one would not want to occur in reality; and a sad passage one rereads simply for the pleasure of it. Aim for eight to ten questions in all, and end with a short scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the leaves.
