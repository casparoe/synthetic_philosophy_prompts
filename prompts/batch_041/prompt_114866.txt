Every couple years my mom's side of the family does a big reunion, and this time we're also unveiling a little plaque for my great-grandfather, who it turns out did some genuinely bad things that the family spent fifty years not talking about. I'm the one giving the speech. The trouble is nobody agrees what the plaque is even for. My Turkish aunt thinks the whole idea is a betrayal and would rather we say it was never really him. My Alabama uncle wants us to keep stressing that by the standards of his time he was also a good man. My German cousin wants a full reckoning, no excuses, no softening. And my wife's Japanese family would honestly be happiest if we just didn't make a fuss. I've been reading around about how different countries deal with the worst thing in their history, and I've definately ended up with a view I want the speech to stand for. I think there are only two kinds of remembering: the kind you do in order to get something out of it — a lesson, a warning, absolution, pride, closure — and the kind you do because it's owed, where you get nothing back. All four of my relatives want the first kind, and I want this speech to be the second kind, which is why I can't do the usual "look how far we've come" finish and I can't do "never again" either. But I still have to stand up in front of thirty relatives who are expecting to feel okay by the end. So write me the speech. Plain prose I can read out loud — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, nothing but paragraphs, like a long letter. And don't cite anything, it's a speech, nobody wants a bibliography read at them.
