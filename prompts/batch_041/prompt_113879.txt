I'm in a seminar on big history and this week we did Peter Turchin's cliodynamics, the whole program of turning history into something quantitative, with models and databases and predictions. Our professor assigned a long magazine piece about it and honestly I found it pretty convincing, but a couple of things in it have been nagging at me and I don't know the literature well enough to tell whether I'm right to be bothered. The gist of the piece was this:

"Turchin's research program, which he and a few colleagues call cliodynamics, shows that complex societies move through a repeating secular cycle that takes centuries to run its course. The engine is inequality. Population growth presses on resources, the surplus gets sucked upward, ordinary people end up immiserated, and the elite grows fat. Once the gap gets wide enough, the society snaps: revolution, civil war, or state collapse, after which the cycle resets. Turchin also has a variable for asabiya, roughly a society's stock of collective solidarity, a term he lifted from the medieval historian Ibn Khaldun, which he measures off the historical record and which explains why some societies come out of a collapse renewed and others don't. He built the Seshat database to feed all of this, and in 2010 he predicted that the 2020s would be a decade of political instability in the US and Western Europe, which is more or less what happened, so the critics who say the whole thing is curve-fitting after the fact have some reckoning to do. The upshot is that history isn't just a chaotic sequence of events, it has a mathematical structure, and if we track the right variables we can say roughly when the next crisis is coming."

Can you go through that claim by claim and seperate them into (a) what a cliodynamicist would nod along to, (b) what's garbled or overstated, and (c) what Turchin would say he never claimed in the first place? The part I really can't judge is the inequality line. I have a feeling that "inequality is the engine" is a simplification of something more specific, but I don't know what the more specific thing would be, or whether it matters for how the rest of the article reads.

The second thing is really why I'm writing. A friend in the seminar used the piece to win an arguement with me. He said that if history has a mathematical structure like that, then anyone who says revolutions turn on contingency, choices, accidents, individual actors, is just wrong, and historians should stop doing narrative and ethnographic work and hand their material over to the modelers. I didn't have a reply. Does his conclusion actually follow from the article's version of the theory, and does it follow from Turchin's actual version, assuming those differ?
