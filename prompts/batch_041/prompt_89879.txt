I'm giving the short talk that opens our chamber-music society's season next week — eight minutes, standing at the front before the players come on, then we all sit down and they play. The room splits cleanly in two: our regulars, who've been coming for decades and know the repertoire backwards but visibly wince at anything that sounds like philosophy, and a block of academics we invited this year from the philosophy and psych departments who'll notice immediately if I mangle a view. I want to hang the whole talk on Schopenhauer's theory of music — the claim that music is the one art that isn't a copy of the Platonic Ideas but a direct expression of the Will itself, and that its four voices trace the grades of the Will's objectification, bass at the bottom (blind striving, mere matter) up through the middle voices to melody at the top (human life and striving), so the structure of nature is supposedly laid out in the harmony. The wrinkle that makes this hard: Schopenhauer also holds that music never denotes anything in particular — it gives the emotion itself in the abstract, not your sorrow or mine — so a talk that tells people what the music 'represents' is quietly working against the very view it's promoting, and the regulars will feel that falseness even if they can't name it. There's a second wrinkle I'd like worked in if it fits: for him the other arts give a temporary release from the Will by letting us contemplate the Ideas, but music, being a copy of the Will itself rather than of the Ideas, has a stranger and more ambivalent relation to that release. Write me the talk. The program opens with a piece built on a repeating ground bass and the second half starts with an unaccompanied violin piece, if that's useful. Our founder, who died in March, used to give this talk herself and would want it kept light. Don't lecture, and don't quote anything at me — just the talk, in a voice that can carry all of this out loud to a room that mostly came for the music.
