I am a PhD student working on the foundations of decision theory, and I run a fortnightly reading group for philosophy and economics graduate students. I would like you to design a 90-minute session for that group: twelve to sixteen people, all comfortable with conditional expectation and Bayes' rule, none of whom has met the result I want to teach.

The result is the one usually stated as follows: for a decision-maker who maximizes expected utility, free information can never lower the expected utility of her best available act, since she can always ignore it and act as though she had never received it. I will name and attribute it in the debrief; the activity itself should not use the name. What makes it worth a session rather than a paragraph is that almost everyone's first reaction to it is confident and wrong. They produce cases — a genetic test, a partner's true feelings, a diagnosis — in which they insist that knowing could only make things worse, and they cannot see what the theorem is leaving out. I want them to have that reaction in the room, commit to it in writing, and then watch it be scored.

Design a runnable activity in which participants repeatedly choose to accept or decline a free test that bears on an unknown state determining which of two acts pays. Structure it as a sequence of worlds differing only in the institutional rules around the test, so that the same two acts and the same payoff table recur and the value of the test changes sign. I want at least: (i) a clean world in which the test is free, private, and does not alter the participant's options, and in which accepting weakly dominates — this is where the theorem bites, and the decliners should be able to see that they lost; (ii) a world in which the test is free and private but its result carries an obligation that removes an option, so that declining is genuinely optimal, and the arithmetic must actually support that; and (iii) a world in which the test is imperfect, so that in some realizations the informed participant does worse than an uninformed one would have — the point being that the theorem survives noise, and that the noise is what will make the group doubt it. Then turn the second half of the session over to the group: they are to try to construct a case in which free, costless information that leaves the options untouched still hurts an expected-utility maximizer. They should fail, and the failure should be the lesson. The debrief should then locate the escape hatches honestly — information can hurt only if it is not free in the relevant sense, or it changes the option set, or the agent is not an expected-utility maximizer, including the case in which her future self will not act on it as she now wishes — and should say which of these the activity has staged and which it has merely asserted.

Give me the full rules (materials; how states are assigned without a computer; how accept/decline is recorded before the result is known; the payoff tables; and scoring that needs no money); a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes; the explicit expected-value arithmetic for accepting versus declining in each world, so that I can check it; a debrief that maps each world onto the assumption of the theorem it isolates; the failures I should expect on a first run — I anticipate at least the problem that a single unlucky realization in the noisy world will convince the group the theorem is false — with a fix for each; and a frank note on what the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
