Last month I spent a long train journey arguing with two friends about the annual world happiness rankings. The ranking itself rests on a single survey item: people in each country are shown a ladder, told that the top rung represents the best possible life for them and the bottom rung the worst possible life for them, and asked to say where their current life falls; a country's score is simply the average of those answers, and the other variables that get published alongside it — income per head, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, perceptions of corruption — are used only to explain the score, not to compute it. My friend the novelist claimed this meant a country could climb the ranking without making a single life better, merely by changing what its citizens picture at the top of the ladder, or by changing the story they tell themselves about the life they already have. My friend the economist said that was cheating, then discovered he could not say precisely which rule was being broken, since the ladder is anchored to each respondent's own imagination and the question asks for an appraisal of a life rather than a report of how that life feels. We christened the manoeuvre "ceiling work", and then spent the rest of the journey failing to write a joke about it.

Please write the joke we could not finish. Set it in a small fictional island republic — I have been calling it Cordanza — whose ministry of happiness has just launched a programme it calls the National Ceiling Review, on the theory that the cheapest route to the top of the ranking is to bring the top of the ladder closer to ordinary life, and that a life can also be made to score higher by being told better. A short scene would work well: the minister and her chief adviser on the morning the programme is announced, or a briefing for sceptical schoolteachers who have been conscripted to run it. The humour should turn on the actual mechanics of the item — that the ceiling is whatever the respondent imagines, so lowering the ceiling raises the score; that the question invites an evaluation of a life rather than a report of one, so better stories are worth as much as better lives; and that none of this is technically dishonest, which is precisely what is maddening about it. It should be funny to someone who already knows how the rankings are assembled and why they are disputed, but it should not require the reader to know any particular study, author or country, and it should not be a generic "statistics can be gamed" joke. A closing line for the minister would be a nice touch.
