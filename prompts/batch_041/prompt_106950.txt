Public health systems in many countries decide what to fund by ranking treatments according to their cost per quality-adjusted life-year: each health state is assigned a score between 0 and 1, years lived in that state are multiplied by the score, and the resulting totals are added across patients so that treatments can be compared by how much health they buy per dollar. The same systems insist, in their public statements, that every life is of equal worth. I want to know what this practice presupposes about comparing well-being across persons. Reconstruct the implicit view: whether it requires cardinal and interpersonally comparable utility, whether it treats health as a good distinct from preference satisfaction, and what rule for combining different people's scores it assumes. Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say which rival accounts of interpersonal comparison — pure ordinalism, the view that only health itself matters rather than any broader welfare, or a prioritarian weighting that gives extra weight to the worst off — it rules out and which it remains compatible with. Then take the apparent tension head-on: does scoring a year of disabled life below a year of healthy life commit the practice to treating disabled lives as worth less, or can the claim of equal worth be preserved without changing how the scores are built? Finally, tell me what would have to change in practice if we adopted a view on which these comparisons are literally meaningless.
