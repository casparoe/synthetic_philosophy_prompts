I'm in a seminar on AI alignment and epistemology and our unit on factored cognition just wrapped. For the final paper I'm arguing that HCH (the recursive-acronym thing: "humans consulting HCH" — an infinite tree where each node is a bounded human answering one small question, and each parent node assembles its children's answers into a slightly bigger answer) isn't only an idealization of what a machine could do with unlimited compute. It's also the cleanest idealization of what we mean when we say a person or a committee really thought something through. Roughly: deliberation just is factored cognition, and HCH is its limit.

Here's where I try to push past the standard discussion. In HCH a parent node reads its children's answers for free — checking costs nothing. I dont think that's true of real deliberation, so I define a two-parameter version: depth d (how far you decompose) and verification cost v (what it costs a parent to actually confirm a child got its sub-question right). HCH is just the v = 0 corner. My claim is that real human deliberation lives at finite v, and that this explains something the usual framing can't: why "just break the problem down further" stops helping after a point. Past some depth you spend more on checking sub-answers than you saved by not doing the thinking yourself. My carrying example is an editor who wants to lean on a fact-checker but can't confirm the check without basically redoing it.

Second thing I'm less sure about: if verification itself has to be decomposed the same way, don't we get a regress? My move is to say it bottoms out in judgments that are cheap but unverifiable — perception-ish stuff — so deliberation is ultimately grounded in something non-deliberative. I like that conclusion but I suspect it's doing a lot of work for me and I've definately stacked a metaphor on top of an argument somewhere.

Can you give me comments? Specifically: (1) do I have HCH right, or am I quietly turning it into something else? (2) is the verification-cost refinement an actual contribution, or just a dressed-up version of the standard objection that factored cognition assumes sub-tasks are independently checkable? (3) is the regress a real problem or a fake one? (4) is any of this interesting to someone who isn't already in the AI safety bubble, or does it only sound deep from inside? Write it in American English.
