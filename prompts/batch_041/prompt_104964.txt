I am a high-school student who has been teaching myself decision theory in my spare time, and for the past few weeks I have been arguing with a friend from our mathematics club about a claim that keeps appearing in the material we read and that I suspect is an analogy doing the work of an argument. The claim, as it is usually put, is that a single person making decisions over the course of a long life is like a single gambler playing the same game round after round, so that the only thing a rational agent should care about is the rate at which his or her stake compounds; and from this it is said to follow that maximizing expected value is a mistake, that the correct policy is instead to maximize the geometric mean, and that no rational person should ever accept a wager carrying any real chance of ruin. The illustration always offered is a coin flipped over and over in which your money is multiplied by 1.5 on heads and by 0.6 on tails: across a large crowd of players, each of whom plays only once, the average fortune grows by five percent per round, yet almost every individual player is ruined, because a few enormous winners drag the average upward while the typical trajectory shrinks toward nothing. My friend has taken all of this as a general theory of rationality and now announces that any gamble offering a large but unlikely prize is irrational even when it is plainly favorable on average, and that institutions — governments, insurers, universities — ought to reason exactly as an individual does.

What I would like from you is an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on gambling. Please begin by setting out as explicitly as you can what is meant to correspond to what — the crowd of players and the lone player, the bankroll and the person, one round of play and one decision in a life, the average of the crowd and the trajectory of the individual — and then tell me, as carefully as you can, where the correspondence breaks. I am especially curious about the points at which the comparison seems to smuggle in the conclusion it claims to prove: whether the fact that I cannot split myself into a hundred copies really forbids reasoning about the distribution of outcomes; whether the same argument would condemn an insurance company or a state that genuinely can average over very many people; whether "never risk ruin" follows from the mathematics or was assumed at the outset; and what becomes of the analogy when the thing at stake is not money that compounds but health, or friendship, or a decision one faces only once in a lifetime. I would also like to know whether the conclusion my friend has drawn — that maximizing the geometric mean is the uniquely rational policy — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion the whole time, and if so, what comparison would do the job better.

Please keep your answer to no more than eight hundred words, and organize it as a short table of correspondences followed by at most five numbered paragraphs on the disanalogies that genuinely matter, ending with a brief verdict stating plainly how much of the argument, if any, the analogy can support.
