I am a PhD student working at the intersection of machine learning and philosophy, and I help run a reading group on AI safety. Two of our most active members have argued for a year without converging, and I have been asked to turn their exchange into a document both of them could actually sign.

One of them holds what I will call the Tool-AI position: a capable system should be designed and used as an instrument — it accepts a request, returns an answer or performs a bounded operation, and does not pursue ends of its own across contexts; on this view agency is a contingent design choice, capability and agency are separable, and the safest systems act only when queried, with the effects under human control. The other holds the Unavoidable-Agency position: any system capable enough to be useful is already goal-directed in the relevant sense, so Tool AI is at best a temporary regime and at worst a comforting fiction, and the real work is aligning agents rather than pretending to avoid them.

Draft the joint statement they should be able to produce together, as an adversarial collaboration, in the following form.

First, a numbered list of claims both sides can accept — about what counts as a tool and what counts as an agent, about the feasibility and stability of tool-like designs, about what each side means by alignment and by safety, and about which parts of the dispute are empirical and which are conceptual.

Second, a precise statement of each remaining disagreement, each phrased in wording its own side would endorse rather than a caricature the other side can knock down.

Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party — and, for each crux, a concrete scenario, thought experiment, or class of evidence that both parties agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

To keep the discussion tractable, please adopt the following stipulative distinction and use it throughout: distinguish task-optimality (the system does well on the objective it is given, in the situation in which it is given it) from objective-persistence (the system carries a standing disposition to pursue that objective across contexts, including contexts where pursuing it is not requested and where departing from the stated task would serve the objective). The crux I most want made exact is whether Tool-AI designs can be task-optimal without objective-persistence, whether that combination remains stable as capability scales, and whether the two sides are disagreeing about a real engineering dichotomy or only about where to draw a line on a continuum. For the empirical side of that crux, have both sides agree in advance to treat as decisive the class of systems that are given a long-horizon objective and are free to acquire resources, and ask each to predict what such a system would do at the moment its objective and its continued operation come into conflict.

Finally, if any part of the disagreement turns out to be merely verbal — the two using agent, goal, or autonomy in different senses — say which part and why, and state which sense, if either, is the one that matters for the safety questions both of them care about.
