I recently moved from a wet-lab systems-neuroscience postdoc into a philosophy department, and the transition has made a topic that used to be merely abstract feel uncomfortably personal: at two job talks and one reading group this term, my modeling work has been waved away with the phrase "that's just greedy reductionism," and in each case the person using the phrase seemed to mean something slightly different. Next month I give a departmental colloquium in which I defend a paper whose thesis is this: the crane/skyhook contrast and the charge of greedy reductionism have been fused into a single thought-terminating move, and pulling them apart again shows most of what gets called greedy reductionism to be guilty only of a different, lesser fault.

The argument I plan to run goes roughly as follows. First, Dennett's original contrast, in Darwin's Dangerous Idea, is between two ways of explaining how something apparently designed came to be: cranes, processes built out of available parts that do real lifting, and skyhooks, free-floating miracles that do the lifting for nothing. Greedy reductionism is a separate diagnosis: it names the vice of skipping the intermediate levels of explanation, the way one might try to explain why a chess program plays well by talking about the voltages in its transistors rather than about search, evaluation, and the structure of the game. Second, I claim these two have been conflated, so that any explanation pitched below the phenomenon is now accused of both sins at once — a skyhook-free crane that is also somehow cheating by not stopping where the accuser would prefer. That is incoherent: skipping levels and denying the need for cranes are different failures, and an account can commit one, the other, both, or neither. Third, my constructive proposal: replace the pair with two independent axes, and define greedy reductionism as the failure of what I call level-tethering — the requirement that an explanation, at every level it passes through on the way down, must leave that level's pattern recoverable from the level below rather than simply asserting that the lower level gives rise to the upper one. On this view the sin is not reaching downward too far; it is reaching downward without paying the toll at each stratum.

The audience is about fifteen people. Roughly five are philosophers of mind in the Dennett tradition, who will think I am attacking a caricature none of them has ever held. Three are philosophers of biology working on emergence and downward causation, who will hear my proposal as a restatement of the reductionism they oppose. Two are complex-systems physicists who will insist that levels are not scales. One is a Kantian who regards the whole exercise as naturalism overreaching. The remainder are graduate students who will ask what, concretely, any of this changes.
