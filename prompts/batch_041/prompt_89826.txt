I am trying to find out whether there is an established name for a certain move in problem-solving, and if there is not, what it ought to be called. The move shows up in puzzles of this shape: one is given a starting string of symbols, a handful of rules for rewriting any string, and a target string, and one is asked to get from the first to the second. After a long stretch of fruitless derivation, one stops deriving and instead asks what the rules, taken together, do to some feature of a string; one finds a feature that every rule leaves untouched and that the target lacks, and concludes that the target cannot be reached at all. What has been produced is not a derivation but a claim about the whole system, and it was available only from outside it. A second case, which I suspect is the same move in different dress: a proof that is intractable in one notation becomes immediate in another, where the second notation was not one the original rules permitted. I know about thinking outside the box and about stepping back to audit one's own reasoning, and this seems to be neither: the point is not to drop an assumption, and not merely to check one's inferences, but to stop treating the rules as instruments to be followed and start treating them as objects to be described. I also want to know what separates it from the counterfeit, where someone merely bolts another rule onto the system and calls that a step outside. Is there a name for this? I want the nearest established concepts, how what I have described differs from each of them, and, if nothing quite fits, a term with a definition and boundary cases; a bare label will not help me. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion.
