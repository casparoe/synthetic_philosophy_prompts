ok so I've got an essay due for my ethics of communication class and I keep flip-flopping on it, so I want to just use the essay to actually settle where I land. The question is whether there's a genuine moral difference between lying and merely misleading. The line we got in lecture is that there is one: a lie asserts something the speaker thinks is false, so it breaks the norm that's supposedly built into assertion, whereas a misleading statement is true and merely invites the listener to infer something false — so the listener does part of the work themselves, and that's supposed to matter morally. I find that appealing but I think it falls apart once you look at a third case, which I've been calling "selection" (I made the term up, rename it if you want).

Selection is when you say nothing false and you don't even lean on any false implicature — you just control which true things get said, in what order, and with how much salience, so the listener walks away with a badly distorted picture. Like a hiring manager who, for each candidate, only ever reports true facts but picks the facts by how they'll read. Or a lab that reports a true subset of its results. Or a doctor who answers every question truthfully but chooses which questions to invite.

So what I want the essay to figure out is whether the lying/misleading distinction survives once you seperate (a) lying, (b) misleading via false implicature, and (c) selection. Some options I can see: the distinction survives and selection is just further down the same scale; or it collapses and the lying/misleading line was never the real joint; or the real difference-maker is something else entirely, like whether you took on responsibility for the listener's belief at all, or whether they had fair warning about the convention. I'm genuinely unsure which, so argue for whichever you think is right.

Two things I keep getting stuck on and want addressed. First, auditability: a lie is catchable in a way that selection isn't, since there's no false sentence to point at, and I can't tell whether that makes selection worse (unaccountable) or better (nobody's trust was cashed in). Second, conventions: if everyone knows that, say, references only ever contain true positives, does that make the faint-praise version not deceptive at all, or just the listener's problem? And does it change anything if the framing is set by a neutral process rather than a person with a stake in the outcome?

I want an actual verdict, not "it's complicated, both matter." Start the essay with the verdict in the first sentence and then give the argument for it — no throat-clearing, no restating the question, and please don't pad it with a reading list, I don't need citations for this one.
