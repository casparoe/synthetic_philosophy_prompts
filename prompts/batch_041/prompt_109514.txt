I've been arguing about anthropic reasoning with a friend, and we've built a chain that seems to let me settle empirical cosmology from my armchair. I can't find the bad step. Tell me which one to reject, why it looked compelling, and what rejecting it costs.

Setup: two rival theories make identical predictions for every local observation I could ever make. The small theory says the total number of observers who will ever exist is N. The enormous theory says it is K·N, where K is astronomically large. Two rules are on offer for reasoning about my own place in the world. The self-sampling rule: reason as if you were an observer drawn at random from all observers in your reference class. The self-indication rule: multiply a theory's prior credence by the number of observers it posits, on the grounds that a larger world contains more slots for someone like me to occupy.

(1) Rational credence tracks likelihood: I should favor the hypothesis under which my evidence is more probable.
(2) My evidence includes the fact that I exist and am having these experiences.
(3) Under the self-indication rule, the probability of finding myself existing is proportional to the number of observers the world contains.
(4) The enormous theory posits K times as many observers as the small theory, and predicts my local observations exactly as well.
(5) So my evidence is K times more likely under the enormous theory.
(6) So I should already be nearly certain the enormous theory is true, without running any experiment, however modest its prior.
(7) But then any hypothesis that multiplies observers without bound would win by the same move, and cosmology would be settled by armchair arithmetic.
(8) Something has gone wrong.

Which step do I give up? I'd rather keep (1) and (2). Tell me why the culprit step looked fine, and what I pay for dropping it — in particular, whether escaping this conclusion forces me to accept some other unwelcome result about my own existence. Answer in under 400 words.
