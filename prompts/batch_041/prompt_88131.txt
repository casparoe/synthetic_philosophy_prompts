I'm a systems engineer with no formal philosophy training, and next month I'm presenting at my company's internal reading group on minds and machines. The thesis I want to defend is that the internal states of a trained language model have genuine content, and that the reason is etiological rather than tracking-based: a state means what it was selected to do, not what it happens to correlate with. The selection process I have in mind is the training-and-curation pipeline itself -- the repeated cycles of filtering, deduplication, human preference labeling, and gradient updates that winnow a randomly initialized network into one whose states reliably do something. I've been calling this pipeline teleosemantics. The wrinkle is that the selective environment is made of decisions by engineers and annotators rather than a niche out in the world, and I think that's a feature, not a bug.

The room will be about ten people. Most are ML and infrastructure engineers who treat meaning as whatever the evaluation set measures and who will want an operational test; two did cognitive science degrees and know the etiological tradition and its standard objections better than I do; one product manager cares only about hallucination and grounding and will ask what any of this buys us in practice; and one colleague thinks all talk of content is a category error.

Prepare my Q&A sheet: the eight or so objections this particular room will actually raise, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way that person would put it, with a ninety-second reply and an honest note on where the reply is thin. Tell me which objections to concede up front rather than fight, which single exchange has to go well for the position to survive, and where my own argument is weakest so I can defend it deliberately instead of hoping nobody notices. No reading lists, please -- I won't have time to chase them down. End with the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
