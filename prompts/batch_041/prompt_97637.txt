I teach a graduate seminar on theories of freedom, and every year we arrive at the same impasse. On one side is the familiar Berlin-style contrast between negative liberty as non-interference and positive liberty as self-mastery, together with Berlin's warning that the positive conception can be turned to coercive ends in the name of a person's allegedly truer or deeper interests. On the other side stands the neo-republican claim — Skinner's historical work and Pettit's constructive program — that non-domination is a third concept, irreducible to either of the other two: you are dominated, and therefore unfree, when someone else has the capacity to interfere arbitrarily in your affairs, even if that capacity is never exercised. The cases that do the work are the slave whose master is benevolent and never in fact interferes, and the dependent spouse who is never forbidden anything because she has internalized what is expected of her. The negative-libertarian verdict is that these people are free, since nobody is interfering; the republican verdict is that they are paradigms of unfreedom, since they live at the mercy of another's will. The critics — Kramer is the one I assign, though he is not alone — reply that once you specify what "arbitrary," "capacity," and "interference" are supposed to mean, non-domination collapses into a modal variant of negative liberty, freedom from the mere possibility of interference, and that the third-concept claim is a matter of rhetoric rather than substance. We read the rejoinders, we read the objections to the rejoinders, and by the end of the term neither camp has moved.

My own suspicion, which I would like you to test rather than assume, is that this is not one disagreement but two superimposed on each other. One is about the extension of the concept — which cases are to count as freedom and which as unfreedom — and it may be that the slave case is not shared data at all, but a contested intuition that neither side can argue for. The other is about what kind of concept liberty is meant to be: whether it names a describable condition of the agent (how many doors are open, and how securely) or instead a relational status, a standing within a political order, the difference between a free person and a dependent one. If the latter, then the two sides may not be disagreeing about a fact at all, but about the point of having the concept in the first place — which would make the dispute closer to a verbal or meta-level one than either camp is willing to admit.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a survey and not a verdict for one side. Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, substantive, a clash of intuitions about cases, two different questions being answered under a single word, or a genuine empirical or formal question in disguise — and defend the diagnosis by appeal to what each side actually says. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given that diagnosis: a distinction, a stipulated redefinition, a formal result, or an argument that none should be expected. Work from your own grasp of the positions and arguments rather than from any reading list; do not offer citations or sources, and do not invent them. Keep the whole reply under 800 words.
