Two years ago I would have said that to be free is simply for no one to be stopping you. Then I spent a stint working under a founder who never once overruled me, yet held — in writing, and by every rule of the place — the standing right to undo anything I did, for any reason or none at all. Nothing was ever done to me. I left anyway, and I still cannot explain to my friends why that arrangement felt less like liberty than like a leash that was never pulled; they think I am being dramatic.

Write me a short story that makes that distinction legible to someone who has never felt it. Set it in an invented place — a company town, a station, a settlement — where a single figure or office holds sweeping arbitrary power over ordinary lives but has not used it in living memory, so that by every outward measure no one is being interfered with. Let the characters disagree about whether their unease is warranted: one should argue that since nothing is done to us we are plainly free, while another senses a difference between not being touched and not being at anyone's mercy. Include at least one moment where the power is very nearly invoked, so the reader can see what a dormant power and an absent one would each look like. I would rather the story resolve nothing and preach nothing — I want a reader to close it able to state both positions in their own words. Around 1,500 words.
