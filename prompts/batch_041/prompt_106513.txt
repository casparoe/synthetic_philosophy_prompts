I chair a standing harm-appraisal panel at a national public-health agency. Three weeks ago I was made chair, after eleven years as an epidemiologist on the panel's staff side, and the remit is now mine to run. Every proposal that reaches us has the same shape: to secure some benefit, the agency would impose either a very small burden on an enormous number of people or a severe burden on a few. The panel must return a recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — within ten working days, and it must be defensible in public. Our members are competent people with backgrounds in epidemiology, law, and health economics; none of them is a philosopher, and none will read anything I circulate that is longer than a page.

I have read enough to know that the underlying question has a name in the literature — it is usually introduced as a choice between one person being tortured and an astronomical number of people each receiving a speck of dust in the eye — and that the dispute is not about arithmetic but about whether burdens of different sizes may be summed across persons at all, and if so whether there is any size of burden that may not be traded against any number of smaller ones. I also know that the same structure reappears when what is traded is not a certain small burden but a very small probability of a catastrophe. I do not want an essay adjudicating the dispute. I want a procedure my panel can actually run.

Act as a decision theorist and practical ethicist who has worked on the aggregation of harms and on decision-making under severe uncertainty. Think carefully before writing. Begin with a short section headed Working notes, no more than 200 words, in which you identify the distinctions that must survive the translation into a usable procedure and the ones you are deliberately dropping; then give the deliverable below. Do not supply citations, reading lists, or names of theories that a reader would have to look up; if you use a term of art, define it inside the step where it appears.

The procedure must meet these constraints. It must be a fixed sequence of questions the panel asks in the same order every time, with the order itself justified — say what depends on what. For each step it must state what counts as a sufficient answer, what happens when the answer is no, and whether the step turns on a fact the panel can establish or on a value judgment the panel must simply make; steps of the second kind must be marked as such rather than disguised as calculation. It must say where the procedure stops with a rejection, where it hands the matter to an official outside the panel, and what that official must record. It must include a step that forces the panel to declare openly whether it is treating the small and the large burdens as commensurable, rather than smuggling that assumption in through a scoring scheme. It must handle at least these features, because they keep appearing in our cases: burdens that are experienced rather than merely risked; burdens that are correlated across the affected population rather than independent, so that an unknown common mechanism could make all the small burdens large at once; proposals whose headline benefit rests on a very small probability of a very large loss; the slide that occurs when someone asks why one more person should not be added to the small-burden side; and the fact that some burdens are reversible at a cost and others are not.

Then run the procedure on three cases of your own construction, in the same table. At least one must be a case where the procedure returns a verdict I will not like and where I nonetheless cannot show the procedure was misapplied. At least one must be a case the procedure cannot settle, so that the panel has to decide by vote, and you must say what the vote is actually about.

Deliver the procedure as a single valid JSON object with exactly these fields:

- procedure_name (string)
- preconditions (array of strings): what must be true, or known, before the panel may apply the procedure at all.
- steps (array of objects, in order), each with: step_number (integer); question (string, phrased as the panel asks it); sufficient_answer (string); if_no (string); kind (one of "fact", "value_judgment", "mixed"); why_this_step_is_here (string, naming the philosophical point the step operationalizes, in one or two sentences).
- handoffs (array of objects), each with: trigger (string); who_decides (string); what_they_must_record (string).
- known_failure_modes (array of objects), each with: failure_mode (string); symptom (string, as it would appear in a panel discussion); mitigation (string).
- what_the_procedure_cannot_do (array of strings).

Deliver the three cases as a markdown table with exactly these columns: case_id; case_description; step_at_which_resolved; verdict; was_this_a_value_call; what_the_procedure_cannot_settle; how_the_chair_will_dislike_this. Keep every cell under forty words.
