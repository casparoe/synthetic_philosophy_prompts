I'm a philosophy grad student who wandered into a divinity-school seminar on the historical Jesus, and we've hit a wall I can't tell is our fault or the field's. Here's the shape of it. For a couple hundred years the method has been to run sayings and episodes through criteria that are supposed to sort the authentic from what the early church made up: embarrassment (nobody invents what embarrasses them), dissimiliarity (only what can't be derived from Judaism or from the early church), multiple attestation, coherence with whatever you've already accepted, and sometimes Aramaic coloring or a Palestinian setting. Every one of these has a standing complaint against it. Dissimilarity looks self-defeating, since it keeps only what Jesus shares with neither his Jewish setting nor the movement that remembered him, leaving a figure nobody would plausibly have followed; the criteria get applied selectively and return probably where people want certainly; they were built to isolate little units of tradition, which assumes those units once circulated on their own, which form criticism asserted more than demonstrated; coherence is circular; and none of them can do its job without already knowing where the church ends and Jesus begins, which is the thing in dispute. Then there's the memory crowd, who say something more radical: the gospels are products of social memory and oral performance, and memory isn't a bucket that either preserves or distorts, its always doing both at once, so authentic-versus-inauthentic may be the wrong cut entirely, and the interesting question becomes what the tradition's reshaping of Jesus reveals about the tradition.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict on Jesus. Is this standoff substantive, or is it verbal -- two senses of authentic, one meaning goes back to the man, the other goes back to the movement -- or is the question itself malformed? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the real work, namely what memory actually does, which the historians are answering by intuition while the psychologists have their own views about it? Or is it downstream of a disagreement about what history is even supposed to deliver, a person or a memory of a person? My own hypothesis is that the criteria could never settle this: they're negative tests, designed to rule things out, and the field has been treating a heap of negative results as if it added up to a portrait. Tell me if that's right or if I'm being too clever. And tell me what would actually count as progress here -- a distinction, a result from the memory literature, a change of question -- or whether this is one of those debates where the failure to progress is itself the finding.
