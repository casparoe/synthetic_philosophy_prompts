I am trying to work through a difficult passage in Aristotle's Metaphysics, Book VII, Part 3, and I would like help understanding how it bears on the contemporary problem of the statue and the clay. The passage reads:

"The word 'substance' is applied, if not in more senses, still at least to four main objects; for both the essence and the universal and the genus, are thought to be the substance of each thing, and fourthly the substratum. Now the substratum is that of which everything else is predicated, while it is itself not predicated of anything else. And so we must first determine the nature of this; for that which underlies a thing primarily is thought to be in the truest sense its substance. And in one sense matter is said to be of the nature of substratum, in another, shape, and in a third, the compound of these. (By the matter I mean, for instance, the bronze, by the shape the pattern of its form, and by the compound of these the statue, the concrete whole.)"

The statue/clay puzzle, as I understand it, is this: a statue and the lump of clay that constitutes it appear to occupy the same place and to be composed of the same material parts, yet they seem to have different persistence conditions. If the statue is smashed, the lump of clay survives (it is merely rearranged), whereas the statue does not. So either they are not identical, despite sharing all their parts, or identity is not sufficient for sharing persistence conditions, or one of our descriptions is not really of a distinct object. Contemporary metaphysicians often try to resolve this by distinguishing constitution from identity: the clay constitutes the statue without being identical to it.

My difficulty is that Aristotle's passage seems to introduce a threefold distinction—matter, shape, and the compound—rather than a twofold distinction between a constituted object and its constituting matter. I want to know how to read the passage in light of the statue/clay puzzle. Specifically:

1. When Aristotle says that the bronze is the matter and the statue is the compound of matter and form, is he committed to saying that the bronze and the statue are numerically distinct? Or is the bronze not a countable object in the relevant sense, so that the puzzle does not arise?

2. If the bronze is the matter and the statue is the compound, what happens to the compound when the statue is melted down? Does the compound cease to exist while the bronze persists? If so, is the compound a substance in the same sense as the bronze, or in a different sense?

3. A colleague has suggested to me that Aristotle's account anticipates the contemporary distinction between composition and constitution: the bronze is not a proper part of the statue, but a portion of matter that constitutes it, and constitution is asymmetric and non-identical. Is that reading supported by the passage, or does Aristotle's talk of a "compound" resist it? I am especially interested in whether the passage gives us any reason to think that the statue and the clay are not two things but one thing under two descriptions, or whether it instead multiplies entities.

I would appreciate a careful reading of the passage that helps me see how the matter/form/compound distinction is supposed to work, and how far it can be mapped onto the modern debate without anachronism. Please avoid giving me a bibliography or reading list; I am interested in the conceptual reconstruction.
