My best friend's wedding is in three weeks and I'm giving the best-man speech, and I want it to actually be *about* something instead of the usual list of embarrassing stories. He's an evolutionary biologist, and the thing he's spent his whole career on is basically this: you can say what a trait is "for" -- what job it does, why it stuck around instead of getting weeded out -- without anybody, including the animal, ever having that in mind. The beaver doesn't build the dam "in order to" do anything, but there's still a completely respectable answer to why the dam is that shape. He has some name for this, I can never remember it, but that's the idea. And I want the toast to run on it, not just nod at it: what I want to say is that he and Maya are right for each other for real reasons -- not that it's arbitrary -- while also not pretending anybody planned it or that some matchmaker or fate arranged it. That's the tightrope. My instinct is that the second I say "you two were made for each other," the devout half of the room hears God and his labmates hear me endorsing a designer; but if I overcorrect and say it's just a lucky accident, the whole toast turns into "could've been anybody," which is false and also a terrible wedding speech. Roughly half the guests are his grad-school friends and current lab (a few of whom will absolutely notice if I wave "function" or "purpose" around sloppily, and one of them is a philosopher of biology who has published on pretty much exactly this and will 100% come up to me afterward to say I've confused "there is a reason it works" with "someone had a reason" -- I'd like the speech to survive that conversation), and the other half are Maya's family, who are warm and fairly religious and don't know a ribosome from a rhododendron.

So: write me the toast. Maybe five or six minutes out loud, warm up front, one or two real stories I can slot in (they met because they got double-booked into the same hotel room at a conference, if that helps), and the conceptual part has to be load-bearing rather than a lecture -- it should be doing the work of holding the whole thing together. If at some point I have to choose between getting the philosophy exactly right and landing the room, tell me which line to sacrifice and what to say instead. And please don't have me say they "complete" each other, it's a cliche and I also just don't believe it.
