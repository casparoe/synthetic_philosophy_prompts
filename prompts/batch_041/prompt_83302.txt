In my graduate seminar on democratic theory, the unit on epistocracy reliably produces the same argumentative move, and I would like you to subject it to a proper audit. A colleague of mine — a democrat who is willing to grant the epistocrats their empirical premises — meets them with an analogy: the electorate, she says, is a measuring instrument. She tells the old fairground story in which a crowd is asked to guess the weight of an ox and, though the individual guesses scatter widely, the average of them (some tellings say the median) lands almost exactly on the true weight. Her gloss is that individual voters are ignorant and biased, but because their errors are numerous and independent they cancel, so the aggregate judgment about what to do tracks the truth better than any individual could, and better than a panel of experts whose blind spots are shared. The epistocrats' premise — that the typical voter is incompetent — is therefore true but irrelevant, and any scheme that gates the franchise on competence rests on a mistake. A colleague from computer science has lately sharpened the analogy for her: an electorate, he says, is an ensemble of weak learners, and averaging many decorrelated predictors beats the single strong one, so the crowd's competence is not a happy accident but a theorem.

Please give me a full audit, on the following lines. First, set out the correspondence: what plays the role of the ox's weight, of a single guess, of the aggregation rule, of the independence of the errors, and of the truth of the matter — and say, for each slot, whether the target supplies a genuine analogue or only a placeholder. Second, identify where the mapping breaks, and distinguish the breakpoints that are load-bearing for her conclusion from the ones that are merely picturesque. Third, say what the analogy quietly imports: in particular, what must be true of the object of judgment — a single, well-defined, independently checkable quantity — for the ox story to apply at all, and whether politics supplies such an object or whether its existence is precisely what is in dispute between her and the epistocrats. Fourth, assess the sharpened version: does recasting the crowd as an ensemble of weak learners repair any of the breakpoints, or does it inherit them and add fresh ones (correlated error, the question of who fixes the loss function, the absence of a held-out test set, the fact that the predictors are also the predicted and have preferences about the output)? Fifth, tell me whether her conclusion survives the audit or whether the analogy was doing all the work; and if a better analogy is available for the point she wants to make, give it to me and say what it would have to get right.
