I have no philosophical training, so I apologize in advance if I'm reinventing something obvious, but there's a problem I've been circling for about a year, and I'd like a serious essay on it. It started as a joke among my friends about how a good salesperson never lies and never pressures you, and yet you walk out of the shop with something you didn't want, and the next day you can't find the moment where you lost the argument. From there I started noticing the same shape everywhere. A doctor I know admitted he presents identical survival statistics differently depending on the patient, because a nine-in-ten chance of living lands very differently from a one-in-ten chance of dying. A friend of mine got out of a bad relationship largely because another friend kept arranging for her to run into people who had left similar relationships and were thriving — every encounter true, every one deliberate. I've caught myself choosing the time of day, the lighting, and the order of my arguments when I want my partner to agree with me, which is not lying but is certainly not nothing. And then there's the slow kind, where no one decides anything at all: years of advertising and social pressure that gradually change what a whole generation even wants, so that the question of what someone would have chosen otherwise has no answer. What I can't work out is whether there's a real line here or whether I'm simply using the word manipulation for influence I disapprove of and persuasion for influence I approve of, in which case the whole distinction is rhetoric dressed up as ethics.

So I'd like an essay that takes a position on this controversy: is manipulation a distinct and specially objectionable kind of influence, or is the familiar contrast between manipulation and legitimate persuasion just a way of laundering our approval or disapproval of an outcome? If you think the distinction is real, say what it is a distinction in — the means the influencer uses, the aim the influencer has, the relationship between them, or something else — and then defend that answer against the cases above. If you think it isn't real, say what we lose by giving it up, because I suspect something is lost.

I'll put my own candidate on the table so that you have something concrete to attack or improve: an influence is manipulative just in case the influencer, imagining the other person afterwards learning exactly how the influence worked, would expect that discovery to undo the result or to damage the relationship. Test it. The doctor, I think, comes out as a manipulator on this test, which feels wrong to me; the charming salesperson comes out clean, which feels right; the friend who engineered the coincidences is a genuine puzzle, because I believe she would cheerfully tell my other friend exactly what she did, and yet it still feels as though something was done to her rather than offered to her; and the slow generational case seems to slip past the test entirely, which may mean the test is looking in the wrong place. Tell me what it gets wrong and how you would fix it, or why it cannot be fixed. I would rather you reason it out from scratch than summarize what famous writers have said, and please don't hedge: end with a verdict, then give the strongest objection to your own verdict and explain why it doesn't sink you.
