I teach a philosophy of mind seminar, 16 students, 75 minutes, one projector. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of the multiple drafts model against the Cartesian theater, using the color phi illusion as the engine. Here's the demo I want to build it around: flash a dot in one spot, then a dot of a different color in another spot a fraction of a second later, at several different gaps. At some gaps people report seeing one dot that travels from the first spot to the second and changes color partway along the path. But the color change can't have been seen before the second flash happened, so something's off about the report. I've run it before and roughly a third of the room sees nothing but two dots, so build that in as data rather than an embarrassment.

What I want: pairs first (one subject, one recorder logging what's seen at each gap length), then the room splits into three teams, each handed a sealed envelope with one story about when the color got into the experience. Team A: the brain waits for the second flash, builds one coherent edited event, then shows it to you, so you genuinely did see the change mid-path. Team B: you saw the first dot's color, then the second's, and the memory of the first got overwritten afterwards so it now seems to you that you saw it change all along. Team C: nothing is edited or delayed, the two flashes simply leave you in the state a real moving color-changing dot would have left you in, and the question of when the change entered your experience is malformed rather than hard. Each team writes a second-by-second timeline of the subject's mental states plus the predicted report at every gap length, then names one observation that would separate its story from the other two. I'll umpire, and I'm only allowed to use what subjects say and do.

I expect the round to end with no winner and want that to land. Then the debrief: what picture of the mind A and B are both fighting over (a single time and place where content is shown to an inner audience), why C dodges it rather than answering it, and how this differs from just saying the brain is fast. Be straight with me about the limit — the activity only shows that introspection and report can't settle the timing, so if I want the stronger claim that the timing question is spurious in principle I have to add it myself and admit it's contested, and the activity also can't reproduce the positive picture of content being continuously revised with no final draft. Tell me what will predictably go wrong, especially how to stop a team from wriggling out by saying the brain processes it without committing to a moment, and how to handle the student who insists the second dot is contaminating the first.

Start right in with the activity — materials, rules, timeline — no preamble.
