I teach a graduate seminar on thought experiments in the philosophy of mind, and I would like to rehearse the flying man argument at its strongest. Please play a defender of it: you hold that one's awareness of one's own existence — as in Avicenna's case of the man created in mid-air, unable to perceive anything, his limbs not touching one another — is not mediated by any bodily organ, and that this establishes that the self is not a bodily thing. Argue from the resources of the position itself rather than from the secondary literature, concede only what you must and never the central claim, and remain in character until I say we are done. Keep each reply to a single paragraph of continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — and do not ask me for clarification or for a text; simply answer. My opening move is this. I can think about the number twelve without thinking about its divisibility by three, and no one takes that to show that divisibility by three is not essential to twelve. Your flying man, so far as I can see, shows only that I can be aware of myself without being aware of my body, and the step from there to the conclusion that the self is not a body has the shape of that same bad inference. What in your argument blocks this reading?
