Aristotle's remarks about money in the Nicomachean Ethics and in the Politics do not sit easily together. In the Ethics, the analysis of justice in exchange turns on commensurability: a house and a pair of shoes cannot be equated directly, so a common measure has to be introduced by agreement, and money is that measure, standing in for need and making reciprocal exchange, and with it a kind of justice, possible. In the Politics, the art of acquisition is divided into a form bounded by the household, which stops at the needs it serves, and an unlimited form that seeks money for its own sake and admits of no natural terminus; lending at interest is singled out as the worst case, because it makes money generate money, which is money used against its own function. One text therefore treats money as the condition of just exchange, and the other treats the pursuit of it as the paradigm of an unnatural and limitless activity.

I want the two accounts compared directly rather than filed under a general remark about Aristotle being ambivalent toward commerce. Test this hypothesis against both passages: that they are consistent once money is separated into money as a measure of need, which is bounded because each exchange aims only at restoring equality, and money as an object of pursuit, which is unbounded because it has no target outside itself. The reconciliation has to outperform the deflationary alternative, on which Aristotle merely approves of acquisition in its proper place and condemns it out of place. It also has to survive a specific worry: if money in the Ethics is conventional through and through, it is unclear what resources that account leaves for calling any use of it unnatural.

Give me a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: the sharpest statement of the tension; the best case for the reconciliation just sketched, and why it beats the deflationary alternative; the strongest residue that the reconciliation cannot absorb; the single distinction that is doing the most work; and what would have to be true for the tension to be not merely unresolved but fatal to Aristotle's position.
