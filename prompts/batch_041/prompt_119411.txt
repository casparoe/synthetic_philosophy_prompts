Terri Schiavo spent the last fifteen years of her life in what her doctors diagnosed as a persistent vegetative state following a 1990 cardiac arrest, a diagnosis her parents contested. In 1998 her husband petitioned to have her feeding tube removed, testifying that she had said, in ordinary conversation years earlier, that she would not want to be kept alive by artificial means; her parents opposed him. Over the next seven years the dispute ran through the Florida courts, which found clear and convincing evidence of her wishes; through the Florida legislature, which passed a law permitting the governor to order the tube reinserted; through Congress, which passed a law in March 2005 granting the federal courts jurisdiction over the case; and through the federal courts, which declined to order reinsertion. The tube was removed for the last time, and she died on 31 March 2005. Take these facts as given rather than revisiting them.

Analyze the episode through one theory of why advance directives have authority: the view that a person's earlier, competent self has a claim to govern her later, incompetent self, because the earlier self's autonomy reaches forward, and because letting the later self's present contentment, or the state's interest in preserving life, override her considered wishes would deny her authorship of her own life. Call this the precedent-autonomy view. Its rival holds that only the present self's experiential interests count, or that the state may override both.

I want the mapping run in both directions. What exactly is the earlier self's claim supposed to be — an exercise of autonomy, a promise to herself, a property right in her future body, a device for protecting the interests that gave her life its shape — and which of these does the view actually need? The case turned on remarks in conversation, not on a document. If the authority comes from the earlier self's autonomy, what work is the written directive doing, and what does its absence here reveal about what was really carrying the argument: evidentiary standards, family testimony, the allocation of decision-making authority?

Then the harder questions. First, the earlier self's judgment is a prediction about a state she has never experienced, while the later self's reported contentment may be the product of adaptation; say whether these two distortions compound or cancel, and which way the view should lean. Second, the view is silent about institutions: the Florida legislature and Congress both asserted authority over the decision, and the theory offers no account of why the earlier self should outrank a democratic body's judgment about the preservation of life. Is that a gap, or does it show the view was never addressed to the question the case actually raised? Third, what theory of personal identity does the view presuppose, and what becomes of it if one denies that identity is what matters, holding instead that what matters is psychological connectedness? Fourth, the counterfactual: had a signed directive existed, what would the view predict, and is that prediction borne out by the way this dispute actually ran?

Close by saying what the case shows about the view — confirmation, embarrassment, or an episode it never really reached — and whether the rival view fits the outcome better than the precedent-autonomy view does. Work the analysis out on this case rather than summarizing the surrounding literature, and be explicit about the points where the theory goes quiet.
