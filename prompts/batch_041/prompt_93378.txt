For the last three weeks my reading group has been working through the repugnant conclusion, and on Tuesday a colleague put up on the board a version of the argument that she says is stronger than the familiar one, because it never asks us to accept anything as contentious as the claim that adding happy people is, by itself, an improvement. I copied her steps down as faithfully as I could, with her numbers, and I have been staring at them since. I cannot accept where they lead, and I would rather not give up either her first step or the transitivity of better than, but I have not been able to identify which move to drop or what dropping it would cost me. I would be grateful for a diagnosis, and, if the diagnosis is that one of the steps I want to keep has to go, for an honest account of the price.

Her worlds are these. 100 is a superb level of wellbeing, 80 a plainly good one, and 5 a level at which a life is barely worth living. A is a world of ten billion people, all at 100. A+ is A with ten billion further people added, all at 5, with no one else affected. B is a world of twenty billion people, all at 80. Her argument runs as follows. (1) Mere addition: A+ is not worse than A, since everyone in A is unaffected and the additional ten billion have lives that are worth living. (2) Same-number comparison: of two worlds containing the same number of people, the one with the higher average wellbeing is better; B and A+ both contain twenty billion people, and B's average of 80 exceeds A+'s 52.5, so B is better than A+. She adds that B also beats A+ on total wellbeing, on the lot of the worst off, and on equality, so she cannot see how it could fail to be better. (3) Better than is transitive, so B is better than A. (4) Iterate: take the current uniform world — twenty billion at 80 — and add twenty billion more at 5, giving a mixed world whose average is 42.5; by (2) a uniform world of forty billion at 70 is better than that mixed world, and by (1) the addition was not a worsening, so by (3) forty billion at 70 is better than twenty billion at 80. Since the new uniform level can always be chosen below the old one and above the mixed average, and the mixed average is always above 5, the operation can be repeated indefinitely: each round doubles the population and lowers the uniform level, so after n rounds we have a world of ten billion times two to the n people, all at some level approaching 5, and by (3) every such world is better than A. (5) Pass to the limit: the sequence converges on a world of astronomically many people, every one of them at 5, and by (3) that world is better than A.

I keep suspecting step (5), but I cannot turn the suspicion into an argument, since she could simply stop at a very large finite round and the result would be repugnant enough already. So: which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to? If the answer is (1), I want to know exactly what I am then forced to say about A+ and A, and whether I can still say anything about why the world at the end is bad.
