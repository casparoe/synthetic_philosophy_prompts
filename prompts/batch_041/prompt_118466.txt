I run grant selection for a small foundation that funds long-horizon research, and three or four times a year a proposal rests its case on a self-location argument rather than on evidence about the world: we should expect to be typical observers, or we should expect to be in the branch of reality that contains the most observers, therefore fund this. I can follow the terms self-sampling and self-indication, and I know that one of them turns on how the reference class is drawn while the other does not, but I cannot reliably tell which one is doing the work in a given proposal, and I am not willing to hand the board a recommendation that amounts to "it depends which assumption you accept." Write me a procedure I can run in an afternoon on each such proposal: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a rejection, and where it hands the decision to the board because the dispute is about values rather than inference. It has to be usable by someone whose probability training stops at an introductory course, and it should mark which of its steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of procedure. Run it on three proposals: one arguing that because we are probably typical observers we should expect to be near the end of the human story and should therefore reorder our priorities toward near-term work; one whose entire case is that our existence is itself evidence for the cosmology containing vastly more observers, so that we should commit to that cosmology without waiting for the physics to settle it; and one that discounts a class of catastrophes on the grounds that no one would have survived to record them. At least one of the three should come out against the proposal in a way I will not enjoy, and tell me what would change the verdict — which consideration or counterargument would flip it, and where the reasoning is most likely to fail.
