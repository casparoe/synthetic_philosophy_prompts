In a seminar on historical memory, a colleague defended the project of ranking historical atrocities by analogy with public-health burden-of-disease metrics. On her picture, comparing evils is like computing disability-adjusted life years: each catastrophe is a condition, its victims are patients, and once we assign a severity weight to the harm of each and multiply by its extent, we can compare and even aggregate across cases — and we should, because the same reasoning that lets health agencies rank diseases by burden shows that the objection that suffering cannot be compared is sentimentalism rather than a serious obstacle. I want an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on her politics. First give me the correspondence explicitly, as a table: what plays the role of the patient, the condition, the severity weight, the duration, the discount rate, the panel that fixes the weights, the aggregate burden, and the decision the estimate is meant to inform. Then tell me where the mapping breaks — which features of the public-health apparatus have no counterpart in the atrocity case, and which of these breaks are load-bearing for her conclusion that ranking is both possible and obligatory, rather than merely picturesque. I am especially interested in three places where I suspect the analogy does illicit work: the assumption that harms are commensurable and additive across persons; the move from a descriptive burden estimate to a normative ranking that could guide resource allocation or recognition; and the appeal to a procedure for eliciting weights as a way of settling what the severity of an atrocity is. For each, say whether the disanalogy defeats her conclusion, merely complicates it, or can be repaired from within the analogy. Finally, if the analogy cannot carry the conclusion, say what a better analogy would be and what that replacement would license.
