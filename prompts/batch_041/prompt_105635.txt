I'm a final-year student in a joint economics-and-philosophy seminar on intergenerational ethics, and next week I present my term paper on the social discount rate. My thesis is that "pure time preference" -- a discount on the value of future welfare as such, once uncertainty about the future and the diminishing marginal utility of consumption have been set aside -- is not merely ethically contestable but conceptually empty: it names whatever residual is left once every legitimate ground for discounting has been subtracted, and I argue there is nothing left for it to name. I go on to claim that the real fault line in the climate-economics literature runs not between high and low discount rates but between discounting consumption and discounting welfare, and that most of the disagreement is a confusion of the two.

The room is mostly PhD economists who build integrated-assessment models, treat the Ramsey equation as the organising frame, and are allergic to being told their parameter choices are unethical; there are also two philosophers, one a totalist about population ethics and one who works on person-affecting intuitions and the non-identity problem. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections I will actually get, phrased the way each of these people would phrase them, ordered by how much damage they would do, each with a short reply and a note on where the reply is softest. Tell me what to concede up front, which objection I should genuinely be worried about, and the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
