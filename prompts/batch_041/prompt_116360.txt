Me and my friend got into a long arguement last night about embedded agency and I need a tiebreaker, because I'm pretty sure I won.

My take: the whole "embedded agent" problem is basically a compute problem. Sure, if an agent is part of the world it's trying to model, then its self-model is going to be lossy — but that's just because no machine has enough cycles to fully simulate itself, right? So the fix is obvious: bigger computer, let it run a compressed model of itself inside itself, iterate until the self-model error is small enough that it can act on it the same way it acts on its model of everything else. Eventually you just brute-force the thing down to nothing.

My friend says no, it's not a resource problem at all. He says an agent that reasons about its own decision procedure can hit a wall where it can't use its own reasoning to establish that its own reasoning is trustworthy, and that adding compute doesn't move that wall an inch, because the bottleneck is what the agent can establish about itself, not how fast it can crunch. He also claimed something that sounds backwards to me: that a smarter, more powerful reasoner can end up doing worse than a dumber one in these setups, because the smarter one can establish more facts about itself and that extra self-knowledge can actually cost it options. He kept citing some theorem starting with L that I didn't catch the name of. I told him that's all self-referential toy-logic stuff that completly misses the point for real agents that learn and act in a world.

So who's actually right here, and if it's him, can you lay out the real obstacle without it just collapsing into "Godel, therefore spooky"? And what do I have to give up to accept his side — does it really mean a real agent can never fully trust its own future reasoning, and if so, how does anything embedded (us included) manage to get by?
