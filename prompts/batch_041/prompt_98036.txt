I'm a novelist — my last book was about a woman who gradually stops speaking, so this is kind of my territory — and I've somehow ended up as the lay/community member on my local hospital's clinical ethics committee, which mostly means I read the packet and ask the naive questions. Next Thursday we have a genuinely hard one, and the chair has asked me to draft the opinion that goes in the minutes and gets circulated to the attending, the family, and the trust's legal people. Here's the case. A 44-year-old man, three years out from a brainstem stroke, is in what the neurologists call a locked-in state: he can blink and move his eyes, that's about it. For the last eight months he's been using one of those eye-tracking/EEG hybrid spelling rigs, and after a long calibration the team says he can reliably answer yes/no questions and pick from a short menu of phrases. His accuracy on the validation battery sits around 70-75%, better on some question types than others, and nobody can tell whether the errors are random noise or the interface misreading him. Two weeks ago, across three seperate sessions, he used it to say he wants the ventilator withdrawn. He'd never said anything like that before, and his wife says it isn't him talking, it's the machine. His adult daughter, who's the health-care proxy, says the exact opposite — that he's been trying to say this for a year. The treating team is split down the middle and one of the nurses has started refusing to sit in on the weaning conversations.

The thing I keep circling is that we built a device that reports what he wants and then we don't believe it, which is either exactly right or a kind of horror and I honestly don't know which. What I need from you is the draft opinion itself, not a general essay: a clear recommendation (do we honor the request, decline it, honor it with conditions, or go get more evidence first — and on what timeline), the reasoning laid out in a form the attending and the lawyers can actually argue with, and an honest statement of the strongest case against whatever we recommend, written so the losing side would recognize themselves in it. Two things my colleagues have floated that I'd like you to take seriously and either adopt, adapt, or kill outright: one of the neurologists wants a rule she calls asymmetric deference — we defer to whatever the device says only when it lines up with things he said before the stroke, and otherwise we discount it; and our chair thinks any output from the device should be treated like testimony from a witness with a known unreliability problem, so it needs corroboration before it can carry weight, except she can't tell me what corroboration would even look like in a man who can only move his eyes. Please be concrete about the metaphysics without giving me a philosophy lecture — the family will read this, and so will the papers, and I'd rather not be the novelist who turned a man's death into a seminar.
