While reporting a piece on risk and prediction, I watched two of my sources — a former professional poker player and an economist — argue about a mutual acquaintance who recently staked his entire savings on a single investment with a positive expected return and a real chance of total loss. The economist said the man had acted rationally, since the investment had positive expected value and a rational agent maximizes expected value; declining it was leaving value on the table. The poker player said that is the wrong benchmark: what matters is the expected growth rate of wealth, not its expected value, and the right stake is the one that maximizes that growth. Betting everything is a mistake even on a favorable bet, she said, because ruin is permanent and you live the typical path, not the average over imaginary copies of you. The economist replied that the growth-rate rule just is maximizing expected log wealth — adopting log utility — and nothing makes log uniquely rational; choose another utility function and a different stake follows, so calling the all-in bet irrational begs the question. She answered that this is not a taste in utility functions but the strategy that overtakes every other fixed fraction in the long run, and that his averaging over an ensemble of hypothetical gamblers is exactly what a person who lives one life should refuse; and since ruin cannot be undone, it is a boundary the growth argument treats as special rather than merely a risk she happens to dislike. He countered that the almost-sure argument applies only when the same favorable bet is repeated many times at a fixed fraction — for a genuinely one-shot gamble it has no purchase — and that even under repetition it establishes only that growth is maximized, which you must already care about. He added that log utility would spurn even an overwhelmingly favorable bet carrying the least chance of total ruin, which he takes as a reductio.

Please adjudicate move by move: say where each of them is right, where each overreaches, and which claims are true as a matter of decision theory rather than of preference. I especially want to know whether this is a genuine clash about rationality or a disguised disagreement about what to value, and what both of them leave out.
