I write a science-and-ideas column and want to close an issue on risk and rationality with a short poem that does real philosophical work rather than illustrating a slogan. The idea I want it to carry is the one that makes expected-value reasoning look either heroic or unhinged: because expected value multiplies probability by payoff, an action with a minuscule chance of an enormous good can outrank a sure, modest one — so a stranger offering, say, an astronomically large reward on a one-in-a-billion chance in exchange for everything you have can, by the maximizer's own arithmetic, appear to be offering a bargain, and the same logic seems to license ruinous wagers without end. The usual protest is that this cannot be right, but it is surprisingly hard to make the protest without simply refusing to run the numbers.

Write a pantoum of four quatrains that stages this. Let one voice be the maximizer and one the skeptic; the interlocking repetitions should enact how the argument returns no matter how often it is answered, and the closing stanza, which by the form's rule must loop back to the poem's opening line, should make the reader feel the trap rather than hear a verdict. It should be exact enough that someone who has wrestled with the problem recognizes the moves — the vanishing probability, the unbounded payoff, the objection that the reasoning proves too much — and clear enough for a reader who has not met it. Do not name any philosopher or cite anything.
