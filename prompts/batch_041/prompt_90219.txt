I'm writing a term paper for a seminar on rational choice and decision theory, and I want to read WarGames through Hofstadter's idea of superrationality. In the film the crisis ends when Falken has the WOPR machine play tic-tac-toe against itself until it grasps the futility of a game with no winner, after which it cycles through its nuclear-war scenarios and concludes that the only way not to lose is to refuse to play — and the machine, notably, is both sides of that game. Superrationality, as Hofstadter presents it, holds that agents who know their reasoning is identical will each cooperate in a prisoner's dilemma, since whatever one of them works out the other will work out too. I want a reading that takes both the film and the idea seriously. Does WarGames genuinely dramatize superrationality, or does it buy its ending by collapsing the two adversaries into one machine, which is exactly the identical-reasoner premise the theory needs and real deterrence never has? Say where the parallel holds, whether the tic-tac-toe sequence is a good picture of a game whose rational solution is not to play, and whether a machine playing itself models the superrational assumption or merely one agent solving one problem. Then say where the film resists the reading: the superpowers are not identical agents, their payoffs are not symmetric, and the resolution may be a deus ex machina rather than a decision-theoretic result. Tell me finally whether the reading needs anything like Newcomb's problem or the dispute between evidential and causal decision theory, or whether the identical-agents premise is doing all the work — and end by saying what the film shows about the limits of superrationality that the theory's own examples do not. Which scenes should I build the paper around?
