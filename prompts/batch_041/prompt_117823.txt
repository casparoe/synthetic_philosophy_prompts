Okay, so I've been chewing on this for months and I want it taken apart properly, not agreed with. Here's the position I've ended up at, and I know its a bit odd.

When a community's memory of its own past and the archival or historical record disagree, I don't think "which one is more accurate" is the question that should decide it. Those are two seperate questions and I think people run them together: whether an account is *about* us — picks out our ancestors, our continuity, our past — and whether it is *true of* us. And I hold that when the two come apart, and no further evidence can settle it, reference has priority over accuracy. A history you can't recognize as yours can't do the work of being your past, even if every sentence in it is better evidenced. So when the record contradicts a community's own memory about that community, the record should be one voice among others, not the court of appeal.

What I want from you: what would count against this. Not the cheap stuff — obviously memory is unreliable and people confabulate, I'm not denying any of that. I want the cases, arguments and findings that should move someone who already grants all of it. Sort them by how decisive they'd be, from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which of them a sympathetic defender would concede as counting against them rather than dismiss as a critic's talking point.

Some things I'd especially like you to press on:

- Recognition looks cheap and manufacturable. States, churches and ad agencies have spent centuries making people recognize a flattering or useful past as their own. If recognition can be produced on demand, does it still carry the normative weight I'm giving it, or have I just handed the win to whoever owns the printing press? Is there a version of the view that survives that, and what does it cost?
- The problem of whose memory. Communities aren't uniform; the memory a community recognizes is usually the memory of its loudest or most powerful part, and the parts that lost often have nothing but the record. Does my view give those people anything or does it bury them? If I bolt on a clause like "the memory of the marginalized takes priority," have I kept my view or quietly swapped it for another one?
- Whether the distinction even holds. Memory is a kind of record — testimony, oral history, commemorations — the archive is a kind of memory with better storage, and a lot of what gets called community memory is an earlier archival claim that caught on. If the two poles bleed into each other, what should the view be restated as?
- What the view commits me to outside the past. If memory governs, what do I say about courts, truth commissions, history departments, museums? I suspect I'm committed to something about how those should run, and I'd like to know if it's something I'd flinch at.
- Whether I've hedged the thing into unfalsifiability. If I say recognition wins but then carve out exceptions whenever recognition is manufactured or exclusionary, is there any case left that would refute me? If not, is that a defect in the view or in my expectations for views like this? And what does my view predict that a plain accuracy-first view doesn't — if the answer is nothing, say so.

I'll say up front what I'd find most persuasive: a case where a community's memory is not merely inaccurate but is the mechanism by which something is kept hidden, where the memory does the work a lie would do. If my view has nothing to say about that case, that's where I'd drop it.

Also, tell me how confident you are in what you're claiming, and single out the one point you're least sure about.
