I'm a second-year PhD student in comparative politics, working on how anti-corruption norms get exported, and I'm stuck on a case-analysis chapter. The case is the Tammany Hall machine in New York: the Tweed Ring era in the 1860s and 70s, then the later machine that George Washington Plunkitt belonged to, and the honest-graft defense the machine men gave when the good-government reformers came after them. The machine handed out jobs, coal, and legal help to Irish and Italian immigrants and got votes and money back; Plunkitt drew a line between what he called honest graft, which was getting in early on a deal you'd heard about, and dishonest graft, which was just stealing, and he and the other defenders basically said the reformers were imposing a Protestant clean-government standard on a community that ran on reciprocity and loyalty. I don't need the history, I know it. What I want is the case read through clientelism theory and the anthropology of the gift: Mauss on the gift as something that creates an ongoing obligation rather than closing a transaction, and the standard distinction in the corruption literature between market corruption, where the favor goes to whoever pays, and parochial corruption, where the transfer tracks kinship, ethnicity, and loyalty. Map the case onto that: who the players are, what's actually being exchanged and what obligations it creates, and whether Plunkitt's honest/dishonest line survives once you run it through the theory, or whether its just the machine telling itself a flattering story.

Then the part I actually care about, where the theory misdescribes things. I've been messing around with a distinction I'm not sure holds up, between what I call a dyadic gift, which creates an obligation between two specific people, Plunkitt helping a constituent, and a positional gift, which buys access to an office or a role rather than to a person, paying the machine rather than paying Plunkitt. Test that against the Tammany material: does it do any work, or does it just restate the parochial/market line in new words? And the normative end: does the case give any comfort to the relativist line that corruption-talk is just Western standards being universalized, or does the fact that the machines own defenders needed a moral vocabulary like honest graft to defend themselves show the universalism claim is confused? I want the case testing the theory in both directions, and I want you to say plainly where clientelism theory goes quiet and stops helping on this one. No citations needed, just the analysis.
