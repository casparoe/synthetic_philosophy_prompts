You are a decision theorist with a specialist's command of dynamic choice under imperfect recall — the family of problems in which an agent cannot tell which of her own decision nodes she is standing at, and in which the plan that is best ex ante and the action that is best at a node pull apart. I have been arguing about this with friends in a reading group: one of them insists that the whole puzzle is an artefact of the standard driving setup and of a silly assumption that a person could not simply improve her own memory, and I want a case that puts that claim under pressure. So please design a problem to order and then show me that it does what I am asking. Think it through carefully, and before you write the case up, sketch two or three candidate structures and pick the one that best satisfies the constraints; a case that stipulates the verdicts rather than generating them from its own structure is worthless to me.

Here is the target. Build a realistic, mundane scenario with three decision points, exactly two of which the agent cannot distinguish from one another; the third must be recognizable to her through an ordinary, non-magical cue — a sign, a colour, a sound, a face — nothing that requires a special device. The agent is fully lucid, not drugged, not hypnotized, and under no exotic affliction: her inability to tell the two points apart must fall out of the repetitive or undifferentiated character of the situation itself, and would affect anyone in her shoes. Since she cannot tell the two apart, whatever she does at one she does at the other. The stakes should be the sort of thing an ordinary adult actually cares about — money, time, mild social embarrassment — and the numbers must be your own: do not lift the numbers from the driving case, and if your case turns out to be a mere rescaling of it, say so. Assume she can randomise as she likes. The case must satisfy four conditions: (i) the ex ante optimal strategy requires randomising at the two indistinguishable points; (ii) at those points the plan calls for an action that, given the plan, is strictly worse than its alternative — a locally dominated action; (iii) the ex ante value of the optimal strategy is strictly greater than the value obtained by the chooser who reasons node by node and never knowingly takes the worse option at a node; and (iv) the agent's probability of being at one or other of the indistinguishable points must not be stipulated — it is generated by her own strategy, so state explicitly how it is fixed and check that it is well defined.

Once the case is built, work it. State the ex ante optimal strategy, including the exact randomisation probability, its value, and the value the node-by-node chooser obtains; demonstrate that the prescribed action at the indistinguishable points is dominated given the plan; and then walk through the three standard responses — the ex ante planner, the node-by-node chooser, and the one who treats the plan as binding and simply sticks to it. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens if I remove the cue that makes the third point recognizable. Then suppose the agent can buy back her recall cheaply — a tally, a note, a colleague's reminder — at some price: work out the highest price at which buying it is worth her while, and say whether the availability of that option shows the puzzle to be an artefact of an artificial assumption about memory. Say also whether the node-by-node chooser's reasoning at the indistinguishable points needs a probability over where she is, and why or why not. No literature review, no citations, no appeal to who said what — I want the construction and the reasoning. Keep it tight, and end with a single final line in exactly this form: Verdict: p = <optimal randomisation probability>, ex ante value = <...>, node-by-node value = <...>, gap = <...>, maximum worthwhile price of recall = <...>.
