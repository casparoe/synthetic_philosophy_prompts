U.S. federal courts follow a gatekeeping standard for expert testimony, set out in Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals (1993): scientific testimony is admissible only if it rests on a reliable methodology, and judges are told to weigh whether the theory can be and has been tested, whether it has a known error rate, whether it has been peer reviewed, whether it is generally accepted in the relevant scientific community, and whether the research was conducted independently of the litigation. The factors pull in different directions. Testability and error rate sound like a falsificationist criterion of scientific status; general acceptance sounds like a sociological or consensus criterion; and the standard has since been extended to non-scientific expert testimony, such as clinical or engineering judgment, that need not be falsifiable at all. What conception of scientific knowledge, or of epistemic warrant, does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say which rival accounts of demarcation it rules out and which it merely fits, judge whether its criteria are genuinely consistent or quietly in tension, and explain what would have to change in the doctrine if a court adopted falsificationism as the single criterion — or, instead, treated general acceptance as sufficient by itself.
