Reading group thing I can't get out of my head, and I need someone to find me the escape hatch. I wrote this up as a mini-argument for a seminar and now I think it proves way too much, but I can't locate the bad step.

(1) When we say someone prefers A to B, all we ever have to go on is that she chose A over B when both were on the table. The whole point of the revealed-preference program — Samuelson's move, the thing that got demand theory away from unmeasurable mental states — is that preference is what choice shows.
(2) So if she picks A over B, she prefers A to B. Not "her choice is evidence for a preference," but: her choosing A is what her preferring A consists in.
(3) "Utility" is then just bookkeeping for those rankings. A utility function represents the choice pattern, and any increasing transform of it represents the exact same pattern, so it's the same facts written differently.
(4) So there's no fact about someone's utility that her choices could get wrong. You can't be mistaken about your own ranking; whatever you do is your ranking.
(5) My uncle has smoked for thirty years, says he wants to quit, has tried and failed. By (4), that want isn't a real preference — it isn't revealed in any choice he makes, so it's noise, or at best it's a preference he doesn't have.
(6) So there's no sense in which the smoking makes him worse off by his own lights. His lights are his choices, and his choices are the smoking.
(7) Same for the guy who takes $50 now over $100 in a year, and same for anyone whose stated preferences and behavior come apart: behavior wins, by definition.
(8) So no policy can make a person better off against her choices, paternalism can never be justified on welfare grounds, and "she's hurting herself" is never a reason for anything.
(9) And nothing can ever count as a counterexample, because every awkward case gets redescribed — the smoker has "a different preference at a different time," the inconsistent chooser has "no well-defined preference" — so the theory can never be wrong.

(8) and (9) are the parts I think are absurd. But here's my problem: I *like* (1) and (2), they're what makes the whole approach attractive to me (no spooky inner states, no comparing feelings across people), and (9) looks like it just follows from them, which is what worries me. I've thought about this alot. If I give up (2) and say a preference is a mental state that choice merely *reveals*, then I've let the spooky stuff back in, and I owe an account of what a preference is when the person never acts on it, and how anyone could ever know it.

So: which step do I drop, why did it look fine, and what does dropping it cost? I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than (8), so price it for me. Also there's a wrinkle I keep noticing — "utility" in expected-utility theory is cardinal and constrains risk-taking in a way that isn't just a ranking, so does that break (3) on its own, or is it a side issue?
