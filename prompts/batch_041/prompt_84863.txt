I've fallen down a rabbit hole on the liberty question — not the Berlin positive/negative split, the other one, where the republicans (Pettit, Skinner, that crowd) say freedom is non-domination rather than non-interference. I've mostly talked myself into it, and the cases do real work on me: the slave with a kind master who never actually interferes is still unfree; the employee whose boss could fire her for nothing is unfree even if he never would; and the lived texture of it seems right, the constant background math about what the powerful person will tolerate, the deference, the not-quite-meeting-their-eye thing. Set against the Berlin-style picture where the kindly master's slave is free so long as the interference never actually shows up, it looks like a straight upgrade.

But I want to stress-test it instead of just nodding along, so: what would actually count against the claim that liberty just is non-domination? I want the specific cases, intuitions, and structural arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd really be — and especially the ones a committed republican would themselves have to concede count against them, not just the stuff critics lob from outside. Some things I'm already half-aware of that I want properly assessed. First, whether non-domination secretly collapses into negative liberty plus a probability clause — robust non-interference with a modal gloss — which would make it a refinement of the thing it claims to replace rather than a third concept. Second, the old triadic-analysis point (freedom is always freedom of some agent from some constraint to do something; I associate it with MacCallum but dont quote me), which makes me wonder if "non-domination" is just a new entry in the constraint slot and the whole novelty is rhetorical. Third, domination-without-a-dominator: someone stuck under an impersonal market, an algorithm, or a bureaucracy no particular person controls — if that isn't unfreedom the view seems to miss a lot, and if it is, the agent-focused machinery starts looking optional. Fourth, power that's rule-bound and accountable — the legal guardian, the well-disciplined agency, the HOA with a strict but fair covenant process — is that non-arbitrary enough to be no domination at all, and does that match what we'd actually say about the people living under it? And fifth, unfalsifiability: the claim is partly about what our concept of freedom is, and if every apparent counterexample gets redescribed as "well they were really dominated in some subtle way," I want to know whether that's a genuine insight or a rescue move that's eaten the whole test.

Rank the defeaters from fatal down to merely awkward, tell me which would force a real revision versus a footnote, and be honest about whether the position as usually stated is well insulated — and if it is, whether that's a bug or just what a conceptual claim about freedom is doomed to look like. What would move me most personally is one clean case where someone is plainly dominated and we still don't want to call them unfree, or the reverse, since if the concept can't track those I'll give it up. No reading lists please, just the actual cases and the arguments.
