I teach a graduate seminar on the ethics of memory, and I am preparing a session on the long-running dispute between two broad positions. The first, associated with Avishai Margalit, holds that a community's shared memory of what it has suffered and what it has done is constitutive of the ethical relations that bind its members — that a duty to remember is a genuine obligation rather than a pious sentiment, though a puzzling one, since it appears to be owed to people who are no longer alive to be wronged or benefited. The second, pressed by David Rieff, holds that the case for forgetting has been badly understated: that collective memory is at least as often a machine for perpetuating grievance as a resource for justice, and that a well-ordered polity may sometimes have sound reason to let certain things fade rather than insist on their preservation. My students tend to reduce this to a tidy opposition between the pious and the cynical, and I want a dialogue that dislodges their complacency.

So please write one, and let me specify what I am after. I want the familiar alignments deliberately inverted, so that neither speaker can fall back on a stock script. Picture a town where, four generations ago, one community perpetrated a massacre against another. Today it is the descendants of the perpetrators who are pressing to build a memorial and to institute an annual curriculum of remembrance — moved partly by genuine remorse, partly by a wish to be seen to have reckoned with the past — while a substantial part of the victims' descendants want the whole apparatus abandoned. They argue, among other things, that the commemorations have made a permanent identity out of their ancestors' suffering, that their community has built a good and ordinary life it does not wish to keep reliving, and that a duty owed to the dead cannot rightly be discharged by the living at the living's own expense. Let the two speakers be philosopher-advocates, one for remembrance and one for forgetting, equally well informed and equally serious, both aware of the inversion and prepared to reckon with it.

I want them to press one another on the particulars of this case rather than trade generalities, and to concede where they must. The defender of remembrance should have to say what grounds the duty once the dead cannot be wronged and the living decline the burden — and whether the duty attaches to the event, to the dead, or to the relation among the living who inherit it. The defender of forgetting should have to say why the commemorative wish of the perpetrators' descendants should not simply be honored as an act of repair, and what is genuinely lost when a community lets its past go quiet. Neither should be a strawman, and I would rather they not resolve the matter neatly. Something in the range of 1,200 to 1,800 words, please.
