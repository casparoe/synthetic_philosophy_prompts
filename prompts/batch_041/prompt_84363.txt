Alright, background on me: I write longform features — science and ideas, the kind where you spend three hours on the phone with a philosopher and then cut ninety percent of it. But our magazine runs a fiction issue every December and I always toss something in, and this year I've got a premise I can't shake and I keep failing to make it work on the page. So I figured I'd just hand it too you and see what you do with it.

It started with a piece I did years ago on parasitic wasps. Short version: there are reasons for things in the living world that don't belong to anybody. The wasp stings the caterpillar in exactly the spot that keeps the host fresh the longest — there's a reason for that, a genuinely good reason, and you can say it right out loud: so the larvae have something to eat when they hatch. But no wasp ever worked that out. Nobody's home. The reason is perfectly real, it just isn't anyones. No reasoner, only the reason. Some philosopher I interviewed once had a tidy name for the whole business and I've since lost it, which is annoying, but you get the shape of it.

That's the engine I want for the story. Not a lecture with characters standing around — a story where the reader keeps walking into purposes that have no owners, and it starts out as a neat bit of trivia and curdles into something worse. I want at least one scene where somebody pushes back hard, insists that if there's a reason then somebody, somewhere, has to be doing the reasoning, and the story does not rescue them from that. And it'd be great if by the end the reader isn't totally sure the protagonist's own reasons are made of anything different.

Couple of asks: keep it on this planet, no robots, no spaceships — I really don't want this collapsing into a sci-fi thing about AI. And please don't explain the concept back to me, I know the concept, I've been chewing on it for a decade. Just write the story. Say 1500-2500 words.
