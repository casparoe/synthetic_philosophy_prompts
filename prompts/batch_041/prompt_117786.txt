I'm giving a talk next month to a room that's half philosophers of science and half interpretability researchers, and before I lock the slides in I want somebody to audit the position I'm about to defend. I have a nagging feeling it doesn't hang together and I'd rather find out now than in front of the room. Here's the whole package:

(a) A mechanism is individuated by its causal structure -- the parts, and the activities those parts engage in -- and not by what it's for. Two systems can crank out the exact same output by different causal routes and be different mechanisms. That's not a defect in the individuation, its the point of it.

(b) So saying two things are the same mechanism means there's a structure-preserving map between their component-level causal organizations. Same mechanism is isomorphism at the right grain, not behavioral equivalence.

(c) What counts as the right grain, and where the mechanism starts and stops, isn't handed to us by nature. It's fixed by the explanatory question you're asking. Ask a different question, get a different but equally correct decomposition.

(d) Now the part I actually care about. I want to defend what the ML people call mechanistic anomaly detection: you inspect a model, you characterize the causal route it uses to produce its answers, and later you flag an input as anomalous when the answer is coming from a different route than the one you signed off on, even when the answer itself looks perfectly fine.

(e) The selling point is that this is a check on the mechanism rather then on the behavior. By construction the anomalous route is behaviorally indistinguishable from the endorsed one, so anything that only looks at inputs and outputs is blind to it.

(f) I'm a multiple-realizability guy. Same computation, different substrate, no difference -- silicon, neurons, doesn't matter. Substrate differences are never by themselves evidence of a different mechanism.

(g) And I think you can't find out what the parts of a mechanism are without intervening on it. You locate components by poking: knock something out, clamp it, wiggle it, see whether the phenomenon changes. Anything that makes no difference to the phenomenon isn't part of that mechanism.

Which of these are genuinely in tension, and which only look like they are? Put them in order of how bad the problem is, and for each one give me the cheapest fix and tell me what that fix costs me somewhere else in the list. If there's a single revision that would clean up more than any other, name it and say why. I honestly can't tell where the fault line runs, so don't read any hint into the order.
