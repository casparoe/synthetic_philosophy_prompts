Ok so I'm a grad student in a philosophy-of-mind-adjacent program, and for the past year I've been moonlighting as the "concept person" for a friend who works on animal welfare policy. The recurring question is which creatures we should actually be worried about -- the ones whose suffering we should weight heavily, versus the ones we count as sentient-on-paper but not really worth a lot of resources. We kept reaching for "cognitive capacity," and the more we reached, the more it seemed to just be a polite name for how much brain the thing has. So I sat down and wrote the argument below, trying to make the case that neural quantity is genuinely morally relevant, and now I think it proves way too much and I cant find the loose thread.

(1) A being's moral status is grounded in its capacity for morally relevant experience -- its capacity to suffer, to flourish, to have things go well or badly for it. (2) That capacity is realized in, and only in, the information-processing activity of a nervous system. (3) More neural processing power, other things equal, means more of that capacity: a system with more neurons, organized the same way, can represent more, discriminate more finely, integrate more, so it can have more morally relevant experience. (4) A being's moral status is proportional to how much morally relevant experience it is capable of. (5) So, other things equal, a being with more neurons has a higher moral status than one with fewer. (6) Humans differ from one another in neuron count, and there's nothing in the premises that privileges our species, so by (5) the ranking of beings by status is just the ranking by neural quantity. (7) So some humans have a lower moral status than some animals, and some humans have a higher moral status than other humans.

I'd like to keep (1) and (4) if I can -- without them I don't even know what would ground moral status at all. But (7) is grotesque, and it also drags me into empirical claims about specific animals that I don't want to have to defend. A friend says the problem is obviously the move from (5) to (6), that I'm smuggling a species-level fact into a within-species comparison, but that feels too easy -- (3) never says anything about species, and (5) is just (3) plus (4). So which step do I actually give up, why did it look fine when I wrote it, and what does giving it up cost me -- in particular, if I surrender (3), what stops the whole thing from sliding to "brain size is morally irrelevant, so nervous systems are too, so nothing grounds the difference between a cow and a rock"? Don't just hand me a fallacy name; two people have already said "equivocation" and neither told me what I'd be buying. And tell me what would change your mind about your diagnosis -- is there a reading on which the argument is actually valid and the only real question is which premise is cheapest to sacrifice? I'm not confident either way, and I'd rather know where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong than get a clean answer I can't use.
