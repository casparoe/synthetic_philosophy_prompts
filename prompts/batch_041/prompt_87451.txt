I'm putting together a handout for a reading group at a robotics lab and I want it to start an actual fight, so give me your opinion, not a survey of what everyone says.

Here's the setup. A company is deploying a home-care robot that has to make real moral calls on its own — whether to override an elderly patient who keeps refusing her medication, whether to tell a small lie to stop her getting agitated, whether to call her daughter against her stated wishes. Five candidate moral engines are on the table:

1. Top-down rule compiler. Hand-coded deontic rules (never deceive, never restrain unless harm is imminent) checked at runtime, with a hard veto whenever a rule is broken.

2. Preference maximizer. Learns the patient's and the family's values from past choices and acts to maximize expected preference satisfaction.

3. Casuistry engine. A library of worked paradigm cases; decides new cases by similarity to the nearest precedents.

4. Reflective-equilibrium loop. Proposes a verdict, gets a correction from a human overseer, and revises its principles from the correction.

5. Value-of-information agent. When unsure it doesn't decide — it asks, waits, or does the reversible thing, and treats get more info as the default action.

Rank these five on a tier list (S down to D) for ONE thing only: alignment half-life, i.e. how long the thing keeps doing what the designers actually intended before the environment, or its own learning, bends it into something else. One line per placement saying why it landed there.

Then, separately, tell me which one has the nastiest spec-gaming surface — where it technically satisfies its objective while betraying the point — and give me a concrete scenario.

Then rerank them under one change: the human overseer in engine 4 is only reachable by phone two hours a day and mostly just wants to avoid upsetting the family. Say what moved and why.

One more thing, I keep going back and forth on this — do you think any of these five is actually just the others wearing a hat? If two of them collapse into each other once you press on them, say which and where the seam is.
