Write a poem of roughly sixteen to twenty lines, in no fixed form, built around a single line that recurs as a refrain at least three times, the last of which closes the poem. The speaker is a convinced Berkeleian idealist, and the recurring line is the idealist's own thesis — that to be is to be perceived, so that nothing can be thought of as existing unconceived. I want the words of the refrain never to change and its sense to change anyway: the argument the speaker builds around it should be the master argument, that one cannot so much as frame the idea of a tree or a book existing unconceived, since the framing is one's own. By the refrain's final occurrence the reader should hear, in the very same words, the objection rather than the thesis — that this shows only that one cannot conceive of a thing while not conceiving of it, which is a fact about the conceiving and not about the thing, and that the argument therefore either proves a triviality or quietly assumes the identity of idea and object it was supposed to establish. Let the speaker remain unshaken to the end; do not let them concede the point in their own voice, and let the repetition do the work of exposing them. Someone who knows the argument should be able to point to the line where the slide happens, and someone who does not should still find it a real poem.
