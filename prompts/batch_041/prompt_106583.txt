I've been sitting in on a social choice reading group this term (my background is econ, not philosophy, so forgive me if I fumble the vocab) and I've come out the other side half-convinced of something I can't quite shake, so I want you to try and shake it for me. The thing I've landed on is roughly this: Harsanyi's aggregation theorem says that if you take each person's preferences to be rational in the expected-utility sense, and you ask society to rank risky prospects in a way that is itself rational in that same sense and that respects the things people unanimously prefer, then the social ranking has to come out as a weighted sum of individual utilities. Add one more thought, that behind the veil you're equally likely to turn out to be anybody, and the weights come out equal, i.e. plain old utilitarianism. So my position is that utilitarian aggregation isn't a taste or a tradition you opt into, it's the only coherent way to add people up once you accept that we're choosing under uncertainty and that nobody's preferences get to be simply ignored. What I'd like from you is an honest map of what would count against that.

Not just the famous grumbles that everyone repeats -- I want to know which premise is actually doing the work, which objections are fatal versus merely awkward, and what a committed Harsanyi person would themselves concede counts against them rather than only what the critics shout. A few things I keep going in circles on and would love untangled: (a) is the interpersonal comparability of utility something the theorem hands you or something it quietly assumes, because if it's assumed, isn't that the entire ballgame smuggled in at the door? (b) someone in the group insists that the moment the people disagree about the probabilities of the relevant events the whole thing falls apart and you get a unanimity that only looks like unanimity -- is that a real result or are they confused? (c) the ex ante versus ex post business, where ranking the lottery itself seems to favor a coin flip over handing the good to whoever would benefit most for sure, which to me feels plainly wrong but I can't articulate why. And I'd like you to tell me whether the view has by now been patched up so heavily (extra premises about common priors, about what even counts as a prospect, about welfarism, about excluding fairness as a value in its own right) that it's stopped risking anything at all -- and if so, whether that's a problem for it or just what a good theorem looks like once you see its edges. Rank the defeaters from fatal down to cosmetic, and please flag anything that I, as someone who'd happily nod along to the thin-sounding premises, would find genuinely hard to swallow, since that's the only kind of thing that'll actually move me.
