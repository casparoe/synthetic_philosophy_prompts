For the past two years I have been arguing myself into a position I never expected to hold: that the way we elect our legislature is fundamentally unfair, and that the cure is not to tinker with the present arrangement but to replace it. Next month I have been invited to make that case at the monthly meeting of our town's civic association — about forty people, mostly retired professionals and small-business owners, with a handful of active members of the two main parties. They are not political scientists and they have little patience for abstract argument, but they are far from ignorant: they have absorbed, secondhand, most of the standard defences of the present system, and they will recite them back at me. My case will be that a party can win an outright majority of seats on well under half the votes, and that we should therefore adopt a specific replacement rather than a vague call for "reform": keep a locally elected member for every district, then add a smaller tier of regional top-up seats so that each party's total share of seats matches its share of the vote, with the top-up seats filled from open lists that let voters choose among the candidates, a modest threshold to keep out the very smallest parties, and — this is the part I care most about, and the part I expect to be attacked on — the top-up calculated region by region rather than nationally, so that a party which piles up its votes in one corner of the country is not rewarded for that concentration.

I would like you to write me the question-and-answer preparation sheet for that evening. Tell me the objections these particular people will actually raise — phrased the way they will actually phrase them, not the way a political scientist would — ordered by how much damage each would do to my case; say which of them will come from the party loyalists, which from the small-business owners who fear instability, and which from the general suspicion of anything that sounds complicated; and for each objection give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, together with a candid note on where that reply is weakest. I need to know what I can concede without appearing to retreat, what I should hold firm on even if the room turns against me, and which single objection I must answer well or the evening is lost.

Finally, tell me the question I am most afraid of — the one I hope nobody in the room thinks to ask — and what, if anything, I could honestly say if someone does.
