Hey — I'm a journalist, I mostly cover science and ideas, and I'm working on a feature that I'm a little worried is gonna turn into the same "nature vs nurture" slugfest everyone's already read. It's about the gender-equality paradox: the counterintuitive finding that the more gender-equal and prosperous a country is, the bigger the sex gaps get in things like how many women go into engineering or computer science, or how people sort into "people" jobs versus "things" jobs. Poorer, less equal countries sometimes look more balanced on paper, and nobody really agrees why.

Here's the angle I want to try. I want to run a thought experiment: what would John Stuart Mill say about this? Mill wrote that big essay about the subjection of women, and the whole move of it is that you can't read off what women are "naturally" like from how women currently are, because their situation has been shaping them since birth — the way a plant grown in a dark cellar tells you nothing about what it would do in sunlight. His line, roughly, is that we'll only find out what's natural once women are actually free, and until then any claimed natural difference is basically the shadow of their subordination.

So: if the paradox holds up, does that sink Mill, or can he wriggle out of it? I don't want a vague "he'd probably say it's still culture" — I want you to actually get in the weeds. A few things I'd love you to work through:

- How would he even parse the finding? Would he read it as the dark-cellar plant finally getting light and choosing the cellar anyway, i.e. evidence the differences are real? Or would he say it's still culture, and if so, which culture story — that richer/more equal countries just give people more room to follow gendered preferences, that gender-essentialist attitudes are actually stronger in those societies, or that the effect is partly a measurement/statistical artifact? Tell me which of these he'd be most drawn to and why, given what he actually argued.
- Does his escape hatch make him unfalsifiable? Because it feels like a view where every possible result — big gaps, small gaps, gaps that shrink, gaps that grow — gets filed under "still the shadow of subordination." I want you to be honest about whether that's a genuine empirical claim or a dodge, and what it would take to actually test Mill.
- Then the justice part. Mill cared a lot about equality of opportunity. If the paradox is real, does it undercut the case for equality, or is that a non sequitur? Keep seperate the question of preferences from outcomes, the specific STEM pipeline from general occupational sorting, what's chosen from what's constrained, and a population-level gap from any claim about an individual woman.
