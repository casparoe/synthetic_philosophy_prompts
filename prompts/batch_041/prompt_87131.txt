Two theorists are arguing about how a reasoner ought to behave when it must assign a credence to a claim whose truth-value it cannot compute: a claim about the output of a program it cannot run, an unproved conjecture, a sentence in a formalism it cannot evaluate.

The disputed rule is this. When the reasoner has no information bearing on such a claim, it should assign the credence it would assign if the claim's truth were settled by a fair, independent lottery over the space of possibilities, and should hold that credence until it can derive an actual dependency between the claim and something else. Call this the presumption of independence: unproved claims are treated as though they were the outcomes of independent chance events, and revised only when a connection is proved.

A concrete case the two should return to more than once: a reasoner is shown two sentences, X and Y, written in a notation it cannot parse, and is asked for its credence in each and in their conjunction. Presuming independence, it answers one half, one half, and one quarter. It later learns that X is the negation of Y, so that their conjunction is impossible and its joint credence was simply wrong.

The first theorist defends the rule; the second rejects it. Write their dialogue.

The defender should argue that some such default is unavoidable — that a reasoner which refuses to assign credences to unproved claims can neither act nor learn, and that the presumption is only the explicit and revisable version of the hidden priors any reasoner must already carry. They should connect it to the older thought that symmetrical ignorance licenses symmetrical credence, claim that the presumption is what allows a reasoner to acquire evidence about logical facts it cannot derive, and say what a heuristic argument, as opposed to a proof, can establish in its favour.

The critic should go past the bare charge of arbitrariness. The rule smuggles in a substantive assumption — that the domain is unstructured or generic — which is exactly what fails for structured computations and for adversaries who know the rule. It cannot be applied consistently: every collection of claims carries hidden logical relations that the presumption will violate, the reasoner may never find them, and the order in which it finds them may make its corrections order-dependent. The case above shows that the interim credences were confidently wrong; and a claim can be constructed to refer to the reasoner's own credence in it, so that no stable assignment is available at all. Since no computable procedure converges to the truth about arbitrary logical facts, the presumption appears to buy definiteness at the price of systematic error.

The defender should answer each objection in turn rather than restate their position: that the demand for a guarantee is one no prior could meet; that exploitability attaches to any credence function and not specially to this one; that where the reasoner cannot see a dependency, its credences simply are the best it can do; and that the alternative to an explicit presumption is not the absence of a presumption but an unexamined one.

Let them also disagree about the analogy with chance — whether the space of logical possibilities admits any canonical symmetry, or whether the fair lottery is a metaphor concealing a choice — and about what a norm of reasoning is for: whether it must track the truth, or need only render the reasoner answerable to criticism. Let each notice when the other's argument has landed.

Write the dialogue so that each character responds to the other's actual words. Neither is a fool or a strawman; each states the strongest version of their view. Include at least one point on which they find they agree, and at least one on which they agree about the facts and differ only about what follows. Aim for roughly a dozen exchanges, and let it end without a forced resolution.
