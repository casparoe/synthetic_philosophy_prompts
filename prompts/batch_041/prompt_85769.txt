I'm writing the final for my upper-level seminar on aggregation (4th-years plus a couple of MA students) and I want one essay question built on the dust specks vs torture case. Problem is everyone's seen the standard version, so if I just ask "one torture or a huge number of people each getting a dust speck in the eye?" I'll get forty restatements of the same two positions. What I want is to force them to prise apart two things the usual version smushes together: whether a very large number of very small harms can outweigh one enormous harm, and whether harms to distinct people aggregate at all. So build it around a variant where all the specks land on one person -- one mild annoyance a day for the rest of a life, nothing worse than that -- so the interpersonal question is off the table and they have to say whether the intrapersonal version is still the same problem, and if it isn't, which premise was doing the real work in the original. Then hit them with a chain: a sequence of cases stepping from one torture to a very large number of dust specks, each step swapping one torture for some number of specks, where the first case is obviously worse than the last but each individual step looks like it isn't a worsening. Ask what has to give -- transitivity of "at least as bad as", a threshold in the badness of mild annoyance, or the judgement that the steps are improvements -- and make them commit to one and pay its cost.

Give me the question, a model answer of around 700 words that actually commits to a line rather than surveying, and a marking scheme out of 20 naming the moves an A answer has to make (at least: separates the two issues cleanly, handles the single-person variant on its own terms rather than treating it as a warm-up, and says what the chain argument costs whichever way they jump), plus a one-line note on how students typically fumble each of those. No citations, they can't bring notes in anyway.
