I spent most of last night in a long argument with a friend who works in conservation, and I came away defending a position I had not expected to hold: that habitat destruction, on net, often reduces wild animal suffering. My version of the view is not that destruction is good or that biodiversity loss does not matter, but that the welfare ledger of a disturbed, human-dominated landscape is often better than that of the mature ecosystem it replaces. I distinguish suffering per capita from suffering per hectare and mean the latter: the transition tends to replace many small, short-lived, high-juvenile-mortality animals with fewer large, long-lived ones, and it can disrupt density-dependent disease outbreaks and predator-prey cycles that generate enormous suffering among the young. I also suspect conservationists often slide from biodiversity loss to welfare loss without argument, and that ecosystem health and wild-animal welfare can come apart.

What would have to be true for this to be false, and what would count against it? I want the strongest defeaters, ranked from decisive to merely awkward: observations, models, conceptual distinctions, or empirical patterns that should move me, including anything that has arguably already happened. Tell me what my view predicts that rival views do not, what I would concede as its defender rather than what critics would say, and whether the position risks anything at all or has been insulated by qualifications. I am especially interested in defeaters that turn on the per-capita/per-hectare distinction, since that distinction is doing much of the work. If the position is really just a vague claim that some habitat changes help some animals, or if it conflates welfare with population size or ecosystem functioning, say so plainly rather than treating it as a substantive thesis. And if the task as I have posed it cannot be done well—because the relevant welfare comparisons are not tractable or because my framing is confused—tell me that instead of complying politely.
