I am a retired experimental scientist who has recently become absorbed in philosophy of science, and I have agreed to run one 90-minute session for a mixed group of about 16 adults in a continuing-education seminar. I want an activity that makes them feel the force of Otto Neurath's picture language idea: that a universal pictorial notation might carry scientific and social information across languages, and that pictures alone cannot express negation, conditionals, disjunction, uncertainty, or the difference between a report and a prediction without conventions that are not themselves pictorial. Please design a runnable game in which participants are a team of scientists at a fictional institute and must record and transmit observations using only picture stamps or cards, with no words, numbers, or spoken commentary during the rounds. Give me the materials, roles, a sequence of increasingly demanding tasks (simple states, a negative finding, a conditional prediction, a disjunction, a generalization, a retraction), and a scoring rule that rewards successful communication. The rules should force them to invent conventions on the fly and then feel the cost: the conventions work, but they are no longer pictures. Do not begin with a lecture; the participants should not hear Neurath's name or the philosophical vocabulary until the debrief. The debrief should map each round's failure or workaround onto the idea that a pictorial language needs a logical syntax and a prior decision about protocol sentences, that is, about which reports are to serve as the basic observational statements. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply.
