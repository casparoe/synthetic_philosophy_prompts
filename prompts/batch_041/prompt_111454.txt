I keep trying to pin down why I think human extinction would be so bad, and the deaths never seem to cover it. My gut on some cases:

— A meteor painlessly vaporizes everyone next Tuesday while we sleep. Obviously awful. But when I ask what's awful about it over and above the deaths, I come up empty, and yet the verdict doesn't budge.

— Everyone alive now lives a full, happy life and dies content in old age, nobody cut off early, nobody left to mourn. I still feel something's been lost, and it bugs me that I can't name a single person who's worse off.

— Same dying out, except a few centuries later some flourishing beings — not us — inherit the science and art and care about the same things we did. That feels close to fine to me.

— Humanity survives forever, biology intact, but frozen: no new inquiry, no new art, everyone content but doing nothing I'd call mattering. Honestly this strikes me as worse than the extinction case, and I can't square that with the rest.

— I plant a tree that won't matter for 200 years, or work on a cure I'll never see used, and these feel like the most worthwhile things I do. Tell me humanity ends in 50 years and both are moot, and I notice I stop caring. That bothers me — it suggests my caring was staked on a future I won't be around for.

I've heard a version of this: that extinction's badness isn't mainly the deaths, it's that it pulls the rug out from under what we value now, becuase those values are staked on there being a human future — an afterlife for the species rather than the person. Is there a principle along those lines that captures my verdicts, or close? Run it against the frozen-forever case and the inheritors case especially, and tell me which of my gut reactions I'd have to give up.
