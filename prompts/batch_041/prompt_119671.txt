Hey, I'm a third-year PhD student in political theory working on formal stuff about group judgment, and I've been circling a pattern for months without being able to name it. Figured I'd just describe it and see if it has a name, because right now I suspect it's three or four things I already know wearing a trenchcoat.

Here's the shape of it. You've got a body that decides by majority rule, and you want to know how reliable the body is. Condorcet-ish reasoning: if each member is better than a coin flip and their errors aren't correlated, the majority gets more reliable as you add members. Fine. What I keep noticing is that the group's track record can't tell you which of those worlds you're in. Say a seven-person committee is right about 80% of the time. That's consistent with each member being right roughly 65% of the time with errors more or less independent of each other — in which case the committee is genuinely aggregating information, and a bigger committee would be better. It's also consistent with every member being right 80% of the time and making exactly the same mistakes — in which case the "committee" is one person with six echoes, and adding members does nothing at all. Same observable number, opposite practical advice. Unless you can see the individual votes alongside ground truth case by case (and in the settings I care about you usually cant), the group's hit rate is all you get, and it's compatible with both.

I see versions of it everywhere. A forecasting team where all five analysts are downstream of the same data pipeline. A hiring committee that all read the same dossier first and then voted "independently." And the move that really bugs me is when someone says: it's a theorem, therefore the institution is justified — when the input the theorem actually needs, namely how correlated the errors are, is precisely the input the institution can't measure. Worse, at some point the premise that members are better than chance seems to get supported by pointing out that they agree with each other, which is the thing the theorem was supposed to underwrite in the first place.

So: does this have a name? I dont think it's just "the independence assumption" — that's the thing being assumed, not the phenomenon of it being uncheckable. It smells like identification or observational equivalence from econometrics, but those are about describing data, and what I'm describing is a normative argument that quietly eats its own premise. It's also near the problem of the criterion and epistemic circularity, but those feel broader. You're a philosopher of social science who actually knows the formal aggregation literature — start by brainstorming the candidates, then tell me which one fits and where the others come apart from it, and if nothing fits, coin a term and give me its definition, its edges, and a couple of cases that fall outside it. I want the carving, not a label. No need to point me at papers.
