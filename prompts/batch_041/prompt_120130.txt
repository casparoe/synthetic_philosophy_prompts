I retired three years ago after a career in analytical chemistry — stable isotope work, mostly on food fraud. Real Manuka honey versus clover honey with a nice label, real Parmigiano versus wood pulp and sawdust, wine from the wrong valley. It's a satisfying kind of problem because it's a detection problem: there's always something in the sample that gives the game away, and the whole craft is making that measurement cheap and fast enough to be worth running on a pallet of jars.

What I've been doing since is reading philosphy, badly and out of order, and I've just come off a stretch of Dennett — the essay he wrote not long before he died, where he says the companies making fake people are committing an act of vandalism, that it should be a crime to build them or pass them along, and that we should watermark them the way banknotes are watermarked with that little ring of circles that photocopiers are built to refuse. And I keep noticing he's framing it as my old trade: put a mark on the thing, put a detector on the mark, done. But the same man spent fifty years telling us the self isn't a special ingredient in the universe, that it's more like the center of gravity a story has — a person being something made out of words and other people and held together by our taking each other at our word — and that we go on holding each other responsible anyway, on purpose, because that's the only game going. Plus his other point, that once a thing talks back to us sensibly we can't stop treating it as a person no matter how well we understand the machinery.

Put those together and I honestly don't know what the counterfeit is supposed to be a copy of.

So I'd like a short story, 1500-2500 words, that puts this on its feet instead of explaining it. Some things I'd ask for:

- Somebody in it works in authentication, whatever kind you like — art, wine, documents, signatures, soil samples, a lab. Its up to you; I just want the reader to feel the ordinary texture of that job, including the part where the assay works perfectly.
- At least one scene where the test works, the fake is caught, and the damage happens anyway. The harm isn't a failure of measurement. It's that everyone now has to measure.
- A character who argues that we were always counterfeit — that people are made the same way the fakes are — and who isn't a strawman. Let them be persuasive enough that the protagonist can't wave them off, but don't hand them the trophy either.
- No character at the end who explains the right answer. No philosopher turning up to settle it. A small ordinary gesture or an unresolved decision beats a lecture.
- Keep the scale small and domestic. No montage of civilization collapsing, no courtroom climax. Near-future is fine, but it should be a recognizable street and a recognizable job.
- Assume the reader has never heard of Dennett or of the intentional stance, so everything has to come through the story's own furniture. Don't quote anyone and don't lean on real events, dates, or people — invent what you need.

I'm not asking you to take a side. I'd rather have a story where both positions cost the characters something.
