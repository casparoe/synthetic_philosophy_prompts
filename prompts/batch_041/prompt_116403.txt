I am assembling a three-year, three-person research project — myself, a postdoctoral fellow, and one doctoral student — devoted to conceptual rather than empirical issues in the units-of-selection debate. We will collect no new data and run no experiments; the outputs are a handful of papers and a short monograph. What I need from you is a defended research agenda, not a survey, not a reading list, and not a set of citations.

Please treat the following as the framing, and tell me where the framing itself is mistaken. My first conviction is that the formal-equivalence results — the demonstrations that kin-selection, multilevel-selection, and contextual-analysis bookkeeping can be made to agree — have not resolved the debate but relocated it, so that the live disputes now concern causation and the individuation of evolutionary entities rather than the arithmetic of partitioning fitness variance. My second conviction is that the most productive pressure on the debate comes from cases in which a group-level trait is heritable and causally efficacious even though the group is not plausibly a reproducer or an evolutionary individual in its own right, and from the distinction between a trait's merely being selected and its being the feature for which selection occurred.

Given that framing, which open questions should we take up, in what order, and why? Candidates I can see include whether there is a determinate fact about the level at which selection acts; whether group-level causal claims can be reduced to individual-level claims without explanatory loss; whether the concept of an evolutionary individual is prior to or derivative from the concept of a unit of selection; and how far the debate should be extended to cultural evolution and the major transitions. Add what I am missing and discard what is not genuinely open. For each question you keep, tell me how much downstream work depends on it, whether conceptual argument alone can advance it, how neglected it is, and whether settling it would change what anyone believes or does; and where two questions compete for the same three years, say which you would drop and why.

Finally, tell me what would change your ranking — which argument or finding would move a question up or down — and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. I would rather have an ordering that exposes its own weak point than a balanced inventory.
