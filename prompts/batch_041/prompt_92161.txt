I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for a long time, and my reading group has more or less become an extension of that habit. Last week two members spent most of a session arguing about the identity of indiscernibles, and I ended up promising to write the exchange up for someone who could judge it better than I could. Neither of them knows I am asking, and I have kept my own view out of the summary below.

(1) Ada: The principle is false, and the two-sphere case shows it. Two iron spheres of the same diameter, color, temperature, and surface pattern, the same distance apart, in an otherwise empty universe. Nothing distinguishes them, and yet there are two of them. So either the principle is false, or we have to say that this universe contains one sphere, which is absurd.

(2) Emmett: You have shown that your description is incomplete, not that the principle is false. If there are two spheres, then they differ: one of them is identical with the first sphere and the other is not. So the principle comes out true, but only because being that sphere counts as a property. Stated without restriction it is a tautology, and nobody ever needed a thought experiment for a tautology.

(3) Ada: Then it is a tautology with no content, and the version worth arguing about is the one restricted to properties you could state without naming the individual — what things are like, as opposed to which things they are. Restricted that way the spheres refute it, and the restriction can be stated cleanly in terms of symmetry: no possible world admits a nontrivial symmetry, nothing can be permuted around while every qualitative fact stays fixed, and the two-sphere world fails that test immediately, since swapping the spheres changes nothing. The restricted version also does real work elsewhere — for instance, in the argument that a uniformly shifted universe would be a world with no discernible difference from ours, which is supposed to embarrass anyone who treats space as a thing over and above its contents.

(4) Emmett: Two replies. First, 'qualitative' is doing all the work, and nobody has given a principled test for it that is not gerrymandered to protect the principle — you are drawing the line exactly where the counterexamples stop. Second, the physics may already have settled this against you: identical particles are not merely similar, they are permutable. The formalism does not distinguish the state in which electron 1 is spin-up and electron 2 is spin-down from the swapped state; for fermions the swap flips the sign of the state vector, and no measurement can register that sign. If there is no fact of the matter about which particle is which, then the principle starts to look like a constraint that physics respects rather than a falsehood.

(5) Ada: I read the physics the other way. Helium has two electrons, and that count is not a bookkeeping convention — it is measurable. In the ground state the two electrons are in a joint state in which neither has a definite spin of its own, so there is no description that assigns each electron a property the other lacks. That is an actual case of numerically distinct things that nothing qualitative separates, which is exactly what the spheres were supposed to be a thought experiment for. If the principle were true, the count would have to come out as one.

(6) Emmett: Then we are not disagreeing about the world, we are disagreeing about vocabulary. 'Discernible' is relative to a language: name both spheres and they are trivially discernible, use only qualitative predicates and they are not. The principle is true in one language and false in the other, and there is no further fact that could decide between them.

I would like a move-by-move adjudication: for each of (1) through (6), rate the move from clearly mistaken through contestable to clearly right, with a short justification, and tell me who is right about which specific point rather than who wins overall. I am especially interested in a verdict on whether the last move is correct, that is, whether the whole thing collapses into a verbal dispute, and on whether the quantum case is being asked to carry more weight than it can bear. Finally, tell me what both of them are missing — the distinction or the version of the principle that would make this disagreement tractable. I am not looking for reading recommendations; I want the arguments assessed as they were made. Please answer in American English.
