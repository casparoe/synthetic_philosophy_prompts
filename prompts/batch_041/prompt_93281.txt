I do screening at a small national competition authority — I'm the economist on the team, not a lawyer — and my job is triage: complaints and market-monitoring hits come in, maybe a dozen a month, and each one gets a yes/no on weather to open a formal investigation, usually inside two weeks. At that stage we only have public information and whatever the firms volunteer, no subpoena power, and whatever I write has to survive being read by a board member who isn't an economist and then challenged by the firms' lawyers.

The legal test is our lawyers' problem and they're happy with it. My problem is the thing underneath it. Over and over we get a set of firms that behaved in parallel, and I have to say something about whether they were acting together or each just doing their own sums in a market where everyone can watch everyone else. I read a bit of the collective-intentionality literature years ago — Bratman on shared activity, Searle on we-intentions, Gilbert on joint commitment, Tuomela on the we-mode, List and Pettit on group agents — and it felt like exactly the right toolbox, except none of it is in a form I can use in a two-page note.

Can you turn it into a procedure I can actually run? The questions in the order I'd ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a close, and where it has to hand the file to a human because what's left is a values call or a legal-risk call rather than a question about whether there was a joint intention. Then run it on three of our live files, names changed:

1. Three ferry operators on one coastal route. Operator A announces a fuel surcharge in a trade newsletter and on its own site; within nine days B and C file surcharges at the same percentage. No calls, no meetings, nothing between them. B had been undercutting A's fares for two years. A's commercial director later told a conference that they had to be transparent about it, you can't have a race to the bottom.

2. A trade association working group, three firms' commercial directors, adopts a voluntary standard on pack sizes that happens to kill off the cheap small sachet that had been driving a price war. The minutes talk only about shelf presentation and consumer clarity. Nobody mentions price, and everyone in the room knew what it would do.

3. Two supermarket chains pull out of the same low-margin region within a week of each other. One internal email: if they pull out we stay out, if they come back we're back in within a month. Both were at the same industry conference the month before, though there's no evidence they spoke.

Then tell me where the procedure breaks. I suspect Bratman's thing is built for people who are actually cooperating, and my firms would happily undercut each other given half a chance, so maybe it just doesn't transfer. Is the residue my vocabulary missing something, or the philosophers arguing about something we don't care about? And say which of the three the procedure makes me close even though it feels dirty, and which one it makes me escalate even though the numbers look harmless. No reading list please — I'm not going to read more philosophy, I need the thing I can run.
