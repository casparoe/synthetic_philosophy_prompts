I am a teaching assistant for a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind, and I am preparing a handout for the week on self-deception. The students come away from the reading with the impression that the debate is essentially "the Freudian picture versus everyone else," and I want a diagnostic instrument that displays the genuine variety of positions. Please write a decision tree, in flowchart form, that leads someone from the plain observation that a person can be systematically mistaken about their own motives to a definite account of self-deception. Build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a roster of authors: first the questions that actually divide the accounts, then the accounts located at the resulting leaves.

The tree should turn on at least the following questions. First, does self-deception essentially involve an intention to bring about a belief that one already disbelieves — the traditional paradox — or can it be understood without any such intention? Second, must the self-deceiver simultaneously hold two contradictory beliefs, or can the account make do with a non-doxastic state, such as pretense, imagination, hope, or a felt inclination that falls short of belief? Third, does the account need to partition the mind into sub-agents or subsystems, or can it be told in terms of a single agent whose reasoning is merely biased? Fourth, is the phenomenon to be explained at the personal level or at some subpersonal level? Phrase every branch node as a question a student could answer without already knowing which label it leads to. Each leaf should carry a descriptive name for the position (or "unnamed" if nobody has defended it) and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment. I want leaves for at least the following, whether or not they end up adjacent: a dual-belief intentionalist account; a partitioned-mind or homuncular account; a deflationary account on which self-deception is just motivated belief-formation and there is no deceiver; a pretense or imagination account; a Sartrean bad-faith account; and an eliminativist account on which the concept is incoherent and we should speak only of motivated irrationality.

Finally, please test the tree against a small set of concrete cases, saying for each which leaves it separates and which it does not: the person who "forgets" the appointment that would have delivered bad news; the jealous spouse who reads every ambiguous sign as confirming an affair; the patient who sincerely reports a test result and then plans as if it had come out negative; the person who says, after the fact, that they knew all along; and the person who manages a difficult truth by refusing to look at it rather than by believing its opposite. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and for any branch of the space that no position occupies, say briefly whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it has simply not been explored. The instrument should sort students by what they say about cases, not by which label they have memorized, so no question should be answerable simply by recalling a position's name.
