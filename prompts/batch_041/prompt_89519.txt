I'm puting together a set of writing tasks for a model that can't search the web, so nothing in your answer can lean on citations, quotes or a reading list — and equally, you can assume whoever reads it already knows Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling and the problemata about Abraham well enough that you don't need to explain the basics. Take the role of someone who's taught that book for years and knows exactly where its joints are. Think it through properly before you start writing, but don't show me your working — just give me the piece.

Here's the job. Write a formal case review, around 650 words, from a body I'll call the Faith-Based Practice Exemption Panel: a standing committee at a large public institution that rules on requests from staff and students to be excused from ordinary policies on religious grounds. The panel has been handed the Abraham case. A man is told to take his son up a mountain and kill him; he tells neither his wife nor his servant nor the boy what he means to do; he goes; at the last moment it's called off. The panel has to produce a ruling on two things — whether the case falls within the exemption at all, and whether his silence towards Sarah, Eliezer and Isaac was acceptable. The panel is not stupid and not a strawman; it genuinely wants to be fair to him, and it has already interviewed him. He was asked to state his reasons in writing and produced almost nothing the panel could use.

What I want out of it: keep the register deadpan and bureaucratic. This is a real report written by real administrators after a real meeting — minutes, proportionality assessments, a safeguarding plan, a note about what further evidence would satisfy them — not a comedy sketch, and nobody in it should be winking at the reader. The humour has to come from the actual shape of the problem. As the panel works through the file — whether 'I was told to' can ever function as a reason, what would count as corroboration, whether they can simply record his sincerity and move on, whether they should treat it as an unusually hard case of obedience or an unusually clear case of attempted murder — you should be quietly showing that every way of disposing of the case either convicts him or stops being a review at all, and that the panel's own procedure already assumes an answer to the question it is supposed to be deciding. The distinction doing the real work is the one between a man whose act could be explained to a community and a man whose act could not, so put a couple of the panel's comparisons to more intelligible cases of sacrifice or obedience to work and then let them quietly fail. End with one short dissenting paragraph from a single member who says very little, but says it in a way a careful reader can tell is the whole point.

Formatting: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no numbered clauses — continuous prose only, the way one would write a long email or a letter. British English throughout. Don't quote Kierkegaard at me and don't cite anything; paraphrase or allude.
