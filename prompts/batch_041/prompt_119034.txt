I'm a high school senior, and for about a year I've been running a small custodial brokerage account my parents set up for me, plus a few hundred dollars of my own that I keep in a prediction-market account I argue about with friends. I've read enough personal-finance writing to be genuinely fluent in its vocabulary: I can talk about compounding, log returns versus arithmetic returns, volatility drag, sequence-of-returns risk, rebalancing, position sizing, fractional stakes, why you shouldn't bet the whole bankroll even on a coin you're confident is biased, and why survivorship bias makes most 'I turned five hundred dollars into fifty thousand' stories worthless. What I have is essentially no training in decision theory or philosophy, which is why I'm writing. On a long bus ride to a math competition, a friend from statistics club kept telling me about the Kelly criterion and about something he calls 'geometric rationality,' and I had the strong feeling that I already knew the thing in a different language and wanted to find out how much of it is actually the same thing.

So here is the task. Take the position that a rational agent facing a repeated or branching situation should maximize expected log wealth — the growth-optimal bet, staking a fraction rather than everything, treating ruin as categorically different from a drawdown, and the further claim that this is not merely a rule for gamblers but a general account of how to act under uncertainty — and restate it, as faithfully as you can, entirely in the vocabulary I actually use: bankroll and position sizing, compounding, log versus arithmetic returns, volatility drag, diversification and rebalancing, sequence-of-returns risk, ruin, fractional Kelly as a safety margin, survivorship bias, and the gap between the average outcome across many people and the outcome along the single path I actually live. I suspect the arithmetic will come through almost perfectly and the philosophy will vanish, and I want to know whether that's the whole story.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about most. Which pieces of the position survive translation unchanged, and which ones can I state only by quietly changing the subject? In particular: (a) is 'maximize expected log wealth' a preference I'm supposed to have, or a theorem about which strategy survives — and does my vocabulary even have room to make that distinction, or does everything in it flatten into 'this is what works'? (b) I've heard the argument that this isn't really about money at all, that the same geometry governs how you should spread yourself across anything you care about, and that the reason to stake only a fraction has something to do with being one of many copies or branches of yourself; does any of that survive, or does it degrade into an empty metaphor about diversification? (c) I've read that the best growth rate you can achieve is somehow the same quantity as the information you have about the bet, which sounds to me like a coincidence of units rather than a real identity — does the translation make that less mysterious, or does my vocabulary simply lack a slot for it? And (d) is there anything the decision theorists and philosophers are genuinely arguing about here that has no counterpart in what I'd call good bankroll management — and if so, is that because bankroll management is missing something, or because their disagreement is thinner than it sounds? Be honest about the translation, including where it breaks down, and don't tell me the two frameworks are 'deeply connected' unless you can point to the exact sentence that survives and the exact sentence that has to be thrown out.
