I'm putting together lecture material on Moore's paradox and I'd like to open with a joke, but the usual offerings only get a laugh at philosophers' expense and teach the students nothing. Please write me a joke whose humour actually turns on the structure of the paradox. Two things should shape it. First, it should exploit the difference between the omissive form — "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining" — and the commissive form — "It's raining, but I believe it isn't raining" — since only the second wears its contradiction on its sleeve. Second, it should engage what makes the omissive form worth puzzling over: the sentence can be true, because I might simply be wrong about my own belief, and yet it cannot be asserted, because answering the question whether I believe that p is normally a matter of settling whether p rather than of inspecting my own mind. A joke that treats "It's raining but I don't believe it" as an outright contradiction, or that could be told by someone who had never encountered the paradox, is not what I want. Give me the joke first, then a section titled "Commentary" (about a paragraph) explaining your choices, flagging where the joke takes liberties with the ideas or leans on a contested reading of them, stating how confident you are that it gets the paradox right, and singling out the point you are least sure of.
