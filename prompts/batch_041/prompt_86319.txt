Ok, so here's the situation, and I need a favor. I'm a science journalist — I don't have a PhD in anything — and in about two weeks I'm giving a talk at a cognitive science seminar about a long feature I've been working on. The claim is that an ant colony is a real mind: not a metaphor, not a convenient way of talking about a lot of little minds, but one subject that has beliefs and makes decisions and maybe even has something going on experientially. The old Aunt Hillary thought experiment, except I'm saying Aunt Hillary is actually there and not just a cute way of talking.

My argument, roughly: (1) no single ant represents the colony's goal — a scout that finds a good nest site just lays down pheromone, and the 'we should move over there' representation only exists at the level of the whole; (2) the colony's memory lives in the nest architecture and the pheromone field, not in any ant's head — the structure still encodes where the food was long after the ants that built it are dead; (3) the hook I'm definately leaning on: over a season, every worker in the colony gets replaced, and the colony's behavior — its house-hunting preferences, its foraging schedule, its attitude toward intruders — stays basically the same. Whatever persists through that turnover isn't the ants, it's the pattern. And colonies split by budding, so I have a line about identity being narrative rather than material.

The room is mostly philosophers of mind, and they're functionalists, happy to let mental states be multiply realizable, which you'd think makes them natural allies, but they're allergic to minds popping up everywhere. Then there are two ant biologists who own the actual data and think 'colony cognition' is a modeling convenience for people who don't want to learn ant biology. And one neuroscientist who works on split-brain patients, who is going to want to know what makes a colony one subject instead of two, or fourty, or a thousand.

So: build me the Q&A prep sheet. The objections I'll actually get from this crowd, in the order they'll come and ranked by how much damage they do, phrased the way each group would phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where my reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front, which objection I should just surrender on, and the one reply that has to land for the talk to work. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
