My flatmate Ravi and our friend Meera got into it after the decision theory seminar on Tuesday and I ended up just sitting there with a beer, so I'm the one reporting this, and I genuinely can't tell who won. (1) Ravi said the Ellsberg urn case isn't a paradox and isn't a bias, it's just what honest bookkeeping looks like: in the two-urn version you know the first urn is 50/50 and you know nothing at all about the split in the second, so there's no fact of the matter about the second urn's chance of red, "expected value" is a comparison you literally can't run, and picking the urn whose odds you know is picking the one option you can actually evaluate. (2) Meera said that can't be right, because if you refuse to put any number on the mystery urn then you've made the known urn better by fiat -- you can't even say you prefer it, since a preference has to be a preference for something, and you've just declined to say what the second option is worth. (3) Ravi said that's the indifference fallacy with a suit on: "unknown proportion" does not mean "uniformly random proportion," and if a prankster or a coin filled that urn then saying 50/50 is simply false. (4) Meera said fine, take my version then: the second urn's composition is set by flipping a fair coin a hundred times, one flip per ball, so there's no mystery about the process, and by symmetry the chance of red is exactly a half. Now if you still reach for the known urn, whatever's bugging you isn't missing information, because there's none left to miss, so we can finally ask whether it's a bias. (5) Ravi said that half is an average over a hundred different urns and the urn in front of him is one of them; he's betting on that urn, not on the average of the family, and "the average composition is half red" is not the same claim as "this urn is half red."

(6) Meera said that move proves too much, since every probability is an average over possibilities -- and then she went to the whiteboard and did the three-colour one: 30 red, the other 60 split however you like between black and yellow. If you'd rather bet on red than on black, she said, the sure-thing principle forces you to rather bet on "red or yellow" than on "black or yellow," because those two bets differ only in the red and black states and pay identically in yellow; but the same people who take the first bet refuse the second, so the preference flips without any payoff changing, and that's incoherent before you've said a single word about probabilities. (7) Ravi said the sure-thing principle is exactly the thing on trial: the two acts differ in more than the payoffs across red and black, they differ in how much he knows about red and black, and if a rule of rationality is blind to that difference then it's the rule that's wrong.
