Somebody on a podcast I listen to did a whole episode on "cognitive penetration" — whether your beliefs change what you actually see, not just how you read what you see — and he gave this tidy summary at the end that I wrote down almost word for word so I could check it. Here's the gist:

'Cognitive penetration just is top-down processing. Your brain is a prediction machine: the higher, cognitive parts send signals down into the visual cortex, which is why what you know, what you want, and even what mood your in changes the picture in front of you. The classic demo is that 1940s study where poor kids saw coins as bigger than rich kids did — pure want. Then the banana studies, where knowing a banana is yellow makes you see a yellowish tint that isn't on the screen. For a while people fought about it, but Firestone and Scholl basically ended that: they went through the literature and showed every one of these effects is really attention, memory, or response bias, so cognition does not affect perception, full stop. The only thing left to argue about is how big the effects are in the cases that survive. And on the theory side it's over too, because the resistance all came from Fodor's idea that perception is a sealed-off module, and nobody in neuroscience believes in modules anymore. The real upshot is political: it's why two people can watch the same video and see different things, and it means perception can never justify a belief, because your beliefs are already smuggled into what you see.'

He also floated a test he calls the swap test: take two people who disagree, have them trade beliefs, and if they still see the same thing, then cognition isn't penetrating. Is that a good test?
