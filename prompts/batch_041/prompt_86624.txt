I am midway through a novel set in the mid-1920s, and I have reached a scene I cannot write until the argument inside it is honest. Two of my characters — invented people, not historical figures, so give them names, ages, and voices of your own — are the ablest members of a small marine insurance syndicate, and they have been handed a risk with no history behind it: a new class of cargo on a new route, against a hazard that has not yet had its first loss, so that there is no run of cases to average over. They must put a figure on it, or decline to. One of them has been reading Keynes on probability and has taken from it that probability is a relation between a body of evidence and a proposition; that this relation is sometimes not measurable by any number, though it may still be rankable against others; and that evidence has a second property besides its bearing — how much of it there is — which can be thin while the probability itself is high. The other holds that probability is nothing but the degree of belief a coherent person will act on, that the sole test of such a degree is the odds at which one will bet, and that talk of unmeasurable probabilities is a confession of indecision dressed as a discovery about the world.

Write that dialogue. I need the following from it:

- Both characters are first-rate. Neither is a straw man; each must state the other's position in its strongest form before going after it.
- Genuine back-and-forth. No speech that goes unanswered, no talking past each other, and at least one concession from each that actually costs them something.
- The argument must be carried in the words two men would use across a table, not in seminar shorthand. No verbatim quotations, no titles of works, no citations. They may refer to what they have been reading, but only paraphrased in their own voices.
- At least these matters must be fought over: whether one probability can be ranked above another without either being measured; whether a man who prefers to bet on an urn whose composition he knows over one whose composition he does not is being prudent or incoherent; whether an evidence base can be too thin to support any figure at all, and what "too thin" could mean if it does not mean "unlikely"; and whether a man who refuses to name a single distribution and instead carries a range of them — or a whole set — has said anything determinate enough to bet on. Let the second character press hardest on that last one, and let the first answer him properly.
- The scene ends in a decision: a premium quoted, or a refusal entered in the minutes. The argument must visibly determine which.
- Under about 1,200 words, and the two voices should be distinguishable on the page without speech tags.

Then, on the last line, give me the crux in a fixed form I can paste into my notes:

Crux: [one sentence, no more than 30 words, naming the precise point at which the two positions cannot be reconciled]
