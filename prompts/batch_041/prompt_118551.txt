I chair my institute's biosafety advisory panel, and I need the written decision we will adopt when we meet on the 14th. The case before us forces the panel to say, on the record, whether a particular chemical system is alive, and our own policy gives us no way to dodge the question. The trial it concerns is scheduled to begin early next month, so we cannot defer.

The system. A synthetic-biology group has built what it calls a protocell platform: microscopic fatty-acid vesicles, each enclosing a small network of non-genetic reactions. Fed continuously with a simple ester fuel and lipid precursors, the vesicles take up material, swell, and — when the reactor's stirring deforms them past a threshold — pinch in two. Nothing inside the vesicle copies a template. What passes to the next generation is the composition of the membrane and of the internal reaction network, which biases how quickly the daughter grows; when the group re-seeds a well-mixed reactor with its fastest-growing vesicles, growth rates rise over successive rounds, so the bias is real and cumulative rather than an artifact of where a vesicle happens to sit. Starve the system and it lyses within hours; keep feeding it and it runs indefinitely. The group now wants to move the platform out of a sealed bioreactor and into a semi-open greenhouse trial that vents to the outside, and has asked us to classify it.

Our policy. Level 2 containment applies to living organisms. Level 1 applies to non-living chemical systems, however complex. Our operative definition of a living organism is a chemical system that maintains itself far from equilibrium and is capable of Darwinian evolution. The group argues that its platform is non-living on three grounds: there is no replication of a template, so no genuine heredity; division is driven by our stirrer rather than by the system itself; and the system cannot feed itself but depends on fuel we supply. Two panel members disagree with the group, and one of them has asked, in writing, whether a finding of "non-living" would mean that any sufficiently ingenious autocatalytic chemistry could escape Level 2 merely by being built one criterion short of the definition.

Precedent. Two years ago we classified a self-replicating RNA construct as Level 2 without much discussion. Last year we classified a prion preparation as Level 1, on the ground that it propagates a conformation without maintaining itself.

I am not asking for a treatise on the nature of life; I need something the panel can vote on and the lab can live with. Please draft the decision: the classification we should adopt, the criterion that drives it, how that criterion handles each of the group's three arguments, why the two precedents do and do not bear on this case, and the strongest case for the opposite classification together with why we should not follow it. Write it so that the lab, which will almost certainly appeal, and the funder, which wants a rule it can apply to the next case without reconvening us, both find it usable. A recommendation with conditions and a date by which they must be met is acceptable — and probably preferable, if that is where the reasoning lands.
