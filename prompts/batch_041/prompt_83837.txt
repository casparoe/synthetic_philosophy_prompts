My friend in a policy program keeps running this argument by me and I still can't find the loose thread. (1) A country's score in the world happiness rankings is basically the average of its residents' answers to a question that asks them to put their current life on a ladder from 0 (worst possible life) to 10 (best possible life). (2) People answer that kind of question by comparing their life to what they take to be normal — their neighbors, the people they see on TV, their own past — and the question is asking for a judgment about life as a whole rather than how good they feel this minute, so the comparing is doing alot of the work. (3) So when a whole country gets richer at once, everyone's yardstick rises along with their income and the average answer doesnt budge. (4) There's also adaptation: gains in circumstances wear off within a few years, so even a temporary bump in the answer gets erased. (5) Therefore no country can raise its average score by actually making life better for its people. (6) The only levers left are to change what people compare themselves to — narrow the visible gap between rich and poor, hide the rich, tell a good story about how well everyone's doing — or to change how people use the scale. (7) So a government that wants to top the rankings should work on the story, not on the conditions. Every step looks fine to me and the conclusion is crazy. Which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me downstream? I'd love to keep (5), so tell me if that forces me to say the rankings measure nothing real, or whether I can keep the rankings and just claim something narrower. Answer in American English.
