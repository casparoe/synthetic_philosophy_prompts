I'm in a graduate seminar on value and normativity, and we've spent the last two weeks on the fitting-attitude analysis of value -- the idea that for something to be good or valuable is for it to be fitting to value it, where fittingness is cashed out in terms of reasons: roughly, there is reason to have the relevant pro-attitude toward it. The familiar objection is the demon case, and I thought I understood it, but I've written out a version of the argument that now seems to me to prove too much, and I can't tell where the cost should fall. Here it is:

(1) For any object x, x is valuable just in case there is a reason to value x. (The fitting-attitude analysis.)
(2) A demon credibly threatens to torture me tomorrow unless I admire a thoroughly mediocre paperclip today.
(3) The threat gives me a strong reason to admire the paperclip.
(4) So there is a reason to admire the paperclip.
(5) So, by (1), the paperclip is valuable.
(6) But the paperclip is not valuable; it is mediocre.
(7) So (1) is false.

The standard reply, I take it, is that the threat is a reason of the wrong kind: it gives me a reason to want to admire the paperclip, or to bring it about that I admire it, but not a reason to admire it -- not a reason that bears on the fittingness of admiration. So (3) equivocates, and (1) survives. But I've been trying to make the reply precise, and it keeps slipping. If I say the threat is a reason for a different attitude (wanting to admire) or for an action (bringing it about that I admire), then I need a principled account of why the reason attaches to that attitude or action rather than to admiration itself, and it looks like the account will have to appeal to the very notion of the right kind of reason that we were trying to explain. If instead I say that the threat is a reason for admiration but merely a reason of the wrong kind, then I owe an account of what makes it the wrong kind -- and every account I try either lets in too much (e.g., cases where the attitude's being had is itself part of what makes the world go better, as when trusting someone tends to make them trustworthy, or believing in yourself improves your performance) or rules out too much (e.g., it seems like a reason to intend to do something that intending will make you more likely to do it, even if the act itself is not otherwise reason-giving). I'd like to keep (1) if I can, and I'd like to keep (6) -- I really don't think the paperclip is valuable. What I want from you is a diagnosis: which step or steps should I reject, why did that step look compelling in the first place, and what does rejecting it commit me to? If there are several viable repairs, lay them out and say what each one costs, especially with respect to the harder cases I just mentioned. I'm not looking for a bibliography; I want the argumentative map.
