Compose a dialogue between two epistemologists, call them A and B, who are epistemic peers — equally competent and equally well-informed on the matter at hand — yet who disagree about how to respond to disagreement with a peer. A is a conciliationist: she holds that learning that a peer disagrees rationally obliges you to move toward the peer's view, at minimum by suspending judgment or splitting the difference. B is a steadfast theorist: he holds that you may retain your belief, treating the peer's dissent as one more item of evidence to weigh against your own first-order reasons rather than as a command to revise.

Arrange the dialogue so that A and B are themselves peers on this very question and each knows it, so that the conversation is an instance of the phenomenon it concerns. Let them argue over a concrete invented case: two auditors, each with an identical and mutually known track record, independently examine the same firm's books and reach opposite verdicts — one sees fraud, the other sees ordinary aggressive accounting. Each has already studied the other's working papers and found them unpersuasive. Should each now move toward the other's verdict?

In a genuine back-and-forth, have them press one another on the following: what exactly makes someone a peer, and whether sharing the same evidence is part of peerhood or an independent condition; whether the bare fact of a peer's disagreement carries evidential weight on its own or only the reasons behind it, and what this implies in a case where each party has already judged the other's reasons defective; and whether conciliationism is self-undermining, given that the experts themselves disagree about conciliationism.

Neither interlocutor should be a strawman; each should answer the other's actual points rather than talk past them. Close the dialogue by having each of them state, in their own voice, what consideration would change their mind. Then add a brief note outside the dialogue identifying where the exchange takes liberties with these positions or leans on a contested reading of them.
