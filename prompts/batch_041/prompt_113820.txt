I'm in a small reading group that's been working through some philosophy of mind material, and last month we did the view where the felt side of experience -- the sting of a cut, what coffee tastes like -- is perfectly real, not an illusion, but it doesn't do any work: the wiring does all the causing and the feeling just rides along. Fine, whatever. But there's a move that kept surfacing in that discussion and I've now bumped into it three or four times in completely unrelated areas, and I honestly can't tell whether it's one argument or several that just happen to rhyme.

Here's the move as I see it. Somebody proposes that there's a whole domain of facts that are real but causally inert -- nothing anyone believes or does is caused by them. Then somebody else says: alright, but if those facts can't push on us even a little, how is it that our beliefs and our talk about them are mostly *right*? We'd have to be wired to land on the truth about a domain that has no way of reaching us. That's a miracle, or at least an enormous unexplained coincidence. In the mind case the defender answers: no coincidence, the very same physical event causes the feeling and causes the report, so the two automatically line up. And then the objector comes back with: that explains why they line up, but it doesn't explain why the feeling is there at all, or why it's *this* feeling rather than nothing. I've seen the same shape in a conversation about whether numbers are real (if arithmetic facts are real but inert, how did we get so good at latching onto them?) and in one about moral facts (they're real, they don't cause anything, and our moral beliefs are plainly produced by upbringing and evolution, so why would they track the facts?). I even tried to run it on the past -- the past is gone and can't act on us directly -- but a friend said no, that case is different, the past leaves traces, and now I can't tell if that's a genuine disanalogy or a dodge.

So: does this move have a name? Is it one thing that different fields have given different local names to, or is the philosophy-of-math version a seperate argument from the mind version? If it does have a name, I want to know what it gets confused with -- it feels different from a plain 'you couldn't possibly know about that' skeptical move, because here everyone grants that the facts are real and that our beliefs about them are mostly true; and it also seems different from a view that denies the domain exists at all, since that view doesn't appear to have the problem. What I'm really after is the edges: what exactly has to be true of a domain for the coincidence complaint to even get off the ground, and is the 'one cause produces both the belief and the fact' reply a real escape or just a restatement of the problem? And a last thing, possibly naive: does anyone ever lodge this complaint against a view where the facts *do* cause our beliefs? If not, doesn't that make the whole thing a roundabout argument for letting the facts be causes -- which can't be right, since plenty of people seem to notice the coincidence and just shrug at it.
