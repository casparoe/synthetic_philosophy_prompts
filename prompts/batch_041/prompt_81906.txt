I'm a novelist, and the book I'm working on has a character who's a retired actuary, so I've been reading decision theory for about a year now and I've ended up believing something I'd like you to try to break. The view is this: rational choice just is maximizing expected value, full stop, and the Pasadena-style cases don't refute it. In case it helps to have the case spelled out — I don't assume you've got it memorized — you flip a fair coin until the first heads; if that first heads comes up on toss n, you get (-1)^(n+1) times 2^n over n, so the payoffs run +2, -2, +8/3, -4, +32/5, -64/6, and so on. To get the expected value you multiply each payoff by its probability (1 over 2^n) and add them up, and the terms collapse to 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., the alternating harmonic series, which happens to converge to about 0.69 if you take it in that order but which you can rearange to converge to any number you like. So there's no fact of the matter about the game's expected value, and on the standard account it has no expected value at all. My position is that this is not a counterexample. Undefined expectation just means the theory declines to rule, and a theory of rationality isn't obliged to have an answer in every possible situation — it's obliged to give the right answer whenever it gives one. Silence isn't error. (I'm assuming linear utility in dollars, no diminishing marginal value of money, otherwise the whole thing goes mushy and the puzzle dissolves.)
