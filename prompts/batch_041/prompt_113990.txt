I am a doctoral student in philosophy writing a chapter on the metaphysics of supertasks, and I would like your credence in one specific proposition, with the reasoning decomposed term by term. I care more about which sub-question carries the weight than about the final number.

The proposition: Thomson's lamp, specified as a real physical system rather than as an abstract sequence of states, has a determinate brightness at the end of the supertask, so that the puzzle's apparent indeterminacy is an artifact of underdescription rather than a genuine gap in the facts.

The setup is standard. At t = 0 the lamp is switched on; off at t = 1/2; on at t = 3/4; off at t = 7/8; on at t = 15/16; and so on, the remaining interval halving each time, so that infinitely many switchings occur before t = 1. The familiar verdict is that nothing fixes the state at t = 1: the pattern on, off, on, off, ... has no limit, and the story never says what happens at the limit point, so it is consistent both with the lamp being on and with its being off.

A colleague resists this. The description, she notes, describes no physical object, only a bare pattern of binary states. Replace it with an incandescent bulb whose filament has thermal inertia, and the physics may close the gap: filament temperature is a continuous quantity with its own dynamics, and once the switching is fast relative to the thermal time constant, the filament tracks the time-average of the driving signal rather than its instantaneous value. On that view the lamp has a determinate intermediate brightness at t = 1, and the verdict that there is no answer was an artifact of the idealized binary description.

I would like your credence that she is right, that the physical description determines the outcome. Please decompose it, giving a rough probability and one line of justification per term, including at least:

1. the probability that the scenario is physically possible at all, that is, whether anything forbids infinitely fast switching, infinitely precise timing, or the packing of infinitely many operations into a finite interval;
2. the probability that, conditional on its being possible, the filament's physical state has a well-defined limit at t = 1;
3. the probability that, where the pointwise limit fails, the correct value is the limit of the running time-average (a summability answer) rather than no value at all;
4. the probability that the better verdict is instead that the idealization has broken down, so that there is no fact of the matter about a system that cannot exist;
5. any further term you think the answer turns on.

Then combine the terms into a single credence, tell me which term it is most sensitive to and what would move it most, and, if you accept either the thermal-tracking or the summability answer, say what brightness it yields. Please write in American English.
