I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a course on the philosophy of punishment, and this term I also sit as the student, non-voting member of my state's Sentencing Advisory Commission. The Commission must vote next week on what to recommend to the legislature regarding a bill its drafters are calling the Innocence Risk Amendment. The bill would retain capital punishment but (i) narrow eligibility to a closed list of aggravating circumstances, (ii) create a standing Innocence Review Unit empowered to petition a court to commute a death sentence whenever it concludes that the probability of factual innocence exceeds five percent, and (iii) require the Commission to publish an annual estimate of the wrongful-conviction rate in capital cases. I've been asked to draft the short written opinion that will circulate before the vote: it should take a position — recommend the amendment, reject it, or recommend it with specific changes — give the reasoning, and be written so that the commissioners who most disagree with me can recognize their own position stated fairly before I answer it. I'd like a concrete bottom line, and where the bill's provisions matter, a concrete recommendation about what the language should say.

The part I actually care about is the philosophical undergrowth, so please don't stop at the usual slogans. Commissioner Alvarez has been arguing that the amendment is cosmetic: the Review Unit operates ex post, after conviction, whereas whatever risk we impose on innocent defendants is imposed ex ante, at the moment we authorize a capital scheme at all — so on her view the bill neither reduces the number of innocent people exposed to execution nor changes the deterrence calculus, it merely relabels a few of them as commuted. I want you to take that seriously and decide whether it is right. I also want the memo to work through the retributive angle honestly, including the possibility that a retributivist should be more opposed to this risk than a deterrence theorist is, rather than less, and through the trade-off a deterrence theorist has to own: if executions deter, they deter by being real, and the lives saved are bought partly with the risk imposed on the innocent, who are precisely the people retribution forbids us to punish. Please also address the five-percent threshold on its own terms — why five, why not one, why not zero — and whether a rule permitting us to keep a practice so long as its error rate stays under some number differs in kind from the ordinary toleration of wrongful conviction in non-capital cases, with attention to the fact that life without parole is also, for practical purposes, irreversible for someone who dies in prison.

Two further requests. The Commission's staff has given us working estimates — that something like four percent of capital convictions are of factually innocent defendants, and that each execution may deter some number of homicides — and I don't want the memo to rest on those figures as though they were settled; I'd rather it tell me how sensitive the recommendation is to them and what it would take for the estimates to reverse the conclusion. And at the end, please say plainly where your reasoning is most likely to fail: which single argument or piece of evidence, if it turned out to be true, would lead you to a different bottom line.
