Here's my line. I argue that the usual story about mental content gets the direction of determination backwards. Everyone tries to fix content on the producer side: a state represents whatever it was selected for, or is disposed to covary with, at the point where it's generated. I say that's the wrong end of the wire. What a state represents is fixed by what the systems that *consume* it are built to do with it — content lives at the producer/consumer interface, not in the detector. The frog's bug-detector doesn't mean fly, or small-dark-moving-thing, or frog food; it means whatever the snapping machinery, the ducking machinery and the mating machinery are each built to do with it, and where those come apart there's either no fact of the matter or a much finer-grained fact than we thought. Two consequences I lean on: the swampman case comes out fine with no historical or selection story at all (his consumers do the same jobs, so his states mean the same things), and misrepresentation is easy, because a consumer can be wired to do things the state doesn't actually support.

The room, roughly fifteen people. Four or five build computational models of navigation and decision-making in insects and rodents and think content-talk is a convenience at best. One philosopher of cognitive science is openly instrumentalist about content and will announce it in the first five minutes. One person came from biology and does insect neuroethology, and knows the messy data on the frog-type cases better than I do. And there's a Kant person who comes to everything and asks about normativity — nobody else in the room cares, but it lands, because I definately don't have a good answer.
