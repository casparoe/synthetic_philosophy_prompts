I sit on a departmental committee whose chair settles contested decisions with the line that two heads are better than one, and therefore that whatever the majority says about a proposal is the committee's judgement and more likely to be right than any of ours taken alone. Last week the proposal was one that our rules make acceptable only if it is both affordable and non-duplicative, and the vote went as follows. One member judged it affordable but duplicative, and voted against. A second judged it affordable and non-duplicative, and voted for. A third judged it unaffordable but non-duplicative, and voted against. On the proposal as a whole the majority was against; on each of the two conditions taken separately the majority was for. The chair treated the majority on the whole as the committee's verdict, and leaned on the slogan to say that verdict was the better-informed one.

What I want is the menu of precise claims that 'two heads are better than one' could actually be making: better at what — hitting the truth, avoiding error, having standing, or merely getting through the work — on what assumptions about the heads, and for what kind of question. Sort the readings into those that are near-platitudes, those that are substantive and genuinely defensible, those that are question-begging, and those that are simply false; say which of them the chair most plausibly means; and for each say whether it would actually license his move of treating the whole-package majority as the committee's considered judgement, and if so under what conditions. Where a reading turns on some procedure for combining individual judgements, set out what that procedure assumes and where the assumptions bite. Lay this out as a list I can take to the next meeting rather than settling on one reading and defending it.

If I have misdescribed the case, or if the request itself rests on a confusion about what a majority verdict is or about what such a slogan could be doing, tell me so plainly instead of working around it; I would rather be told that the line cannot bear the weight the chair puts on it than be handed a tidy list that pretends it can.
