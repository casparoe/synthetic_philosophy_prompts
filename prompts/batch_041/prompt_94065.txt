I write a humor-ish column for my old grad program's alumni newsletter, and this month I want to do a satire about Aumann's agreement theorem — not the usual "Bayes lol" stuff, I want something where the jokes actually come from the theorem's machinery, so that someone who works in decision theory laughs instead of wincing. We were arguing about it at a bar last week and someone said "we can't agree to disagree" like it was a threat, which is sort of the whole seed here.

So: you're a comedy writer who also happens to have read the economics literature — write like someone who knows the 1976 paper, not someone who's read a blog post about it. The conceit is an internal policy document from the People Ops team at an invented company (call it Brightpath Dynamics, or invent your own) that has adopted Aumann's result as binding corporate policy. I want it as a numbered-clause handbook section plus a short FAQ at the end, roughly 650-750 words, completely deadpan — no winking, no narrator stepping out to explain the joke, let the reader figure out what's being mocked. The humor has to come from the theorem's actual conditions: the common prior, common knowledge of the posteriors, updating by Bayes' rule, and the fact that it's a result about probabilities rather than about values or preferences. Let the company get those conditions subtly wrong in ways a specialist would catch but a hasty reader would miss. Some bits I'd love in there, feel free to reorder or cut: a laminated "Founding Prior" issued at onboarding; a mandatory Slack channel where everyone posts their credences until they converge; a clause that forbids employees from having private information while cheerfully permitting them to have different priors (i.e. exactly backwards); a rule that drags value disputes — office layout, whether the Q3 launch date is realistic — under a theorem that has no grip on them; and a deadpan admission somewhere that because every disagreement must be reported, nothing is ever common knowledge, so the policy is satisfied vacuously. Voice should be bureaucratic and cheerfully coercive, and invent a signatory (some kind of Chief Belief Officer) who misstates the theorem in the closing quote.

Before you start writing, think it through first — actually jot down Aumann's assumptions and decide which ones the company should violate, since the whole piece depends on that mapping being right. Then, after the satire, add a short author's note addressed to me (clearly outside the piece) that separates what's settled about the theorem from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this — whether the common prior is a modeling convenience or a substantive commitment, whether the result has any real teeth for actual human disagreement, whether it says anything at all about values. Don't sell a contested reading as the consensus view, and flag the spots where the satire takes liberties with the formalism for the sake of the joke.
