Question.

(a) [8 marks] The familiar "torture versus dust specks" case asks a chooser to compare two options: (A) one person is tortured for fifty years, and (B) each of 3^^^3 people (a tetration, vastly larger than any number we could write out) receives a momentary speck of dust in the eye. The case is usually presented as a stress test for the aggregation of harms. For this question, grant for the sake of argument that disutilities are real, comparable, and summable across persons. State precisely what the aggregating consequentialist is committed to in the original case, and say what the case is meant to show about ordinary moral intuition.

(b) [12 marks] Now consider a variant. Suppose disutility is not additive within a person: a second speck in the same eye hurts strictly less than the first, a third strictly less than the second, and so on, with each additional speck still adding some positive disutility. Suppose further that the specks may be distributed among the 3^^^3 people however the chooser wishes. Does the aggregating consequentialist's verdict between (A) and (B) change? Explain why or why not, and say what your answer shows about whether the force of the original case comes from the sheer size of 3^^^3 or from the arithmetic of adding harms.

Student's answer.

"There are two commitments at work in the aggregating consequentialist's position. First, the disutility of a single dust speck is a real disutility: it is small, but it is the sort of thing that enters the sum rather than being discounted to nothing. Second, disutilities belonging to different people can be added into a single total. Given both, the verdict in the original case is forced. Option (B) carries the disutility of 3^^^3 specks, and however small one speck's disutility is, multiplied by 3^^^3 it becomes enormous, far exceeding fifty years of torture. So the aggregator must prefer (A). The case is meant to embarrass untutored intuition, which recoils from the torture and reaches for (B) without noticing that it has thereby endorsed a far greater quantity of suffering than the one it was trying to avoid.

Part (b). The variant changes the per-speck disutility, but I do not think it changes the verdict. The total disutility of (B) is the sum of the marginal disutilities of the individual specks. Diminishing marginal disutility tells us that a speck's marginal disutility depends on how many specks the same person has already received — but the chooser cannot change the total by rearranging who receives what. Every speck delivered still contributes some positive disutility, and the multiset of contributions is fixed by the number of specks rather than by their arrangement. So (B) remains enormously worse than (A), and the aggregator still prefers (A). The size of 3^^^3 is doing the work here, not the arithmetic.

I can see one place where the arithmetic might matter. If all the specks could be concentrated on a single person, then, because each additional speck hurts less than the one before, the running total would grow more slowly than if the specks were spread across many people. But this is only the same point about diminishing returns, and it does not touch the fact that there remain 3^^^3 specks' worth of disutility to be paid. It is worth adding that this whole family of cases is an instance of the repugnant conclusion: once you permit a small amount of good or ill to be traded against a sufficiently large number of people, the arithmetic swamps everything else."
