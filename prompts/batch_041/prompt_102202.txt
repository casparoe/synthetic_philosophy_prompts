I sit on the ethics advisory panel of a small rewilding trust, a seat I took up last autumn after years of volunteering on its reserves; until this term the panel's business was almost entirely badger culling and volunteer safety, so I am out of my depth now that we have something substantive to decide. The trust proposes to reintroduce a native predator to its largest reserve, a four-hundred-hectare lowland site enclosed on every side by a deer fence and a ring of drainage channels. The aim is to bring down an overabundant rabbit population that is stripping the ground flora. The ecologists expect the predator to multiply quickly, overshoot its food supply within four or five seasons, and then decline through starvation, with a good deal of the predation falling on juveniles, and because of the fence nothing can disperse. We are also being asked to endorse the trust's draft welfare policy, which permits intervention in the lives of reserve animals only where the suffering in question is caused by humans, and construes that narrowly, so that predation, exposure and disease are classed as natural and left alone. One panel member, a retired biologist I respect, has already told me the whole difficulty is sentimental: the rabbits do not mind dying, she says, because they cannot grasp a future they are being deprived of, and the ones that are eaten are gone before they suffer much; the only real question is whether the vegetation recovers. Another member argues that the fence settles it, since everything inside it is in our care whether we like it or not, and the trust is therefore not restoring nature but running a very large outdoor enclosure, which should be governed as such.

Please draft the short written opinion I shall circulate before the panel meets on the fourteenth. It should recommend a concrete course of action — approve the reintroduction, reject it, or approve it subject to stated conditions — together with a timetable for review and a clear statement of which factual uncertainties would change the recommendation. The reasoning has to be something the ecologists and my retired colleague can actually answer, rather than a restatement of the claim that wild animals suffer; and it should engage seriously with the strongest case on the other side, which I take to be that a policy of intervening in predation would quietly convert every wild animal on our reserves into a managed one, and that the trust's purpose is to restore ecological processes, not to run a hospice. It would help if you said explicitly whether the fence changes the moral situation, and if so why, since that seems to me the hinge on which the whole decision turns.

Lead with the recommendation, and keep the whole opinion under six hundred words, in British English.
