I'm a doctoral student working at the boundary of philosophy of biology and cognitive anthropology, and I'm putting together a two-page proposal for a one-year departmental writing fellowship. I'd like you to turn a half-formed idea of mine into something submittable. The idea concerns the standard story about cumulative cultural evolution: roughly, that it requires transmission faithful enough for improvements to accumulate rather than dissipate, and that distinctively human imitation, teaching, and norm enforcement are what push transmission over that threshold. My worry is that most of the cases I actually care about — folktales, recipes, ritual sequences, tunes — are transmitted reconstructively rather than replicatively: the receiver rebuilds the item from partial cues, and the rebuilding is systematically biased. The usual repair is to say that the biases converge on stable forms (attractors in the technical sense), so the attractor supplies the invariance that fidelity was supposed to supply. I suspect this repair quietly runs together two different things — the fidelity of a transmission channel and the stability of a transmitted trait — and that once you separate them the threshold claim becomes either trivially true or untestable. What I want to know is whether the threshold claim can be restated so that it is substantive and does not presuppose a particular way of individuating the trait being transmitted; and I have a nagging suspicion that trait-individuation and transmission bias are not independent at all, which would make the question as usually posed ill-formed. I've argued about this with a friend who works on gene-culture models and we keep talking past each other, which is part of why I think the framing, rather than the evidence, is where the problem lives.

Please write the proposal. It should state the question so that it has a determinate answer; say what the main positions in the cultural-evolution literature currently hold and exactly where they leave the question open — refer to positions and arguments rather than to particular papers, since I'll supply the citations myself; set out the method and say why a conceptual argument is the right instrument here rather than, say, building a formal model or running a transmission experiment, and what the model-building option would actually buy that the argument doesn't; describe concretely what I'd be doing across the twelve months, including the point at which I'd abandon the framing rather than patch it; and say what a successful result would look like (I'm imagining a criterion for cumulative selection under reconstructive transmission, plus an argument that the criterion isn't vacuous) and what would count as the project failing. I'd like a small comparative case-study component — a couple of traditions with different transmission ecologies — and I need you to be honest about whether those cases can carry any real argumentative weight or are just decoration. Keep it to two pages, write it in the first person, and don't imply that I intend to collect new data.
