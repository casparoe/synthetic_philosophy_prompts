I am preparing the opening session of a graduate seminar on the unity of the proposition, and I want to begin with a poem rather than a lecture. Please write a villanelle (19 lines, with the usual rhyme scheme and refrains, in loose iambic pentameter) that enacts Bradley's regress rather than merely describing it. The regress, as I set it out for students, is this: suppose a proposition has two constituents, a and b, and a relation R that is supposed to unite them into a single thought. For R to do that work, R must itself be related to a and to b; but that further relating is a new relation, R1, which must in turn be related to a, R, and b; and so on without end. The unity of the proposition is the demand that the whole be one thing rather than a mere list, and the regress threatens to show that any relation invoked to secure that unity is just one more constituent needing to be united. The poem should be spoken by the relation, or by a constituent that keeps being asked to relate, and its two refrains should be the two horns of the problem: one refrain should express the regress's demand for another relation, the other should express the unity claim that the proposition is already one. Let the refrains alternate and finally meet in the closing quatrain, so that the form itself shows why the two positions keep passing each other. Do not use the name Bradley in the poem; you may use the word regress at most once and the word unity at most once. Do not resolve the tension by appealing to a primitive unity or by declaring the regress vicious; the final lines should leave the reader with the pull of both. It should be accurate enough that a metaphysician would recognize the problem, and it should work as a poem for someone who has never encountered it.
