I'm leading a session for a social epistemology reading group on what Condorcet's jury theorem is actually worth once you stop treating its assumptions as free, and I want to run it against a real decision rather than a toy model. The episode I've settled on is the launch decision for the space shuttle Challenger on the night before its final flight, on 28 January 1986. The facts I'll supply so we're all on the same page: engineers at the contractor Morton Thiokol, including Roger Boisjoly, argued that the unusually cold overnight temperatures would compromise the resilience of the solid-rocket booster's O-ring seals and urged a delay; a teleconference with NASA managers followed, in which Thiokol's own management reversed the engineers' recommendation under pressure, and the launch proceeded the next morning, ending in the loss of the vehicle and its crew. Please take the role of someone expert in both social choice theory and the history of engineering disasters, and give me a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions -- the theorem illuminating the case, and the case testing the theorem. Think carefully and show your reasoning rather than compressing it into a verdict.

Here is the framing I'd like you to work with, though I want you to interrogate it rather than simply apply it. Condorcet's theorem says that if each of a group of voters is more likely than not to get some binary question right, and their judgments are independent and sincerely expressed, then the probability that the majority is correct increases with the size of the group and tends to certainty. Two refinements should be in play. First, what the proof really needs is independence of errors, not independence of persons: if the individuals share a common bias or take their cues from one another, the group can converge on the wrong answer even as it grows. Second, distinguish aggregating conclusions from aggregating premises. A group can vote directly on the final verdict -- launch or delay -- or it can vote on the component factual premises from which that verdict follows, such as whether cold degrades the seals; the two procedures can deliver different outcomes even when every individual's judgments are held fixed.

Now map the episode onto the theory and back. Who are the jurors, and what exactly is the proposition being decided? What is each actor's competence, and where does independence or sincerity break down -- through hierarchy, through deference, through the pressure that produced the reversal? Does the choice between aggregating premises and aggregating conclusions change what the group should have concluded, and does the actual decision look more like one rule than the other? Then tell me what the episode does to the theory: is the theorem refuted, merely inapplicable, or confirmed once its assumptions are repaired, and what would a repaired version predict about a body like this one? Be explicit about the places where the theorem is simply silent -- about the content of the premises, about how competence is distributed, about what makes an error correlated. Please open with a short brainstorming list of candidate mappings before you commit to one, and be concrete throughout; groupthink is a label, not an analysis.
