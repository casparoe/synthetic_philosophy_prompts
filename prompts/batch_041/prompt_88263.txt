I teach a 90-minute session to eighteen upper-level undergraduates in a joint history-and-philosophy seminar on the origins of the First World War. They have had one lecture on the July Crisis, so they can place Sarajevo, the ultimatum to Serbia, the declarations of war and the invasion of Belgium in order, and they have heard nothing at all about the historiography. I want a runnable activity, not a lecture, whose point is the difficulty of the question "who is to blame for the war?" — and specifically the discovery that this question has no stable answer until you have decided *what kind of thing* you are blaming (a state, a government, a decision-maker, a population) and *what standard* you are applying (did it cause the war, or did it fail to prevent it, or did it intend it). I want them to make that discovery by being forced to produce a verdict themselves, under rules that make the choice of unit and standard unavoidable, and to feel the ground shift when the evidentiary basis of their verdict is disturbed. Nothing about this should be announced before they have done it.

The evidentiary record you build must cover at least these items, each with a date and each stated so that a reader can see how it might be turned either way: the assassination at Sarajevo and the Austro-Serbian quarrel it crystallised; the Austro-Hungarian ultimatum and Serbia's reply; the German assurance of support to Austria-Hungary in early July; the Russian decision for general mobilisation and the grip of the military timetable on that decision; French planning assumptions and the Franco-Russian alliance; British ambiguity and the Belgian question; the Anglo-German naval race; the Moroccan crises and the Balkan wars; and the German war-aims memorandum drawn up in September 1914. That memorandum must be presented as the record presents it in reality — drafted after the fighting had begun — so that the group has to decide for itself how much it can prove about intentions formed before it.

The activity must issue in a single artefact: a table apportioning responsibility for the war across the six principal powers, summing to one hundred per cent, agreed unanimously by the whole group. Design the rules so that the participants, not I, generate the pressure that makes this hard — the choice of unit, the choice of standard, the double-edgedness of the record, and the fact that a unanimous table will be reached by social convergence rather than by evidence. The verdict must then acquire stakes inside the fiction: it will be entered into the treaty as the legal basis for reparations, and it binds each nation as a whole, so that a delegate who dissented must still sign for their nation. Build in two or three later reveals, dropped at fixed minutes, that destabilise the verdict they have just reached — including, near the end, a reveal of the private individual tables they filled in before discussion began, so that they can see how wide the pre-discussion spread was and what their unanimity actually cost.

The debrief must map particular moments of the activity onto particular ideas, and it must name the Fischer controversy at the point where the group has already lived through it: the 1961 thesis that Germany deliberately sought a war of expansion and the objections that responsibility was distributed through a system of alliances and mobilisation schedules that nobody controlled. It must also connect the verdict to the actual war-guilt article of the Versailles treaty, whose words speak of the responsibility of Germany and her allies for loss and damage caused by the war and which supplied the legal basis for reparations without ever using the word "guilt", and to the German reaction to it. On the philosophical side, the debrief must map moments onto: causal versus moral responsibility; collective responsibility and the problem of many hands; the unit-of-agency question; counterfactual dependence as a test of causation and its failure under overdetermination; and the difference between assessing a decision as it looked ex ante and as it looks ex post. Do not lecture these; attach each to something the group did.

And be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by me in words: at minimum, the real temporal distance between the verdict and the people who paid for it, and the fact that the historiographical quarrel turned partly on archives that became available only decades later.
