I run a 90-minute ethics session at a philosphy summer school — 16 people, mixed undergrads and a couple of grad students, no reading assigned beforehand. I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the difference between a hard choice and a genuine moral dilemma: a case where both duties really bind, you can't do both, and so whatever you do you've done something you shouldn't have. Most of them think 'dilemma' just means 'tough call,' and I want the activity itself — not my voiceover — to rub their noses in the difference.

The bit I especially want them to feel in their gut is the agglomeration thing: if you genuinely ought to do A, and genuinely ought to do B, doesn't it follow that you ought to do A and B? And then the genuine-dilemma person has to deny that inference, and I want them to feel how strange and costly that denial is rather than just hear me assert it.

So: give me roles, rules, materials, a timeline, and a scoring scheme that doesn't need real money, arranged so they walk into the conflict themselves before anyone names it. Then a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the live positions — 'one duty always overrides the other, so there are no genuine dilemmas,' 'both bind and you do wrong whichever way you go,' and 'what looks like a dilemma is always really just uncertainty about which duty wins' — plus where the leftover bad feeling fits into each (I dont want them hearing the word 'remainder' until the debrief). Tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how to patch it live, and be honest about which feature of the view the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to just explain. Keep it playable with nothing but paper and a timer, and don't let any philosopher's name into the room until the debrief.
