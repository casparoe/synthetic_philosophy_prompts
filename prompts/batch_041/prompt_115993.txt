My partner and I signed the consent forms for our first IVF cycle last week, which has moved the ethics of embryo selection from something I read about to something I have to initial. Alongside the standard chromosomal screening, our clinic sells a panel of polygenic scores: one number per embryo, said to capture a small share of the variation in traits such as metabolic and cardiovascular risk, height, and educational attainment. My partner wants nothing to do with it, on the ground that using it would make us participants in something she wants no part of. My own instinct runs closer to the opposite: we are already choosing among embryos, and it seems to me a kind of self-flattering squeamishness to refuse information that bears on the child's prospects. I would like your actual credence — not a survey of what various authors have said, and not an assurance that reasonable people differ — in the following claim.

P: Given that we intend to have a child by IVF and will transfer one of the viable embryos, we have a defeasible moral reason to transfer the embryo with the highest expected well-being, and that reason is weighty enough that transferring a lower-scoring embryo stands in need of some countervailing justification.

Please decompose. Give a rough probability and one line of justification for each of the following, and add any term you think I have missed:

- whether a claim of this form is determinate enough for a number to attach to it at all;
- whether the comparison class is the embryos we happen to have, or also those we could produce by further cycles — i.e. whether the requirement is to pick the best available or to keep going until better is available, and what work the word available is doing;
- whether well-being is comparable across possible people, and whether it is a single scalar that a score can stand in for;
- whether the child's identity varying with the choice blocks the claim that anyone is benefited or burdened;
- whether a polygenic score is a proxy for a proxy, and whether the scores would even be valid for embryos of our ancestry and in the environments we would raise a child in;
- whether aggregate or expressive effects of the practice — inequality, stigma, the fact that the clinic is selling this — defeat the reason or make the act impermissible even granting it exists;
- whether the stance a parent takes toward a child, as a project rather than as something received, carries independent moral weight.

Then combine them into your credence in P, say which term you are least confident in and what evidence would move it most, and — since my partner's number and mine differ by roughly 0.4 — say which single sub-question most likely accounts for that gap. Finally, tell me whether your verdict on the permissibility of using the panel could come apart from your credence in P, and if so, how.
