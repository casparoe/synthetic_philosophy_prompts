I write about philosophy and the human sciences, and for the past three years I have been reporting on a dispute that has settled into two camps. Both camps have now agreed to an adversarial collaboration, I have been asked to draft the joint statement they will sign, and I would like you to write it.

The camps. The first holds that when you do not know which moral theory is correct, you should maximize expected choiceworthiness: take your credence in each theory as a weight, take each theory's verdict about how good an option is as a value on that theory's own scale, and choose the option with the highest weighted total. These people grant that the scales do not arrive in common units — one theory's ten is not another theory's ten — but they hold that the scales can be aligned by a normalization. Their favored device is to rescale each theory so that the spread of its verdicts across the options on the table is the same for every theory: divide each theory's values by the gap between its best and worst verdict on the menu, or by the standard deviation of its verdicts across the menu. Once that is done, they say, no theory dominates merely because its numbers are large. The second camp holds that the alignment is a fiction: there is no fact about how one theory's units map onto another's, so every normalization is a stipulation, and because different stipulations yield different verdicts, the weighted total is a number with no authority behind it. This camp prefers a non-aggregative rule — act on the theory you take to be most likely correct among those you take seriously, or confine yourself to acts that no theory you take seriously condemns.

The document. It should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered list of claims both camps can sign. I do not want platitudes; each agreed claim should be something at least one of the two camps once denied. Include at least: what the disagreement is a disagreement about; which cases are uncontroversial between them; what each camp concedes to the other; the conditions under which the disagreement makes no practical difference to what anyone should do; and what both would count as progress.

2. The disagreement itself, stated once in the words of each camp, in wording that camp would accept as its own. Where no single sentence can be offered that both accept as a fair rendering of one camp's position, say so, and give that camp's preferred sentence.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one of them would move at least one camp. My own list is below; correct it if it is wrong, and add what I have missed.

4. For every crux, the test, argument or case that both camps agree bears on it, together with each camp's prediction, written in advance and in a form that could be checked.

5. A closing diagnosis: which parts of this dispute are merely verbal or definitional — for instance about what counts as a theory, about what kind of claim the second camp is making (about the world, about our evidence, or about our moral vocabulary), or about which "should" is at issue — and which parts are substantive.

Candidate cruxes, offered as raw material: (a) whether intertheoretic comparisons are meaningful at all; (b) if they are, whether any normalization can avoid making the ranking of two options depend on what else is on the menu; (c) whether moral theories are the right things to hold credences in, given that the space of theories can be carved up in more than one defensible way, and whether verdicts change under re-description; (d) whether the anti-fanatical tendency of normalization — a very small credence in an extremely demanding theory does not swamp everything else — is a virtue to be explained or an artifact to be exposed; (e) what either view says about theories that assign infinite or unbounded stakes.

Cases to use, with the numbers I have been given:

- Two theories, held 50/50. Option A: first theory 10, second theory 0. Option B: first theory 0, second theory 10. My understanding is that both camps treat A and B as on a par here; if either denies it, the document should say why.
- Now add option C, which both theories rank below both A and B — say minus 90 for the first theory and minus 990 for the second. My rough arithmetic suggests that the standing of A against B is not stable under the addition of C. I would like both camps to state their predictions, and to correct me if I have gone wrong.
- A re-description case: take the same moral content and redraw the theory space — split one theory into two variants, or redraw the verdicts so that a theory that was spread out becomes flat — and say whether the verdict changes.
- The fanatic: you are 99.9 per cent confident in a moderate theory and 0.1 per cent confident in a theory on which the difference between two options is astronomically large, on the order of 10^30 units. Does the tiny-credence theory swamp the rest? Does the answer change when there are a hundred such theories, each held at a thousandth of a per cent, pointing to different options?
- The infinities: a theory on which the stakes are literally infinite, so that no finite probability multiplied by an infinite value can be compared with anything else.

Housekeeping. No citations, no reading list, no appeals to authority: the document has to stand on its own, and both camps know the literature. Format it as the numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table with columns for the crux, each camp's position in its own wording, the test, each camp's prediction, and what would move whom. Do not adjudicate who is right. Where a formulation could be put in two ways and one of them tilts, say so. The test of success is that both camps can point to the sentences they endorse and sign at the bottom.
