You're a philosopher who works on rational choice and decision theory, and you're good at defending the side of a debate that everyone else thinks is dead wrong. Before you commit to anything, brainstorm a little — write out the strongest version of each side's case, then give me the argument.

Here's the situation. Me, Dana, and Marcus are in a little club together. On Friday each of us seperately decides whether to mail in a postcard. If exactly one of us mails one in, that person gets $1,000. If two or three of us mail one in, the $1,000 gets split evenly among whoever mailed one in. If none of us mails one in, the $1,000 goes into the club's fund instead — and all three of us would honestly rather have that fund than the roughly $333 each we'd pocket if all three of us mailed one in. We all know all of this, and we all know we're all chewing on it right now.

Dana says we shouldn't send. Her argument: the three of us are going to reason about this the same way, so we'll all land on the same answer, which means either all three of us send or none of us does — and none of us sending is better for every one of us. My other friends think Dana's lost it, because sending is just better no matter what the other two do (if neither of them sends I get the whole $1,000; if one or both of them do, I still end up ahead of where I'd be if I sat out).

I want you to argue that Dana is right and that I shouldn't send. Take the best version of the objection seriously instead of waving it away, and say plainly what has to be true about the three of us for your argument to actually go through.

American English, please, and keep it tight — 600 words max.
