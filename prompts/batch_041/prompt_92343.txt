I am a retired natural scientist who has spent the past several years reading philosophy of biology, and I have been asked to interview a philosopher for our departmental seminar series. The guest defends a causal-role account of biological function—roughly, the view that a trait's function is the contribution it makes to a capacity of the system in which it occurs—and is skeptical of etiological, selected-effects accounts that tie function to a history of natural selection. I want the interview to be substantive rather than a survey of the debate. Please act as an expert philosopher of biology and, before you write anything, brainstorm the strongest pressure points for each account and the most likely replies. Then draft eight to ten main interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer the guest is most likely to give. The questions should be in the weeds, and they should be answerable by a careful model without consulting the literature; do not ask for citations, reading lists, or references to specific papers.

At minimum, include questions that do the following:

1. Force the guest to confront the system-selection problem. If any effect can be a function relative to some system—circulating blood, making noise, occupying space—what principled way does the causal-role account have to pick out the relevant system and the relevant effect without appealing to history? Anticipate the likely reply that the choice is relative to explanatory interests or a designated capacity, and follow up by asking whether that makes function claims interest-relative in a way that undermines their use in biology.

2. Press on malfunction and normativity. Causal-role accounts seem to say that a broken heart has no function, or has a function only in an idealized or type-relative sense, whereas etiological accounts can say it has a function it is failing to perform. Ask what grounds the difference between a malfunction and a mere absence of effect. Follow up on whether the account can explain why we should care about restoring the function, or whether it reduces function talk to description.

3. Ask about exaptation and the distinction between selection of and selection for a trait. Use a familiar case such as feathers or a swim bladder derivative. If a trait was selected for one effect and now reliably produces another, does the causal-role account call the new effect a function immediately? Does the etiological account need a further episode of selection, and if so, how long or how reliable must it be? Follow up on whether the guest's answer makes function attributions hostage to future evolutionary events.

4. Ask about recent evolutionary novelty, where a trait has a clear causal role but little or no selection history—for instance, a de novo gene in a lineage, a trait maintained only by drift, or a structure produced by developmental plasticity. What does each account say about whether it has a function? Follow up on whether the etiological account can avoid saying that a perfectly functional novelty is functionless until selection catches up, and whether the causal-role account can avoid saying that a selectively neutral byproduct has a function.

5. Ask about functions in ecology and ecosystem biology, where the bearer of the function is a population or species rather than a trait—for example, a keystone predator or an ecosystem engineer. Selection may act on individuals, not on the ecosystem-level role. Can either account make sense of ecosystem function without illicitly treating ecosystems as units of selection? Follow up on whether the guest would accept a pluralist or pragmatic account that allows different senses of function in different biological subfields.

6. Turn the tables with a molecular biologist who uses function talk constantly—for example, about the function of a regulatory sequence, a noncoding RNA, or a protein domain—but who has no evolutionary history in mind and often means only a causal role in a current cellular process. Ask whether the guest thinks this is a merely verbal dispute or a substantive disagreement about what biology ought to mean by function. Follow up on whether the causal-role account can capture the explanatory force of function claims in molecular biology without collapsing into the claim that anything a molecule does is its function.

7. Ask about trait tokens versus trait types and the reference-class problem. If a particular heart is malformed, or a particular token fails to pump, does the function belong to the token or to the type? How does each account avoid making every token's function depend on an idealized type? Follow up on whether this is a problem for both accounts or only for one, and what work the notion of a normal type is doing.

8. Ask about synthetic biology and engineered organisms. In a designed bacterium or a gene circuit, the function of a part is fixed by human intention and design, not by natural selection. Does the causal-role account handle this better than the etiological account? Does the etiological account need to appeal to artificial selection or design, and if so, does the analogy between natural selection and design do real explanatory work or is it a metaphor? Follow up on whether the guest would call an unintended but reliably produced side effect of a gene circuit a function of the circuit.

After the questions, add a short note identifying which one or two questions are most likely to expose an unstable commitment in the guest's position, and why. Please write in a polished, seminar-ready style, and do not draft the guest's replies—just indicate the likely reply in a phrase and then ask the follow-up.
