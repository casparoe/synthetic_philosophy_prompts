Since retiring from experimental physics, I have been reading philosophy in earnest and leading a continuing-education seminar for retired scientists and engineers; next month's session is on the distinction between an epistemic bubble and an echo chamber. I do not want the distinction explained to me—I want a plan for teaching it. The audience will arrive assuming the two terms are synonyms and that the whole phenomenon is a social-media pathology from which their scientific training immunizes them. I intend to frame the difference as one between a sampling failure and a calibration failure—an environment that withholds inputs versus one that has taught you to discount certain inputs—and I want that framing to earn its keep rather than become a gimmick. Please plan the explanation: the order in which the ideas must be introduced and why each depends on the last; the one carrying example from scientific practice, not the usual algorithmic feed, and what it must contain and leave out; the misconceptions this audience will form, including the immunity illusion, and the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off; the single closing question that would show whether they have grasped the distinction rather than memorized the labels; and what I should explicitly refuse to cover at this level. Begin with a single sentence stating the order in which the ideas must be introduced; then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of this request.
