I take heterophenomenology to be the right method for a science of consciousness: treat a subject's reports as data about how things seem to them rather than as authoritative reports about what is actually going on in their mind, combine those reports with everything else available—behavior, physiology, environment, relevant theory—and explain why the subject says what they say, treating the deliverable as a theory of the subject's beliefs about their own experience rather than a transcription of that experience. On this view there is nothing left over: for any question of the form "why do I experience X," the tractable question is "why does the subject say they experience X," and answering that exhausts what a science of consciousness can or needs to know.

I want the case against it, played straight. Which findings or arguments would count as genuine defeaters—say, cases where subjects are systematically and predictably wrong about their own experience in ways the method cannot capture without circularity; or phenomena that seem to require first-person access because no third-person substitute preserves what matters; or results suggesting the method's chosen data (reports, and the beliefs they express) are the wrong target altogether? Distinguish defeaters that should move a committed heterophenomenologist from ones they would rightly dismiss, and rank them from fatal to merely awkward. Then tell me which of these the heterophenomenologist would themselves concede as counting against the view, as opposed to what only critics say.

Finally, be honest about exposure: does the position risk anything, or has it been insulated by the move from "method" to "the whole of what there is to know"—and if the latter, is that a vice or simply what it means to take the third-person stance seriously?
