I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on the epistemic problems of consequentialism, and this year I want to open the term with Frank Capra's It's a Wonderful Life (1946) rather than with a toy case. The film turns on a device that audiences accept without argument: George Bailey, ruined and suicidal on a bridge on Christmas Eve, is granted a view of Bedford Falls as it would have been had he never been born — his brother dead, the town in Potter's hands, the people he loves diminished — and what he sees settles for him the question of what his life was worth. I want a critical reading that treats that device as a piece of moral epistemology rather than as sentiment, and that works in both directions: what the film shows about the problem, and where the problem exposes the film.

The problem I want you to work with is the one usually called cluelessness. Consequentialism assesses acts by their consequences, but for most acts the consequences run out indefinitely, into causal chains no agent can trace, so the agent cannot form any defensible estimate of an act's total value. Two grades of this should be kept apart and put to use. First, ordinary uncertainty, where the outcomes are enumerable and can be given rough probabilities, so that expected-value reasoning is at least formally available. Second, the deeper case, where the relevant effects are diffuse, non-enumerable, or so sensitive to distant contingencies that any probability assignment would be arbitrary, and where the expected-value remedy therefore begins to look like a formalism rather than a solution. Two further distinctions bear on the reading: the difference between an objective standard (what the act in fact brought about) and a subjective one (what the agent could reasonably have expected), and the fact that any consequentialist evaluation requires a comparison class — a baseline against which the act's difference is measured — where the choice of baseline can by itself reverse the verdict.

With that in hand, address at least the following, and say where the film resists the reading as well as where it supports it:

1. The film supplies its protagonist with precisely the epistemic good the cluelessness argument says is unavailable: a counterfactual oracle, delivered by an angel, that reveals the difference his existence made. Does the film therefore dramatize the problem, by showing what it would take to overcome it, or does it stipulate the difficulty away? Note that the revelation is given to the agent himself, and that it arrives only at the end of the story, when nothing is left to decide.

2. The vision is narrow: one town, one human lifetime, a handful of named relationships, no distant strangers, no future generations, no non-human effects. What work does that truncation do? Would the verdict survive a wider window, and on the film's own showing is the sign of the difference even determinate?

3. The baseline. George is compared with his own non-existence, the most flattering comparison available to him; he also had the option of leaving Bedford Falls, which he wanted and which the film treats as a temptation. Is the choice of baseline principled, or does it do the moral work invisibly?

4. Pottersville. Is the counterfactual world a welfare comparison or a moralized tableau — the film's own convictions about marriage, drink, sex, and commerce dressed as consequences? What does that tell us about how consequences get valued?

5. The ending. Does the film resolve George's case consequentially at all, or does his relief come from being known and loved by the town, which is a relation rather than an aggregate? If the latter, say what the film is doing philosophically in its last ten minutes.

6. The traceable chain. The film gives us two visible causal chains — the pharmacist prevented from poisoning a customer, the brother pulled from the ice who grows up to save a troopship — and these are exactly the sort of chains real agents never get to see. What does the film's reliance on them suggest about how we tell stories about consequences, and about why cluelessness is hard to dramatize?
