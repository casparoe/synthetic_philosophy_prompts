I have been arguing with two friends about whether left and right pick out anything real or whether they are just team jerseys, and I would like to know what principle actually fits my own gut verdicts rather than the slogans people repeat. So here are the cases I have worked through, with where I land. A pro-market libertarian who wants to abolish the minimum wage, privatize public services, and shrink welfare to nothing, but who is also for open borders, drug legalization, and same-sex marriage: right, no doubt about it. A single-party state that abolished private ownership of industry but jails dissidents and controls speech, movement, and the press: left, I think, and I resist calling it right even though it is as hierarchical as anything on my list. A nationalist party that runs a generous welfare state, nationalizes key industries, restricts immigration, and wants to restore traditional family roles: right. A conservative who defends a large welfare state and heavy redistribution, but only on the grounds that it keeps the poor from revolting and holds the country together: right. An anarcho-syndicalist who wants worker councils to replace both the state and the firm: left. A technocrat who says most political disputes are really engineering disputes and should be handed to experts and markets: not left, and if I am honest, closer to right than I would like. A rural environmentalist who wants to dismantle industrial agriculture, relocalize the economy, and return to smallholding and inherited custom: genuinely unsure, and the fact that I cannot place this one bothers me. A social liberal who cares only about equality of opportunity and not at all about inequality of outcome: left, but weakly.

My two friends offer rival accounts. One says the distinction is one-dimensional and moral: left means you think the prevailing hierarchies are unjustified and ought to be flattened, right means you think they are justified or at least tolerable. The other says there is no single principle at all, that left and right name coalitions which congealed historically around opposed blocs, and that a position counts as one or the other mainly by which coalition it belongs to or gets recruited into, so the content can drift while the label sticks; she likes to say the labels are a meeting point for people who already expect to be on the same side, and that is all.

What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of competing principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a fresh case without asking you again. For each clause, tell me which of my verdicts above forces it. Then run the principle on cases I have not given you, at least these: a movement that is culturally traditionalist and economically socialist; a billionaire-funded campaign for animal welfare and against factory farming; a movement that wants to secede from a liberal democracy in order to run its own illiberal enclave; and a party whose only plank is hostility to a foreign power. Tell me where my verdicts and the principle part ways, and which of my own verdicts the principle forces me to give up. If the honest answer is that the whole exercise rests on a mistake, that left and right do not carve anything at a single joint and no principle will fit, then say so plainly rather than manufacturing a tidy axis; I would much rather be told the question is confused than be handed a formula that papers over it. And tell me if any of this is parochial to the United States or to the last century, since I cannot tell from inside my own intuitions how much of them is just local weather.
