I'm not a historian — I fix bikes for a living — but I've gotten weirdly obsessed with what to do when a community's memory and the written record say different things. It started at Thanksgiving: my aunt tells the story of how my great-grandfather's farm was taken from him by the bank, and my cousin, who does genealogy as a hobby, has the actual deeds, and the deeds say he just stopped paying for three years. My aunt's version is, as far as the paper goes, false. And I noticed I didn't want my cousin to correct her. Not at the table, not ever, really. But then I started running other cases through my head and my gut got inconsistent fast, so what I want from you is to figure out what I actually think — I'm pretty sure there's a principle in there somewhere but I can't see it.

Here's where I land, case by case. (1) The old church downtown: the congregation's story is that their grandparents built it with their own hands, brick by brick; the archive shows a mill owner paid for the whole thing and the parishioners mostly just showed up for the photo. Leave that story alone, even though it's plainly wrong and even though it flatters them at the expense of a dead man. (2) The statue of the town founder: the records show he didn't found the town, he bought in a decade later and lost the land, and the date on the plaque is off by eleven years. Leave it, don't even fix the plaque. (3) The factory fire: the historical society's exhibit says thirty workers died, but the cemetery rolls and the old insurance file say eleven. That one I want corrected loudly, descendants told, plaque redone — memory doesn't get a vote there. (4) My own neighborhood's line about how "this was always a working-class neighborhood, we've always been the same people here" — the census and the parish records show it was mixed for decades, and people say that line at zoning meetings to fight new apartments. Record wins, no contest, and I'd say it out loud in the meeting. (5) A town that claims a right to graze on a certain common because of an agreement made "with the old families": the archive has no such agreement and the deeds run the other way. I think the record wins, sincere and old as the belief is. (6) But then the kind of case that's always in the news — a valley community whose oral history says the land was theirs and was taken, against a treaty and a survey that say it was ceded. There I think the memory wins and I don't much care what the archive says. And I know that's structurally the same as (5) and I can't tell you why I flip. Also, in both (5) and (6) the "record" isn't even clean: the survey in (6) was drawn up by the people doing the taking, and the archive in (5) turns out to be a 1930s compilation of interviews with people remembering things fifty years on. A friend of mine took one anthropology course and now won't shut up about how memory is either load-bearing or else it's just scenery, and you can tell which by asking what falls down if you take it away — I don't know if that's a real distinction or something he made up in the car, but it's the closest thing I've got to a theory.

So: is there a principle, or two, or three, that gets all six? If there is, state it as precisely as you can and tell me which clause is doing the work in each case — and if my friend's load-bearing/scenery thing is basically right, say so and say where it breaks. Then tell me what your principle says about a case I haven't considered: a town whose bridge went out in the flood of '55, and whose entire flood-control campaign for seventy years has been built on that memory, except the records show the bridge had been condemned for bad concrete and was pulled down by the county two years before the flood ever came. And I want you to be honest about how shaky this is — where is your reasoning most likely to be wrong, what would you have to find out about one of these cases to flip your verdict, and which of my six do you think I'm going to end up having to give up? I'd rather you tell me that than make it all fit.
