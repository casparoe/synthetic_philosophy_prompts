Write a deadpan mail-order catalog page — the kind of thing a mid-century hobbyist supplier would have printed and someone has since scanned onto the internet — for a consumer product called the Rigid Designator, offered by an invented firm. Give the firm a plausible name and the item a catalog number and a price, and write the page in the catalog's own voice throughout: no framing paragraph before or after, no winking at the reader, no explaining the joke. Around 700 words, in American English, arranged as follows: the product name with a one-line tagline; a short prose description; a bulleted "Specifications" list; a "Frequently Asked Questions" section with four or five entries; a warranty and returns clause; and three customer reviews, each followed by a one-line reply from the company.

The comedy has to come from the semantics, not from puns, so that someone who works on names, reference, and modality laughs rather than winces, while a reader who has never heard of any of this still finds it funny as a catalog page. The specifications and answers must therefore get the following right, without naming any book, author, or date and without citing anything:

- The device designates the same object in every possible world in which that object exists, and it does not — cannot — tell you which object that is. Reference is not descriptive content, so nothing resembling a sense, a cluster of descriptions, or a stereotype comes in the box.
- Reference is borrowed rather than computed: the term works only while attached to an unbroken chain of communication reaching back to an initial dubbing. Broken chains produce dangling or empty terms, and the grammar gives no warning.
- The premium add-on is the necessary a posteriori, and the joke about it is that it is a discovery, not an installation: you find out by ordinary empirical means, and only then learn that the finding could not have been otherwise. Identity statements joining two rigid designators behave the same way — necessary if true, known only afterward.
- Substituting a definite description for the term changes what is said, and the substitution fails precisely in the counterfactual cases the customer cares about.

Include, as the rival product, a competing item from a descriptivist firm — a definite description, cluster model, sold with the assurance that it picks out whatever uniquely satisfies the associated descriptions, in this world and in any world that happens to satisfy them, which is the only world the customer was ever going to visit. Let the target of the satire be the habit of treating these distinctions as gadgets that carry their referents around with them and as upgrades one can purchase in advance, rather than as a set of claims about how reference and necessity actually work.

Do not name any living person, do not include a bibliography or reading list, and keep the company, its staff, and its customers entirely invented.
