I'm running the last session of a final-year seminar on fiction and the dead, and this term it's Amadeus — Forman's film, though Shaffer's play would do just as well. What I want from you is a reading of it through the moral philosophy of dramatising real dead people: an essay-length piece I can use in the seminar, not a plot summary (I know the film, and I've sat through the Pushkin too — the poisoning is invented, 1830, and the film simply inherited it).

The philosophy I want worked through is the old puzzle about whether the dead can be harmed at all, given that a harm has to land on something. I've been splitting the possible landing sites into three, and I'd like you to test the split rather than assume it. One: the record — what actually happened and what the surviving evidence supports. Two: the person's standing — how they're held in memory, which isn't the same thing, since standing can be ruined by truths and repaired by falsehoods. Three: their project — whatever they were building, and whether the dramatist co-opts it for ends they'd have refused. I've also got a second gadget I've been calling the reanimation licence: the thought that fiction gets a graded licence to inhabit the dead, wider for the long-dead and the public, narrower for the recent and the private, and sensitive both to whether the invented material flatters or defames and to whether the work presents itself as rumour, confession or record. Push that idea hard. Tell me whether it earns its keep or is just a tidy way of saying some cases are worse than others, and if it breaks, say where.

Then the case itself. The film is built as a confession — the old Salieri in the asylum, telling a priest his version — and the audience is in on the joke that the version is false. So: does that frame get the film off the hook, because it flags itself as one man's story rather than history, or is it worse than a straight biopic, because it puts a falsehood into the mouth of the the very person it wrongs and then makes us sympathise with him for saying it? What is the harm here, if there is one, and who holds the complaint — Salieri, his music, the people who cared about him, or only the future of the record? I'm inclined to beleive that dramatisations don't overwrite the record so much as settle on top of it, and that later audiences can't prise the two apart. Say whether that's a real harm or an aesthetic anxiety in moral dress.

Be honest where the film resists all this, too. My students will tell me it's a fable about mediocrity and divine unfairness and that nobody who matters thinks Salieri really did it, and I want something to say to that beyond huffing. I'd also like two or three discussion questions for the group, and a line or two on whether your verdict flips for the other obvious case — Mantel's Cromwell, where the fiction repairs a reputation instead of wrecking one.

No citations or reading lists, I'll do the references myself, and don't quote the film at me at length. British English, please — I'll be marking this up and I'm tired of fighting the spellcheck.
