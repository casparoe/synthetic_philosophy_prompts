For a philosophy club presentation, I'm defending a position I know sounds heartless: that total utilitarianism comes out of the utility monster case unscathed, and that the right response to the monster is not to patch the theory but to stop trusting the intuition that recoils from it. What I want is a map of what would count against that — which cases, arguments, or formal results should actually move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and which of them a committed total utilitarian would concede rather than dismiss as question-begging. Two things worry me in particular. First, whether the monster is a special case at all: if the same verdict falls out of a large enough population of ordinary beings whose lives are barely worth living, then I am defending something broader and stranger than the monster. Second, a variant I invented for the talk — a "reverse monster," a single being whose misery grows so fast that any increase in anyone else's happiness makes it vastly worse off — and I cannot tell whether total utilitarianism's apparent instruction to everyone else to accept a miserable life in order to spare it is a reductio of my position or just the same bullet I already claimed I would bite. Tell me which of these is load-bearing, what if anything could falsify my claim, and whether I have quietly rigged the position so that nothing counts against it.
