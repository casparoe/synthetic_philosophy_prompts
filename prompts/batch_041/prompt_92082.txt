I'm in a low-key reading group on ancient ethics — no grades, about eight of us, we take a chunk a week and argue for ninety minutes — and I've been handed the opening of Cicero's Cato Maior (De Senectute) to kick off next week. I've never read it before, and I'm going off the old Loeb translation by Falconer that's floating around online, with occasional glances at the Latin when I can't tell what the English is doing. What I can't get to sit still is a stretch near the beginning, where Cato has just finished telling Scipio and Laelius that griping about old age is a character problem rather than a problem with age itself — 'to those who have not the means within themselves of a virtuous and happy life every age is burdensome,' and the men who say old age 'stole upon them' faster than they expected have only themselves to blame, since old age creeps up on youth no faster than youth creeps up on childhood. Then he says: "I am wise because I follow Nature as the best of guides and obey her as a god; and since she has fitly planned the other acts of life's drama, it is not likely that she has neglected the final act as if she were a careless playwright. And yet there had to be something final, and — as in the case of orchard fruits and crops of grain in the process of ripening which comes with time — something shrivelled, as it were, and prone to fall. But this state the wise man should endure with resignation. For what is warring against the gods, as the giants did, other than fighting against Nature?"

What I want help with is what exactly he's claiming here and whether the pieces actually fit together. Is the argument meant to be a design argument — nature is a provident playwright, so the last act must be well made too — and if so, doesn't he give it away in the very next breath by calling the end of life shrivelled and ready to drop off the tree? Or is the fruit simile doing something more clever: the fruit is good precisely when it's ripe, and the shrivelling and falling just is what ripeness looks like from the outside, so nothing is conceded? I keep going back and forth on whether 'endure with resignation' is a climbdown from 'not burdensome at all' or just a fancy way of saying the wise man isn't going to throw a fit about a process that's completing its own nature. My tentative thought is that he's running two arguments that got bundled together by the translation: (i) old age isn't bad for the virtuous, because virtue makes you independent of bodily pleasures and other externals — that's the 'means within themselves' line — and (ii) old age may well be bad, but it's natural, and fighting nature is impious, which is the giants line. Those seem to me to pull in different directions, since (ii) sounds like it grants the complainers their premise and then tells them to shut up. Relatedly, I wondered whether (i) is an ancient cousin of what people now call the disability paradox or response shift — the finding that people who lose capacities often report their lives going about as well as before — except Cato doesn't seem to be saying you adapt to the loss; he seems to be saying the complainers were wrong all along about what makes a life good, so there's nothing to adapt to.

One more thing, since a friend in the group who's read alot more Cicero than me keeps bringing it up: in the preface Cicero says he gave the speech to Cato rather than to Tithonus — the fellow who got eternal life but not eternal youth, and who Cicero says he passed over because there'd be 'too little authority in a myth.' My friend thinks that choice is doing real work, because Cato the Censor was the stern anti-Greek moralist who supposedly only took up Greek letters late in life, so putting the pro-old-age case in his mouth makes it more credible as lived experience and less like a philosopher's consolation. Is that right, and does it cut the other way anywhere? And can you help me see whether 'follow Nature as a god' commits Cato to thinking that whatever nature does is thereby good — which would make the resignation line vacuous, or worse, make any complaint about aging automatically impious — or whether there's a distinction available between a process being natural and its being good that I'm just missing because I'm reading in translation? If it helps, tell me which reading you'd defend and what in the passage would have to be doing the work for it.
