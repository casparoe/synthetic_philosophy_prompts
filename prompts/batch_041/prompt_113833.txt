I'm a neuroethics-ish PhD student and there's a pattern I keep tripping over that I want someone to actually unpack, no lit review please, I can't look anything up right now.

First practice. Where I did my clinical rotation, we declared death on the whole-brain standard — brainstem included, no respiratory drive, apnea test, the works — and then the transplant team took organs. Same hospital, different ward: patients in a persistent vegetative state, cortex basically gone, no credible evidence of any experience at all, and they're alive, full moral status, nobody withdraws anything without family sign-off, nobody would dream of harvesting. If the thing that matters morally is the computation that supports experience, those two groups look like they belong on the same side of a line and they're on opposite sides. So: what does the whole-brain criterion actually presuppose about which computations matter? That the morally relevant thing is the integrated functioning of the organism as a whole rather than consciousness? That we've quietly assumed brainstem function is necessary for any experience and that assumption is false? Or is the practice just underdetermined — consistent with several different views about where the line falls, with the diagnosis doing pragmatic work rather than metaphysical work?

Second practice, same machinery I think. My lab's institution recently decided that cephalopods and decapods are in scope — cuttlefish get anaesthetic, they get welfare oversight, there's a committee. The fruit flies in the room next door have a centralized brain, descending modulation of nociceptive signalling, and do something that looks a lot like motivated avoidance of noxious heat, and they get nothing, no protocol, no committee, no paperwork. Nobody in that building believes the fly computation was measured and found insufficient. So what commitment does the invertebrate list presuppose? A threshold or cluster view where you need a particular architecture — central nociceptive pathway plus some kind of integrative or self-modulatory capacity? A view where the real criterion is whether there's a plausible evolutionary story about what suffering would do for the animal, so it's the adaptive function and not the mechanism? Or is it tractability and who lobbies, with a theory bolted on afterward?

Here's the part I actually want the time spent on. Suppose my PI wants a lab policy that isn't a list of taxa but a criterion you apply to any system: a system is covered if it has (i) receptors that transduce noxious stimuli, (ii) a central pathway carrying that signal, (iii) evidence the signal modulates the system's own processing or behaviour in a way that isn't a fixed reflex, and (iv) a plausible account of what the experience would do for the system. What does that presuppose that the taxa list doesn't, and what does it quietly rule out — does it rule out insects, or does it rule in something like a software system that satisfies i through iv, and if so is the policy fine with that or does it need an extra clause about being an organism? Related: is the attribution in these practices happening by matching a case to a paradigm (adult human, warm, moving, familiar) or by inference to the best explanation, and does that choice explain the two practices better than any claim about the underlying computation does?

And then tell me where the hospital practice and the lab practice would have to come apart philosophically, and where they can share the same underlying view. Which parts of each practice are actually forced by the theory they presuppose, which are just convention or institutional history, and what would have to change on the ground if we swapped in a rival view about what computation matters.
