I am a fourth-year PhD student in machine learning working on robustness and safety, and in three weeks I will present at a small interdisciplinary workshop whose stated theme is what a safety guarantee ought to guarantee. The audience will be about a dozen people, and I need to prepare for the question period rather than the talk. Four of them are statistical learning theorists who work on PAC-style and agnostic bounds and on adversarial robustness, and who treat a guarantee as something that must hold against an adversary free to pick any input from a specified set. Three are researchers from the catastrophic-risk side of AI safety who reason in terms of adversarial optimization and tail events and who are reflexively suspicious of anything that looks like averaging the disaster case away. Two or three are empirically minded people who build and evaluate systems and have little patience for purely definitional disputes. There are no philosophers and no decision theorists in the room.

I am going to defend the following thesis: that in alignment, the right target is neither a worst-case guarantee nor an average-case guarantee in the usual senses, but what I am calling a policy-relative guarantee. Let me fix the terms here so the objections have something concrete to bite on. A worst-case guarantee, as I will use the phrase, says that the system's loss is bounded by some epsilon for every input in a specified set S, typically an input ball or a distribution-free set. An average-case guarantee says that the expected loss under a distribution D is bounded. My proposal is a third object: fix the deployment policy pi that actually selects which inputs the system is driven to see; define the reachable set R(pi) as the inputs that pi can produce with non-negligible probability; and require that the loss be bounded for every input in R(pi), while permitting arbitrary failure on inputs outside it. I will defend this as the correct target on three grounds: that alignment failures only matter on inputs the system is actually driven to; that the worst case over S is frequently vacuous or unachievable for reasons that have nothing to do with alignment; and that the average case is the wrong shape because it licenses a small probability of catastrophe, whereas a policy-relative guarantee keeps the tail while localizing it to the reachable regime. I will also claim that this is a genuinely different object from distributional robustness and not merely a reweighting of it, and that its achievability profile differs from both of the standard targets.
