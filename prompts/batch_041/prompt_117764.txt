Q4 (10 marks). Suppose a neurosurgical technique is developed that lets a single neuron be replaced with a silicon chip that does exactly what the neuron did, so that the patient's behaviour, memory and personality are completely unaffected at every step. Sam undergoes the procedure over the course of a year, one neuron at a time, until every neuron in his brain has been replaced. And here's what the student wrote. They had about 15 minutes, closed book, no notes:

This is the Ship of Theseus problem applied to a brain, so the answer depends on which theory of personal identity you accept.

Take animalism first. If we are essentially living human organisms, as Olson argues, then Sam is the animal, and the animal dies when the last biological neuron is removed and thrown away. What is left in the skull is a very sophisticated machine with all of Sam's memories, but it is not Sam, in the same way that a robot with my diary is not me. The animalist says the silicon being is a replica of Sam.

If we accept a psychological criterion instead, the answer comes out differently. Locke's memory criterion on its own will not do: Reid's brave officer case shows that memory links do not chain together in the right way, since the general remembers the brave officer and the brave officer remembers being flogged as a boy, but the general does not remember the flogging. Memory gives different answers depending on how you link the chain, so we need more than memory — which is where Parfit's Relation R comes in.

Parfit is a reductionist: he thinks there is no "further fact" about personal identity over and above the facts about psychological connectedness and continuity, which is what he calls Relation R, and his claim is that identity is not what matters — R is what matters. On this view the question "is the silicon being Sam?" is not the important question. Since Sam's memories, character, plans and dispositions are completely preserved at every step, R is never broken, so what matters survives the procedure. Parfit also thinks that personal identity comes in degrees: after the first 50 neurons are swapped Sam is 99% the same person, after half the brain is swapped he is 50% the same person, and there is no sharp point at which Sam stops being Sam.

Parfit's closest continuer theory gives the same result. The silicon being at the end of the year is the closest continuer of Sam, so it is Sam. This has the same shape as his teletransporter case: the person who is rebuilt on Mars has all of your memories and traits, and Parfit says that counts as surviving in the only sense we should care about.

So the objection from animalism does not work, because it assumes that being the same organism is what matters, and that is exactly the assumption Parfit rejects.
