My epistemology supervisor's verdicts on logical omniscience cases are these. A mathematician who has proved A and A→B in separate sessions but has never put them together does not know B. A student who knows the premises and sees the conclusion immediately but has never explicitly formed the belief does know it. Someone who believes a contradiction does not thereby know an arbitrary Q. A computer that has a proof of P in memory but whose output is disabled, so it never forms the belief P, does not know P. An agent whose belief is logically equivalent to another in a different notation, and who has not recognized the equivalence, does not know the other. Next week I am presenting a new case: an agent has a reliable unconscious inference module that has already formed the belief P in her from her other beliefs, and she would assert P if asked, but the derivation is extremely long and she can neither access nor understand it; she has no other route to P. What verdict will my supervisor give, and why? Which rule is she implicitly running -- explicit representation, feasible inference, reliable process, or accessible justification -- and which of her earlier verdicts force her to abandon that rule? Which further case would distinguish the remaining candidate rules?
