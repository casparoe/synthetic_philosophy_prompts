Im putting together a reading group on decision theory and I want an essay on Death in Damascus. Quick reminder of the case in case you need it: Death tells you he has an appointment with you tomorrow, but won't say which city; you can stay in Damascus or flee to Aleppo; Death has already predicted which you'll do, and whichever city you pick is where you die.

What I want is a proper argument, not a survey. The thing that interests me is the charge that causal decision theory comes out looking not just wrong here but incoherent — that it has no stable recommendation, because whatever you're about to do, it tells you the other option is better; and that the standard patch (letting the agent settle on a mixed strategy at the point where every option looks equally bad) is either an ad hoc extra rule or a quiet concession that the case isn't really a decision problem at all. Take a side on that. Does Death in Damascus genuinely embarass CDT, or is the instability a sign that the scenario is under-described, or that an agent who reasons this way has already made some mistake about what she controls? Is evidential decision theory any better off, given that it seems simply to shrug and say you're doomed either way — is that a verdict or a cop-out?

Then test your own conclusion against two variants. First, Death's prediction is only 70% reliable rather than certain: does that change which theory looks bad, and does it change your verdict? Second, you can pay a small cost to have the choice made by a genuinely chancy device, a coin say, rather than by you. Some people think this is where the case really bites, because any theory that says the coin is worthless seems to have missed something. Work out whether it has.

End with a clear verdict about which theory, if any, the case tells against, and how confident you are.
