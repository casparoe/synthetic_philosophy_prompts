After forty years in a metallurgy lab I retired, and for the past three years I have been reading philosophy with far more appetite than method, chiefly epistemology. I am trying to write up a handbook of the corrosion data my group accumulated over those decades: some four hundred claims, each one resting on runs I would once have staked my professional reputation on, and each one, I think, still something I am entitled to assert. It has also seemed to me that the only honest preface to such a book would record that at least one of its claims is wrong, since no handbook of this kind has ever come through a decade of use intact. That preface sentence is, I notice, itself one of the book's claims. A philosopher in my reading group tells me this is the old preface paradox, and that it is not a curiosity about prefaces at all but an argument that proves far too much: on his reading it shows that no one can rationally hold a large body of results, and, pressed a little further, that the whole business of accumulating knowledge by accumulating claims is incoherent. He suspects the trouble is a quantifier slip in the second step, but I do not see that the step needs any particular claim to be the false one, so I have written his argument out and cannot find the weak link. (1) For each of the four hundred claims, my evidence is strong enough to make believing it rational. (2) The track record of comparable handbooks, together with the sheer number of claims, makes it rational to believe that at least one of them is false. (3) If I rationally believe p and rationally believe q, then I rationally believe their conjunction. (4) So I rationally believe each claim, and, by repeated application of (3), I rationally believe their conjunction. (5) But by (2) I rationally believe the negation of that conjunction. (6) So I rationally believe a contradiction, which cannot be. (7) Therefore one of (1) to (3) must go, and since I will not give up (1), either the track-record belief or the closure principle has to be abandoned.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I would rather keep both (1) and (2) and surrender (3), but I want to see the bill before I pay it, and I am worried that the bill is larger than it looks: if I give up (3), what becomes of the weaker things that seem to survive it — that I should not be taken aback when someone asserts the conjunction of two things I believe, that I should be willing to act on the two of them together, that I should accept a bet whose payoff requires both, that a single false line somewhere in four hundred is not a scandal but the expected state of a working handbook? I would also like to know whether it matters that my honest preface is one of the claims that (1) covers, and whether this really is the same problem as the lottery, where one ticket must win and yet each ticket looks hopeless — or whether the resemblance is a trap. Tell me where the argument goes wrong, or, if it does not go wrong, which of its plausible steps is cheapest to give up and what the alternatives cost, including what I lose if I keep (3) and drop (2), and what I lose if I keep both and simply decline to believe any of the four hundred outright. Please write your reply as continuous prose, in the manner of a long letter — no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold or tables.
