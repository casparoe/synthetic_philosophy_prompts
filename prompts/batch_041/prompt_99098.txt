So my reading group has been going at the demandingness objection for what feels like forever and I'm starting to think we're not actually disagreeing about the thing we think we're dissagreeing about. Here's the shape of it as I see it. The critics say: look, if this theory is right then almost everything I care about — my friendships, my research, the novel I'm half-way through, the fact that I want to spend a Saturday doing nothing — is at best a permitted indulgence and at worst a standing violation, because there's always someone I could be saving with the money and the hours. The consequentialists come back with one of a few moves: that the objection just assumes a non-consequentialist picture of what reasons we have and so begs the question; or that the theory was never supposed to match our pre-theoretic intuitions anyway and the demandingness is a feature, not a bug; or that a suitably built theory can give the agent room — a prerogative, a threshold, a rule that leaves space for the personal. And then the critics say none of that touches the real complaint, and the consequentialists say the real complaint was never made precise, and we go round again. It's been like this for a year. Nobody's changing their mind and everyone thinks the other side is just refusing to see it.

Here's my hypothesis, and I'd like you to stress-test it rather than just tell me it's right or wrong. I think two different complaints are getting filed under the same word. One is a straight normative claim: the theory gets the weight or the content of some reasons wrong — it fails to register that I have reasons to live my own life that aren't merely the reasons everyone else has, or it mis-locates the line where permissible self-concern stops. That one is a real disagreement about reasons and could in principle be settled by better arguments. The other is not really about what the theory says I must do but about what it makes of me: it's the thought that if the theory is true then a whole way of being — someone who is committed to a project for its own sake, who loves particular people in a way that isn't exchangeable — becomes unintelligible or self-deceived, and that this is a cost the theory can't pay off. That second one feels like it's about meaning or self-understanding rather than about duties, and I suspect it's the one doing the real work in the impasse and the one nobody can argue for or against.

And then there's a third thing I've been chewing on that I havent seen us discuss properly, and I wonder if it's where the hidden question actually lives. The usual demandingness cases are single big asks — give up your kidney, donate half your income, throw yourself on the grenade. But what about the aggregate of small ones? No single act of answering the email from the student, or taking the extra shift, or covering for a colleague, is unreasonable on its own; each is clearly permitted, maybe even required. And yet a life made entirely of those is consumed, and the theory seems to say that's fine because each step is fine. So demandingness might not be a property of any act at all but of a pattern, and the two sides may be running single-act reasoning against a pattern-level complaint. If that's the real seam, then neither the prerogative move nor the begs-the-question move is aimed at it, which would explain why the whole thing feels like ships passing.

So what I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right. Is this a substantive disagreement about reasons, a verbal one about the word demanding, two groups answering different questions (one about the moral status of acts, one about the intelligibility of a life) under a single heading, or a hidden question about act-level versus pattern-level evaluation? Which reading best fits what each side actually says? And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly what would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result about aggregation, a case that forces one side to commit — or whether this is one of those debates where no amount of argument should be expected to end it, and why. Write it out in plain continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or bold or tables, like you're explaining it to me in an email. I'm not after a reading list or who-said-what; I just want the shape of the disagreement itself.
