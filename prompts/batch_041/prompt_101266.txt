I sit on the admissions council of a small league of states that was set up on roughly Kantian lines -- the whole idea being that peace comes from a federation of free states rather than from one world republic. Our votes over the years have been pretty consistant but I've never been able to say what rule we're actually running. We admitted a federal republic with an elected legislature and an executive that can't go to war without the legislature's vote, and we admitted a small merchant republic with a very narrow franchise -- property owners only -- whose assembly nevertheless has to approve every tax and every campaign, and which hasn't fought anyone in eighty years. We turned down an absolute monarchy that swore up and down it would never attack anybody, and we turned down a state that calls itself a republic but whose 'first consul' picks his own successor and can raise troops and money on his own say-so. Next month we vote on a hereditary principality: the prince inherits the office, but he can't declare war or levy taxes without the consent of an elected assembly, and the place has been at peace for decades and wants in.

Tell me what we should decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves. I suspect we're running one of three rules -- (a) the real test is whether the legislative power is separated from the executive and holds the war power, in which case a constrained prince is fine; (b) the real test is that the rulers answer to elected representatives, in which case any hereditary ruler is out no matter how constrained; (c) the labels don't matter and we're really just asking whether the constitution makes war hard and the state has kept the peace. Say what each rule predicts about the principality, which of our past votes are consistent with which rule, give me your best guess and the reason for it, name the closest analog among our precedents, and say honestly what principle we'd be adopting either way. Then name the one further case -- not the principality -- that would actually tell the three rules apart, and why that case rather than another.

Do it as: Recommendation (one paragraph); Candidate rules (three short paragraphs); What each predicts (a few lines, a small table is fine); Closest precedent (one paragraph); The case that would settle it (one paragraph). No citations, I don't need them.
