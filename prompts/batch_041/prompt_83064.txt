Write a sestina on the problem of universals.

The speaker is sorting a tin of buttons into piles: the ones that go together and the ones that don't. The poem must stay inside that activity. It may say what the hands are doing, what the light is like, what the speaker notices and fails to notice, what gets set aside; it may not step outside the scene to explain it, and it may not name the problem or any position on it. The philosophy has to live in the sorting.

Formal requirements:

- Six stanzas of six lines, then a three-line envoi. The six end-words are the same six words throughout, permuted in the traditional way (the last end-word of a stanza opens the next, then the first, then the fifth, then the second, then the fourth, then the third). The envoi must contain all six, with at least three of them occurring mid-line rather than at a line-end.
- The six end-words must be ordinary words, not terms of art. None of them may be a word a philosopher would use as a label — no "universal," "particular," "property," "essence," "concept," "kind," "resemblance," "abstract," "type," "instance," and the like — and no such word may appear in the body of a line either. Among the six, include at least one noun, one verb, and one adjective.
- One of the six must be "same." The poem must exploit the difference between numerical sameness (this is the button that was in my hand a moment ago) and qualitative sameness (this button is like that one), and at least one line must leave the reader unable to settle which is meant. Do not disambiguate that line anywhere in the poem.
- Without naming any of them, the poem should enact at least three positions from the debate over what makes a class a class. Draw on at least three of the following, which are described here so that the poem need not presuppose them: a pattern existing before and independently of the copies (buttons struck from one die); a pattern wholly present in each copy; the unifying work done by the word, or by the sorting itself, rather than by anything in the tin; a chain of pairwise likenesses with no shared pattern anywhere, each button resembling its neighbours and none resembling all; and the view, found in the medieval Latin tradition, that what grounds a true common predication is neither a thing shared among the particulars nor a concept in the sorter's mind but a "status" — each particular's being-something, its being red or being round, which is not itself a further item in the world. Use at least three of these, and let the poem come down in favour of none of them.
- The envoi must address a second person — a child who will inherit the tin, the next person to open it, or the reader who has now met the same six words in seven orders — and the repetition itself should be doing the work there rather than being remarked upon.

A reader who knows the debate should be able to say which positions the poem has staged; a reader who doesn't should find a poem.

After the poem, give a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "form": a one-line statement of the form as executed, giving stanza and line counts.
- "end_words": array of the six end-words, in order of first appearance.
- "sense_shift": array of objects, one per end-word that carries more than one sense, each with "word" (string), and "occurrences" (array of objects, each with "sense" — a short gloss — and "where" — stanza and line, e.g. "3.4", counting the envoi as stanza 7).
- "undecidable_line": object with "text" (the line, quoted), "location" (stanza and line), and "why_undecidable" (one or two sentences).
- "positions_enacted": array of objects, each with "position" (a short label), "where" (the stanzas and lines carrying it), and "how" (one sentence on the device that carries it).
- "envoi_layout": object with "end_words_at_line_ends" (array), "end_words_within_lines" (array), and "addressee" (string).
- "left_open": one or two sentences on which question the poem refuses to answer.

Output the poem and then the JSON, and nothing else.
