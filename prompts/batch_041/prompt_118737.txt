I'm taking a grad seminar on causation and I've gotten kind of obssessed with causation by omission, to the point where my friends are sick of hearing about it. I want to write a dialogue I can bring to the seminar to organize my own thinking, and I'd rather you didn't just hand me the standard textbook lines. Could you write it for me? Take the role of someone who really knows the metaphysics of causation — difference-making accounts, preemption, the whole toolkit — and then split that expertise into two characters who genuinely disagree. One of them thinks an omission can be a bona fide cause (the gardener's not-watering really did cause the plant to die, in the same sense that watering would have caused it to live), the other thinks that's a category mistake — that only events stand in causal relations, and "omission-causation" is really our way of assigning responsibility or of picking out the standing conditions we happen to care about. Neither should be a dummy; I want to actually worry about which one is right.

To keep it from floating off into abstraction, have them argue about a concrete case I keep getting tangled up in: a small town floods because the operator whose one job was to crank open the levee gate didn't do it — the gate was held shut by a spring that's always been there, so nothing "happened," it just stayed shut and the water went over the top. Now here's where I want it to get in the weeds, because the surface question is boring and I already know both of the easy answers. I want them to fight about (a) whether "the omission caused the flood" and "there was a cause which happened to be an omission" are actually two different claims or the same one dressed up, (b) whether an omission can only count as a cause relative to some norm or expectation — if no one had ever assigned the operator that job, would we still say his not-cranking caused the flood, or would we just say the spring did? — and (c) a thing a friend keeps bringing up that I don't fully get: whether an omission should show up as its own node in a causal diagram, or whether the honest picture just leaves the edge out and the "omission" is an artifact of how we draw the graph. Have each of them press the other on the cases that are hardest for their own view, not just the ones that are easy.

A few ground rules: no reading lists, no citations, don't tell me who said what in the literature — I can't look anything up right now anyway and I don't want the dialogue to turn into a name-dropping exercise. Both characters should be equally smart and should actually respond to each other's specific points rather than talking past each other, and it should feel like a real argument that could definately go either way, not a setup where one side wins. Before you write the dialogue, brainstorm for yourself for a few lines about the strongest version of each position, and then write it out. Maybe 800 to 1200 words is fine.
