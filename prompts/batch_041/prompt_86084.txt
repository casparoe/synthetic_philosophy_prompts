I'm teaching a unit on free will and want to pair Anthony Burgess's A Clockwork Orange, novel and Kubrick film together, with Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility. Strawson's regress runs: you do what you do because of the way you are, so being ultimately responsible for an action would require being ultimately responsible for the character that produced it, and no one can be, since character is handed over by heredity and environment, and any earlier act of self-making would raise the same demand one step further back. The novel literalizes the premise, since the state's Ludovico technique manufactures Alex's docility by direct causal means, and the prison chaplain's protest that a man who chooses wickedness is better than one who has goodness forced on him is the natural objection. Strawson's argument, though, makes that protest cut further than the chaplain means it to: if engineered conditioning is disqualifying, then ordinary upbringing, which no one chooses either, disqualifies in exactly the same way. Give me a reading that keeps both the work and the argument honest. Does the book dramatize the regress, or does it try to slip out of it — say, in the final chapter that the film drops, where Alex's taste for violence simply drains away, an unearned change he can no more take credit for than he can for the cure? And what should we do with the film's evident relish in Alex's cruelty and its use of Beethoven as the sound of both ecstasy and torture — does the telling undercut whatever thesis the story means to press? Point me to the scenes that carry the most weight, tell me where the reading strains and an honest critic has to concede, and finish by saying what the novel and film see about responsibility that the argument on its own does not.
