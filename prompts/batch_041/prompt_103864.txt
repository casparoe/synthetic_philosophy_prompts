I'm taking a philosophy of mind seminar and we've spent the last two weeks on the color phi phenomenon and the whole Orwellian-vs-Stalinesque business, and I keep bumping into a summary of it that I can't tell if I should trust. It's from a popular neuroscience book a friend lent me (she's got it back now, so I'm going off my notes), and the gist is this: 'When you flash a blue dot at the top of a screen and then a red dot at the bottom a fraction of a second later, you don't see two dots -- you see one dot that travels down the screen and changes color abruptly halfway. The strange part is that you see the color change in the middle of the path, which is before the second dot has even been presented. That can only mean the brain isn't building your experience in real time; it waits until both dots have arrived, then writes a single smooth movie of the event and projects it back onto your memory, so what you "saw" is really assembled after the fact. This is Dennett's color phi argument, and he uses it to show that the choice between an Orwellian and a Stalinesque revision is a false choice -- Orwellian being the brain holding back the experience until all the evidence is in, Stalinesque being the brain letting you have the experience and then editing your memory of it -- because the two stories predict the same reports and the same brain activity, so there's simply no fact of the matter about when you became conscious of the color change. In the end, then, consciousness is just a useful fiction the brain tells itself, and there's no real now of experience.'

My instinct is that the phenomenon is real and that Dennett does want to deflate the question of timing, but alot of this feels off, and I don't trust my memory of his actual argument well enough to say where.
