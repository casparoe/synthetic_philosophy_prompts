My friend is putting together a little zine of philosophical short fiction and guilt-tripped me into contributing, so I've been reading about Ian Hacking's stuff on psychiatric classification — the looping effect, "interactive kinds," how classifying people can change the people being classified, which then changes the classification, round and round. It's kind of fried my brain and I want to write a story that makes someone *feel* that instead of just having a character explain it at a dinner party (please, no lecture-in-dialogue, no wise mentor figure spelling out the theme).

So here's my seed, use it or bend it: a near-future town, not too flashy, where a new diagnosis has just entered the manual — call it something plausible and a little seductive, like "Cyclical Cognitive Overload" or whatever you think works better. My protagonist is among the first wave of people to recieve it. I want the story to move through three phases: her getting the label and the strange relief of it, her finding the online community of others with the same label and how they collectively talk about what it "really" is, and then a later moment where the diagnostic criteria themselves have shifted — partly because of how all these people started describing themselves — so that the thing she was diagnosed with isn't quite the thing anymore. The trick I'm after: one scene where you can see the label change *her* in a way that plainly wouldn't have happened otherwise, and one scene where her behavior, or the community's, visibly bends the category back. Don't resolve it neatly; I'd rather it end unsettled. Also — and this is the part I care about most — make it clear she isn't just a passive victim of the label; there's something real going on with her, the category isn't pure fiction, and that's what makes the loop so creepy. Third person, maybe 1200 words or so. If you need to invent clinical-sounding details, go ahead, just keep them believable and don't have anyone quote any actual researchers.
