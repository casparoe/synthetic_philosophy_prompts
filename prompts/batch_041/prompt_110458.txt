I sit on the well-being metrics working group of my city council. We are drafting a motion that would commit the city to "measuring and improving resident well-being," and because our background note leans heavily on the annual World Happiness Report, I need to be sure I can stand behind what the note says about it. A colleague has circulated an op-ed from a general-interest magazine that claims to summarise what the report actually measures; the author writes for a serious outlet and the piece reads as competent, but another member of the working group dismissed it as methodologically confused without being able to say where. The passage runs roughly as follows: "Each year the World Happiness Report surveys people in well over a hundred countries and ranks them by how happy their populations are. A country's score is an index built from six measurable ingredients — GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceptions of corruption — which together account for most of the variation between countries. The Nordics dominate the top of the table because strong welfare states and high taxes reliably supply those ingredients. The report's central finding is the Easterlin paradox: once a country passes a modest income threshold, further growth buys no additional happiness. The upshot for policymakers is straightforward: treat the six ingredients as levers, prioritise GDP per capita and healthy life expectancy, and expect the effects of reform to show up in the rankings within a few years."

What I want is an accuracy audit I can act on, not a grade of the author. Please take the role of someone expert in the measurement of subjective well-being and in the philosophy of social science — the kind of reader who has worked through the report's methodology chapters closely and has thought hard about what it means to "measure" happiness and what a ranking can and cannot support. Before judging anything, brainstorm the several things each sentence could be taken to mean, since a fair audit depends on the most charitable reading as well as the literal one. Then go sentence by sentence and sort each claim into four buckets: what a well-being researcher would accept as accurate; what is a defensible simplification or piece of shorthand that the report's own authors could live with; what is a misrepresentation they would reject; and what is straightforwardly false. Where a claim is wrong, say precisely where the mistake lies — a conflation of two things the methodology keeps apart, an overstatement of a modest finding, a causal claim read off a correlation, a technical term used in its everyday sense — and make clear which errors matter for my purposes and which are merely cosmetic.

Finally, answer the two questions my note actually turns on. First, does the op-ed's policy conclusion survive your corrections — that a government should treat the six ingredients as levers and expect measurable movement in the rankings within a few years — or does it depend entirely on the parts you flagged? Second, if the rankings rest on what you say they rest on, is "topping the table" a coherent target for a city or a country at all, and what would a defensible version of my motion say instead? I do not need a bibliography or a reading list; I need the reasoning, and where you are uncertain, tell me what turns on the uncertainty. My own inclination, for what it is worth, is to trust the op-ed, so be direct with me if that instinct is misplaced.
