So I'm working on a long feature for a general-reader ideas magazine about slurs — the linguistics and philosophy of them, not the politics per se — and I keep hitting the same wall: every expert I call has a different story about what the offense actually *is*, and each story handles my favorite examples fine adn then falls apart on the weird ones. I've boiled what I've heard down into five families of views, and I want you to rank them. (1) The plain-content view: a slur is just a word for a group plus some negative descriptive content, so using it is like asserting something nasty or false about the group. (2) The boo view: slurs don't describe anything at all — they're the verbal equivalent of a boo or a raised fist, they express the speaker's hostility, and that's the whole of it. (3) The between-the-lines view: the word itself just picks out the group, but it presupposes or signals the derogatory content off to the side, so the insult rides along without being part of what's literally said. (4) The taboo view: the sting isn't in any content — a slur is simply a word whose use is socially forbidden, and the offense is the violation of the prohibition, roughly the way an obscenity works. (5) The perspective view: slurs work by pulling the hearer into a certain way of seeing the group — invoking a stereotype or a viewpoint — and the harm is in being made to occupy that perspective.

Here's the part I actually need help with, and it's been sitting on my desk for three weeks now. The standard case (a certain word, outsider speaking about the group, malice intended) doesn't separate these views, since they all predict offense. What I want is a ranking of the five by how much of the *weird* territory each one can explain, and I have five odd cases I keep circling: (a) a slur whose target group no longer exists — the group assimilated or died out generations ago and the word survives only as an insult; (b) a slur coined five minutes ago by a novelist for a real, living group, with no history of use and no community that has ever been called it; (c) a word that is a slur in one language and a perfectly innocent noun — a kind of pastry, say — in a neighboring one, same pronunciation; (d) a member of the target group using the word affectionately about a fellow member; and (e) a word whose target has drifted over the centuries, so it now insults a completely different group than the one it started out aimed at, and nobody alive remembers the original target. For each of the five views, tell me which of these cases it gets right, which it gets wrong, and where it would need a patch, then rank the views from most to least explanatory overall — a tier list is fine if that's cleaner than a strict order — with a one-line why for each.

Skip the names and citations; I'll chase those down myself, and anyway half the people I talked to disagree about who said what. Just give me the positions and your verdict, and keep actual slurs out of it — use placeholders like [SLUR] if you need a word.
