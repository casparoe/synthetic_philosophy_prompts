I want to defend a broadly Moorean account of value, and I would like to know exactly what it exposes me to. The account has three parts. First, intrinsic value is plural and is not reducible to any natural property such as pleasure or desire-satisfaction. Second, among the most valuable things we know or can imagine are personal affection and the enjoyment of beautiful objects. Third, and most important for me, the value of a whole is not the sum of the values of its parts: some wholes are organic unities whose value exceeds or falls short of the aggregate of their parts, and Moore's own model case is retributive justice, where two bad things — a vicious deed and the suffering that answers it — compose a whole better than either alone. I want to keep all three claims. Tell me what should count against the package, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which parts a committed Moorean would concede as genuine costs and which he would wave away.

I would rather you not simply recite the standard objections. Stress-test these instead:

(i) The isolation method. If I am to assess a part's intrinsic value by imagining it alone, but the parts of an organic unity are partly constituted by their relations to one another, what is left to isolate? Is the method incoherent, or can it be restated in a form that survives? And if I drop it, what do I lose that I actually need?

(ii) The extension to friendship. Moore's model of an organic unity is retributive justice; I want to see what happens when I run the same machinery on personal affection. Consider three cases I have constructed: a long, devoted affection that is never returned; a mutual affection that is genuine but rests, on both sides, on a systematically false picture of the other; and a friendship deepened by having survived a betrayal and a reconciliation. If organic unities permit a whole to exceed its parts, does the third case come out better than an untroubled friendship of equal warmth? If it does, is that a reductio or a bullet I should bite? And can the theory even describe the first case, or does it simply fall silent before it?

(iii) The substitution test, which I have invented and do not trust. Take a whole with value V and replace one part with a part that is intrinsically identical but differently related — the same affection, directed at a different person, or at someone who is not what the lover takes her to be. If the value changes, then either the part was not intrinsically valuable after all, or the value of the whole is relational in a way my third thesis does not capture. Which horn should I take, and what does taking it do to the rest of the view?

(iv) Partiality. If the good is a general type — personal affection as such — does the theory hand the lover the wrong kind of reason, since she loves this person and not the type? Is that an objection to the theory of value, or only to using it as a decision procedure?

Finally, I want an honest verdict on insulation. With self-evident intuitions on one side and an unlimited licence to posit organic unities on the other, is any observation, case, or argument left that could count against the view, or can every counterexample be absorbed by declaring a new whole? If the view turns out to be well insulated, say whether that is a defect or simply what a correct theory of intrinsic value looks like. What does my account predict that a hedonist or a desire-satisfaction theorist does not — and where would I have to be wrong for one of them to be right? What would move me most is a case the theory cannot even describe, rather than one it merely ranks differently than I would.
