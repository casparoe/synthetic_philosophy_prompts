I have been keeping a list of my unreflective verdicts on cases in which someone continues a course of action because of what they have already put into it, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they express. Finishing a mediocre restaurant meal because the money has been spent: a mistake, and a mistake for precisely the reason usually given, namely that the money is gone whether or not I finish. Walking out of a film I am not enjoying: fine. Abandoning a bad novel halfway through: fine. Remaining in a job I have come to dislike because I have already given it six years: a mistake. A firm sinking more capital into a failing product in order to vindicate the capital already sunk: a mistake. A government escalating a war so that the earlier casualties shall not have died for nothing: a mistake, and the worst on the list.

At this point the list stops cooperating. Ending a friendship or a marriage of long standing strikes me as a genuine loss, and when I imagine staying partly because of the shared history, I do not think I am being irrational; the history seems to function as a reason rather than as a trap. Buying an expensive gym membership and then attending regularly, partly so that the money shall not be wasted: I want to call this rational, or at least not irrational, although the money is every bit as gone as the meal's. Someone who refuses to abandon a doomed project because he will not be a quitter: I disapprove of the particular decision, yet I think there is something to the rule. And a company that continues a money-losing line because retreating would teach its rivals that it retreats: I think this is rational, though I suspect it is not really about the sunk cost at all.

I have a tentative principle and I want it tested. It holds that a past expenditure is a legitimate reason to continue only where the past is partly constitutive of the good at stake — where continuing preserves something whose value lies partly in its having a history — and never where the past is merely a price that was paid to acquire a present benefit. On that principle the friendship stays and the meal goes. But I can feel the principle straining. Does it save the gym case, or does that case force me to concede that what I care about is whether the sunk cost leads me somewhere good or bad, and not the quality of my reasoning? Is the refusal to be a quitter rescued by the principle, or does it require a second and different clause, one about rules and self-commitment? And does the case of rival deterrence belong to the family at all, or am I conflating a sunk cost with a forward-looking one?

Please state the principle, or the small set of principles, that best captures these verdicts; say precisely which case forces each clause; draw out what it implies for cases I have not listed — someone who keeps reading a series only because of the volumes already read, a researcher who defends a hypothesis because of the years spent on it, a person who stays at a party because of the time it took to get there; and tell me candidly which of my verdicts it cannot keep, and why I should surrender that one.
