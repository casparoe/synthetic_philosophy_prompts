Until last year I thought of archives as neutral storage. Then I spent eight months trying to trace my grandmother's family through a provincial registry whose volumes for the relevant decades are simply missing — probably destroyed, possibly never kept in the first place — and I ended up volunteering with a small community archive that is trying to document a population the state had little interest in recording. I now have a year, one part-time research assistant, and a modest budget, and I need to decide which conceptual questions are actually worth our time.

Which open questions about archives and power matter most, and in what order? I want a defended agenda, not a survey. For each, tell me why it is upstream of the concrete choices a community archive faces — what gets collected, whose provenance claims get honored, how gaps get narrated, whether an absence is presented as loss or as a fact about the record-keeper — how tractable it is for someone with a historian's training rather than a philosopher's, and what evidence over the next year would move it up or down the list. I am especially interested in the distinction between a silence produced by destruction and one produced by a classificatory scheme that never had a category for the people in question, and in whether a community archive that adopts the state's categories has done more than invert the archive's politics. Include at least one question you think is currently overrated, and say what separates a question that is genuinely hard from one that is merely fashionable.

Please be explicit about how confident you are in the ranking you give, and single out the one claim in it you are least sure of.
