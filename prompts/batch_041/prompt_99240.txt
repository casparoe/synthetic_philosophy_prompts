I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I'm writing a book where the chapters are deliberately out of order and I want the metaphysics underneath it to actually hold up instead of collapsing the second anyone pokes at it. So I'm going to just tell you what I seem to believe about time, case by case, and I want you to tell me what position that adds up to -- if it adds up to anything.

Role-wise: put on your metaphysician hat, someone who knows the presentism/eternalism debate cold but can also explain it to a stubborn fiction writer without hiding behind jargon. Take your time. Start by brainstorming the cases out loud and sketching the candidate positions before you commit to one.

Here's my gut, case by case. When I say dinosaurs existed, I think that's true. But I don't think dinosaurs exist. And if you asked me whether the past is still somehow going on somewhere, I'd say no -- once it's gone it's gone. Same for the future, which I don't think is sitting there waiting; it's open, nothing's written. So it looks like only the present is real, which I think is called presentism, and fine.

But then "now" has to be doing all this heavy lifting, the one real moment, and the second I try to say what makes a moment the real one I get the feeling it's only real because it's the one I'm in -- which is just me, not the universe. Also, a friend once explained that for two events far apart in space, one observer can say A happened first and another can say B happened first and neither is wrong, and ever since then I can't shake the feeling that "simultaneous" isn't a fact about the world, it's a fact about your slice of it. Which sounds more like the opposite view -- everything that ever happens is equally real, all laid out, no special now.

And that view horrifies me, because if all times are equally real then the worst afternoon of my life is still there, permanently, and so is everyone's, forever, and nothing ever actually stops hurting. But presentism has its own horror: if the past is gone and the future isn't here, then nothing is ever really happening except this instant, and change -- the one thing I'd bet my life on -- becomes impossible to state without cheating.

Two more gut reactions. I feel like things genuinely become; a block where all times just sit there equally real seems to deny the most obvious feature of time, that it passes. But I also can't tell you what "passes" means without smuggling in a second time for it to pass in, and then a third, and it gets silly.

So: is there a single principle, or one of the standard positions -- presentism, the growing block, the moving spotlight, plain eternalism -- that gets all of these verdicts, or nearly all? Which one do I have to give up if I want the rest to hang together, and what does giving it up actually cost me? And then take a case I haven't listed -- say, whether it makes sense to talk about the dead as still existing in some sense, or whether "the past is another country" is a metaphor or a literal claim -- and tell me what your chosen principle predicts about it, even if I won't like the answer. Don't just hand me the taxonomy and call it a day; I want to see you actually choose.
