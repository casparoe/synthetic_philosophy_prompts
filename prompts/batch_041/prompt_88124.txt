The unit is intellectual property, and the thing I keep bumping into in the readings is that the pro-IP argument usually sounds like it's about property — you made it, so it's yours — but an idea isn't used up when someone else uses it, so the property analogy seems to be doing way less work than it sounds like it's doing, and the real argument has to be about paying for the making of things rather than about the nature of the thing that gets made. I want my classmates to feel that in their bodies before I say a word about it, and I'd rather they build it than me.

Here's my rough idea, and you should throw it out if you can do better with the same idea. I give each team a small budget of trial tokens, and there's a secret 4-symbol code in a sealed envelope that my professor picked before class; submitting a guess costs a token, a correct code is worth a big pile of points, a wrong guess is worth a couple. Phase one: no rules at all about who may use a code once it's found, so the second one team cracks it, everyone copies it down and everyone scores — and the team that burned tokens searching ends up no better off than the teams that just sat there waiting, which I'm hoping stings enough to notice. Phase two: I add a rule that the finder can charge a fee to anyone who wants to use their code, and we see what that does to who searches and what the total comes out to. Then the part I care most about: I hide a second, much bigger code that can only be assembled out of two components, and I rig it so two different teams end up holding one component each, meaning the big prize is only reachable if those two can come to terms — I'm pretty sure there's a name for this reverse-tragedy-of-the-commons thing where too many overlapping rights block a product from ever existing.
