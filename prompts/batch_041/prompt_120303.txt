I convene a third-year undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of science: sixteen students, one ninety-minute session, no set reading, a room with movable chairs and a projector. I want to run an activity that makes the group feel the force of incommensurability from the inside — not the loose slogan that worldviews differ, but the narrower and more uncomfortable thing: that two communities can share a body of observations, agree about every one of them, and still be unable to translate each other's classificatory vocabulary, because the terms are fixed by their mutual relations rather than by anything the observations settle; that whether something counts as a puzzle, a result, or an anomaly depends on the scheme you are working in; and that changing schemes can lose as well as gain. I want the loss to be felt, not merely announced. The words paradigm, incommensurable, normal science and revolution must not appear in any material the students see, and Kuhn's name must not be spoken until the debrief.

These are the beats the activity needs, whatever content you invent for it.

1. Two communities. Split the group into two halves, each with its own self-contained kit: a lexicon of eight to ten kind-terms with definitions, three worked exemplars, a set of puzzles, and its own scoring rule for what counts as solving one. Both kits must be applied to the same pool of cases, described in vocabulary that neither kit owns. Neither kit may be obviously superior: their strengths must be orthogonal, so that each is better than the other on part of the material. Within each lexicon there must be at least one term whose correct application depends on the correct application of other terms in the same lexicon, so that it cannot be translated piecemeal, and at least one term for which the rival kit offers a plausible but wrong counterpart — a false friend that the students will reach for first.

2. Anomaly. Each community then receives a sealed case that its kit cannot classify cleanly. The case must be one that can be absorbed by adding an auxiliary clause, so that they are tempted to patch rather than abandon; the facilitator notes what they do.

3. Cross-examination. Each community must translate the other's lexicon and deliver a verdict on a case both claim. They should be scored at least once under the rival kit's rule, so that the scoring schemes, each internal to its own kit, turn out not to be a common measure.

4. Forced comparison. Finally, each community must nominate a criterion for choosing between the kits, and the other must show that the criterion is internal to its own kit. Let them discover whether any criterion survives, and be ready for either outcome.

The debrief should map moments in the activity onto the ideas, and should at minimum connect what happened to: the later, more qualified version of incommensurability, on which the trouble is local — attaching to particular clusters of kind-terms and their interrelations rather than to whole worldviews; the distinction between having a translation manual and learning to use a foreign vocabulary, and the thought that the historian may end up bilingual rather than in possession of a neutral key; Kuhn's treatment of theory choice as governed by shared values — accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, fruitfulness — which can conflict and be weighted differently by people who accept all of them, so that they constrain without determining; the loss of previously solved problems that a change of scheme can bring; and the objection that untranslatability is incoherent, since to identify a failure of translation we must already have understood something in common. Be explicit about how the activity's own design concedes a point to that objection.

Give me the materials to prepare, the rules, the timing, the roles, the scoring, and how the facilitator should handle what will predictably go wrong the first time — at a minimum, a group that code-switches too easily and declares the exercise merely a matter of labels, and a group whose kit proves so much stronger that the comparison collapses into the obvious answer. Be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I must supply myself; say so plainly rather than hedging. No reading list and no citations: the kits have to be self-contained, since the students will have nothing but the room.
