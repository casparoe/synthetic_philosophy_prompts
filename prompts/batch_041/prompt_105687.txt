I'm in this reading group on levels of selection and I keep getting tangled up in it, so I want to see the thing argued out loud instead of summarized again. Can you write a dialogue between two evolutionary biologists who are colleagues and friends — both of them sharp, neither one a strawman, neither one there to lose — arguing over whether selection in their study system is really acting on genes, on individual organisms, or on groups. The system: a made-up beetle, the aspen leaf-borer (just invent the details as you go, but keep them consistent and don't have either of them appeal to any real species). Larvae hatch in clutches of a few dozen on a single leaf, and in some clutches a fraction of the larvae are a sterile sacrificer morph that never reproduces — it just patrols the clutch and goes after parasitoid wasps. Clutches with more sacrificers lose fewer larvae, but within any given clutch the sacrificers do worse than their selfish sibs, and clutches that throw off a lot of sacrificers also send out more dispersers that found fresh clutches on new leaves the next spring. One of the two is a gene-centric adaptationist: for them this is all just inclusive-fitness bookkeeping, the sacrificer allele spreads because it's helping copies of itself in the clutch, and there's no extra fact about the group doing any work — the gene's-eye view was never a claim that only genes get selected, it's a claim about where adaptations get assembled and which entity carries the design. The other one thinks that's a redescription that quietly drops the causal story: they want to say the clutch itself is a unit of selection, with its own heritable variation in composition, its own fitness (how many dispersers it sends out) and its own adaptations — a defence corps with a division of labour that makes no sense for any single larva — and they're suspicious of the move where you average the members' fitnesses, call the average a group fitness, and then announce that group selection has been demonstrated.

Have them actually go at each other rather than trade speeches. The gene person should press on whether the group view ever predicts anything the inclusive-fitness account doesn't already, and should accuse the group person of equivocating between two very different claims — (a) groups differ in the aggregate success of the individuals inside them, which is fine but is still individual selection in a groupy setting, versus (b) groups are themselves the bearers of adaptations, which is alot stronger and much more contestable. The group person should push back on whether a bookkeeping trick that can always be redone at a lower level is even an explanation if it never rules anything out, and should try to say what kind of evidence would actually seperate (a) from (b) here. Let them concede where they're wrong, let them get genuinely stuck, and let it end in a real standoff or a partial convergence rather than a knockout — I don't want a tidy winner.
