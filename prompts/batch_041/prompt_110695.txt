I'm a third-year philosophy student writing a long essay for a course on Kant and post-Kantian epistemology, and I've come to think that the literature on transcendental arguments is stuck in a way that nobody quite names. The shape of it, as I read it: one side builds arguments that begin from some supposedly indispensable feature of our experience or judgement — that our experience is unified, that we can identify and re-identify objects, that our representations purport to be about a mind-independent world — and concludes that some corresponding feature of reality must obtain, so that the sceptic who doubts it is refuted. The other side objects that the most such an argument can deliver is a conclusion about what we must believe, or about how we must conceptualise things, and that a claim about our cognitive constitution is not a claim about the world; the sceptic can grant every premise and remain a sceptic. The first side answers that this objection lands only against a caricature, and that a conclusion about what we cannot help believing is already a conclusion about what there is. Then the exchange simply repeats.

My hypothesis, which I'd like you to test rather than assume, is that this is not one disagreement but two, and that the stalemate comes from running them together: (a) a question about what the premises mean — whether the term 'experience' in the premise is being used for experience merely as it occurs or for experience as veridical, so that on one reading the argument is analytic or question-begging and on the other it is substantive; and (b) a question about what refuting scepticism is supposed to require — showing the sceptical scenario to be false, or merely showing it to be unintelligible or unmotivated. If that is right, the two sides may not even be contradicting one another; if it is wrong, I want to know where the real disagreement lies.

To keep this from collapsing into a survey, please use the following as a test case. A friend of mine has sketched a transcendental argument that starts not from the unity of experience but from the possibility of error — from the fact that we can be wrong about how things are — and concludes that there must be a way things are independent of us, since error requires a standard that one can miss. Does the standard objection apply to this argument in the same way, or does it fail against it in some instructive way? Use that case to decide whether my two-part diagnosis holds.

Give me a diagnosis with reasons, drawn from what each side actually claims, tell me what would count as progress on it (a distinction, a reformulated argument, a change of question) or why none should be expected, and end with a single line of the form \answer{...} whose contents are one of: verbal, substantive, different questions, mixed.
