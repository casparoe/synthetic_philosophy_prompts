Bit of an odd request, so bear with me. I took early retirement from a field ecology job about four years ago — thirty-odd years of mostly counting waders, then a long stretch on fisheries data, so I'm comfortable with messy numbers — and since then I've been trying to teach myself the philosophy side of the subject I spent my life on. I've drifted into a reading group at the university and they've asked me to write down where I stand on signalling so they can take a swing at it. Trouble is I'm not sure it's a coherent package, and I'd rather find that out here than infront of them. Here's where I've landed:

(1) Zahavi was basically right, and the handicap principle is the general theory of honest communication: if a signal is to be trusted about something you can't see, it has to be costly, and costly in a way that only a genuinely good animal can afford. (2) That said, I've never been convinced the waste is the point. I think what's doing the work is differential marginal cost — the low-quality signaller finds it dearer to say the same thing — and I'd be perfectly happy if the whole thing turned out to be a story about marginal cost with no conspicuous waste anywhere. (3) Plenty of honesty in nature isn't strategic at all. A toad's croak is honest about how big the toad is because of physics, not because the toad is paying for anything; nothing is being spent and nothing can be faked. I don't treat that as a special case, I think it's the normal case. (4) On humans, I'm sold on the costly-signal reading of religious ritual — fasting, pilgrimage, animal sacrifice, all the useless-in-the-narrow-sense stuff. It survives because it proves you're committed to the group in a way a mere declaration can't. (5) I'm not a group selectionist though; whatever's going on has to be paid for out of individual fitness, or gene's-eye if you prefer. (6) And I think language makes us a special case: cheap talk is fine when interests line up, and where they don't, gossip, reputation and the fear of being shunned do most of the enforcement work. Most human communication doesn't need a handicap at all.

So — is this a package or just a pile? I dont want a lecture on signalling theory, I want an audit. Go through the commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, which only look like they are and why they're actually fine, and for each real one what the cheapest revision would be and what I'd lose by making it. Rank the real ones by severity rather than telling me everything is equally bad, and finish by saying which single concession would tidy up the most mess. Don't bother pointing me at papers or a reading list — my desk is already a hazard — I want to know whether the thing hangs together on its own. And if you could write it in British English I'd be grateful; I've picked up the -ise habit over here and it now looks wrong the other way.
