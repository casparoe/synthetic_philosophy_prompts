I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on causation — eighteen students, ninety minutes, most of them with one prior course in metaphysics or philosophy of science. I want a session in which they come to feel the difference between a difference-making (counterfactual) theory of causation and an interventionist theory, and in particular why an interventionist thinks the counterfactual theorist's central instrument is too blunt. I would much rather they collide with the divergence than hear me narrate it, so I want the whole thing run as an activity with rules, roles, and a decision or scoring procedure, and with no framing lecture up front.

Please design that session. Use the following fictional scenario as its spine, though you may extend it: a commercial greenhouse's irrigation line is fed by two independent pumps, A and B, each triggered by its own soil-moisture sensor, and either pump alone delivers enough water for the beds; there is also a manual override lever that forces both pumps on. The output of interest is simply that the beds get watered. I want the activity to move the group through a sequence of cases in which the two theories visibly disagree — at minimum: (i) a plain case where only one pump's sensor is dry and everyone agrees on the cause; (ii) a preemption case in which A fires a fraction of a second before B and B's water arrives too late to matter, so that removing A alone would leave the beds still watered; (iii) a symmetric case in which both pumps fire simultaneously and either alone would have sufficed; and (iv) a case where the candidate cause cannot be isolated by any intervention the participants can perform, because the sensor readings and the beds' actual water needs are both driven by a common ambient factor, so setting the reading would move the need as well. Let the participants do the causal reasoning themselves and record verdicts at each stage, and make the rules such that the moment where the two theories part company is something they generate, not something I announce.

Then give me the debrief: map each stage of the activity onto the part of the disagreement it is meant to expose, say explicitly which verdicts each theory endorses at each stage and where the verdicts come apart, and distinguish the objections that are decisive against a naive difference-making account from those that a more careful counterfactual theorist can absorb. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — where students will import notions of blame or responsibility, where they will find a case intuitively obvious and therefore miss why it is supposed to be troubling, and how I should handle each — and be honest about which feature of the ideas the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself, whether that is the formal apparatus, causation by omission, or something else.

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on causation: do not present either the counterfactual approach or the interventionist approach as the consensus view, and flag wherever your pedagogical choices depend on a contested reading of one of them.
