You are an expert in decision theory and the philosophy of practical rationality, with a sharp sense for the gap between a formal representation theorem and a normative claim. Work carefully, and reason on the page before you commit to any verdict.

The setting is the coherence argument for expected utility maximization. Roughly: if an agent's preferences over options form a complete weak order, are continuous or Archimedean in the relevant sense, and satisfy an independence or sure-thing condition, then those preferences can be represented as maximizing expected utility; and if they fail the conditions, the agent can be exploited — exposed to a sure loss, run as a money pump, or led through a sequence of choices she would have rejected at the outset. The conclusion is supposed to be that expected utility maximization is not one decision rule among others but the shape any coherent set of preferences must take.

I have collected six responses to that argument. The labels and glosses are my own; do not spend effort attributing them to particular authors or pointing me at a literature — I want your assessment, not a syllabus. Read each as the strongest version of itself.

1. Incomparability. Deny that rational preference must be complete. Some pairs of options are not rankable at all — there is no fact of the matter about which you prefer — so the representation theorem never gets its premises, and neither, on this view, does the charge of incoherence.

2. Thresholds. Keep completeness and transitivity, reject continuity. Certain goods are lexically protected: no finite chance of losing them is worth any amount of anything else, and you will not trade a hair of the sacred for a heap of the ordinary. You fall outside the expected-utility representation while insisting you are perfectly coherent.

3. Framing. Reject independence. What you prefer between two gambles may legitimately depend on what else was on the menu, so the value of an option is not intrinsic to it; the same bet may be acceptable in one setting and unacceptable in another without contradiction.

4. Time-slicing. Keep expected utility at each moment of choice, deny that the moments must add up. An agent may rank a whole course of action highest in advance and then, at the point of choice, rank its continuation lowest, without irrationality — there is no requirement that your ex-ante ranking of a life bind your ex-post ranking of the next step.

5. Redescription. Keep the axioms by changing the objects of preference: let your own dispositions enter the ranking, so that being a person who does not calculate, or keeping your hands clean, is one of the things outcomes are scored by. The theory is satisfied, but it now blesses whatever you were going to do anyway.

6. No bridge. Grant the theorem and grant the pump, but deny that exploitability is a defect of rationality. Being pumpable is a fact about other people's opportunities, or at most a failure of prudence, not a failure of coherence.

Keep the axes distinct; the interesting entries are the ones that score low on survival and high on defensibility.

Third, rank the six from most damaging to least — that is, by ascending survival score — and group them into tiers (S, A, B, C; ties allowed), giving the one-sentence reason each lands where it does. Fourth, test the top tier against two cases. Case one: a woman declines an actuarially favorable gamble that risks her house, saying that no sum of money compensates for the chance of losing it. Case two: a man pays a broker a fee to make an option unavailable to him, because he knows he will take it if it is there. Which of the six handles each case, and is there any single response that handles both without strain?
