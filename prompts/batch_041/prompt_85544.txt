Two friends and I have been arguing for months about suffering risks — futures in which things go on rather than end, and go on with an enormous amount of suffering in them: a system pursuing some objective that happens to require keeping a great many minds in states they would give anything to escape; a vast number of animals living and dying badly under conditions someone engineered; an arrangement that simply finds it efficient to produce misery at scale. We keep getting stuck in the same place. One of us says that a world with more suffering in it is worse, and then the other asks whether suffering that no one experiences counts, whether someone who arranged a whole life around pain and got exactly what they wanted has thereby been made worse off, whether one mind in agony for a thousand years outweighs a thousand minds miserable for a year, whether it matters that the suffering was aimed at rather than merely permitted, and whether a future full of minds that never get to be anything at all is a suffering risk or a different kind of loss altogether. I do not want another definition presented as though the hard cases were already settled by it. What I want is the specification underneath: the requirements that any account in this area — any account of what makes such futures bad, how bad, and which interventions make them less likely or less severe — would have to satisfy before I would trust it to guide anything.

So write me the desiderata. Which cases it has to get right, with the case standing behind each requirement; which distinctions it cannot collapse (suffering as opposed to disvalue more broadly; intensity against duration against number of subjects; suffering that is actually experienced against states we merely disapprove of on the subject's behalf; suffering that is chosen against suffering that is imposed; deliberate against incidental; the deprivation of goods against the presence of bads); and which facts about the world it has to remain consistent with — that the relevant futures are unobservable, that we are ignorant of most of the causal paths leading to them, that no one can check the counterfactual, and that an account under which nearly every future contains a suffering risk is an account that has stopped discriminating. Mark which of these are genuine requirements and which are merely commitments of one camp — the people who care mostly about animals now versus the people who care mostly about machines later — and say which one is non-negotiable. I expect at least one pair to be jointly unsatisfiable, and I would rather have that stated as a result than smoothed over: my own suspicion is that any account broad enough to catch every kind of suffering worth catching loses the ability to tell me which of two interventions to fund, and that an account sharp enough to rank interventions buys its sharpness by quietly dropping some suffering out of the count. If you think that is wrong, say so and say what resolves it. I would also like to know which single requirement does the most work in ruling out otherwise attractive accounts, and I am content for that to be the only comparative judgment in the answer.

I should say what the list is for, since adequacy is presumably relative to a use: I am not building a taxonomy, I am trying to decide where a limited amount of money and time should go, and I want to know what an account would have to do to be worth using for that purpose even approximately. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for who has said what — no reading list and no citations; I would rather have the criteria worked out than the positions summarized. And I would like you to be explicit about how confident you are in each claim you make, and to single out the one point in the whole answer you are least sure of, with a note on what evidence or argument would move you.
