In constitutional monarchies the head of state is unelected, hereditary, and legally unaccountable, yet still appears throughout the machinery of legitimate state action: summoning and dissolving the legislature, appointing the head of government, assenting to bills, commanding the armed forces, accrediting ambassadors. By convention the monarch does none of this except on the advice of ministers answerable to an elected assembly, must remain politically neutral, and bears no personal responsibility for anything done in their name — ministers take the responsibility instead. Where reserve powers survive, everyone treats their exercise as a constitutional crisis rather than as ordinary lawful government, and in some monarchies the formal role has been pared back almost to nothing while the institution itself persists with broad public support.

What theory of political legitimacy does this arrangement presuppose? In particular: is the practice committed to the monarch's having some independent authority, or does it require only that everyone treat the monarch as having none? Does the convention of acting on ministerial advice presuppose popular sovereignty, or merely its appearance? Can an unaccountable hereditary office be a genuine source of legitimate authority in a democracy, or does the practice presuppose that legitimacy attaches wholly to the elected branch and the monarch is a legal fiction that must be kept up? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, identify which rival views it quietly rules out, and say whether its parts presuppose consistent things — especially whether the neutrality convention and the survival of reserve powers can be held together.
