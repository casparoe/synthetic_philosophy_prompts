I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for a long time — long enough to have absorbed the habit of trying to turn my intuitions into something I could actually compute with, and long enough to be tired of the dust-specks-versus-torture standoff, which never seems to settle anything. What I want from you is the principle behind a set of verdicts I already hold about the trade-off between pleasure and pain. I will state the verdicts as plainly as I can, then state the principle I have been working with, and I would like you to tell me what actually fits my cases and what I have to give up.

Stipulations, so that the cases are well defined: everything hedonic is measured on one scale, pain negative and pleasure positive; the magnitude of an episode is intensity times duration; all subjects carry equal moral weight; there is no uncertainty, and no distributional consideration except those the cases themselves force. I am not asking for a reading list, citations, or a survey of the literature — I want the decision procedure, not the bibliography.

My verdicts:

1. One person endures a year of the worst pain I can imagine; the alternative is any finite number of people each enduring a momentary mild headache. The first world is worse, whatever the number.
2. Ten billion people each with a momentary mild headache is worse than one person with a momentary mild headache.
3. One person endures a year of the worst pain and then an arbitrarily large lifetime of the most intense pleasure; the alternative is that same person with the same year of pain and nothing after. The first world is not better. The pleasure does nothing at all.
4. For every pair of intensities I have actually compared — 19 versus 20, 50 versus 51 — a large enough heap of pleasure makes the higher-intensity world better than the lower-intensity world with none. I cannot find an intensity at which that stops being true, and I do not believe the exchange rate between pain and pleasure ever becomes exactly zero.
5. One person suffering one second of the worst pain is better than an enormous but finite number of people each suffering a mild headache for a year.
6. Preventing one person's year of mild headaches is better than giving one person a year of mild pleasure, but only by a modest factor; those two are commensurable.
7. One person's year at intensity 100 is worse than another person's year at intensity 99.999, but only barely; the two are comparable at a finite rate.

The principle I have been working with, which I call lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism (LTNU): there is a threshold intensity t below zero. Pains more intense than t are severe; pains less intense than t, and all pleasures, are mild. Comparison is lexicographic by tier: if two worlds differ in the total magnitude of severe pain, the world with less severe pain is better regardless of what the mild tier contains; if they tie on severe pain, compare the combined mild pain and pleasure.

LTNU keeps 1, 2, 3, 6, and 7, but it seems to lose 4 and 5. It also treats mild pain and pleasure identically, whereas my verdicts treat them differently: verdict 3 says pleasure has exactly zero weight against severe pain, while verdict 5 says a large enough quantity of mild pain does outweigh a small amount of severe pain. That asymmetry is not something I put there on purpose, and I do not know what to do with it.

I would like you to state the principle, or the two or three candidate principles, precisely enough that I could compute verdicts with them; to tie each clause to the verdict that forces it; to say what each candidate predicts for cases I have not listed — in particular whether the severe tier is internally lexical (does one year at intensity 100 outweigh any number of years at intensity 90?), what happens when the threshold is crossed by duration rather than intensity (a long stretch of just-below-threshold pain accumulating to a magnitude larger than a brief burst of just-above-threshold pain), and whether the threshold is a property of an episode, a life, or a population; and to tell me plainly which of my verdicts the best principle cannot keep.
