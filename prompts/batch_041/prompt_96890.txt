I'm writing a feature about the disputes that erupt when museums, insurers, and artists' estates have to agree on whether something is the object that was insured, the work that was lent, or a replacement — and behind each of those disputes sits the older question of what it is for one thing to be made of another. Before I start reporting, I want the specification rather than the literature: what does a theory of material constitution have to be able to do before it is any good? Take the standard of adequacy to be this — good enough to license verdicts in cases where money, custody, and conservation decisions turn on the answer — and then tell me what that standard actually demands.

Set out the requirements such a theory must meet, and for each one give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the findings from the sciences it cannot contradict, the facts of ordinary practice it cannot simply declare mistaken. Say which of these are non-negotiable and which are house rules of a particular camp.

Let these composites do the work, since the disputes I see are rarely about a statue and a lump of clay in the abstract:

- a topiary horse maintained in the same shape for eighty years, in which every living cell has been replaced several times over, and whose dead limbs are grafted from a different shrub;
- a bust carved from a single block of rock salt, shown in a humid gallery, that sheds about a gram a day and is meant by its maker to remain the same sculpture until the last grain is gone;
- a bronze whose mold was destroyed at the foundry and which was later re-made by taking a new mold from a surviving cast of the same edition, so that the second bronze descends from the first without containing any of it;
- a marble block already sawn to a rough cylinder by the quarry before the sculptor touches it, so that the matter of the finished statue is itself a made thing with its own maker and its own history;
- a Roman coin embedded in a sculpture's base, unknown to the artist, removed for study, and returned.

Then tell me what the theory is not competent to settle. My sources slide constantly between whether two things are identical and whether they are the same work for legal or curatorial purposes, and I want to know whether that slide is a confusion on their part or a genuine question the metaphysics has to answer.

Finally: flag any requirements that cannot be met together — that is the part I most want, since it would explain why these disputes never close — and name the single requirement that rules out the most candidate theories. I don't need a scorecard of the going views; I need the spec.
