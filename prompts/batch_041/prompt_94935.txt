I work on multimodal representation learning — contrastive pretraining, cross-modal retrieval, and zero-shot transfer between encoders trained on different sensory channels. I have enough philosophy to know what Molyneux's problem is: the question, traditionally attributed to Molyneux and raised in his correspondence with Locke, of whether a man born blind who has learned to distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch would be able to tell which is which by sight alone, the moment he gained his sight, before touching them again. I also know the shape of the traditional answers — the negative one, that the two senses share nothing but a learned association and so no recognition could occur, and the affirmative one, that both senses deliver information about the same spatial structure and so recognition should be possible.

Please restate the problem and both answers in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can: encoders, latent spaces, shared versus modality-specific representations, alignment objectives, paired versus unpaired data, zero-shot transfer, inductive bias, downstream decoding and probing. Do not simply map the philosophers' words onto my terms and present the result as a translation; I want to know where the mapping is honest and where it is a fudge. Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the debate survive translation intact? Which survive only in a distorted form? And what, if anything, in the philosophers' dispute has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? For each piece of residue, tell me whether you take it to mark a genuine lacuna in how I think about cross-modal learning, or whether it is an artefact — something the philosophers were gesturing at that turns out to be less substantive once it is stated in terms I can actually test. I am especially interested in whether the historical disagreement, viewed from my side, reduces to a disagreement about architecture and training distribution rather than about concepts, and if so, what is lost by seeing it that way. One further thing I would like you to adjudicate: the original question concerns a single subject gaining a new sense, whereas my framework always concerns a pair of separately trained systems that may or may not share a space. Tell me whether that difference is load-bearing or merely incidental to the problem.
