I am trying to understand a stretch of Nietzsche's late autobiographical book Ecce Homo, specifically the opening of the chapter in which he turns to nutrition, place, and climate. I am working without the German and without a reliable translation in front of me, so please treat what follows as a report of the content rather than a rendering of the text.

The paraphrase: Nietzsche says that the matters he has come to take seriously are small and unpromising ones — nutrition, place, climate, recreation — and that these are incomparably more important than anything he had previously held important. He reports his own regimen: alcohol is bad for him, he is suspicious of heavy cooking and of the table customs he associates with Germany, he attends closely to where he lives and what the air is like, and he treats lapses in these matters not as bad luck but as failures of instinct. He ties such failures to idealism, by which he seems to mean the habit of despising the body and the concrete conditions of one's own life. Elsewhere in the book he says that he is himself a decadent and at the same time the opposite of one, and that his long illness taught him something about health.

What I cannot get clear on is what this dietary material is doing in a book that is otherwise about his significance as a thinker. Is it literal practical advice, a diagnostic of instinct, or a figure for something else — the conditions under which a thought can be lived, say, or a kind of philosophical hygiene? And how should I read the apparent tension that a man whose adult life was largely spent in poor health makes health a criterion of value? Is his illness a counterexample to his own standard, or is it somehow part of the point? Please help me understand the passage and the role it plays in the larger argument, and be explicit about where the text is genuinely ambiguous and where an interpreter simply has to make a decision.
