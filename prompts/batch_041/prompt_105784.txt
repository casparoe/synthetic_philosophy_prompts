Write a deadpan mock press release, roughly 500 words, announcing that a fictional company — invent a name; do not use a real firm — has brought to market a control protocol it describes as strategy-stealing-proof: no competing system, however capable, could obtain the same advantage merely by adopting the protocol. Format it as a real corporate announcement, with headline, dateline, two or three paragraphs of product description, a quotation from the company's fictional chief scientist, a quotation from a fictional outside game theorist who is described as concerned but is given only reassuring things to say, a quotation from a fictional regulator, and a boilerplate section that includes a forward-looking-statements disclaimer.

The satire should come from getting the concept right rather than from winking. Distinguish, without ever labeling them as such, two things that share a name. The first is the game-theoretic strategy-stealing argument: the kind of existence proof, familiar from certain symmetric board games, that shows a first player wins without exhibiting the winning strategy, on the ground that a would-be second-player strategy could be appropriated by the first player after an idle move. The second is the alignment-flavored assumption that no control measure confers a durable competitive advantage, because any strategy available to a controlled system is available to an uncontrolled one that can simply copy it. The release should treat the second as though it had been solved by the first: the company claims to have exhibited a strategy that cannot be stolen, and the joke is that the announcement is itself a strategy, and the disclaimer is where it gets stolen.

The chief scientist should explain the protocol in terms that sound rigorous — symmetry, non-transferability, the idle move — while the concerned theorist should raise the standard objection in a way that the release's framing turns into a selling point. The forward-looking-statements boilerplate should concede, in the flat register of legal caution, that the strategy described may be available to any sufficiently capable agent, and the release should not notice that this is the whole problem. The regulator's quotation should be bland enough to be damning.

Constraints: no real company, no real person, no quotation from any actual text; invent all names and all quotations. Do not put anything in quotation marks that comes from a real source. The piece should be legible to a reader who knows neither the board-game argument nor the alignment discussion, and funny to a reader who knows both. Around 500 words, headline included.
