I'm a philosophy PhD student working on moral psychology, and I keep generating verdicts about when an action has moral worth without being able to make them add up to a principle. Please act as an expert on the moral worth of action, reason carefully through each case before generalising, and begin by brainstorming the candidate principles that could generate the pattern below before you commit to one. I'm not asking for a literature review; I want to know what to say.

My verdicts:

1. A pharmacist charges fair prices and never overcharges, because overcharging would ruin a reputation that is worth money to him. What he does is right, and has no moral worth at all. He is not, however, a bad person.

2. A passer-by pulls a drowning child out of a canal on pure unreflective panic and sympathy, without a moment's thought about duty or reasons. This strikes me as a paradigm of moral worth, about as admirable as conduct gets.

3. A soldier helps a comrade desert, because he cannot bear to see him shot, while believing throughout that desertion is wicked and that he is doing something vile. I want to say the act was wrong and yet that he is admirable in a way that has something to do with morality.

4. A woman gives a tenth of her income to the poor, as she was raised to, never having questioned it; not doing it would strike her as unthinkable and faintly frightening. This has less worth than 2 but more than 1, and I cannot defend that ordering.

5. A man takes a pill that makes him markedly more compassionate, then keeps a costly promise to a stranger out of the compassion it induces. I do not think the worth of his act is reduced — yet in 2 I seemed to be crediting the sympathy itself rather than any endorsement of it.

6. A woman keeps a promise at considerable cost largely because breaking it would destroy her self-image as someone who keeps promises. I want to call this genuine moral worth, though it is as self-directed as 1.

7. Someone visits a dying friend out of love, but also reminds herself on the way that it is her duty, and feels a little pleased with herself for doing it. I find this slightly worse than the same visit without the reminder — which sits badly with 3, where the agent's moral convictions are entirely wrong and yet the act still seems admirable.

My tentative principle was that the moral worth of an act is the extent to which it is explained by the agent's grasp of the moral reasons actually in play. I can see it failing on 3, and perhaps on 5.

Give me the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. State it precisely, identify the case that motivates each clause, and say plainly which verdict it forces me to give up. Then tell me what it implies for two or three further cases that you choose because they discriminate between your surviving candidates. I also suspect I have been running two questions together: say whether one principle can cover both the worth of acts and the worth of persons, or whether I need separate accounts. Be committed — pick a principle and defend it rather than laying out the options. Please answer in British English.
