I have written fiction in the first person for the better part of two decades, and for the past several months — while drafting a novel whose narrator is, on her own account, unreliable about the events that made her — I have been reading philosophy of personal identity in the evenings. I have now read enough to want a specification rather than another position. Before I commit to any account of the self as a center of narrative gravity, give me the desiderata: what any adequate theory in this area must deliver. Set out the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the results it has to remain consistent with; put behind each requirement the particular case or consideration that motivates it; say which of the requirements you take to be genuine and which to be the prejudices of a single camp — I suspect that much of the demand for unity in a life is a demand made on behalf of one temperament rather than a discovery about selves — and flag any pair of requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied. If the list turns out to be unsatisfiable as a whole, that is itself a result and I want it stated as one. Tell me, finally, which single requirement does the most work in ruling candidate theories in or out. Take the purpose to be a theory good enough for someone who must build selves on the page and also inhabit one, and which must therefore accommodate the atypical case without treating it as a defect.

Four things I want the specification to confront, because they are what sent me looking. First, the narrator and the narrated are not the same person; in fiction they come apart constantly, and I want to know whether such a theory locates the self in the teller, in the tale, or in the teller's protagonist, and what it says when the three diverge. Second, a narrative can be false and still load-bearing: my narrator's story about her childhood is, on the evidence I have given the reader, wrong, and she is not less a self for it — which suggests that truth cannot be made a condition of selfhood, though I do not see how it can be made irrelevant either. Third, not everyone is narratively organized at all; there are people who live from episode to episode, contentedly, and would find the description of a life as a story alien or even oppressive, and a theory that must call them defective, or not selves, has failed a case rather than made a discovery. Fourth, the self seems to be kept partly in other people's keeping: if a woman is the last surviving member of a family, and the last person who remembers her as she was dies, something about her seems genuinely altered although nothing has happened to her brain — I want to know whether the theory can say that without dissolving the self into mere reputation.

Against the list, give me a brief accounting of how the leading candidates fare — the Dennett-style self as a useful abstraction, the narrative self-constitution accounts, the minimal-self views for which the narrative layer is real but not constitutive, and the Parfit- and Buddhist-inflected no-self positions — but keep the weight on the criteria. The scoring is the part I can do myself once I know what I am scoring against.
