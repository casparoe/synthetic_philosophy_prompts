I am a doctoral candidate in the philosophy of law, and I have been trying to state a version of legal moralism that is precise enough to be argued with rather than merely rejected by slogan. The version I want to defend runs roughly as follows. Criminalisation requires wrongdoing, but the wrongdoing need not consist in harm to any determinate person. Among the wrongs that can justify prohibition is the erosion of what I call the moral environment: the background of shared norms, expectations, and dispositions on which individuals depend if they are to form and sustain the commitments that give their lives shape. I treat that environment as a public good with the structure of an environmental public good — non-excludable benefits, diffuse and cumulative degradation, thresholds below which the good collapses, and high costs of repair once it has. On this view a person who acts against the environment wrongs others even where no one is made worse off in any way a harm-based account can register, because the wrong consists in a change in what it is reasonable for everyone else to expect: a shift in the default rather than an injury to a victim. The position is a descendant of the one Devlin defended in his critique of the Wolfenden Report and that Hart contested, though I want to rest it on the structure of the good in question rather than on an argument about social disintegration or on the sentiments of the ordinary person.

What I want is a map of its exposure. What would count against it? Please rank the defeaters by how decisive they would be, separating those that would show the view false, those that would show it redundant (that everything it licenses is already licensed by a plausible harm principle), those that would show it unjust or dangerous, and those that would show it unfalsifiable. I am especially interested in what a committed defender of this kind of view would concede as counting against it, as opposed to what critics assert from outside. I would also like to know what the view predicts that its harm-based rivals do not, and what the world would have to look like — which comparisons across jurisdictions, which trends in trust or in the incidence of the relevant practices, which natural experiments — for the erosion claim to be disconfirmed rather than merely restated.

Two further matters. First, the view is plainly most exposed where it distinguishes enforcing a morality from enforcing a majority's preferences; I want to know whether that distinction can be sustained once the account is stated this precisely, or whether the moral environment simply is whatever the majority presently wishes preserved. Second, tell me whether the difficulty of specifying what would refute the view is a defect peculiar to legal moralism or a feature of any account that treats diffuse social goods as proper objects of criminal protection — and if it is a defect, whether it is fatal.

For what it is worth, a bare restatement of the harm principle would not move me, since that is the point in dispute. What would move me is a demonstration that the moral-environment apparatus does no work a harm-based account cannot do.
