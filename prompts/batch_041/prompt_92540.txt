Design a 90-minute in-class betting game for a 20-person undergraduate seminar on decision theory, with no real money, only a scoreboard. The goal is for students to feel, in their own bankrolls, the gap between the strategy that maximizes expected wealth and the strategy that maximizes long-run growth, and to leave able to explain why the two come apart.

The engine: each round the facilitator flips one shared coin that lands heads with probability 0.6. Before every flip each student privately chooses a fraction f of their current bankroll to stake (0 ≤ f ≤ 1, revisable each round); heads doubles the stake and returns it, tails forfeits it. Everyone starts at 1,000 points. Run 30 rounds and post the room's running mean and median wealth after each one.

Then the debrief: map what happened onto the arithmetic-versus-geometric mean of wealth, the fact that going all-in maximizes expected wealth (1.2× per round) while almost surely bankrupting you, and the difference between an ensemble average (mean across many parallel students) and a time average (one student's own path), which is the non-ergodicity that pulls them apart. Only now name the growth-maximizing fraction, 20%, and the fact that it is the fraction maximizing the expected logarithm of wealth, and have them check it against the survivors' choices.

Close frankly on what the game cannot deliver: it cannot show that growth-maximization is the rational policy, since a one-shot bettor with linear utility should go all-in, and it cannot reproduce betting when the coin's bias is unknown, where overbetting past twice the growth-optimal fraction is punished harder than underbetting. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle it.
