I'm a backend engineer, no formal philosophy training (one ethics elective in college, plus way too many podcasts), and I've somehow ended up owning the design doc for an internal tool we're calling the "why-bot." The idea: when someone asks "why does this service retry with 300ms of jitter instead of exponential backoff," the tool gathers everything it can find — the original PR description, review comments from whoever wrote it, comments added years later by other people, the commit history, the tests, and the code itself — and answers. The retrieval part is fine. The hard part is the conflict policy: the PR description says one thing, a comment from three years later says another, the tests enforce a third, and the original author left the company in 2019. Someone on the team said "just trust what the author meant," someone else said "the code is the source of truth, stop psychoanalyzing people who don't work here anymore," and we've been goign in circles ever since. So here's what I want: act as an expert on interpretation and the philosophy of authorial intention, and tell me which open questions in that area actually matter for this design decision, and in what order. We're two engineers with about six weeks before this becomes real infrastructure, so "go read all of hermeneutics" isnt an option.

Start by brainstorming what the live questions even are. I know there's a big debate about whether meaning is fixed by what the author intended, or by the conventions of the text, or by the community that reads it, or somewhere in between — but I dont know where the genuinely unresolved parts are versus where people are just talking past each other, and I'd rather hear the map before the ranking. Then rank the ones that matter for us and defend the ranking: which are upstream of the others (if we settle X, does Y get easier, or does Y just stop being a question?), which are actually tractable for two engineers in six weeks, and which are fascinating in the abstract but wouldn't change a single line of our conflict policy. Think it through step by step before you commit to an order, and flag where you're genuinely unsure.

Two things about our case that I suspect mess with the usual answers, so please tell me if I'm wrong. First, our text is executable. Unlike a poem, there's a fact of the matter about what it does, and no reader and no author gets to vote on it — I've been calling the test suite the "shadow author" and I have no idea if that's a real distinction or just me being cute. Second, the unit of meaning is unclear: is the "text" the diff, the file, the whole repo, or the org's unwritten conventions? A commit message can be perfectly clear about the diff it describes and completely misleading about the file it lands in, and our tool has to pick a unit before it can pick an authority. Given both of those, tell me what would change your ranking — what would we have to learn or observe in the next month for you to reorder the list — and what's the one thing you'd deprioritize that everyone else would put first. No reading list or citations needed, I just want the reasoning.
