I am trying to find a name for an argumentative pattern I keep running into, and I would rather have the phenomenon properly carved up than merely labelled.

The pattern, as I first met it, concerns perception. A hallucination and a veridical perception can be subjectively indistinguishable: the person undergoing either is in no position to tell which is which. That single fact — the indistinguishability — is then used twice in the same discussion, in two directions that pull against each other. It is used to explain why the person cannot be faulted for believing as she does, why no further check is available to her, why her epistemic position is precisely what it is. But when someone offers the very same indistinguishability as grounds for thinking that the two states have something in common — that they share a core present whether or not anything is actually perceived — the fact is disowned: indistinguishability for the subject is said to be a fact about the subject's position, not about the states, and to carry no weight whatever as to what the two states are.

I have since met what looks like the same shape elsewhere. Two people, one of whom genuinely remembers and one of whom has a confabulated 'memory', cannot be told apart from the inside; that is offered as the reason the confabulator is not to be blamed for asserting what she asserts, and then denied when it is suggested that both have the same kind of mental state, differing only in what caused it. I believe I have seen a third version of it in discussions of confabulation following split-brain surgery, though I could not state the shape there with the same confidence.

What makes this more than ordinary inconsistency is that both uses are licensed by a real distinction — between how things are for a subject and how things are — and that distinction is then applied selectively, admitted where it does explanatory work for one side of the dispute and excluded where it would do evidential work for the other. The nearest things I know of are the argument from illusion, which seems to be the setting rather than the phenomenon, and the charge of begging the question, which is too coarse to capture the point: the trouble is not that a premise is assumed, but that one and the same fact is treated as evidence-bearing in one place and evidence-barren in another.

Is there an established name for this — something that isolates the asymmetry rather than the family of views it is used to defend? If there is, I want to know how it relates to the nearest concepts and which of them fits best. If there is not, please coin a term and define it. Either way, what I am after is a carving rather than a label: whether this is one phenomenon or a family of them; what exactly the asymmetry consists in; the conditions under which it is a legitimate consequence of the difference between the order of knowing and the order of being rather than a fallacy; and where the boundary lies — for instance, whether the move changes character when the commonality being denied is a claim about the subject's evidence rather than about the states themselves, or when the two cases are not merely indistinguishable in practice but impossible to distinguish in principle.
