Here's the setup: 16 undergrads, one 90-minute slot in a seminar on concepts and analogy, and one idea I want them to feel in their bones rather than hear me say. The idea is that applying a concept isn't applying a rule. Take the little microworld Hofstadter's group built Copycat in — strings of letters, puzzles like "abc → abd, so ijk → ?" — where solving one of these turns out to be a pile of tiny local decisions about how to carve the string up (what counts as one unit, which position is doing the work, what exactly the change is a change of), with the answer falling out of those decisions. Which means your concepts can slide under you halfway through a problem and you won't notice, and two people who both "get the rule" can land on different answers with neither of them being sloppy or dumb. I want the room to feel that, not be told it. Ideally they come away unable to say what the rule was, while still feeling that their own answer was obviously right.

So design the activity. No lecture up front, no vocabulary up front — they shouldn't hear the word slippage, or the name Hofstadter, untill the debrief.

What I've been sketching, take it or leave it:

- Materials: index cards, pens, one printed sheet of four or five puzzles, a big visible timer, a whiteboard for scores. No computers.
- Puzzle set, in this order: (1) abc → abd, ijk → ? (2) abc → abd, xyz → ? (the obvious move is blocked by the end of the alphabet) (3) abc → abd, mrrjjj → ? (here's the one where it matters whether a unit is a letter or a group) (4) abc → dbc, ijk → ? (5) abc → abd, aabbcc → ?. Everyone agrees on the first. By (2) or (3) they shouldn't.
- A slip mechanic: everyone holds three cards, each naming a dimension they're allowed to let move — which letter gets changed, what counts as one unit, direction, whether the relation is a step in the alphabet or a sameness/difference. Playing a card is legal and costs points, but your answer has to be re-derived after you play it, not patched.
- A temperature board. Each table reports, after each puzzle, how well their reading hangs together on a 0–10 scale. High temperature means they're not done — they keep revising until it drops or the timer runs out.
- Two currencies that pull against each other: points for landing on the same answer as other tables, and points for a low temperature (a reading that hangs together with nothing left over). I want the tension to be real — an answer that fits beautifully but that nobody else gives should feel like a live option, and so should a popular answer that needs an ugly patch.
- Tables of four, one committed answer and one sentence of justification per round, and no adjudication from me, ever, even if they beg.

Then the debrief, which is where I do the actual work: map what happened onto the model — the tiny local decisions as codelets, the sliding of concepts as activation spreading through a network so that neighboring concepts bleed into each other, the temperature as the program's running sense of how well the pieces cohere and the fact that it just keeps going until that score drops, and the fact that nowhere in the machine is there a stored rule and nowhere in the room was there a judge. Plus why Hofstadter thought this was the core of cognition and not a puzzle trick.

Tell me the full rules, a timeline for the 90 minutes, how the scoring works without real money, the exact debrief moves, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and — honestly — which part of the idea the activity can't reproduce. I already suspect the big one: sixteen people arguing in a room is going to feel like there's somebody deciding, and Copycat has no decider, just thousands of dumb processes whose aggregate settles. If there's a way to stage it that gets closer to that, I want it. And I know I lose whatever comes from concepts having real perceptual content — the letter world is thin on purpose, so tell me what the thinness costs me.
