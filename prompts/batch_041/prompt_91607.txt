I'm a software engineer with no formal training in game theory or philosophy, and I've been trying to get my head around program equilibrium. As I understand the setup: two players face a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, but instead of choosing directly, each submits a program, and each program gets to read the other's source code before it decides. Because a program can condition its move on the other program's code, mutual cooperation can be an equilibrium even though there's no repetition, no reputation, and no way to make a promise or a threat in the ordinary sense — a program that cooperates exactly when the other program is a copy of itself, and defects otherwise, does well against a copy of itself. I've also run into a version where each program tries to prove what the other will do rather than simulate it, and I can't tell whether that's the same idea or something subtler.

What I can't get straight is what is actually doing the work here. Is the "commitment" real, or just an artifact of assuming the players are deterministic and mutually readable? Is this the repeated-game or reputation logic in disguise, or does it genuinely solve the one-shot problem? Why can't two people do the same thing by reasoning about each other's character? And what happens if I break the idealization — say I cap each program's running time so neither can fully simulate the other, or make the source readable in only one direction?

Please explain the conceptual issue: the structure of why program equilibrium works, and what it does and doesn't establish. Rather than just restating the setup, I want the explanation that makes the moving parts visible. Where the topic is contested among people who actually work on it, please separate what's settled from what's genuinely disputed, and don't present a contested position as the consensus.
