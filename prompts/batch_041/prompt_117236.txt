I've come to accept a view about pain that I want stress-tested: that the badness of pain belongs entirely to a separable affective component rather than to the sensation itself. Nociception tells you where and how much; a distinct insular–limbic circuit supplies the distress; strip the circuit and what remains is an evaluatively neutral sensory state. It's the picture I take from the two-dimensions-of-pain literature, and it happens to be the one I want to be true. My main evidence is the asymbolia cases — insular damage, and in weaker form cingulotomy or opioid analgesia, leaves patients reporting that a noxious stimulus hurts while showing no withdrawal, no distress, and no apparent wish for it to stop. Don't take my description on faith; correct it if it's wrong.

What would count against the view? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, separating what should genuinely move me from what a defender can shrug off, and include what defenders themselves ought to concede rather than only what critics would say. Two things I especially want addressed. First, whether the view can only be held by stipulating that the sensory component is what "pain" picks out and the affect is what "badness" picks out — and if so, whether that insulation is a fatal defect or the honest price of a correct decomposition. Second, whether its practical corollary — keep nociception as a warning signal, abolish the suffering — survives the fact that asymbolic patients don't act on the signal. I'd be most persuaded by a case in which unpleasantness is absent yet the subject is worse off in a way they cannot detect.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of my question.
