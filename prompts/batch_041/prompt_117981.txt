I am currently taking a course on nationalism and collective memory, and I would like to clarify my own commitments before a seminar. My current position is as follows: (1) nations are imagined communities sustained by narratives that are often false; (2) a political community nonetheless needs a shared civic myth to sustain solidarity and motivate sacrifice; (3) historians have a duty to debunk false founding myths, even when those myths are socially useful; (4) we should not judge historical actors by present moral standards, but should try to understand them in their own terms; (5) the Confederate Lost Cause, Masada, Kosovo and Dunkirk are all national myths that turn defeat or deliverance into a warrant for renewal, and I think they are morally pernicious when they sanctify loss and license future violence; (6) a national myth is best understood as a symbolic narrative rather than a factual claim, so its literal falsity is not itself a defect — what matters is the political work it does; and (7) commemoration should aim at truth rather than social cohesion. Which of these are actually in tension, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly way to make the set consistent? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and tell me what each repair would cost me.
