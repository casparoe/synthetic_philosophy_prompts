I've been reading around the "commitment races problem" that comes up in discussions of bargaining among sufficiently sophisticated rational agents -- it surfaces in the AI-safety-adjacent literature on open-source game theory, but it clearly has roots in older Schelling-style thinking about commitment devices. The rough setup, as I understand it: if agents can pre-commit to strategies (say, committing to never swerve in a game of chicken), then each agent has an incentive to commit as early as possible, before the other side does, since whoever locks in the aggressive strategy first effectively wins the game. But this generates a strange dynamic -- agents are pressured to commit before they've reasoned carefully about what they're committing to, because further deliberation is itself dangerous (the other side might commit first while you're still thinking things through).

I get the basic mechanics, but I'm struggling to isolate what's supposed to be distinctively paradoxical here, as opposed to just a fairly mundane case of first-mover advantage that shows up all over game theory. Is the puzzle really about the value of deliberation being self-undermining -- i.e., that the correct amount of reasoning to do before committing is itself something you'd need to reason about, creating a regress? Or is it more about the fact that "logical time" and commitment order aren't well-defined for agents that can potentially predict or simulate one another, so the race isn't really over calendar time at all? I'm putting together a lecture for a formal epistemology seminar and want to give students the intuitive core of the problem without wading into the full technical apparatus of program equilibrium or logical induction. Could you walk me through, at a gut level, what makes commitment races a genuinely hard and somewhat paradoxical problem rather than a straightforward strategic story, ideally with a simple example that isolates the distinctive difficulty?
