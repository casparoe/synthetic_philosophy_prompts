For a project on the history and philosophy of science, I have been trying to reconstruct the internal tensions in Johannes Kepler's attitude toward astrology. As imperial mathematician, Kepler spent years casting horoscopes and writing astrological almanacs, yet in works such as Tertius Interveniens (1610) and Book IV of the Harmonices Mundi (1619) he tried to occupy a middle position between astrology's outright defenders and its outright critics. He dismissed the zodiacal signs, the house system, and the whole apparatus of planetary rulerships and dignities as arbitrary human bookkeeping with no basis in nature, yet he insisted that the aspects, the angular relationships between planets such as conjunctions, sextiles, squares, trines, and oppositions, exert a genuine causal influence on weather and on human temperament. His reason was that these angles instantiate the same small set of constructible harmonic ratios that the soul recognizes in music, and that the earth itself, endowed on his view with a kind of soul, responds to the geometry of the planetary rays converging on it.

I would like you to write a dialogue between Kepler and a defender of the full traditional astrological system of his day, someone committed to signs, houses, and rulerships as much as to aspects, perhaps in the mold of his real-life interlocutor Helisaeus Röslin. Imagine the two seated across a table cluttered with ephemerides and half-drawn horoscope charts, working through their disagreement point by point. The traditionalist should press Kepler on why aspects get to count as physically real while the zodiac does not, given that both are, in the end, geometrical schemes projected onto the sky by human observers; Kepler should answer with his actual reasons, drawn from his harmonic theory of the soul and his account of celestial rays, rather than simply insisting that aspects feel more scientific. I want the two figures to be evenly matched, engaging with each other's specific objections rather than talking past one another, and I would rather the exchange end wherever it naturally does than be steered toward a tidy resolution.
