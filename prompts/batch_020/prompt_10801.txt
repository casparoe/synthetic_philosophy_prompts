A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about cognitive penetration and ended up talking past each other, which has bothered me ever since. We started from Susanna Siegel's "Downgrade Thesis," illustrated by her case of Jill, whose unfounded fear that Jack is angry with her causes her to have a "hijacked" experience of his neutral face as hostile. On Siegel's view, once an experience's causal history includes a rationally assessable but substandard state like fear or prejudice, the experience is epistemically downgraded and can no longer do the justificatory work an untainted experience would. My friend thought this generalizes straightforwardly to real-world worries about biased threat perception. I pushed back with cases of perceptual expertise — a radiologist's training penetrating what she sees in a scan, a birder's field-mark knowledge penetrating what he sees in a half-second glimpse — where nobody thinks the resulting experience is thereby downgraded. My friend's answer was that reliability separates the two kinds of case; I wasn't convinced that "reliability" can be cashed out here without either begging the question against a perceiver who is reliable only relative to a biased environment, or simply collapsing into "downgraded whenever we independently disapprove of the belief."

Could you write a dialogue between two philosophers who take up these two positions, actually engaging with each other's strongest moves rather than talking past one another as we did? I would like them to get concrete about what, if anything, could fill in "reliable" (or some alternative, such as the rational standing of the penetrating state) so that it tracks the difference between Jill's case and the expert-perception cases without simply presupposing the verdict. A genuine stalemate is a fine outcome, so long as they have pinpointed exactly where the disagreement bites.
