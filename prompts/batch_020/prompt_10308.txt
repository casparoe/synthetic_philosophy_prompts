I teach a graduate seminar on jurisprudence and also host a small podcast in which I put questions to scholars working in legal philosophy. My students and I spent much of this term back inside the original 1958 *Harvard Law Review* exchange, which sharpened my sense of how quickly the standard summary — "Hart separates law and morals, Fuller doesn't" — glosses over where the real argument happens. Next month's guest has built her career reviving and refining Fuller's side of that exchange, defending the claim that the eight desiderata he sets out in *The Morality of Law* — generality, promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, non-contradiction, the possibility of compliance, constancy through time, and congruence between the rule as declared and as administered — amount to a genuine "internal morality of law," rather than mere craft standards for ruling by rules as such. Her signature move, whenever someone raises a wicked-yet-orderly regime against her, is to insist that serious injustice always shows up, on close inspection, as a failure of one or more of the eight desiderata.

I'd like your help drafting eight to ten interview questions that press on this position rather than inviting her to restate it. Please include: one question built on Hart's charge, from his review of the book, that the eight desiderata are indistinguishable from the "morality" of an efficient poisoner — principles of technique compatible with any end, good or evil — with a follow-up keyed to the reciprocity-between-lawgiver-and-citizen response she is likely to reach for; one on whether all eight desiderata are really doing equal moral work, since congruence looks like it is carrying most of the weight while something like clarity looks closer to pure craftsmanship, with a follow-up asking whether granting this shrinks her thesis into something Hart could accept; and one that uses the grudge-informer case — the postwar prosecution of a woman who had denounced her husband to Nazi authorities under a wartime statute — to force a direct answer about whether that statute was valid law at the time she invoked it, with a follow-up asking whether her answer is really distinguishable from Hart's own preferred solution of frank, openly retroactive legislation. Then give me a short escalating sequence of three regimes — Nazi Germany, apartheid-era South Africa (which Fuller himself judged a gross departure from the eight desiderata, though some of her colleagues argue it satisfied all eight about as well as any real legal system ever has), and a present-day authoritarian state's wartime "anti-disinformation" statute — meant to make her signature deflection progressively harder to sustain as the cases get closer to home. Close with a question asking her to specify, concretely, what a legal system would have to look like to count as a decisive counterexample to the internal-morality thesis, and whether she can point to anything real or realistic that comes close.
