A few of us on my team run an informal AI-safety reading group over lunch, and two people got stuck arguing about "embedded agency" -- the idea, from Garrabrant and Demski's write-up, that a real agent is part of the environment it has to model and act in, rather than sitting outside it with a clean interface the way something like AIXI does. I'd like a neutral read on who actually had the better of it.

1. Nadia said the framework is overblown: give a Solomonoff-induction-style agent a large enough hypothesis space over computable environments, and you can just include "a complete description of the agent itself, sitting in this environment" as one more hypothesis, updated by Bayes' rule like everything else. Embedded agency, on her view, isn't a new kind of problem, just an old induction problem that happens to be hard.

2. Ben objected that this fails even with unlimited compute: the hypothesis space is itself generated by the agent's own reasoning, so a hypothesis meant to be a complete, exact description of that very reasoning, sitting inside the space it generates, hits the same kind of self-reference wall as the halting problem. It's not a "too little compute" problem, it's a "the map can't losslessly contain itself" problem.

3. Nadia conceded you can't get an exact self-model, but said that costs her nothing: no real system, biological or artificial, ever has an exact model of itself, so this is just ordinary approximation error -- the same bias/variance tradeoff every ML engineer already lives with -- wearing a philosophy costume.

4. Ben replied that approximating yourself isn't like approximating a sensor reading, because your model of what you'll do can change what you do, and a system trying to formally trust its own future reasoning (or a successor's) runs into real obstacles from Löb's theorem that have no analogue when you're just smoothing noisy data. Calling it "just approximation error," he said, quietly drops the one feature that makes it hard.
