So I've fallen down a bit of a rabbit hole on this "hard choices" idea from the philosopher Ruth Chang (started with her TED talk, then read a bunch of her essays online). I run a little casual philosphy discussion group with some friends, nothing official, and I like to throw everyone a question each week just for fun. This week I asked:

"Chang argues that a genuinely hard choice (like between two very different careers) isn't just a case where the two options happen to be exactly equal in value. Using her small-improvement argument, explain why she thinks that, and say what relation she thinks actually holds between the options instead of the usual better/worse/equal."

One of the group, Sam, sent in this answer:

"Say you're stuck between becoming a lawyer and becoming a musician, and neither one seems clearly better than the other — that's what makes it hard in the first place. The old-school view is that if neither is better than the other, they must just be exactly equal. Chang's move is to say: okay, if they were really exactly equal, then improving the lawyer option just a little bit (a bit more salary, whatever) should be enough to tip it into being the better option now. But in an actual hard choice, even a slightly improved lawyer option doesn't obviously beat the musician option — you'd still be torn. So they weren't exactly equal to start with. That's why Chang thinks there's a fourth relation, next to better/worse/equal, which she calls being 'on a par.' It's basically her spin on Isaiah Berlin's incommensurable values — since the lawyer-value and the musician-value are on totally different scales with no common unit, there's no way to stack them up against each other, so really they're incomparable, and it's that incomparability that makes the choice hard and, in a way, ours to make."

I don't have any formal philosophy background so I honestly can't tell how good this answer actually is.
