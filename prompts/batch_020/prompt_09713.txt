In the philosophy elective I'm taking this semester, our teacher assigned us a short excerpt from L. A. Paul's work on transformative experience, and I have not been able to stop thinking about it since, because it seems to describe exactly the situation I'm in right now with the college decisions I'm supposed to be making this year. As I understood the reading, Paul distinguishes between experiences that are "epistemically transformative," meaning you cannot really grasp their character until you have had them (the way you cannot know what an unfamiliar fruit tastes like from a description), and experiences that are additionally "personally transformative," meaning they reshape your core preferences and sense of self. Her central illustration is the thought experiment of choosing to become a vampire, which she treats as a stand-in for real decisions like becoming a parent, and she argues that this combination undermines the usual model of rational choice, in which you imagine each possible outcome, estimate how much you would value it, and choose whichever option has the highest expected value, since you cannot assign a value to an outcome whose subjective character you cannot yet access and which would change the very self doing the evaluating.

What I'm stuck on is what to actually do with this idea when it comes to choosing between two real options in front of me: staying close to home at a small university where I'm fairly confident I would be happy, or moving somewhere completely unfamiliar for a program everyone describes as "life-changing." A few of my friends think Paul's argument basically proves that this kind of decision cannot be made rationally at all, while I suspect there must be more useful guidance hiding in the theory, or in responses to it such as Agnes Callard's notion of "aspiration," than simply "you cannot calculate your way to an answer." Could you lay out the most important implications of Paul's framework for how someone actually in the middle of a transformative choice like mine should reason about it, rather than concluding that deliberation is pointless?
