Three weeks ago, in the middle of a sleep-deprived push to finish my dissertation, I convinced myself I had patched a gap in a proof my advisor had already flagged as unsound. A friend who researches sleep deprivation later told me, half-joking, that at that level of exhaustion my reasoning was probably about as trustworthy as if I had been drinking -- and yet now, fully rested, the patch still looks airtight to me, and no one has located an error in it. That gap between the excellent higher-order evidence I have of my own unreliability and how compelling the first-order reasoning still feels is exactly what I want to explore with next week's guest on my podcast: a philosopher known for arguing, against the enkratic principle that dominates the field, that this kind of epistemic akrasia -- knowingly holding onto a belief while having good reason to doubt the process that produced it -- can itself be fully rational. Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her restate the position. I want at least one question on how her account would distinguish a case like mine from simple motivated reasoning, one on the worry that licensing akrasia here hands a cheap defense to anyone whose first-order convictions feel solid no matter what they learn about their own reliability, and one on whether she takes the retained belief to be positively rational or merely blameless -- and for each, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give.
