So I went down a bit of a wikipedia rabbit hole about peacocks and evolution a while back, and there's this one detail that stuck with me ever since. Darwin was apparently so bothered by the peacock's tail that he wrote in a letter (to a botanist friend, I think it was 1860, so basically right after Origin of Species came out) that just looking at the thing made him feel sick, because it seemed like a genuine problem for his own theory - why would nature ever select for something so heavy and impractical that it basically makes you an easier lunch for predators? He patched this up later with "sexual selection" (females just like flashier tails, so they keep getting flashier over generations), but from what I read even that doesn't really explain why females would evolve to prefer something so wasteful in the first place, instead of just going for whoever looks healthiest and easiest to feed. Apparently that part of the puzzle just sat there unsolved for like another hundred years.

Then I found out about this Israeli biologist, Amotz Zahavi, who in the 70s came up with the "handicap principle" - basically that the wastefulness IS the whole point, since its the one thing a weak or sickly peacock literally could not fake. Only a genuinely strong bird could grow that ridiculous tail and still dodge predators, so the cost itself is what makes the signal trustworthy instead of just empty bragging. Kinda like proof-of-work, but for biology lol.

Anyway it got me wondering - if you could somehow sit Darwin down today and walk him through Zahavi's idea, do you think he'd feel like it finally solved the exact thing that was bugging him back in 1860, or would something about the whole "costly signals are honest signals" logic have rubbed him the wrong way given how he normally liked to argue for things? And do you think he'd have been willing to run with that same logic to explain all the weirdly wasteful status stuff humans do now - things like outrageously expensive weddings, hazing rituals, or buying a car specifically because its impractical - or was he too careful a thinker to make that jump from peacocks to modern people?
