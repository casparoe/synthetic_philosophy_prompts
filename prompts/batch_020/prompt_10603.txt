I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I recently came across Ian Hacking's notion of "looping effects": his claim that classifying people under a psychiatric category (his own examples include multiple personality disorder and autism) changes how those people understand and present themselves, which can in turn reshape the category itself. For Hacking, this feedback loop is what marks off "interactive" human kinds from "indifferent" kinds like quarks or rocks, which do not respond to how we classify them. This struck me as similar to something I know from machine learning: deploying a predictive model, such as a recommender system or a recidivism-risk score, changes the behavior of the very population it was trained on, so that its target keeps shifting beneath it — a phenomenon sometimes called "performative prediction" in the machine learning literature. Please construct the strongest argument you can that Hacking's looping effects are simply a special case of this more general feedback phenomenon between a predictive or classificatory practice and the system it acts on, rather than a distinctive metaphysical feature of human kinds as such. If that argument succeeds, psychiatric classification would be in no deeper epistemic trouble than plenty of purely engineered predictive systems. In building the case, engage directly with Hacking's own stated reasons for treating human kinds as special, particularly the role of self-understanding in the loop, rather than resting on the surface-level analogy alone.
