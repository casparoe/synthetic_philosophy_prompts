This past summer I enrolled in an online philosophy-of-physics seminar for advanced high schoolers, partly because reading Sean Carroll's book on time and entropy, *From Eternity to Here*, over spring break left me with more questions than my AP Physics class had equipped me to answer. The course uses mandatory peer grading: before the platform releases my own grade for a given week's short-answer assignment, I have to score three classmates' submissions against the point breakdown the instructor posts, and she says she periodically spot-checks our grades against her own, so I want to make sure I'm applying the rubric carefully and consistently for every submission rather than skimming and matching the overall tone of the writing.

This week's question was: "Explain how a directional 'arrow of time' can arise even though the fundamental dynamical laws of physics (Newtonian mechanics, electromagnetism, the Schrödinger equation) do not themselves prefer one time direction over the other. Your answer should address (a) why the Second Law of Thermodynamics is statistical rather than exceptionless, (b) why this requires assuming something specific about the universe's distant past, and (c) how our subjective sense of remembering the past but not the future fits into the picture." The instructor's point breakdown for the 20 available points is: 4 points for correctly identifying the time-reversal symmetry of the fundamental dynamical laws; 4 points for a correct statistical, Boltzmann-style account of why the Second Law is probabilistic rather than absolute; 4 points for correctly explaining why a special low-entropy condition in the distant past is required, and accurately characterizing that requirement; 4 points for correctly connecting entropy increase to why memories and records track one time direction rather than the other; and 4 points for accuracy and relevance if the response brings in any additional physics beyond what the question strictly asks for.

Here is the submission I've been assigned to grade:

"The tension dissolves once you separate the dynamical laws from the initial conditions they act on. Newton's laws, Maxwell's equations, and the Schrödinger equation are all invariant under time reversal: for every trajectory a system can follow, running it backward is an equally valid solution. So the arrow can't be sitting in the fundamental dynamics; it has to come from something we're feeding into those dynamics. That something is statistics. On Boltzmann's account, entropy measures how many microstates are compatible with a given macrostate, and because high-entropy macrostates vastly outnumber low-entropy ones, a system that starts out low-entropy will overwhelmingly tend toward higher entropy, simply because almost every direction it could evolve in leads there. That's why the Second Law is probabilistic rather than exact: entropy decreasing isn't impossible, just astronomically unlikely. This raises the obvious question of why the system, or the universe, should have started in a low-entropy state at all, since the same statistical reasoning that predicts entropy will rise toward the future predicts it should also rise toward the past, which is absurd. Boltzmann's answer, which he called the Past Hypothesis, is that we just have to posit a very special, low-entropy initial condition and not ask for a deeper reason. Memory works the same way underneath: recording an event is itself an entropy-increasing process, so records only reliably track the lower-entropy direction, which is why we remember the direction we call 'the past.' It's also worth mentioning that the fundamental laws aren't perfectly time-symmetric after all: neutral kaons violate T-symmetry directly, so in some sense that microscopic violation is the ultimate source of the asymmetry between past and future that shows up everywhere else."
