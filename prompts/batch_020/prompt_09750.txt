I'm about two-thirds into a novel where the central image is this old brass bell that a family of bellfounders keeps melting down and recasting into a totally different bell every generation — different shape, different tone, everything — and I want one of my characters (a bitter, semi-retired philosophy don who lives above the foundry) to be able to rant convincingly about why this is a genuinely hard metaphysical problem and not just a nice image for the book jacket. So a few weeks ago I went down a rabbit hole reading about the "statue and the clay" puzzle in metaphysics, and now I'm the one who's stuck on it — my partner thinks its ridiculous that I've stalled on chapter 14 because of this.

I get the headline version: the statue can't survive being squashed but the lump of clay can, so by Leibniz's law they can't be strictly the same thing, except they also seem to be made of literally identical matter occupying literally the same place at the same time — so what actually are they? Once I started poking around, though, I found a whole zoo of rival answers: some people say the statue and the clay are two distinct things that just happen to "coincide" rather than being identical, some go four-dimensional and say the whole puzzle rests on a mistake about how persisting things are built out of temporal parts in the first place, some apparently just deny that statues (or bells) exist as objects at all over and above particles arranged statue-wise (I think the guy's name is van Inwagen?), and then someone — Gibbard, maybe? — apparently just bites the bullet and says identity itself can be contingent, which seems like kind of a huge move if it's actually defensible. Could you write me something like a proper essay on this: lay out the strongest version of each major position, actually weigh them against each other instead of just listing them side by side, and then land somewhere and defend it — no "reasonable people disagree" cop-out. I want my professor character's position to be one a real philosopher could hold with a straight face, not just something that sounds good read aloud in chapter 14.
