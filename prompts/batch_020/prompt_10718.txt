In his *Encomium of Helen*, the sophist Gorgias defends Helen of Troy against the charge of bringing ruin on herself and her city by arguing that if she sailed off with Paris because his speech persuaded her, she is no more blameworthy than if she had simply been abducted by force. Persuasion, on his account, is not an appeal that leaves the will intact: speech acts on the soul the way a drug acts on the body, and a listener who has truly been won over by words has undergone a kind of compulsion rather than made a free choice. Aristotle's *Rhetoric* offers a very different picture of the same phenomenon: rhetoric there is the legitimate counterpart of dialectic, a systematic art of discovering the available means of persuasion in a given case, one that operates through an audience's reasoning, its judgment of the speaker's character, and its emotions, rather than simply overriding them. On this view, someone who has been rhetorically persuaded has still, in some meaningful sense, judged and chosen.

I keep coming back to this old disagreement when I think about how superintelligent AI systems ought to be permitted to talk to people, given that such a system could plausibly be more persuasive than any human orator, propagandist, or advertiser has ever been. Compare Gorgias's picture of persuasion as a kind of compulsion with Aristotle's picture of persuasion as a reason-engaging craft, and use that comparison to work out which one is the more accurate description of what would actually happen if a superintelligent system set out to talk someone into something. Is there a threshold of sheer persuasive skill beyond which Aristotle's picture collapses back into Gorgias's, however sound the AI's individual arguments might be? Defend a position, and spell out what it implies about the constraints such a system's speech ought to operate under.
