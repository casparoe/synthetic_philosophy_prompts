A friend of mine, who is finishing a PhD in formal epistemology, and I — an auditor in the same seminar, with no degree riding on it — spent most of an overlong dinner last week arguing about how you're supposed to revise your beliefs when the evidence you receive doesn't hand you a proposition to become certain of. We started from common ground: Jeffrey's old example of examining a piece of cloth by candlelight and coming away more confident that it's green than that it's blue, without ever becoming certain of either, and how his rule of "probability kinematics" handles this by redistributing your credence across the green/blue partition while holding your conditional probabilities on everything else rigid. Neither of us has any quarrel with that case. The trouble started when she brought up van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin problem, and by the time we gave up, each of us was convinced the other was missing something quite basic.

Here is the setup, for context. Judy Benjamin has parachuted into a region where, by her prior lights, four possibilities are equally likely: the territory is held by the Red army or the Blue army (fifty-fifty), and independently of that, whichever army holds it, the specific area she's landed in is patrolled by that army's Headquarters Company or its Second Company (fifty-fifty again) — so a quarter of her credence sits on each of the four combinations. A garbled radio message gets through, and all she can make out is, in effect, that if she's in Red territory, the odds are about three to one that she's in the Second Company's part rather than the Headquarters Company's part. The message says nothing comparing Red to Blue, and nothing at all about how the Blue side is divided. Yet, as my friend delighted in pointing out, if you fold this into a new distribution over the four regions by the seemingly natural, Jaynes-style method of maximizing entropy (equivalently here, since the prior is uniform, minimizing cross-entropy from it) subject to just that one constraint, her credence that she's in Red territory at all does not stay at one-half: it drifts down to somewhere around 47 percent, even though the message never touched the Red-versus-Blue question. My friend takes this as more or less fatal to the idea that there's any uniquely rational way to handle evidence like this: whichever partition you decide to hold "rigid," in Jeffrey's sense, is a modeler's choice dressed up as a discovery, and outside toy cases like the candlelit cloth, talk of "the" rational posterior is closer to a convenient fiction than to anything Bayesianism actually delivers. I think that's too quick. I want to say the puzzle indicts unconstrained maximum-entropy updating specifically, that there's a principled basis for deciding which partition the message's content licenses you to hold fixed, and that needing an interpretive judgment about what a piece of evidence is "about" is not a special embarrassment for Jeffrey conditionalization, since ordinary conditionalization faces exactly the same question about how to individuate the proposition supposedly learned.

We went around on this for the better part of an hour without either of us budging, and it's still nagging at me. Could you write the dialogue we never quite managed to have: between two philosophers who hold these two positions, picking up right at this impasse? I'd like them evenly matched, so that each has to engage the other's actual best argument rather than a weaker stand-in for it, and I'd like the exchange to bear down specifically on whether choosing a rigid partition for evidence like Judy Benjamin's message is a principled inference from what the evidence says or an unforced stipulation dressed up as one — and on whether that distinction is even as clean as my side of the argument needs it to be. They needn't end up agreeing; I doubt there's a tidy resolution. But I'd like them to get further than my friend and I did before we gave up and ordered dessert.
