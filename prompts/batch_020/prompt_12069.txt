I spent close to forty years as a behavioural ecologist, a good part of it tracking reciprocal blood-sharing among roost-mates in vampire bats, and I had assumed retirement would keep me safely out of philosophers' hair. Instead, a small reading group at the local university talked me into Sharon Street's paper on the "Darwinian dilemma" for moral realism, and the problem has been nagging at me ever since. I've written up my objection to it below — more an argument sketch than a polished essay — and would value your comments before I bring it back to the group next month and, I suspect, embarrass myself in front of people who have actually read Parfit.

As I understand Street's dilemma, a realist who thinks some evaluative truths hold independently of what any creature happens to value owes us an account of how those truths relate to the selective pressures that plainly shaped our evaluative dispositions. Either the pressures had nothing to do with truth-tracking, in which case it would be a remarkable coincidence if our moral beliefs turned out true, or they did track truth somehow, in which case the realist must explain why a purely "adaptive link" story — kin selection, reciprocal altruism, and the rest — doesn't already explain the same dispositions more cheaply, with no detour through independent moral facts. What strikes me, coming from the biology side, is that this argument and the standard realist replies to it (Enoch's "third-factor" explanations, the "companions in guilt" appeal to mathematics and logic) all quietly agree on one thing: that traits like fairness, reciprocity, and loyalty are adaptations, shaped by selection for precisely the function they now serve. That is exactly the inference Gould and Lewontin spent their careers warning against. A plausible fitness story is cheap to tell; distinguishing a genuine target of selection from a by-product, a spandrel, or a trait merely co-opted from some earlier use takes heritability data, evidence of an actual selection differential, and the patient exclusion of drift and pleiotropy. I have not yet found anyone holding human "fairness psychology" to that standard of evidence, though I would be delighted to be shown otherwise.

There are three things I would like from you, specifically. First, tell me plainly whether I have represented Street's argument and the realist responses to it fairly, or whether I have oversimplified something load-bearing. Second, is the adaptationist assumption I am flagging something metaethicists have already noticed and addressed — and if some particular author has engaged with it directly, I would like to know who, so that I can go read them instead of reinventing an already-settled objection. Third, and this is the part that keeps me up at night: if the evidence for treating these dispositions as direct adaptations is really as thin as I suspect, doesn't that weaken the debunker's adaptive-link account at least as much as it weakens any realist tracking story — so that the whole dilemma turns out to rest on firmer biological ground than either side currently has a right to claim?
