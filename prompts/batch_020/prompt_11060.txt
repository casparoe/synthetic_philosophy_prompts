I spent thirty-two years as a comparative anatomist before retiring, and I have lately been sitting in on a philosophy-of-biology seminar. Two of the regulars, Devi and Sam, got into a disagreement recently that I cannot stop turning over.

We were comparing etiological (selected-effect) and causal-role accounts of biological function, using the mammalian ear bones as the test case: the malleus and incus are, developmentally, the old reptilian jaw-joint bones (the articular and quadrate), gradually repurposed over millions of years for hearing. Devi said this favors the causal-role view: at some point in that transition the bones were already contributing to hearing, long before that contribution could have been "selected for" over enough generations to count, etiologically, as a function -- so etiological theory must deny them a hearing function they plainly already had. Sam answered that etiological theories need only some actual selective episode, however brief, not eons; the moment survival first tracked the hearing effect, the function was already secured, so Devi's bottleneck was longer than the theory requires. Devi replied that this only relocates the problem: whether a structure has a function now turns on selection differentials, in extinct lineages, that no anatomist could ever recover, whereas causal-role facts come straight from dissection -- which is how biologists assign functions in practice. Sam said that conflates epistemology with metaphysics -- how we learn a fact is not what makes it one -- and added that Devi's view still cannot distinguish a bone malfunctioning from its simply doing something new, a distinction etiological theory gets for free.

Neither gave ground before the seminar moved on. Could you go through the four moves and say who has the better of the argument at each step -- not just who wins overall -- and note anything you think both of them are missing?
