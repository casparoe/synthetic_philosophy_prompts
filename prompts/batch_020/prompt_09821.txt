Last night a friend and I ended up rereading Alan Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" after a long dinner argument about whether any current chatbot would actually satisfy his imitation game, and along the way we got stuck on something unrelated: the last of the nine "Contrary Views on the Main Question" he works through before turning to learning machines, the one on extrasensory perception. My reading has always been that Turing raises ESP mainly to clear it off the table, in much the same spirit as the "heads in the sand" objection a few pages earlier, which he waves off in a line or two as not even worth refuting. A mathematician of his rigor, I argued, would have had no real patience for telepathy and clairvoyance, and was simply being a good sport by devoting a paragraph to the topic before returning to the parts of the essay he actually cared about. My friend was fairly sure this was a misreading but could not reconstruct the actual passage well enough to settle it on the spot, so I said I would put the question to you directly rather than trust either of our memories.

So, assuming my reading is basically correct: why do you think Turing bothered constructing such a specific empirical scenario for the ESP objection -- an interrogator asking a telepathic subject to guess the suits of hidden playing cards -- when he does not extend anything like that courtesy to the "heads in the sand" objection, which he dismisses without argument at all? Is the extra machinery just Turing enjoying himself, or does it suggest that ESP was doing some other kind of work in the essay, one that had little to do with what he actually believed about telepathy?
