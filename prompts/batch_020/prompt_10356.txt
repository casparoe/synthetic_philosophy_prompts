"Popper introduces verisimilitude because, under fallibilism, we want to say that some false theories are nevertheless closer to the truth than others, so science can progress toward the truth even though, by Popper's own lights, none of our theories are known to be true. This is meant to do better than plain probability, since raising a theory's content lowers its probability, so the most probable theories tend to be the least informative ones, wheras verisimilitude should go up when we add true content.

Formally, split the logical consequences of a theory a into its true content Ct_T(a) and its false content Ct_F(a). Theory a has more verisimilitude than theory b iff Ct_T(b) ⊆ Ct_T(a) and Ct_F(a) ⊆ Ct_F(b), with at least one containment proper: a says everything true that b says (and maybe more), and everything false that a says, b also says (and maybe more).

Popper eventually proved that this backfires: whenever a and b are both false, it's impossible for a to satisfy both containments over b, so no two false theories can ever be ranked, no matter how intuitively different their truthlikeness is. Since almost every theory in the history of science is false, this wrecks the whole project.

As an example, take the Ptolemaic model (planets circle a stationary earth) versus the Copernican model (planets circle the sun in perfect circles). Since Copernicus basically got the structure of the solar system right, we can treat his theory as true for these purposes, so Popper's containment conditions rank it above Ptolemy's without any trouble, which is exactly the kind of case the definition was designed to capture.

Niiniluoto and Oddie try to fix this by replacing content classes with a measure of similarity between a theory's models and the actual world (state descriptions, or "constituents"), so that a can count as closer to the truth than b even when neither's content contains the other's, which is exactly the case Popper's original definition couldn't handle."
