I am a novelist currently drafting a book centred on a fractious graduate seminar in metaphysics, and in the interest of getting the intellectual texture right, I have spent the past several months auditing an actual graduate course on material constitution at a university near me. The professor, who has become a friend and plainly regards my presence as more entertaining than useful, asked this week whether I would help grade a batch of take-home exams while she is away at a conference. I agreed, largely out of curiosity about whether I could reliably tell a sound answer from a merely fluent one, but before I commit anything to paper I would like a sharper opinion than mine on the single answer I have been assigned, since I do not entirely trust myself either to wave through something subtly confused because it is confidently phrased, or to penalise a genuine insight because I have failed to recognise it as one.

The question set was this: "Set out the standard modal argument, turning on Leibniz's Law, for the conclusion that a clay statue and the lump of clay that composes it are numerically distinct objects. Then explain how Lynne Rudder Baker's constitution view is meant to accommodate the intuition that the statue and the lump are nonetheless more intimately related than merely being co-located, making sure to explain her distinction between having a property derivatively and having it non-derivatively. Finally, state the 'too many thinkers' objection as it would apply to a constitution view of persons and their bodies, and assess whether the derivative/non-derivative distinction gives Baker the resources to answer it."

Here, transcribed exactly, is the answer I have been asked to grade:

"The puzzle begins from three individually plausible claims that cannot all be true together: first, that the lump of clay and the statue occupy exactly the same place and are made of exactly the same matter at every moment during which they coexist; second, that the lump existed before the statue did (the sculptor rolled it into a ball first) and could survive being squashed back into a shapeless mass, whereas the statue could not survive being squashed; and third, that if the lump can survive an event the statue cannot, then the lump and the statue do not share all their properties. The principle at work here is the Identity of Indiscernibles: if two things share all the same properties, they are one and the same thing. Since the statue and the lump diverge on this modal property, they fail the test, and so, by that same principle, they cannot be identical. What makes the argument interesting is that it shows two objects can share all their matter and occupy the very same place while still being numerically two things.

Baker accepts the conclusion that the lump and the statue are not identical, but resists the idea that this makes their relation a merely accidental one, on a par with two strangers who happen to occupy the same train carriage. She introduces constitution as a distinctive relation: when, and only when, the clay is appropriately shaped, the lump constitutes the statue, and for as long as constitution holds, the statue has, derivatively, all of the lump's material properties (its mass, its being clay, its chemical make-up), while the lump has, derivatively, certain properties that depend on the statue's being an intentionally made artwork (being on exhibition, being valuable as sculpture). Each also retains properties non-derivatively: the lump non-derivatively survives squashing, and the statue non-derivatively depends for its existence on someone having shaped the clay with artistic intent. Because the relation is this tight, Baker can say that for exactly the period during which the clay is statue-shaped, the lump and the statue are, in effect, one and the same object, and that this identity is temporary rather than permanent: once the sculptor balls the clay back up, the statue goes out of existence while the lump, now merely a lump again, persists.

The 'too many thinkers' objection raises the analogous worry about persons: if a human animal constitutes a person the way clay constitutes a statue, the animal has all of the person's mental properties derivatively, so both the animal and the person believe, desire, and are conscious -- and now there seem to be two thinkers exactly where common sense expects one. Baker's derivative/non-derivative distinction is supposed to defuse this: only the person has mental properties non-derivatively, and having a property only derivatively is not full-blooded possession of it, so the animal does not really think in the sense that matters; it merely constitutes something that does. I think this reply succeeds, since once we grant a principled and independently motivated distinction between derivative and non-derivative possession of a property, the inference from 'the animal has mental properties in some sense' to 'the animal is a thinker in the very same sense a person is' simply does not go through -- the objection has force only on the assumption that all property-possession is on a par, which is exactly what Baker's distinction denies."
