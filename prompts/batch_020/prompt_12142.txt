I'm a PhD student in political philosophy, and I co-produce a small podcast on the history of political thought that mostly gets used as background listening for other people's graduate seminars. Next month we're recording an episode with a Hegel scholar who has just published a monograph defending what she calls a "recognition-theoretic" reading of the treatment of war in the Philosophy of Right: on her view, the claim that war preserves what's usually translated as "the ethical health of peoples" is not incidental color but a direct extension, to the level of states, of the logic of the lordship-and-bondage struggle in the Phenomenology — so that a plurality of genuinely sovereign states could never settle into a permanent peace without ceasing to be the kind of self-certain, individual agents Hegel thinks they are. She has also gone on record dismissing the now-standard defense that Hegel is merely describing a systemic tendency rather than endorsing anything, calling that move "a way of getting Hegel off a hook he never wanted off."

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her restate the thesis of the book? I'd like at least one question built around the well-known simile in which war is compared to the winds that keep the sea from the stagnation of a permanent calm — pressing on whether the image is doing real argumentative work about what a state fundamentally is, or is just dressing up an assumption — with a follow-up keyed to whatever move she makes to rescue it. I'd like at least one on how her reading is meant to survive Hegel's own argument, against Kant's proposed federation of states, that the absence of any power standing over sovereign states the way the state stands over quarreling individuals is a structural fact rather than a temporary defect, and what that implies about whether a fully rational, worldwide order could ever actually retire war rather than just postpone it. And I'd like at least one that presses the lordship-and-bondage analogy at its weakest point: the self-consciousnesses in that struggle stake their own lives, whereas states that go to war stake the lives of their citizens, so whatever "recognition" a state wins looks bought with other people's bodies in a way that does not obviously track the original argument. For each of these three, add a follow-up tuned to the answer she is most likely to give, so we don't let her retreat to "it's only a figure of speech" or "the analogy is meant loosely." The remaining questions can range more freely, but please include at least one asking her to square all this with Hegel's account of history as the progressive actualization of freedom: if war remains structurally necessary all the way down, in what sense is later history actually freer than earlier history, rather than merely better dressed.
