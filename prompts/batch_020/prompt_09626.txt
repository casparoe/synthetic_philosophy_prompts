Here's the question (worth 10 of the exam's 100 points): "The 'replication crisis' in psychology and biomedicine is often blamed partly on the field's traditional reliance on null-hypothesis significance testing (NHST). (a) Using the concept of 'researcher degrees of freedom,' explain why a framework organized around a p < .05 threshold is especially prone to producing spurious 'significant' findings. (b) Would switching to Bayesian hypothesis testing and credible intervals, on its own, solve the underlying problem? Justify your answer."

And here's the student's answer: "A p-value is the probability of the observed data, or something more extreme, given that the null hypothesis is true — not the probability that the null is true given the data, which is what people often mistake it for. The problem with NHST as actually practiced is that 'p < .05' functions as a bright line for publishability, while researchers face small, individually defensible-looking choices at almost every stage of analysis — which outliers to exclude, which covariates to control for, which of several DVs or subgroups to report, when to stop collecting data — and any combination of these choices can push a result across that line without anyone deciding to cheat. Simmons, Nelson, and Simonsohn's term for this is 'researcher degrees of freedom.' Gelman and Loken make a related but distinct point with their 'garden of forking paths': even a single analysis, honestly reported, can have an inflated error rate if the choice of that analysis was contingent on the data, because the relevant error rate has to account for all the analyses that could have been run, not just the one that was. Together with publication bias — only 'significant' results tend to get published — this produces a literature full of inflated effect sizes that fail to replicate.

Moving to Bayesian methods would help with part of this, but not all of it. Bayesian updating satisfies the likelihood principle: the likelihood function for the data you actually got doesn't depend on the stopping rule that produced it, so a researcher who checks the data after every participant and stops once the posterior odds look good ends up licensed to draw exactly the same conclusion from that data as a researcher who fixed the sample size in advance. In other words, moving to a Bayesian framework doesn't just make optional stopping less damaging, it dissolves the problem outright, since there's no longer any privileged 'sampling plan' that the evidence needs to be relativized to. Credible intervals are similarly an improvement in principle, though in practice the difference is smaller than advertised: once you've collected your data and have an actual interval in front of you, a 95% credible interval and a 95% confidence interval both end up licensing the same statement — 'there's a 95% chance the true value is in here' — so this isn't really the place where the two paradigms diverge in a way that matters. What Bayesian methods don't fix is that they introduce their own researcher degrees of freedom, in the form of prior and model choice, and as long as journals reward surprising results over careful ones, publication bias will keep distorting the published record regardless of which framework produced the numbers."
