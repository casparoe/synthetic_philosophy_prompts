The question, as the instructor wrote it: "Van Fraassen's (1984) Reflection Principle is often summarized as requiring you to treat your own future credence the way Lewis's Principal Principle tells you to treat known objective chance. (a) State the Special (numerical) form of Reflection precisely, and say informally what it requires. (b) Explain the main argument van Fraassen gives in its favor. (c) Describe a case in which an agent who is certain what her credence in some proposition will be at a later time is nonetheless not rationally required to adopt that credence now, and explain why this is a problem for Reflection as stated in (a). (d) What, if anything, does the case in (c) show about the ultimate fate of the principle?"

Here is the student's answer, reproduced in full:

"(a) The Special Reflection Principle says: for any time t, later time t+n, and value r such that I currently assign positive probability to having credence r in A at t+n, my current credence in A conditional on that supposition must equal r: Pr_t(A | Pr_{t+n}(A) = r) = r. Informally, if I'm certain I'm going to believe A to degree r once t+n arrives, I should already believe A to degree r now. In effect the principle tells you to treat your own future opinion as an expert whose verdict you should defer to before you even hear it -- Reflection is really just the special case of Lewis's Principal Principle for which the 'expert' is your later self rather than objective chance, which is why the two are usually taught together.

(b) Van Fraassen's official argument is a diachronic Dutch Book argument. If you plan now to have some credence in A at t+n other than what Reflection requires given your certainty about that future credence, a bookie can construct a pair of bets -- one struck now, one struck at t+n -- that guarantees you a net loss no matter how things turn out, in exactly the way a synchronic Dutch book punishes violations of the probability axioms, and the Lewis/Teller diachronic Dutch book punishes failure to update by conditionalization. Since no rational agent's beliefs should guarantee her a loss by their sheer structure, Reflection binds in the same way those other principles do.

(c) Suppose I watch a coin land heads and, reasonably, set my credence that it landed heads to 1. I also know that a year from now I will have forgotten how it landed, and will set my credence back to 1/2. Right now, then, I am certain that my credence in 'it landed heads' at that future time will be 1/2. Reflection says I should set my current credence to 1/2 as well -- but that's absurd: I have excellent evidence right now, having watched the toss, that my future dip in confidence will come from forgetting, not from any new information about the coin. Deferring to my future self here just means discarding evidence I currently have, for no good reason.

(d) This kind of case is essentially a proof that Reflection, stated as in (a), is simply false: there's a genuine counterexample, so the unrestricted principle cannot be a correct requirement of rationality, and it's fair to say the original 1984 principle has been abandoned in its official form. What survives in the later literature are various patched-up descendants, built specifically to exempt the cases -- memory loss, intoxication, and the like -- where you already know your future credence won't be tracking the truth any better than your current one."
