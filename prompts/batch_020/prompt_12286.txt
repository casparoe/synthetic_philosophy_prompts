Okay so in my environmental ethics seminar we spent a whole class on Parfit's nonidentity problem and I can't stop thinking about it, because I'm pretty sure it proves something way bigger than my professor was willing to admit. Here's the setup as I understood it: society has to pick between a "depletion" policy, which keeps things cheap and comfortable for a couple centuries before quality of life craters, versus a "conservation" policy that's a little worse now but way better later. The catch is that literally who gets born 200 years down the line depends on all this contingent stuff - who meets who, when couples decide to have kids, etc. So if we go with depletion, every single person alive in, like, the year 2225 only exists BECAUSE we chose depletion. Under conservation, a totally different set of people would've existed instead. Which means nobody living under depletion, even with there fairly mediocre lives, can turn around and say "I'd have been better off under conservation" - because the alternative for them personally wasn't a nicer life, it was never being born at all.

So doesn't that basically dissolve the whole idea that we owe anything to future generations? All the talk from environmental groups about "not harming our descendants" or having "obligations to future generations" seems like a straight up category error once you actually take nonidentity seriously - you can't harm someone just by being a necessary condition of their existence, even if their life could've gone better. My professor kept insisting this is an unresolved "problem" rather than something that settles the ethics in favor of just doing whatever's best for people who currently exist. Am I missing something, or does the nonidentity problem basically just win the argument here?
