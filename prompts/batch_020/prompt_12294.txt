I host a small, self-funded podcast about the psychology and philosophy of blame -- nothing academic, I got pulled into the subject a few years back after serving on a jury and being unable to shake the question of what a person would actually have to be like for a guilty verdict to be more than just useful bookkeeping. Next week's guest has built her career defending Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument," the regress argument concluding that nobody is ever what he calls "truly" or "ultimately" morally responsible for anything: what you do flows from the way you are; to be responsible for what you do, you'd have to be responsible for the way you are; but being responsible for the way you are would require having made yourself that way in some earlier act, which itself flowed from a still-earlier self you didn't make, and so on back without end -- so that the only thing that could stop the regress is being the cause of yourself, which nothing can be. I've spent months with the original essay and a couple of the shorter pieces where he insists the argument doesn't depend on whether determinism turns out to be true, and I don't want the interview to be forty minutes of her re-explaining the regress to my listeners.

Could you draft eight to ten questions that push past that restatement? I'd like at least one on why a bit of indeterminism or randomness in how we're put together is supposed to be no more help to "ultimate" responsibility than strict determinism is -- my own gut reaction was that some genuine chance in the mix is exactly the kind of "could have done otherwise" people usually think free will needs. I'd like one on the distinction she'll almost certainly draw between this "ultimate" responsibility and the ordinary, everyday responsibility we obviously keep relying on in courtrooms and at the dinner table, and whether that distinction does real work or just lets her keep the argument's shock value while paying none of its practical costs. I'd like one that sets the argument beside "Freedom and Resentment" -- I only recently learned that its author, P.F. Strawson, was Galen Strawson's own father -- and asks her to say directly whether that paper's picture of resentment and gratitude as attitudes we're not going to give up regardless of how the metaphysics comes out undercuts the point of calling responsibility "impossible" in the first place. And I'd like one on the gap, which Strawson himself has admitted to, between accepting the argument intellectually and still feeling, in the middle of an ordinary day, like a free agent choosing between real options. For each of these, please add a short, specific follow-up question tailored to the answer she's most likely to give, rather than a generic "could you say more."
