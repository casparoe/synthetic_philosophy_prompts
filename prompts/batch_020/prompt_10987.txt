I've been nerding out on backward induction and the centipede game lately (the one where the pot grows every round and at each turn a player can grab it and end things, or pass and let it grow more). I already knew the standard 'paradox' -- backward induction says the first player should grab it turn one even though both would end up richer if they kept passing, and real people don't actually do that in the lab. Fine. But I just found out theres a weirder fight buried under that, about whether backward induction is even correct in theory and not just empirically. Apparently it's the same Aumann from the agreement theorem stuff who has an actual proof that common knowledge of rationality forces the whole backward-induction chain. Then other people (Binmore? someone like that) hit back saying that's smuggling in the conclusion: the moment a player doesn't grab the pot on turn one, you've already falsified the common-knowledge premise the argument depended on, so nothing tells you what's 'rational' from turn three onward -- you're reasoning about a node the theory itself says can never be reached.
