I'm drafting a novel whose protagonist, Nora, has bipolar disorder. Years ago, while stable, she signed what's sometimes called a "Ulysses contract" in psychiatric ethics: a document instructing her psychiatrist and her sister, Cass, to have her hospitalized and medicated at the first clear sign of mania, regardless of what she says at the time, and stating in advance that any refusal she voices while symptomatic should not count as informed consent. In the scene I'm working on, Nora is now manic but far from incoherent -- she argues fluently and gives specific, not obviously delusional reasons for refusing treatment this time -- and Cass has to decide, in real time, whether to invoke the contract.

I described the dilemma to two friends of mine over dinner, and they ended up in a real disagreement about whether Cass would be right to invoke it. As best I can reconstruct it: (1) Sasha said yes -- the contract was made by a competent, reflective Nora who specifically anticipated a lucid-sounding refusal and asked in advance to be overruled, so honoring it isn't a failure of respect for her autonomy but its fullest exercise, exactly as Odysseus's crew were right to keep him lashed to the mast no matter what he said in the moment, since that was precisely the contingency he'd told them to plan for. (2) Dev objected that the sirens case is disanalogous: Odysseus and his crew both knew, independently of his pleading, that the urge to leap overboard was produced by an external, alien compulsion with no connection to his settled values, whereas nothing guarantees that every preference formed during a manic episode is similarly "not really hers" -- some manic reasoning is reckless or exaggerated without being unmoored from the person's actual values, so treating "manic" as automatically equivalent to "compelled" assumes what's in question. (3) Sasha replied that Nora's own stable self had already made exactly that judgment about this kind of episode and asked to be overruled, and that this advance testimony deserves real evidential weight -- the way we'd weight a sober person's instructions about handling her drunk self. (4) Dev answered that the analogy cuts both ways, since we don't think a sober self is automatically right about everything a drunk or uninhibited self might reasonably judge, and, more importantly, that a contract like this lets one snapshot of Nora pre-empt not just the episode she foresaw but potentially years of later selves who might reasonably feel differently -- so the "fullest exercise of autonomy" story quietly lets an earlier self permanently govern later ones, which is a cost of its own.

Neither of them budged, though I suspect both landed real points. Could you go through the four moves in order, saying who has the better of it at each step, before closing with a one-line overall verdict on whether Sasha or Dev came out ahead, or whether it's a genuine draw?
