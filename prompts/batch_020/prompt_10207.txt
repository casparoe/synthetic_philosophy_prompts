My younger sister is getting married in three weeks, and she has asked me, her maid of honor, to give the toast. I have no background in philosophy and no real qualification for the assignment beyond having spent most of the last month reading about it obsessively, because the situation is more delicate than a normal wedding toast. She and her fiancé have written their own vows, and rather than promise anything "till death do us part," they are going to promise to keep choosing each other "for as long as it's true," with a line about renewing the commitment deliberately every year instead of assuming it will simply last. His parents, who are quietly unhappy about this, have told me privately that a promise you can call off isn't really a promise at all, just a report of your current feelings dressed up in wedding language, and that they don't know what the two of them will actually have vowed to each other. My sister's friends, several of whom (myself included) have already watched an unconditional "forever" vow get broken anyway, think the traditional wording is the dishonest one, since nobody can truthfully promise how they'll feel in forty years.

I keep getting stuck on the same question underneath both complaints: what actually makes a wedding vow binding in the first place, over and above however the two people happen to feel at the moment they say it? I fell down a bit of an internet rabbit hole trying to answer this myself. I found Hume arguing, if I followed him right, that the whole obligation is basically a useful convention we've all agreed to honor rather than anything built into the promise itself; a much older argument, which I think comes from Hobbes, that the entire point of a promise is to hold you to something exactly when you've stopped wanting to do it, since a promise that only counts while you still feel like keeping it isn't doing anything a mere intention wouldn't already do; and then something in a more recent writer named Scanlon about a "principle of fidelity," which seemed to be about the trust and expectations you create in the other person rather than about lifetime guarantees at all. I couldn't work out which of these, if any, would let me say that what my sister and her fiancé are doing at the altar is a real, binding promise, rather than either an empty guarantee or a nice way of describing their current mood.

Could you write the toast for me? It needs to run five or six minutes out loud, sound like a sister and not a seminar, and actually be funny and warm in the places a wedding toast should be. But somewhere in the middle it has to take on the disagreement directly rather than steering around it: by the end, I want his parents to feel their worry about what a promise even is has been taken seriously, and I want my sister's friends to feel the same, even though I can't honestly end up agreeing with both sides. I would rather you pick a real position and make it work as a toast than have it stay neutral for six minutes.
