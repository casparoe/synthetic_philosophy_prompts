I spent thirty-odd years running a biophysics lab before I retired, and lately I've fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole -- specifically Karl Friston's free energy principle, which a former postdoc of mine won't stop bringing up over email like its the second coming of thermodynamics. The maths itself doesn't scare me, I did my share of variational calculus back in the day, but something about how the thing gets defended rubs me the wrong way as an old empiricist. Friston's crowd like to compare the FEP to a physics "principle" in the spirit of Hamilton's principle of least action -- true of any system whatsoever, from which you then derive specific, testable "process theories" for whatever system is in front of you. Fine in physics, where least action still cashes out in equations you can check against a falling apple. But when I try to pin down what a brain, a cell, or even a thermostat could actually do that would falsify the free energy principle itself, rather than just get redescribed afterward in terms of "surprise" and "generative models," I keep coming up empty.

There's also this whole "dark room problem" -- why free-energy-minimizing creatures don't just hide in a featureless closet forever -- which never struck me as properly put to rest, more patched over with talk of "prior preferences" baked into active inference. And I vaguely remember a paper, maybe Colombo and someone (my memory for names is going), making roughly the same complaint I am: that this is less a mechanistic theory you could test than an organizing metaphysical picture dressed up in variational calculus.

So here's what I'd like: an essay that actually weighs the "FEP is an unfalsifiable tautology in a lab coat" charge against the best case for it as a genuine unifying principle of biological self-organization, using the dark room problem and the prior-preferences move as your central test case for whether the theory-savers are doing real explanatory work or just making sure it structurally cannot lose. Don't leave me with a "both sides have a point" shrug -- actually land somewhere and defend it.
