In my seminar on Aristotle's practical philosophy we've just moved from the account of justice in exchange in Nicomachean Ethics Book V to the discussion of household management and wealth-getting in Politics Book I, and I keep getting stuck on how the two arguments are supposed to fit together -- something our professor flagged as a genuine interpretive puzzle without ever resolving it for us in lecture. In NE V.5, Aristotle argues that a just exchange between producers of different goods (his example is a shoemaker and a housebuilder) requires their products to be rendered commensurable by some common measure, and that money (nomisma) plays this role as a stand-in for the real measure, which is need (chreia); he even notes that nomisma is so called because it exists by convention (nomos) rather than by nature, and appears to concede that the resulting commensuration is not fully real, only practically adequate. Yet in Politics I, when he distinguishes natural household exchange from the unnatural pursuit of wealth as an end in itself (chrematistike), and singles out lending at interest (tokos) as the most unnatural species of money-making, on the grounds that money is by nature barren and so cannot legitimately breed more of itself, the whole argument seems to need money to have some fixed, nature-given function that particular uses of it can then violate.

I have a short paper due for this seminar, and I'd like your help thinking through this tension in essay form rather than just getting a summary of both texts. Please write an essay addressing the following question: does Aristotle's admission in NE V.5 that money only conventionally and imperfectly renders goods commensurable end up strengthening or undermining the naturalness argument against usury in Politics I? Set out the strongest version of each answer -- one on which the concession actually explains what is wrong with chrematistike, since it shows that the vice consists precisely in mistaking a conventional proxy for the need it was only ever meant to track; the other on which the concession quietly gives up the nature/convention distinction that the usury argument needs if it is to condemn interest specifically, rather than money-use as such, as unnatural. Then defend a definite verdict rather than leaving the two readings balanced against each other; if you think the truth is some hybrid (for instance, that the concession is compatible with a narrower version of Aristotle's argument but not with the full biological analogy to sterility), say so explicitly and specify exactly which parts of the Politics argument survive on that narrower reading and which do not.
