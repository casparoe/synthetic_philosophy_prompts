During the thirty-odd years I spent running an experimental condensed-matter physics lab, I trained myself and my students to treat any contradiction between two results as a diagnostic signal rather than a fact about the world: if two well-conducted measurements could not both be true, something downstream was miscalibrated, mislabeled, or simply wrong. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy of logic in earnest, partly alone and partly through a small reading group a former colleague organized, and Graham Priest's writing on dialetheism, especially "In Contradiction," has unsettled that old reflex more than I expected. What I cannot get straight, after several passes through the primary material and the more accessible commentary, is exactly how dialetheism relates to paraconsistent logic. The two terms turn up together so often that popular treatments seem to use them almost interchangeably, yet I keep finding footnotes suggesting that logicians and computer scientists built and used paraconsistent systems, ones in which a contradiction does not entail every proposition whatsoever, what I gather is called explosion, without any apparent commitment to Priest's central claim that some contradictions are literally true: da Costa constructing inconsistency-tolerant formal systems for mathematics, or Belnap designing a four-valued logic so that a machine reasoning over conflicting inputs would not thereby be able to derive absolutely anything.

I would like a careful comparison of the two notions, pitched for someone who can follow technical material but has no formal training in logic. Please address, in particular, whether dialetheism should be understood as one further, optional commitment layered on top of an independently motivated paraconsistent logic, or whether the relationship is tighter than that in at least one direction, such that taking some contradictions to be true more or less forces one into a paraconsistent logic on pain of trivialism, even if the entailment does not run the other way; what would distinguish a paraconsistent logic adopted purely as a formal or computational convenience from one meant to vindicate genuine truth-value gluts; and why I keep encountering hints, in discussions of the Curry paradox, that merely blocking the inference from a contradiction to everything is not even sufficient for what a dialetheist needs from a logic, which suggests the two notions can pull apart in more interesting ways than "dialetheism entails paraconsistency but not conversely." Where my sense of this landscape is itself confused in some way a working logician would find embarrassing, I would much rather you correct that than answer the question exactly as I have posed it.
