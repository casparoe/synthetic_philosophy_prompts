I'm a PhD student in metaphysics and I TA our department's personal identity seminar every year, and every single time we get to Parfit's teletransporter the discussion turns into the same five-minute "is it death or is it travel" exchange and then everyone moves on. The version I actually find more interesting, and that we never spend enough time on, is the branch-line case from Reasons and Persons -- the one where the scanner beams a perfect replica to Mars but also wrecks your original heart in the process, so old-you is stuck on Earth for a few more days, fully conscious, chatting with the doctors and knowing exactly whats coming, while new-you is already up and walking around a hundred million miles away. That's the case Parfit uses to argue your attitude toward your own death should soften even though the person on Earth definitely, unambiguously dies.

Could you write me a dialogue set in exactly that scenario, between two people who both know the literature cold: a committed Parfitian reductionist (identity isn't what matters, Relation R is, so the Earth-person shouldn't be all that bothered) and a further-fact theorist who thinks the Mars person is a distinct, if psychologically near-identical, person, and that what's happening in the room is just ordinary death in a sci-fi costume. Don't let the further-fact theorist get away with just asserting "but it's not really you" -- have them press the reductionist on the idea that identity is all-or-nothing (transitive, no degrees, Leibniz's law) and make the reductionist actually answer with something like the fission case or the psychological-spectrum argument instead of just repeating Relation R louder. I want real back-and-forth where each one is visibly making the other's position harder to hold by the end, and honestly it's fine, maybe even better, if it doesn't resolve neatly.
