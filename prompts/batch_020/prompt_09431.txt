A colleague in my philosophy department is retiring this spring after thirty-two years teaching eighteenth-century moral and political philosophy and, on evenings and summers for almost as long, directing the amateur civic theater she more or less built from nothing in our town. The retirement dinner will include a dozen or so colleagues who have sat through her graduate seminar on Rousseau's Lettre à d'Alembert, and another forty or fifty people -- former cast members, her grown children, the mayor -- who know her only as the woman who has put half the town on a stage at some point over three decades. I have been asked to give the toast, and the trouble is that her two careers were never really separate: she has always maintained, quite sincerely, that Rousseau's case against the theater is basically sound, and I am fairly certain she has spent thirty-two years directing in a way meant to answer his objections rather than ignore them.

You know the argument better than I can reconstruct it here, but roughly: that watching staged virtue turns citizens into spectators rather than practitioners of it; that the actor's trade -- feigning passions felt by no one, night after night, for money -- cannot help corrupting the performer, and is worse still for an actress, given what Rousseau thought a woman's public modesty required; that Molière's Misanthrope is a kind of betrayal, since it trains an audience to laugh at Alceste, the one man on stage who won't dissemble; and that the only spectacle Rousseau fully endorses is the Genevan public festival, where actor and spectator are the same people and the distinction disappears. Her theater, as it happens, has no raised stage to speak of, has never paid a performer, casts whoever shows up rather than whoever auditions best, and has produced almost nothing that turns earnestness into a punchline. I don't think this is incidental to her love of theater. I think it is the closest thing to a considered rebuttal of Rousseau that a person can write using other people's bodies instead of a treatise.

I'd like a five-minute toast that takes this seriously -- one that doesn't collapse into generic tributes to "the magic of theater" (which I promise you she would wince at), but that also doesn't require the mayor or her children to have read the Encyclopédie controversy in order to enjoy it. Somewhere in it, lightly, I'd like an acknowledgment that Rousseau himself had a hit opera to his name by the time he wrote the letter, without letting that become the whole joke. And if you can find a real ending rather than merely a warm one, I would be grateful: something about what it means that tonight she is handing her theater over to a town that will carry on running it without her -- which strikes me as either the least Rousseauian thing she could have done with her life, or the most.
