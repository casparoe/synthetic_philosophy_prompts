And here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"The waterfall argument is a descendant of Putnam's proof that every open physical system implements every finite automaton, and it's meant to do to computationalism roughly what the Chinese Room does to strong AI: show that 'computation' talk is cheap unless something else pins it down. Take a waterfall: physically, it just maps some set of initial microstates to final microstates. Since that mapping is a bijection on a finite set, you can always relabel the initial and final states with integers so that the induced permutation matches literally any program you name, including a chess engine or a mind. So which program the waterfall 'runs' looks entirely observer-relative - bad news if you wanted computation to be the objective basis of mentality, à la Chalmers, who first floated the complexity fix for this by requiring that a legitimate implementation mapping preserve an automaton's counterfactual state transitions, not just the single trajectory the system actually happens to take.

Aaronson's rescue, as I understand it, sharpens this into a resource-bounded requirement: the labeling function that turns waterfall-states into chess-states has to itself be cheap to produce - he wants it to have low complexity, in the sense of a short description, so you can't hide an entire chess engine inside a gerrymandered lookup table disguised as an 'interpretation.' If the only mappings that make the waterfall look like it's playing chess are themselves as complicated as a chess engine, the waterfall isn't doing any of the work, and the appearance of computation was smuggled in through the interpretation function rather than earned by the physics. It's basically a formal version of Occam's razor applied to interpretations instead of to hypotheses.

He backs this up with an analogy to NP-completeness: reductions are only informative when the reducing procedure is provably weaker than the class it's reducing to, which is why complexity theorists define P-completeness using LOGSPACE reductions instead of polynomial-time ones - otherwise every problem in P reduces to every other one trivially. In the same way, a 'reduction' from chess to waterfalls only tells us something if it doesn't already secretly solve chess by itself.

I think this succeeds against the triviality charge specifically, since it gives an observer-independent test (how complex must the decoding be) for when an implementation claim is empty. But it doesn't rescue computationalism wholesale, because ruling out silly chess-playing waterfalls doesn't tell us why any physical process, however non-gerrymandered, should have experience associated with running it. That's just Chalmers's hard problem again, wearing a P-vs-NP costume."
