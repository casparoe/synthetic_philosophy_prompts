I don't have any formal training in philosophy, mathematics, or statistics, but I have spent a good deal of time on my own reading about Bayesian decision theory, and one result I keep running into is Good's theorem: for a rational Bayesian agent, the expected value of a free piece of evidence gathered before making a decision can never be negative, so it is always at least weakly better to gather more evidence before acting than to decide blind. What puzzles me is how this squares with cases where refusing to look seems like the obviously sensible thing to do. People at genetic risk for Huntington's disease often decline the predictive test, some investors deliberately avoid checking their accounts during a market downturn, and plenty of expectant parents ask not to be told their baby's sex. None of these people strike me as irrational. Is this a genuine problem for Good's theorem, or is there a more careful statement of what the theorem actually assumes that dissolves the apparent conflict? Write an essay that lays out the strongest version of this tension, weighs the main ways of resolving it, and defends a definite verdict rather than leaving the matter open.
