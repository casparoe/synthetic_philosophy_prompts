I teach a graduate seminar on philosophy of religion, and each term I set one assignment that asks for a story rather than an argument, on the theory that fiction can hold a position under pressure in ways a paper cannot. This term's case is Kurt Gödel's. Late in life he defended, in letters to his mother and in remarks recorded by his friend and interlocutor Hao Wang, a genuinely rationalist argument for personal survival after death: because the world is at bottom rational rather than absurd, and because a rational world would not spend decades cultivating a mind's powers of judgment only to annihilate them at exactly the point — old age — at which they are most fully realized, some form of afterlife must be real. He treated this as a conclusion of the same species as his theorems, not as a consolation borrowed from religion. He died in January 1978, in his last months eating almost nothing, convinced his food was being poisoned, after his wife Adele — who had tasted his meals for him for years — was herself hospitalized and could no longer do so.

Write a short story, set in or around those final months, that puts real pressure on Gödel's argument by staging it against the manner of his own dying. Don't have any character simply recite the argument, and don't let the story adjudicate, on its own authority, whether he was right.
