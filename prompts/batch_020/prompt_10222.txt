A friend and I spent much of an evening disputing whether G.E.M. Anscombe's real target in "Mr. Truman's Degree" was Truman himself or the willingness of an institution like Oxford to honor a head of state without inquiring closely into what he had done. We reached no resolution, but the argument left me unusually immersed in the essay just as I sat down to grade finals for the undergraduate ethics-of-war seminar I TA, and I would rather not have my own reading of the piece color how I mark a student's work. I'd like a disinterested second opinion before I commit to a grade.

The question on the final was: "In 'Mr. Truman's Degree,' Anscombe argues that the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki made Truman a murderer. Reconstruct her argument, addressing (a) the distinction between intending a death and merely foreseeing it, (b) why that distinction is doing the crucial work, and (c) how her objection connects to the wider ethical outlook she attacks elsewhere in her work."

One student answered as follows: "Anscombe wrote the pamphlet to protest Oxford's decision to award Truman an honorary degree in 1956, and her central claim is blunt: Truman is a murderer. The argument rests on the old natural-law principle that it is never permissible to intentionally kill an innocent person for the sake of good consequences, however large. She thinks this is exactly what happened at Hiroshima and Nagasaki: the raids were not cases of civilians being killed as a side effect of an attack aimed at some legitimate military target nearby. The point of the bombing was to kill, or at least terrorize, the civilian population directly, in order to break Japanese morale and force a surrender. Because the civilian deaths were the means to ending the war, and not an unintended side effect of pursuing some other target, the killing cannot be excused by the doctrine of double effect, which Anscombe lays out in this same pamphlet as the traditional test for separating permissible collateral harm from murder. This is why the intention/foresight distinction carries the whole argument: had the deaths been a foreseen but unintended by-product of hitting a genuine military target, proportionality reasoning might have made the raid defensible; because they were intended, no appeal to good outcomes can rescue it. Anscombe was not a pacifist — she thought killing enemy combatants in a just war was perfectly permissible — so her complaint is narrowly about the deliberate killing of non-combatants, not about the bombing campaign or the war as such. Finally, she ties this to a larger target: the pamphlet is where she first names and attacks consequentialism, the view that no act is intrinsically forbidden and that results alone settle what may be done, since it is exactly this kind of reasoning — the bombs shortened the war and therefore saved lives overall — that Truman's defenders used, and that she thinks makes murder respectable by redescribing it as regrettable necessity."
