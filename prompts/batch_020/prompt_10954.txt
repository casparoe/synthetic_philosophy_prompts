For my political philosophy elective, we just finished Rousseau's Social Contract, and one idea from it has stayed with me. Rousseau distinguishes the "will of all" -- merely the sum of what everyone happens to want at a given moment -- from the "general will," which tracks the community's genuine common interest and counts as authoritative even when it diverges from what most citizens actually say they want. He doubts ordinary citizens can reliably identify the general will on their own, which is partly why he thinks a society needs an exceptional "Legislator" who frames its laws from outside the system, without holding any power. This reminded me of "coherent extrapolated volition," an idea from AI-alignment discussions about what a sufficiently advanced AI should ultimately try to satisfy: not people's actual, current preferences, but roughly what they'd want if they understood their situation fully, reasoned about it without confusion, and had resolved their own inner contradictions. Suppose someone explained CEV to Rousseau as a serious real-world proposal. Would he welcome it as finally delivering what he wanted from the Legislator -- a reliable, impartial way of computing the general will that no longer depends on any fallible human institution -- or would he regard it with the same suspicion he reserved for anyone claiming special insight into the people's "true" interest? And what would his notorious line about citizens being "forced to be free" imply about the standard objection that CEV is just paternalism dressed up in technical language? Feel free to say if the Legislator/CEV analogy breaks down somewhere important.
