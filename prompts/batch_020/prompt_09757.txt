I joined my university's Honorary Degrees Committee this term, and my first case has already split the room. The Engineering faculty has nominated retired Air Vice-Marshal Gerald Whitfield, 96, citing the aviation-safety foundation he ran for four decades after leaving the RAF -- but he also spent three years commanding a bomber group under the wartime Area Bombing Directive, which explicitly targeted civilian morale in enemy cities and whose raids are estimated to have killed tens of thousands of civilians. Its defenders, then and now, say it hastened the surrender and averted a costlier invasion, though historians dispute how much weight that claim can bear. One committee member has circulated Elizabeth Anscombe's "Mr. Truman's Degree" -- her protest against Oxford honoring Truman for the atomic bombings -- arguing the cases are identical: intentionally targeting civilians as a means to victory is murder, whatever it accomplished. Others reply that Whitfield was obeying a lawful order he had no real power to refuse, that judging 1942 conduct by later standards is unfair, and that his subsequent decades running that foundation should count for something. I must circulate a written recommendation before next month's vote. Please draft it: an actual verdict -- award, decline, or award with a stated rationale -- that engages Anscombe's argument itself, not just her name, and takes the strongest form of whichever side you reject seriously.
