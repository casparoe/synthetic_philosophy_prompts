A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about whether the Kelly criterion deserves the normative status she thinks it has. Her position, which she called geometric rationality, is that when a favorable bet can be repeated or scaled, the rational stake is whatever maximizes expected log-wealth, since that strategy's growth rate overtakes any other's almost surely in the long run, and since ordinary risk-neutral expected-value reasoning absurdly recommends betting everything you have on any positive-edge wager. I pushed the Samuelson-style objection: that almost-sure long-run dominance is a fact about one particular statistic, that nothing compels a rational agent to prefer the geometric mean of outcomes to the arithmetic one, and that an expected-utility maximizer with any concave but non-logarithmic utility function has no reason to defer to the Kelly fraction just because it wins out "eventually," if all that agent will ever face is a fixed, finite number of bets. Neither of us budged, and afterward it struck me that neither of us had really been arguing the other's best case.

Could you write a dialogue between a committed geometric-rationality advocate and an expected-utility theorist skeptical of any privileged status for log-wealth, set at the moment the two of them must jointly decide how much of a shared bankroll to stake on a known, repeatable, favorable bet? Have each press the other's real weak points -- ergodicity and the time-average-versus-ensemble-average distinction, gambler's ruin, the finite-horizon objection, and the question of why the long run should matter to an agent who might not survive to see it -- rather than talking past one another, and don't let either side simply concede or get the last word by default.
