I teach a graduate seminar on Kant's ethics, and I just finished marking take-homes on the Formula of Universal Law -- the part of the course where we spent two whole sessions on the contradiction-in-conception (CC) vs. contradiction-in-will (CW) distinction, and specifically on the "practical contradiction" reading of it (Korsgaard's term) rather than treating it as some kind of strict logical contradiction. To stop people from just reciting the four stock Groundwork examples off of Wood's translation, I made up a new maxim for the take-home and had students run the test on it themselves instead of on Kant's own cases. Most of the batch was easy to mark, but one answer has me going back and forth, and I dont fully trust my own read on it anymore, so I'd like an outside grade before I finalize things.

Here's the question as I posed it: "Consider the maxim: 'Whenever I am the only person able to verify whether I've actually met some professional obligation of mine (hours billed, work completed, a report filed accurately), and telling the truth would cost me money or standing, I will report that I met it even when I didn't.' (a) Run Kant's universalizability test on this maxim. State clearly whether it fails at the level of conceivability or only at the level of the will, and explain why by reference to how the test actually works, not just by analogy to the stock cases. (b) Kant's own four Groundwork examples split two-and-two between CC failures (perfect duties) and CW failures (imperfect duties). Does this maxim's classification fit that pattern, and if so, which of the four is it most structurally analogous to, and why?"

And here's the student's answer, copied exactly as submitted:

"To test the maxim I have to ask whether I could will that everyone act on it as a universal law of nature. Here the maxim says: whenever I'm the sole verifier of my own compliance and honesty is costly, I'll lie about having complied.

First, can I even conceive of a world where this is a universal law of nature? I think I can: if literally everyone lied on self-reports whenever it was convenient, the world wouldn't become logically impossible or unimaginable, people would just learn that self-reports are worthless and build in outside verification, audits, or witnesses instead. So there's no contradiction in conception here, the practice would just get replaced by a more robust one. That means the maxim is like the beneficence and self-cultivation cases: its a contradiction in the will, not conception. I cannot rationally will universal dishonesty of this kind, because as an agent I depend on being believed by others in plenty of situations, and I'd be undercutting a system I rely on for my own future purposes.

Second, on why this is wrong more broadly: the deeper issue is that when I falsely report to my employer or client, I'm using them merely as a means to protect my own money and reputation, rather than treating them as an end in themselves who deserves the truth so they can make their own rational decisions about whether to keep paying or employing me. That's really the heart of the contradiction in conception here -- treating someone as a mere instrument for your ends is exactly what can't be universalized without contradiction.

Finally, for part (b): since this fits the contradiction-in-will pattern, it's most analogous to Kant's talent-neglect case. Both are about an agent failing to live up to their own rational nature over time -- the talent-neglector lets their capacities atrophy, the false self-reporter lets their reliability atrophy -- and in both cases universal adoption of the maxim is conceivable but not something a rational agent could actually will for themselves in the long run."
