I recently started a part-time research assistantship with a philosopher working on longtermist decision theory, and my very first assignment has been to get properly up to speed on evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL). Since I will soon be helping draft materials that presuppose familiarity with the topic, my supervisor asked me to write a short internal memo pinning down exactly how ECL differs from the more familiar practice of hedging one's actions under moral uncertainty (of the "moral parliament" variety), since this is apparently the confusion that trips up almost everyone who meets ECL for the first time. I have drafted the memo below and would very much like your comments before I circulate it, since I would rather be corrected now than embarrass myself in front of my new supervisor.

My current understanding, as I have written it up, runs roughly as follows. ECL asks us to imagine a universe, or multiverse, large enough to contain vast numbers of decision-makers who cannot causally affect one another but who are nonetheless correlated in how they reason, because some non-negligible fraction of them arrive at their choices via relevantly similar deliberative procedures — an idea usually traced back to Hofstadter's notion of superrational cooperation in the one-shot prisoner's dilemma, just scaled up to a cosmic population of agents with heterogeneous values. If I am one such agent and I choose to act partly on behalf of value systems other than my own, that choice is evidence, in the sense relevant to evidential rather than causal decision theory, that other, similarly-reasoning agents elsewhere are simultaneously choosing to act partly on behalf of value systems other than theirs, including mine. So even holding my own values fixed and fully known to me, I would have reason to compromise, purely because of this acausal correlation, in a way that has nothing to do with epistemic humility about ethics. This looks importantly different from hedging under moral uncertainty, where the entire rationale for spreading effort across several moral theories is that I do not know which one is true, and where I would keep hedging in exactly the same way even in a universe of one, with no other agents to correlate with at all. My worry, which I have raised in the memo, is that this difference in justification does less practical work than it looks like it should, since most informal treatments implement the ECL "compromise" using essentially the same parliamentary-bargaining machinery originally proposed for moral uncertainty, and since anyone who is also a moral realist and treats widespread convergence among reasoners as evidence about which values are correct will end up folding the ECL calculation straight back into their moral-uncertainty calculation. I have therefore suggested that the two considerations are conceptually independent but practically prone to collapsing into one another for exactly the kind of person who is likely to take ECL seriously in the first place.

Could you look this over and tell me whether I have actually located a real and stable distinction, or whether I am overstating how cleanly ECL and moral-uncertainty hedging come apart? I would also value a sanity check on whether I have represented the core ECL argument accurately, since I have been relying mainly on secondary summaries rather than primary sources, and a pointer to any standard objections to ECL itself, independent of this comparison, that a memo like this really should not omit.
