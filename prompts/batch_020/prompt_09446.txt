I retired three years ago after a career as a comparative physiologist, and since then I have been auditing a philosophy of biology reading group at the university where I once held an adjunct appointment. Forty years of thinking about traits purely in terms of current mechanism -- how a valve regulates flow, how a gill extracts oxygen -- left me unprepared for just how contentious philosophers still find the question of what it even means for a trait to have a function in the first place. Next month I am moderating the Q&A after a visiting talk by a philosopher (I will leave him unnamed here) who will be defending the etiological, or "selected-effects," account of function: the view, in the lineage of Wright, Neander, and Millikan, that a trait's function is fixed by the history of natural selection that explains why the trait is there, rather than by anything it is doing now.

I would like the questions to press on the places where this view is usually said to come under the most strain, rather than leave him room to simply restate it once more. Could you draft eight questions for me, each with a short follow-up keyed to the reply I expect him to give? I want at least one question built around the "Swampman" case (a molecule-for-molecule duplicate that springs into existence with no selective history whatsoever, whose heart nonetheless still seems to pump blood for a reason); one on how the account treats a brand-new mutation that has not yet been selected at all but happens to be immediately advantageous; one that uses vestigial structures to test how far "selected effect" and "current usefulness" are really allowed to come apart on his view; one that raises Gould and Lewontin's worry that many traits are architectural by-products or later co-optations rather than the direct products of selection for their current use; and one that asks him to say plainly how a causal-role rival, who ties function to what a part currently contributes to some capacity, would handle a malfunctioning organ, and why he thinks the etiological answer is preferable. Please close with a question asking what discovery, if any, would actually lead him to give up the selected-effects view, rather than simply treat it as one more case the theory happens to accommodate.
