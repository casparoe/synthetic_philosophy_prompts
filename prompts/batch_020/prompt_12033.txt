Me and my friend Jonas got into a long, slightly too-caffeinated argument last night about the "sovereign individual" passage near the start of the second essay of Nietzsche's Genealogy of Morals -- the bit where, after all that setup about breeding an animal that can promise and the morality of custom, Nietzsche describes the end product as someone "master of a free will," sovereign, able to stand security for his own future. Jonas reads this as Nietzsche's actual positive ideal: all that brutal disciplinary history of custom and cruelty was worth it because it produces this autonomous, self-commanding type -- basically Nietzsche's model of nobility, just translated into psychological rather than metaphysical terms. I kept pushing back that this can't be a straightforward endorsement, because Nietzsche spends huge chunks of Beyond Good and Evil (that causa sui passage in section 21) and Twilight of the Idols mocking the very idea of a free, self-causing agent as morality's dumbest and most successful invention, dreamed up mainly so people could be blamed and punished. So why would he turn around and hand us a "master of a free will" as the hero of the story, instead of one more product of the very cruelty-internalizing process (bad conscience and all) that the rest of the essay is busy diagnosing? We went back and forth for like an hour and neither of us landed an actual hit on the other's position, we just got more dug in, which is what happens pretty much every time we do this.

So can you do what we couldn't and write a dialogue between two people who each hold one of these readings -- one arguing the sovereign individual is Nietzsche's genuine (if naturalized) ideal of autonomy, earned rather than given, the other arguing it's an ironic or at least deeply compromised figure thats still on the hook for everything Nietzsche says elsewhere about free will being a fiction invented to make people punishable. I don't want them just restating their own reading at each other though -- the "genuine ideal" side actually has to deal with the free-will-skepticism problem instead of dodging it, and the "ironic reading" side has to deal with the obvious comeback that Nietzsche might just be using provocative language for what's really a modest, naturalistic psychological achievement. Doesnt need to be long, a page is plenty, I just want to see the actual moves get made instead of two summaries stapled together with quotation marks.
