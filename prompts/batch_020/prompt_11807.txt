So I'm a philosophy PhD student working on self-locating belief, and I keep coming back to Piccione and Rubinstein's absent-minded driver problem as the neglected cousin of Sleeping Beauty. Quick setup in case its not fresh in memory: a driver needs to exit a highway at the second of two intersections, X1 and X2, which look identical to him and between which he has zero memory, so whatever he does at one he does at the other -- his whole strategy is really just one number, p, his probability of continuing rather than exiting. Exiting at X1 pays 0, continuing through both and overshooting pays 1, continuing then exiting at X2 (the correct move) pays 4, and the standard answer for the best p, judged before he sets off, is 2/3.

What I want is a dialogue between two decision theorists hashing this out, equally sharp, neither one a pushover. One holds that the driver's job is finished once he's picked p=2/3, and that asking what he should *believe* once he's actually sitting at an intersection is a bad question -- there's nothing there to individuate, so "sequential rationality" imported from ordinary multi-stage choice doesn't obviously apply. The other holds that this ducks the issue: an action that isn't ratifiable given the agent's actual credence in the moment isn't really a rational choice at all, and the credence isn't mysterious -- a frequency argument in the spirit of the thirder move in Sleeping Beauty pins down a specific number for how confident he should be that he's at X1 rather than X2. Have them really engage with each other, and get them to address head-on whether it's philosophically significant that both routes land on the same p=2/3 here, or whether that's just a coincidence of these particular payoffs that would fall apart with slightly different numbers.
