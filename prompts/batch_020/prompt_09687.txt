At work a few weeks ago we got into a surprisingly long argument during a design review about whether our `Square` class should inherit from `Rectangle`, which inevitably turned into the usual rehash of the Liskov substitution principle: why overriding `setWidth`/`setHeight` on `Square` so that it preserves its own invariant ends up breaking any client code that treats a `Rectangle` as freely mutable in each dimension. Afterward, a friend of mine who studied philosophy said the whole mess reminded her of something much older: Gongsun Long's "white horse dialogue," from the Chinese School of Names (名家), a circle of Warring States-era thinkers preoccupied with the relationship between names (名) and the actual things (實) those names are supposed to pick out. The dialogue defends the notorious claim "a white horse is not a horse" (白馬非馬), and, as she explained it, does so with several distinct arguments rather than just one: one turns on "horse" naming a shape and "white" naming a color, so that the compound name picks out something different from the shape-name alone; a second points out that if you ask someone to bring you "a horse," a yellow or black horse will do, but if you ask for "a white horse" it won't, so the two requests can't be picking out the same thing; and a third focuses on the terms themselves rather than on what satisfies them, arguing that "horse" doesn't specify any color at all while "white horse" specifies, and thereby excludes, all colors but one.

I can't tell how much of this is a slippery equivocation on "is" (between strict identity and something more like "is an instance of" or "is a kind of") dressed up as three separate arguments, and how much of it is a genuine, defensible point about names and kinds that happens to rhyme with our software problem, where a `Square` object satisfies every predicate that mathematically defines a `Rectangle`, yet is famously a badly-behaved subtype of it once you care about behavior rather than just the static definition.

Could you describe what you take to be the most important implications of Gongsun Long's arguments, and of the School of Names' broader concern with names and actualities, for how software engineers should reason about "is-a" relationships and subtyping — the kinds of issues that show up in the Liskov substitution principle and the classic Square/Rectangle problem? I'd like you to take an actual position on which, if any, of the three arguments survives as a substantive point rather than wordplay, and to say plainly where the analogy to subtyping holds up and where it breaks down, rather than just narrating the history of the dialogue.
