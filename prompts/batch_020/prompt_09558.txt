Below is a short-answer question from an upper-level philosophy of biology exam, followed by one student's response to it.

Question: In "The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm" (1979), Gould and Lewontin criticize the adaptationist programme in evolutionary biology by way of an architectural analogy.
(a) Explain what a "spandrel" is meant to illustrate about the limits of adaptationist explanation, and give one plausible biological example of a spandrel.
(b) Explain how the concept of "exaptation," introduced a few years later, relates to the concept of a spandrel. Are the two terms simply synonyms?
(c) Adaptationists have pushed back against Gould and Lewontin's critique. Briefly characterize one influential reply and assess whether it succeeds.

Student answer:

A spandrel, in Gould and Lewontin's analogy, is the curved triangular space left over when a dome is mounted on rounded arches: its shape is dictated entirely by the surrounding architecture rather than designed for its own sake, even though it ends up being put to elaborate use (in San Marco, mosaics of the four evangelists). The biological point is that some organismal traits are likewise best explained as by-products of other structural or developmental features rather than as direct targets of selection, so biologists who insist on inventing an adaptive story for every trait are engaged in something closer to Voltairean Panglossianism than to rigorous science. A commonly cited candidate is the human male nipple, plausibly a by-product of shared early developmental pathways between the sexes rather than an adaptation in males themselves.

A few years later, in a follow-up paper, Gould and Lewontin introduced the term "exaptation" for essentially the same phenomenon: a trait that was not built by selection for the role it currently plays. On this view, every exaptation just is a spandrel that has since been co-opted for some use, so the two terms pick out the same category of trait considered at different times: "spandrel" when we are stressing its non-adaptive origin, "exaptation" when we are stressing its later usefulness.

The most influential reply to the spandrels critique comes from Daniel Dennett, who addressed it at length in Darwin's Dangerous Idea. Dennett largely sided with Gould and Lewontin's skepticism, agreeing that adaptationist "just-so stories" are frequently unfalsifiable, and arguing that this is precisely why natural selection cannot, by itself, explain the appearance of design in organisms without help from constraints of the kind Gould and Lewontin emphasize. The reply succeeds insofar as it shows that even a committed selectionist can accept the spandrels critique's central lesson.
