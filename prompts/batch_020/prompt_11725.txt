My best mate Danny is getting married in a few weeks and he's asked me to do the best man speech, which is terrifying enough on its own, but there's an extra wrinkle: him and his fiancée Mia actually met in this odd little book club that decided, for reasons nobody really remembers, to spend four months slogging through Sartre's Being and Nothingness, and apparently bonding over how much the "bad faith" chapter wrecked both of them is basically the origin story of the relationship. I never went to the book club myself but Danny got me reading it on my own after, no philosophy background at all, just me and a library copy and alot of confused highlighting.

The bit that really stuck with me is that bad faith isn't just "lying to yourself" in some vague sense, it's got this specific two-way trap built into it — you can fall into it by treating yourself as basically a fixed thing with no real choice in the matter (the classic move being "it was fate," "she's my soulmate," "we were always going to end up together," anything that quietly erases the fact that you're choosing this every single day), but you can ALSO fall into it the opposite way, by refusing to ever actually commit to being anything, keeping every option open forever so you never have to own a choice (which, not gonna lie, was Danny's entire personality for like a decade before Mia). So I want the toast to actually thread that needle honestly: celebrate that Danny and Mia are choosing each other, not that some cosmic soulmate thing happened to them, but also gently rib/bless them that saying "I do" isn't a magic spell that turns them into a fixed, finished "married couple" who get to stop choosing this from now on, like the freedom doesn't just switch off because they signed a piece of paper.

Problem is, half the room is going to be Danny's uncles and my nan, who will not care even slightly about French existentialism and will get restless if it starts sounding like a lecture, while a handful of people (the book club lot, and probably Mia herself, who's honestly sharper on this stuff than either me or Danny) will absolutely clock it if I get the philosophy wrong or just do the cheap "follow your heart, be free" greeting-card version, which Danny would find genuinely annoying since that's kind of the opposite of what the book is actually arguing. So could you write me the actual toast — like 4-5 minutes read out loud, funny enough to survive the table of uncles, but with the Sartre stuff actually doing real work in it rather than just being a name I dropped to sound clever?
