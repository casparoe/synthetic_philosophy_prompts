So for my school's econ department awards night (I'm graduating, and I was econ club president this year, so I got stuck giving a little speech) I've been trying to build it around this Keynes essay that's NOT the boring IS-LM stuff we did in class -- it's called "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," where he predicts that in about a hundred years the "economic problem" will basically be solved and the real challenge becomes what people even do with all that freedom once they don't have to grind for survival anymore. He wrote it in like 1930, right in the middle of the Depression, which already feels kind of wild to me, predicting all this future abundance while everything around him was falling apart. I found it because our econ teacher mentioned it once in passing during a lecture on unemployment and I read the whole thing that night instead of doing my actual homework, and it felt like a good fit for this speech partly because it's literally about grandchildren, and mine are gonna be sitting right there -- like I guess I'm technically one of the grandchildren he was writing about.

Here's my problem though: every draft I write turns into a generic "the world is your oyster, go chase your passion, money doesn't matter" speech, and when I go back and reread the actual essay Keynes is way more careful, and honestly kind of melancholy, about the whole thing than that. Like, he thinks we're NOT there yet and won't be for a while -- theres this whole bit where he basically calls the love of money a mental illness, but then says we still need greed and hoarding as the engine of progress for another century or so because nothing else is doing that job right now. Seperately, he seems genuinely worried that most people won't know how to use leisure well even once they get it, like it's a skill that has to be cultivated and not something that just happens automatically (I think there's even a specific number he throws out, like a fifteen-hour work week? not totally sure I'm remembering that right). And he splits needs into ones that can actually be satisfied versus status-type needs that maybe never can be, no matter how rich everyone gets. I also vaguely remember him being tangled up with some G.E. Moore philosophy thing from his Bloomsbury days about the good life really being about love and beauty instead of money or achievement, which feels like it should connect to all this but I can't quite make the link myself.

Can you actually write the speech for me -- like 3 to 4 minutes read out loud -- using Keynes's real position with all that nuance still in it? The rooms gonna be half family and underclassmen who've never heard of any of this, and half my econ teacher plus a couple classmates who read the essay with me and will 100% notice if I flatten it into a hallmark card. I want it to still feel warm and actually graduation-appropriate, not a lecture, but I don't want the cautious or melancholy parts hand-waved away -- if anything, that tension between "the future could be great" and "we are so not ready for it yet" is what I want the speech to actually be about, not something to smooth over.
