I recently joined the ethics advisory board of a defense-technology firm that builds AI-assisted targeting software for allied militaries: the system scores potential targets, attaches an estimated civilian death toll to each one, and passes a ranked list to a human officer for sign-off, typically within minutes. Trying to work out what to tell the board, I went back to Elizabeth Anscombe's "Mr Truman's Degree," where she argued that Truman was a murderer for ordering the bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, since he chose the deaths of civilians as the very means of forcing surrender rather than merely foreseeing them as a side effect of striking some other legitimate target, and she flatly rejected the idea that any tally of lives saved by a shorter war could excuse this. Anscombe died in 2001, long before this kind of algorithmic pipeline existed, but her apparatus — the intention/foresight distinction, her rule that legitimate authority never licenses killing the innocent, and her idea, from Intention, that what you intend shows up in what you would count as your plan failing — seems built for exactly this question. Reasoning from her actual arguments rather than a generic sketch of double effect, how do you think she would have judged a targeting process in which expected civilian deaths are estimated and weighed against target value by software before any officer decides anything? Would she treat that prior algorithmic weighing as itself evidence that the deaths are being intended as a cost worth paying, or is there a principled way, on her own terms, to keep such a system on the permissible side of her line?
