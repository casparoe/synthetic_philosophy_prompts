My doctoral supervisor, under whose direction I have spent the past three years working on a dissertation chapter about Sidgwick's "dualism of practical reason," died in her sleep two months ago. About a week before, half in jest, she told me she would try to send a sign afterward, "the way Sidgwick and Myers apparently promised each other" -- a reference to something I had mentioned to her once in a seminar years ago and had not thought about since. I don't feel entitled to grieve this as more than a scholarly loss, but for the first time in three years of treating the dualism as a technical problem in normative theory, I've started to feel its stakes. Below is a short essay I've drafted, working out a thought that has kept me up since: that Sidgwick's twenty years of psychical research were not an embarrassing detour from his ethics but very nearly required by it. I'd be grateful for comments on three specific things: whether the philosophical connection drawn in the second and third paragraphs is actually right, or whether I'm retrofitting a tidy story onto what may just have been a Victorian gentleman's hobby; whether the closing paragraph, about the pact and the cross-correspondences, is accurate enough to keep, since I'm working from memory of things she mentioned in passing and would rather cut the claim than misstate her own reference; and whether the final line earns its weight or is just too neat.

The Empirical Wing

Henry Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics (1874) is remembered as a monument of careful, almost bloodless analytic ethics: egoism, intuitionism, and utilitarianism, each examined with a scrupulousness that refuses to let any of the three off the hook. What is less often remembered is that the book does not end in a win for any of them. Sidgwick argues that practical reason, considered strictly, hands down two equally valid and mutually incompatible verdicts: that it is rational to pursue one's own greatest happiness, and that it is rational to pursue the greatest happiness on the whole, and that nothing internal to secular ethical theory shows these must coincide. He calls this the dualism of practical reason, and says plainly that only some further fact about the universe -- a moral order in which virtue and self-interest are ultimately made to align, which for him means something like a benevolent governing mind and a future life -- could close the gap. Absent that fact, he warns in the book's famously bleak closing pages, the whole rational structure of ethics threatens to collapse into something not far from incoherence.

Sidgwick was constitutionally incapable of just helping himself to that further fact on faith. He had already resigned his Cambridge fellowship as a young don rather than affirm, against his own doubts, the religious declaration it required of him. So the dualism was never, for him, a tidy aporia to note and set aside in a final chapter. It was a live question about whether the commitment at the center of his own life was rationally stable, and he treated it exactly as he treated everything else: as a question to be settled, if it could be settled at all, by evidence rather than by wanting it to come out a particular way.

That, I want to suggest, is the real continuity between the Sidgwick of the Methods and the Sidgwick who, in 1882, became the first president of the Society for Psychical Research and gave most of the rest of his life to investigating mediums, apparitions, and telepathy -- not as a concession to his wife's or Myers's enthusiasms, but as the empirical wing of the very same project. Even weak evidence of personal survival of death would count, however indirectly, as evidence for the kind of moral order the argument of the Methods needed and could not supply from an armchair. Fittingly, he brought the same scrupulousness to the séance room that he brought to egoism, and was by every account the hardest member of the whole movement to impress. He died in 1900, apparently still without the evidence he wanted, a fact his editors and biographers tend to treat as something closer to grief than to the relief of a settled skeptic.

There is a coda here I trust myself least to get right: Sidgwick, Myers, and Gurney seem to have discussed something like a pact to send one another a specific, pre-arranged sign after death, as a test; and after all three had died, several mediums associated with the Society independently produced fragments -- the so-called cross-correspondences -- that some of their surviving colleagues read as a coordinated attempt to do exactly that. Whatever one thinks of the evidential weight of any of it, it looks to me like the same mind at work in both halves of Sidgwick's career: someone who thought that if the rational stability of morality turned out to depend on a question of fact, you did not get to answer that question by deciding how you wanted it to come out. You went and looked.
