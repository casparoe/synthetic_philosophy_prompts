A few of us with no formal background in philosophy started an informal reading group last year, and we've spent the last few months working through Robert Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*. When we reached the Wilt Chamberlain example in the chapter on distributive justice, two people in the group, Elena and Tobias, got into a back-and-forth that the rest of us couldn't settle, and I've been turning it over ever since.

For context, in case it helps: Nozick asks us to imagine a distribution of goods, D1, that we already accept as just under whichever patterned principle we like (strict equality, or anything else). Wilt Chamberlain then signs a contract giving him 25 cents from every ticket sold to watch him play; a million fans freely hand over the money because they want to see him, and by the end of the season he has $250,000 — far more than the original pattern would have given him, so the resulting distribution, D2, no longer matches D1. Nozick says D2 is exactly as just as D1, since it arose from D1 through nothing but voluntary transfers, and concludes that no patterned principle of distribution can be maintained without constantly interfering in people's free choices.

Here is roughly how the argument between Elena and Tobias went:

1. Elena said this shows patterned principles, like Rawls's difference principle, are unworkable in practice: once people are free to do as they like with what's theirs, the pattern breaks down, so preserving it means the state has to keep stepping in.
2. Tobias replied that this only refutes a strawman version of a patterned principle — one treated as a snapshot to be re-imposed after every trade. The difference principle, he said, is meant as a standing rule built into the basic structure from the start, the way a tax system is something everyone is already under; on that reading, nobody had unqualified title to the taxed portion of the money to begin with, so nothing is being interfered with — people are just following a rule they were never outside of.
3. Elena answered that a fixed rule like a tax can't actually hold a pattern in place, because people can move value around it in countless other ways besides official income: gifts, unpaid favors, extra hours worked, or, as in the example, fans handing a player money simply out of enthusiasm. To keep the intended pattern in place against all of that, the rule would have to reach deep into ordinary voluntary life, which starts to look exactly like the interference Nozick was describing, just relocated.
4. Tobias said that was fair, but beside what he took to be the deeper issue: D1 being just is doing an enormous amount of unadvertised work for Nozick specifically, since entitlement theory is historical — whether a holding is just depends on the actual chain of acquisitions and transfers behind it, not just on the rule going forward. A tax rule only has to be fair from here on; Nozick's story needs the entire history leading up to D1 to have been clean, which is a far heavier assumption given how property has actually come about.
