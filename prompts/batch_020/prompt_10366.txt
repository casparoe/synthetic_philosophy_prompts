I spent nearly forty years as a seismologist, much of it devoted to the inverse problem of reconstructing the Earth's interior from surface measurements of seismic travel times. That work left me acutely aware that inverse problems are almost always ill-posed in Hadamard's sense: existence is rarely the difficulty, but uniqueness and stability nearly always are, since many different subsurface models can reproduce the same observed data equally well. We never treated this as grounds for despair; we simply regularized the problem by imposing some prior notion of what a geologically reasonable model should look like, and were content with a stable, useful answer even though it was demonstrably not the only model consistent with the data.

Since retiring I have been reading philosophy in earnest, and it was only recently, within the same week, that I happened across two things that struck me as versions of the same puzzle. The first was a discussion, in the AI safety literature, of inverse reinforcement learning, where researchers observe that any given pattern of behavior is compatible with an enormous range of possible reward functions unless one also fixes assumptions about how rational, or how systematically biased, the agent's decision-making is. The second was Donald Davidson's work on radical interpretation, building on Quine, which argues that an interpreter can never uniquely recover a speaker's beliefs and desires from behavioral evidence alone: any piece of surprising behavior can always be redescribed either as an ordinary desire paired with an unusual belief, or an ordinary belief paired with an unusual desire, with the ambiguity resolved only by imposing something like the principle of charity — the assumption that the speaker is, by and large, rational and right about the world.

What I cannot work out is whether Quine and Davidson mean something essentially stronger by "indeterminate" than I ever meant by "ill-posed": not merely that the currently available evidence underdetermines the answer, but that there is no further fact about a person's real beliefs and desires even in the limit of all possible behavioral evidence. If that stronger reading is correct, does it not follow that the preferences the AI alignment literature hopes to recover through inverse reinforcement learning are not simply hard to infer, but, strictly speaking, are not there to be discovered at all — making the enterprise less like seismic tomography and more like searching for the one authentic translation of a poem that never had an original? I raised this with a former colleague who now works in machine learning; she was unconvinced by the analogy but could not say exactly where it broke down, so I would value your view on the matter.
