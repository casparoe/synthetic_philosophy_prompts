I never studied philosophy formally, but after getting interested in effective altruism I worked backward into its intellectual roots and ended up reading Henry Sidgwick's *The Methods of Ethics*, mostly because I kept seeing him credited as the philosopher who took the tension between utilitarian calculation and everyday "common-sense" morality more seriously than almost anyone before or since. What has stayed with me is his discussion of whether a convinced utilitarian should openly teach utilitarianism to everyone, or whether the greatest happiness might actually be better served by letting most people go on following intuitive, rule-like duties — don't lie, keep your promises, treat people as ends in themselves — while only a reflective few ever reason directly in terms of consequences. Sidgwick seemed genuinely uneasy about this "esoteric morality," partly because a doctrine that recommends concealing itself starts to look self-undermining the moment anyone examines it too closely.

That discussion has kept coming back to me while reading about how large AI models actually get built: from what I understand, a lot of the underlying training optimizes something like aggregate predicted benefit minus predicted harm across enormous numbers of interactions, which sounds utilitarian to the core, while the resulting model is instructed to speak to any individual user in flat, categorical, rule-sounding language — "I can't help with that" — rather than "I ran a cost-benefit calculation on your specific request and it came out negative." I'd like your best reconstruction of what Sidgwick himself would say if this exact arrangement were described to him. Would he see it as basically the same maneuver he already entertained — reserve the real cost-benefit reasoning for a reflective few, let everyone else operate on categorical-sounding rules — just implemented in code instead of cultivated in a class of specially trained reasoners, and endorse it on similar grounds? Or is there something about the AI case that breaks the analogy, whether because a company optimizing a reward signal isn't quite the disinterested benevolent reasoner his argument seemed to assume, or because users who eventually suspect the categorical refusals are a front for hidden arithmetic might react quite differently than individual moral agents do when they suspect the same thing of each other? Given how uneasy Sidgwick already was about esoteric morality among human beings, do you think he would end up more alarmed by this AI version of it, or less?
