Ok this is a pretty niche thing to be preoccupied with but its been living in my head since last week. I started a new job about a month ago doing model evals at a smallish AI company, and my desk is basically inside the interpretability team's pod, so I overhear everything. Last week their daily stand-up turned into a genuine 45-minute argument about whether the "features" their sparse autoencoders keep pulling out of the model are real things the network has, or whether they're more like Rorschach blots that happen to be useful to us. Apparently individual neurons are "polysemantic" -- they fire for a bunch of unrelated things at once -- which is supposedly why you need the autoencoder to untangle them into features in the first place. One guy kept pointing out that you can clamp a single feature direction way up and get the model to bring up the Golden Gate Bridge in every answer no matter what you actually asked it, and said that's about as real as a representation gets. The other one kept coming back to the fact that if you retrain the autoencoder with a bigger dictionary or a different sparsity penalty, a bunch of those "features" split into different, non-overlapping ones that look just as interpretable -- so which decomposition is supposedly the one the model is "really" using?

I've been trying to read around this on my commute (the Anthropic monosemanticity write-ups, then I fell down a rabbit hole into older philosophy-of-cognitive-science stuff about whether mere "receptors" like a thermostat count as representations, which felt way too relevant) but everyone I could actually talk to about it already has a side. So: could you write me a dialogue, maybe 700-900 words, between a committed realist about interpretability -- someone who thinks these SAE features and the circuits built out of them are genuine representations the network is using, not just convenient summaries for us -- and an equally sharp skeptic who thinks "representation" is doing more work there than the evidence actually licenses? Neither one should be a pushover: the realist has to actually deal with the different-dictionaries problem, and the skeptic has to actually deal with the fact that intervening on these features changes behavior the way a real causal-semantic story would predict, not just correlates with it. Honestly it's fine, maybe even better, if it doesn't resolve neatly, since that's basically how the stand-up ended anyway.
