My sister Elena is a moral philosopher who works on population ethics, and for the better part of six years she and her husband circled the question of whether it was even defensible to have a child, given the climate trajectory we're all living inside. The argument she kept returning to in her own writing was the nonidentity problem -- Parfit's observation that a choice can determine not just how well-off a future person will be but who that person is, so that the child born of waiting and the child born of not waiting are never actually in competition for the same place in the world. She's told me, more than once and with some frustration, that she doesn't experience this as the relief a layperson might expect it to be; in her corner of the field it's treated less as a solution than as a standing puzzle that most people who work on it are still trying to explain away rather than accept. They went ahead regardless, and their daughter, Noor, was born in March. Next Sunday we're holding a small dinner to welcome her into the family properly, and Elena -- only half joking -- has asked her non-philosopher brother to give the toast, on the theory that I've absorbed enough of this by osmosis over twenty years of arguments at her kitchen table to be trusted with it.

I write novels for a living, so I'm not usually short of things to say at a dinner table, but I've drafted this particular toast three times now and keep landing on one of two failure modes: either several minutes of hedged philosophical throat-clearing that would make Elena wince, or a version that reaches for the nonidentity problem as a kind of magic trick -- "you can't have wronged a child who wouldn't otherwise exist, so relax" -- which misrepresents both the argument and the six years of genuine unease that preceded Noor's existence. The room will be roughly half Elena's academic friends, who will know exactly what's being invoked the moment I mention Parfit or the depletion-policy case, and half our extended family, who have no idea what any of this means and mostly just want reassurance that nobody did anything wrong, either by waiting so long or by finally deciding not to wait any longer. Could you draft the toast for me -- around six minutes read aloud -- that actually uses the shape of the nonidentity problem, including the fact that most people who study it seem to find its own conclusion faintly troubling rather than comforting, to say something true and warm about why this particular, contingent, only-possible-because-they-waited-exactly-this-long child is the one we're all here for tonight?
