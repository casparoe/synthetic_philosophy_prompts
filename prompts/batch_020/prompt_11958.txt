Question (20 points): Williamson holds that a condition C is luminous just in case, for every case, if C obtains then one is thereby in a position to know that C obtains. Consider a subject sitting outside from midnight, when the temperature is well below freezing and she is unambiguously, knowably cold, until noon, by which point it has warmed enough that she is unambiguously not cold. Using this kind of case, reconstruct Williamson's argument that "feels cold" is not a luminous condition, making explicit the role played by the margin-for-error principle. Then explain what the argument implies for the KK principle, and why this matters for a broadly Cartesian picture of self-knowledge.

Student's answer: For Williamson, a condition C is luminous when, in every case in which C obtains, the subject is thereby in a position to know that C obtains; the Cartesian picture of the mind assumes that conditions like feeling cold, being in pain, and so on are luminous in exactly this sense. To see why "feels cold" fails to be luminous, take the subject described in the question and divide the interval from midnight to noon into cases so short that she cannot discriminate, by feeling alone, between her temperature-sense in any two consecutive cases. This is where the margin-for-error principle comes in: because her ability to discriminate small temperature differences is limited, a belief that she feels cold, if formed in the same way in a neighboring case, would be produced just as confidently whether or not it is true there. A method that could easily go from producing a true belief to producing a false one between two cases that are, from the inside, indistinguishable is not reliable enough to give knowledge in either case. So if the subject knows, at some case, that she feels cold, she must also feel cold in the immediately neighboring case -- otherwise her "knowledge" would have been sustained by nothing more than luck.

Now suppose "feels cold" were luminous. At midnight she knows she feels cold; by margin for error she still feels cold one case later; by luminosity she therefore knows this too; and so on, case by case, until noon, where by hypothesis she does not feel cold at all. That is a contradiction, and the only assumption doing any work besides margin for error was luminosity, so luminosity has to go. This is really the Sorites paradox run on "feels cold": it shows that, like "heap" or "tall," there is no sharp cutoff between the temperatures at which the subject does and does not feel cold, only a wide smear of cases where the matter is simply undetermined.

The same reasoning threatens the KK principle, Kp → KKp, which is just the claim that "knows that p" is itself a luminous condition. If knowing is a mental state on a par with feeling cold, the same margin-for-error argument shows it cannot be luminous either, so a subject can never actually know that she knows something, only be caused to believe it -- which is exactly the introspective transparency the Cartesian picture needed and doesn't get.
