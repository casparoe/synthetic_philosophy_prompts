A friend of mine who is a few years into a philosophy PhD tried to explain her research to me over dinner recently, and I have not been able to stop turning it over since. She works on "dispositions" — properties like fragility or solubility that seem to be defined by what an object would do under certain conditions — and on a family of strange counterexamples philosophers use to complicate that simple idea. One kind, which she said traces back to a philosopher named C. B. Martin, is called a "fink": imagine a wire connected to a device that can detect the instant a conductor touches it and that switches the wire's live-or-dead status at exactly that instant, so that whether or not you get shocked tells you nothing reliable about whether the wire was live a moment earlier. She said David Lewis answered this with a wine glass protected by a sorcerer who makes it briefly tough at the exact moment it is struck, so that it never breaks, even though the glass is fragile in every way that matters on the inside. Then there is a second, different kind of case — I believe it was a philosopher named Bird — involving a poison and its antidote, where nothing about the poison itself changes at all, but something further down the causal chain intervenes and stops it from ever doing what poison does; I gather this second kind gets called a "mask" rather than a "fink," because the underlying property itself is never touched, only its expression is blocked.

What keeps pulling me back to this is my uncle, who has taken the same medication for an explosive temper for something like twenty years now, calmly enough that people who only know him now find the old stories hard to believe. Nobody in the family can say with any confidence which of the two cases his situation is closer to: whether the old temper is entirely intact underneath and simply prevented, every single time, from ever reaching the surface, the way the poison in Bird's example is still fully a poison, or whether two decades of a life lived otherwise have actually changed the man himself, the way the sorcerer's trick might eventually stop being a trick and become true of the glass, if it went on for long enough. I would like you to write me a short story, set wherever and whenever seems right to you, that dramatizes exactly this uncertainty: built around one clear case of a disposition being either finkishly altered or merely masked at the moment it would otherwise show itself, in a way a reader can feel is definitely one of those two and not the other, and that makes clear why the difference ought to matter to how we are entitled to judge the person or thing it happens to. I would rather you not use the words "disposition," "fink," or "mask" anywhere in the story itself; I want the idea carried entirely by what happens, not stated outright.
