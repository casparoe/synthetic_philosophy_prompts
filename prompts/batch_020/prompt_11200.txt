Several months ago, during what turned out to be my father's last lucid conversation before he died, I promised him that I would keep the small parcel of land his own father had left him in the family rather than sell it, whatever the cost to me. No one else was present, he never told my siblings, and as far as anyone else is aware the land is simply mine to do with as I like. A developer has since made me a considerable offer for it, and because breaking the promise would disappoint no living person's expectations — no one but me even knows an expectation could exist — I have had to ask myself what, if anything, is actually binding me. Write a dialogue between two philosophers who disagree about why promises bind us: one who holds that a promise binds because it deliberately invites the promisee's trust, so that breaking it wrongs someone by exploiting an expectation you yourself created in them; and one who holds that a promise binds because making it is itself an exercise of a basic power to obligate yourself through your own will, independent of whether anyone else ever relies on it, learns of it, or is even alive to know of it. Have the two apply their views directly to my situation, press each other on its hardest consequences, and respond to each other's actual arguments rather than merely restating their own positions.
