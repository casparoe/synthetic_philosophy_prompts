So I'm about two-thirds into a novel that follows the judging process of a big literary prize (the prize itself is invented, but I'm trying to keep the actual mechanics realistic), and theres a scene I keep chickening out of writing properly. My veteran judge, Odile, who's done this panel twice before, admits to Priya, the newest and youngest judge, that for two of the twelve longlisted novels she just didn't have the hours -- between her actual job and the couple hundred pages a day the rest of the list demands -- so her "read" of those two is really secondhand: a critic friend whose taste she trusts completely told her they're the real thing, and she's planning to rank them accordingly, in good conscience. Priya is horrified, says that's not a judgement at all, you can't inherit somebody else's verdict on whether a book is good the way you can inherit a fact about, I dunno, when some treaty got signed.

Turns out (I went looking, mainly to check I wasn't rigging the argument in my own favor) this is basically a live debate in aesthetics, under the name "aesthetic testimony" -- can you justifiably come to believe a work is good on someone's say-so, the way you can with most other kinds of facts, or does an aesthetic verdict have to be first-hand in a way those dont. I think there's even a name for the strong version of Priya's view, something like the "acquaintance principle," though I don't fully get what extra work that's doing beyond "you have to see it for yourself." Could you write me the actual dialogue between the two of them? I want real back-and-forth -- Odile isn't just being lazy or cynical, she's actually thought about this, and Priya needs more than "but you have to READ it!" to bring each time. And if it comes up naturally, I'd love for them to get into whether this is a special problem for aesthetic judgement specifically or just a version of the same worry people have about taking moral advice on trust -- I have a feeling that's its own whole debate but don't know it well enough to write it myself.
