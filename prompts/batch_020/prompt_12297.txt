I'm a novelist a little more than two-thirds of the way through drafting the final book of a trilogy I've spent six years building, and I've been circling a decision for the better part of a month now - long enough that I finally aired it over dinner with two writer friends last week, mainly just to hear myself reason it through out loud. My editor has offered to forward me the freelance reader's report prepared on the second volume: a candid, fairly granular assessment of what is and isn't working for readers, available to me right now, before I write a single word of the ending. One friend at the table, who trained as an actuary before she turned to fiction herself, thinks my hesitation doesn't hold up under scrutiny: since I remain completely free to leave the report unread, or to read it and then simply disregard whatever in it doesn't strike her as useful, she cannot construct a scenario in which merely having the option to look leaves me worse off than not having it - at worst nothing changes, and at best it catches some blind spot in the plotting that I'd otherwise carry uncorrected into the ending.

I don't have a clean rebuttal to that, and yet the thought of opening the document before the book is finished produces something closer to dread than curiosity in me, as though reading it - whatever it actually said, flattering or damning - would cost me something her argument isn't counting. I would like to know whether she is simply right, and I am dressing up a superstition about my own process as a principle, or whether there is a genuine argument for treating this kind of advance information as a real risk rather than the costless, one-way bet she takes it to be.
