I'm a PhD student working on formal epistemology and decision theory, and for a graduate seminar on the foundations of rational choice I've been trying to construct the strongest possible version of a position that my advisor, a committed expected-utility theorist, is quick to dismiss as a category error. The textbook line, going back to Samuelson's long-running dispute with defenders of the Kelly criterion (the rule, originating in John Kelly's 1956 work on gambling with side information and later popularized by Ed Thorp, of betting so as to maximize the expected logarithm of one's wealth each round), is that this rule counts as rational only for an agent who happens to have logarithmic utility for wealth. For anyone with a different utility function, following Kelly means over- or under-betting relative to whatever actually maximizes their expected utility; growth-rate maximization is thus just one preference among many, and the well-known theorem that Kelly bettors almost surely end up wealthier than any fixed-alternative bettor after sufficiently many rounds gets waved away as an artifact of a limit that no finitely-lived agent ever reaches.

The position I'd like you to defend is the opposing one: a cluster of views sometimes gathered, loosely and by more than one author, under the label "geometric rationality," and closely associated with the "ergodicity economics" of the physicist Ole Peters and his collaborators. On this view, Samuelson's framing already smuggles in the wrong comparison class: it evaluates a bet by an ensemble average across an imagined population of parallel agents, when the only average that a single agent repeatedly betting on a multiplicative process will ever actually live through is the time average of their own trajectory—and for multiplicative dynamics, that time average converges, by an ordinary law-of-large-numbers argument applied in log-space, to the expected logarithm of the per-round return. On this reading, maximizing expected log-wealth is not a contingent fact about anyone's risk aversion at all; it is simply what falls out of correctly identifying which statistic describes what happens to a persisting individual, and it would hold even for an agent with linear, risk-neutral utility. (The bite of this is easiest to see with a toy case: a bet that multiplies your wealth by 1.5 with probability one-half and by 0.6 with probability one-half has a strictly positive expected per-round return, and yet, repeated indefinitely, drives almost every individual trajectory to ruin.)

Please argue for the geometric-rationality side as rigorously as you can, and in doing so address three things specifically: first, why the charge that "the Kelly criterion is just log utility in disguise" already presupposes exactly what is at issue—namely, that any reason to privilege a particular growth rate must come from an agent's preferences rather than from the dynamics of the process itself; second, why relegating the almost-sure long-run dominance of Kelly betting to a "merely asymptotic" curiosity fails to rescue Samuelson's position, rather than simply restating the underlying disagreement about which average matters; and third, the worry—which I suspect is the hardest one for your side—that treating one's own realized trajectory as privileged over a counterfactual ensemble of copies is itself a substantive value judgment, no less contestable than the utility functions the ergodicity view claims to dispense with.
