My mother died this past February after nearly seven years with Alzheimer's, and I've been asked to give the eulogy at her memorial service this coming Saturday. The part of those years I've found hardest to describe honestly is not the memory loss as such, but a stretch near the end when she seemed to lose her footing on things I would have said were the absolute floor of who she was: at one point she was no longer sure she had a body, and for months she treated my father — who had been married to her for over fifty years — as a stranger who happened to be gentle with her. While I was trying to find some honest way to think about this, rather than just the usual "she lost herself" language, I came across Wittgenstein's book On Certainty. From what I can tell, his idea is that most of what we're happy to call "knowledge" isn't actually held up by evidence or argument at all; it rests on certain things simply standing fast for us, unquestioned, the way the hinges of a door have to stay put for the door to swing at all, and that this fixedness comes from upbringing and practice rather than proof. He also has this image of the bed of a river being able to shift, slowly, even while we go on treating it as the fixed thing the water moves over. I gather he was mainly talking about what a whole community or "form of life" treats as beyond doubt, not one person's mind coming loose near the end of a life, so I may be pulling the idea further than he meant it to go — but it feels closer to the truth of what I watched than anything in the therapists' or support-group language for it. I later found out there's a whole area of philosophy built on this part of Wittgenstein now, called hinge epistemology, though I haven't gotten very far into that.

I'd like you to write the eulogy with this idea doing real work in it, not just decorating it. Almost everyone at the service will be family, or my parents' old friends and neighbors, and none of them should have to sit through a definition of "epistemology" to feel the eulogy land; it has to work as a eulogy first. At the same time, I don't want the easy line that most eulogies for someone with dementia reach for, that "deep down, she still knew us" — that is exactly the picture of certainty as some hidden, private fact underneath the confusion that this idea should make us doubt, and it isn't honest to what I actually saw. I would rather it say something true and specific instead: that whatever certainty she and I shared about who she was, was never a fact locked away inside her, but something the two of us did together, out loud, for fifty years, and that it can come loose the way anything held up by practice rather than proof can come loose. About five minutes long when read aloud, please.
