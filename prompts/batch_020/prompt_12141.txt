For a seminar paper on early modern theories of individuation, I've been working through Leibniz's correspondence with Clarke alongside Max Black's 1952 dialogue on the identity of indiscernibles, and the relationship between the two keeps nagging at me. Black's two-sphere universe is usually presented as a decisive counterexample to Leibniz's principle, yet Leibniz himself relies on strikingly similar symmetric scenarios—a universe shifted uniformly through space, or one in which east and west are everywhere reversed—to argue against Newtonian absolute space, and in each case he denies that the alternative is a genuinely distinct possibility rather than admitting indiscernible-but-distinct entities. Suppose Black had been able to put his spheres to Leibniz directly: would he simply bite the same bullet and insist the scenario really describes only one possible configuration, or does his mature theory of substance—on which individuation ultimately rests on the unique, complete perceptual states of monads rather than on the extrinsic geometric properties of phenomenal bodies—give him a different, perhaps stronger, line of reply? Reconstruct Leibniz's strongest available response, drawing on both the correspondence and his broader metaphysics of substance, and assess whether it actually rescues the identity of indiscernibles or merely relocates the difficulty.
