A friend and I spent a long evening disputing a case involving a pharmacist whose own stakes were considerable — a miscalculated dose could have harmed a patient — but whose work we, much later, only discussed casually over coffee, in a conversation that never became especially demanding. I argued that her own practical stakes alone should settle whether she knew the calculation was correct; my friend argued that since our conversational standards never rose to meet those stakes, contextualist semantics gives us no grounds to deny that she knew, regardless of what had actually been on the line for her. Neither of us could settle the matter once we tried to pull the subject's practical situation apart from the attributor's conversational standards in this way. Write a dialogue between a contextualist and a pragmatic encroachment theorist — who holds that a subject's own stakes fix whether she knows, independently of anyone's conversational standards — in which each constructs a case designed to isolate exactly this divergence, and each presses the other on what their own theory is thereby forced to say.
