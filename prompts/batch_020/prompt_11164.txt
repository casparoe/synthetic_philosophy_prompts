Hey, I could use a hand untangling something in the Zhuangzi that came up while I was prepping my "dream skepticism" week — the unit where I pair Descartes' First Meditation with the butterfly dream. This term I switched the assigned translation from Watson over to James Legge's 1891 version, partly becuase it's out of copyright and I can just drop the whole chapter into the coursepack, and partly because the stiff, Victorian, transliteration-heavy prose (Kâu instead of Zhou, Dze instead of Zi, etc.) actually forces students to slow down instead of pattern-matching to the poster-quote version they've all seen a hundred times. Anyway, a student caught something in office hours that I genuinely hadnt clocked in umpteen years of teaching this text, and I want to think it through properly before I stand in front of the class again on Thursday.

In Legge's translation, the butterfly dream is not its own free-standing little parable, the way it gets presented in basically every popularization. It's the second half of a single, unbroken passage that opens with the Penumbra questioning the Shadow. Here's the whole unit (Book II, "The Adjustment of Controversies," section 11):

"The Penumbra asked the Shadow, saying, 'Formerly you were walking on, and now you have stopped; formerly you were sitting, and now you have risen up:--how is it that you are so without stability?' The Shadow replied, 'I wait for the movements of something else to do what I do, and that something else on which I wait waits further on another to do as it does. My waiting,--is it for the scales of a snake, or the wings of a cicada? How should I know why I do one thing, or do not do another?

'Formerly, I, Kwang Kâu, dreamt that I was a butterfly, a butterfly flying about, feeling that it was enjoying itself. I did not know that it was Kâu. Suddenly I awoke, and was myself again, the veritable Kâu. I did not know whether it had formerly been Kâu dreaming that he was a butterfly, or it was now a butterfly dreaming that it was Kâu. But between Kâu and a butterfly there must be a difference. This is a case of what is called the Transformation of Things.'"

Notice there's no "he said," no new heading, nothing that re-attributes the speech — "Formerly, I, Kwang Kâu, dreamt..." just runs straight on from the Shadow's last question, still nested inside the same quotation marks. It's also worth knowing that a couple of sections earlier in the same chapter, Zhuangzi has already floated the more familiar "great dream" language — the bit about how the dreamer of drinking may wake up weeping, how there is "the great awaking, after which we shall know that this life was a great dream," and how a sage capable of explaining them might not appear for ten thousand ages.

So, three things I'd love your help sorting through: (1) Is there any real textual basis for reading the butterfly bit as literally continuous with the Shadow's speech — i.e., is part of the point that it's the Shadow, of all things, that "dreams" it was Kwang Kâu/a butterfly — or is this just an accident of how the chapter got carved into sections, with the anecdote actually being a free-standing bit that got stitched on right after? (2) If there's something to the first reading, how should the Shadow's opening complaint — that it depends on something that depends on yet another thing, and doesn't even know why it does what it does — change how we hear the famous last line about there "necessarily" being a difference between Kâu and the butterfly? (3) Given the "great dream" language just a bit earlier, do you read the butterfly passage as basically a Daoist cousin of Cartesian dream skepticism (from the inside, I can't tell whether I'm dreaming or awake), or is it making some other, non-skeptical point about "transformation" instead — and does grafting it onto the Shadow's speech push toward one of those readings over the other? I'd like to walk in Thursday with something better than "great question, moving on."
