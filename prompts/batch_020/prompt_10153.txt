I am assembling a cycle of linked historical short stories, each centered on a character forced to act on a philosophical conviction rather than merely defend it, and I've stalled on the entry concerning Descartes' view of animal minds, laid out in Part V of the Discourse on the Method. His argument is more exacting than the modern gloss that animals are "just instinct": he held that no animal can use language flexibly enough to answer whatever is said in its presence, as even a dull-witted person can, and that however precisely a creature performs some particular task — the sort of thing he liked to compare to a clock, which keeps better time than any of us could manage by reasoning — this only shows the creature to be acting from the disposition of its organs, since true reason is not a specialized skill but, in his own phrase, "a universal instrument" fit for any situation. Whether Descartes meant to deny that animals feel anything at all, or only that they think and have souls, is still disputed among his interpreters; his stricter followers concluded that a wounded animal's cry is mechanism through and through, no more morally weighty than the creak of a hinge. Write a short story of about 1,000 words, set in the seventeenth century, in which a character who has genuinely absorbed this doctrine meets a situation that cannot simply be reasoned away. Give the doctrine its full force before anything complicates it, so that a reader who doesn't already share the character's conviction still feels why it was worth holding.
