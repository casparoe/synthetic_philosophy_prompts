I am preparing a handout on Popper's account of verisimilitude for my graduate seminar on the philosophy of science. Popper's qualitative definition ranks theories by comparing their truth-content and falsity-content, and was meant to license the claim that a later false theory can still be closer to the truth than an earlier one. David Miller and Pavel Tichý showed independently in 1974 that the definition cannot deliver on this promise: whenever a false theory is strengthened by adding further content, its truth-content and falsity-content increase together, so a false theory can never come out as more verisimilar than a false rival it exceeds in content. Construct a concrete pair of false theories about some ordinary empirical domain of your choosing such that virtually everyone would judge the second to be closer to the truth than the first, and show exactly why Popper's definition cannot vindicate that judgment. Finally, indicate briefly whether a possible-worlds 'likeness' measure, of the sort Tichý himself, Niiniluoto, or Oddie later proposed, fares any better on your example.
