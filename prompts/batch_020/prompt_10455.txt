Some time ago I was experimenting with variations on the St. Petersburg game and constructed one where, if a fair coin first comes up heads on toss n, you win 2^n/n dollars when n is odd and lose that same amount when n is even (so +$2 for heads on the first toss, −$2 on the second, +$8/3 on the third, and so on). Weighting each payout by its probability of 1/2^n gives the series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ⋯, which sums to ln 2 when the terms are added in that order, so I initially took the fair price of the game to be about 69 cents. Then it occurred to me that this series converges only conditionally, since the corresponding series of absolute values (the harmonic series) diverges — and by the Riemann rearrangement theorem, a conditionally convergent series can be reordered to sum to any real number whatsoever, or made to diverge. So the expected value of this game isn't ln 2, or any other particular figure; it's simply undefined. I later found out that this exact game already has a name in the decision-theory literature, the Pasadena paradox, and that several responses to it have been proposed.
