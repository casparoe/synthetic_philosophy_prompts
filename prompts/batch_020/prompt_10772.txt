I'm a PhD candidate in political philosophy. In this term's seminar on the ethics of migration, we spent three sessions on Michael Walzer's account of communal self-determination in Spheres of Justice and on Joseph Carens's case for open borders, but relegated Michael Dummett's On Immigration and Refugees to a single afternoon, on the grounds that it is, as one classmate put it, "basically Walzer with extra hedging." That verdict has been bothering me ever since, because it struck me as too quick even at the time. Dummett is of course much better known for his work on anti-realism and intuitionistic logic than for political theory, and he spent decades campaigning against racially motivated immigration restriction in Britain; yet in that short book he still concedes that a political community may possess a conditional "right not to be submerged" — as I recall it, a right to limit entry only where unchecked immigration would genuinely threaten to overwhelm a population's existing institutions and way of life, a right he insists must never be invoked as cover for what is really racial or ethnic anxiety, and one he thinks almost no actual immigration regime could honestly claim to be exercising. Walzer, arguing from an explicitly communitarian standpoint in the "Membership" chapter of Spheres of Justice, reaches a formally similar conclusion by a different route: that control over admission is a precondition of a community's capacity to pursue distributive justice at all, and he defends this through analogies to clubs, neighborhoods, and families choosing their own members.

I'd like you to actually test my classmate's dismissive verdict rather than simply assume the family resemblance holds up. Where, specifically, does Dummett's case for a limited right to exclude rest on premises that Walzer's argument does not need, and where does it just arrive at Walzer's conclusion by a more cautious route? Do the constraints built into Dummett's "right not to be submerged" — the high threshold for invoking it, the ban on racial motivation — do independent argumentative work, or could Walzer's communitarian framework absorb them without any real modification? Close by delivering a verdict of your own on which of the two offers the more defensible account of when, if ever, a state may legitimately restrict immigration, and defend it.
