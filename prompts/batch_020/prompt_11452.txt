So a while back I went down a rabbit hole that started with Yudkowsky/MIRI-adjacent stuff on functional decision theory and "logical counterfactuals" (the whole debate about what it even means to say Newcomb's box counterfactually had a million dollars in it), and it eventually dumped me into the actual academic philosophy of language literature on conditionals, which turns out to be its own huge and mostly separate rabbit hole. The example that really hooked me is this minimal pair from Ernest Adams: "If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did" seems obviously true, but "If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have" seems obviously false (no secret backup assassin waiting in the wings). Same antecedent, same consequent, and just flipping indicative to subjunctive mood flips the verdict — apparently that one pair helped spawn decades of argument over whether indicative and counterfactual conditionals need totally different semantics or can be handled by one unified theory.

From what I can tell there's roughly two camps. One (closer to Stalnaker) wants a single possible-worlds machinery for all conditionals, indicative and counterfactual alike, with the mood difference coming down to constraints on which worlds you're allowed to select as "closest" given the context. The other (closer to Adams and Edgington) thinks this is hopeless for indicatives specifically — that they don't have truth conditions at all, that asserting one just expresses a high conditional credence in the consequent given the antecedent, and that only counterfactuals get the possible-worlds treatment. Each side's got it's own classic headache to deal with too: the unified camp has to answer Lewis's triviality results, which seem to show you can't have a truth-conditional semantics that also validates "the Equation" (assertability = conditional probability); the no-truth-value camp has to explain how embedding works (conditionals stuck inside negations, disjunctions, quantifiers) if there's no proposition there to embed, plus deal with weirdness like Gibbard's "Sly Pete" stand-off and McGee's counterexample to modus ponens.

Could you write me a dialogue between a committed defender of each camp? I want real engagement, not talking past each other — have them actually fight over the Oswald/Kennedy pair, over whether the triviality results really force a split rather than just complicating the unified view, and over whether McGee-style failures of modus ponens are a fatal cost or just a predictable feature of the probabilistic camp's own logic. Let each of them make genuine concessions where the other side has a point, without the whole thing just collapsing into one side winning outright — and if you think their disagreement is ultimately more terminological than substantive once you dig into it, I'd rather see that emerge over the course of the exchange than have you just announce it at the end.
