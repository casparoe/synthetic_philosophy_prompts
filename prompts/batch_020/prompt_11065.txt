I've spent the past few weeks reporting a feature on how findings from evolutionary biology have been marshaled against moral realism, and I keep finding that the popular treatments stop right where the argument starts to get interesting. Every explainer walks through the basic move — natural selection shaped our moral beliefs because they were fitness-enhancing, not because they were true, so if there really are robust, mind-independent moral facts, it would be a remarkable coincidence that evolution happened to instill in us beliefs that track them — and then treats the realist as having no real comeback. From what I've since read in the actual journal literature, there is a much more interesting fight over the sophisticated replies to this argument and the counter-replies they provoke, and that's the fight I'd like to see staged properly rather than the popularized opening move.

Could you write me a dialogue set at the reception after a metaethics workshop, between two philosophers who have stayed a little too long at the wine table? One of them should be pressing something close to Sharon Street's original Darwinian dilemma against robust realism: either evolutionary forces bear no relation to the independent moral truths, in which case it's sheer luck that our moral beliefs are accurate, or they do track those truths — a hypothesis the debunker regards as scientifically superfluous next to a leaner story on which certain evaluative tendencies were simply adaptive, full stop. The other should be defending something in the spirit of David Enoch's "third factor" strategy for escaping that dilemma without just asserting a convenient tracking relation. I'd like the debunker to press hard on whether a third factor is really any less ad hoc than the tracking claim it replaces, and on whether it can be stretched to cover the reliability of our fine-grained, actual moral convictions rather than just a handful of bare-bones truths like "survival is good." And I'd like the realist to have genuine resources on hand in return — naturalist moves that tie moral properties directly to fitness-relevant facts, companions-in-guilt arguments borrowed from mathematics or logic, or the charge that the debunking argument, pressed hard enough, undermines the very epistemic standards it needs in order to get off the ground. Please don't let either side win by default: I'd like both of them arguing at full strength and genuinely responding to what the other has just said, since a fair fight between these two positions is exactly what I haven't been able to find elsewhere.
