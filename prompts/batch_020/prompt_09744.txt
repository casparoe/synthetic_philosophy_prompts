I don't have any formal background in philosophy, economics, or decision theory; what I do have is a lot of spare time that I spend reading effective altruism blogs and forum threads, and one idea I keep circling back to without ever feeling like I've properly understood it is "evidential cooperation in large worlds." I gather it used to go by the name "multiverse-wide superrationality," and that the first serious write-up was by a decision theorist named Caspar Oesterheld, if I'm remembering that correctly. As far as I can piece the argument together, the core move is that if the universe, or whatever larger multiverse it sits inside, is sufficiently large, there must be enormous numbers of other decision-makers out there whose reasoning is correlated with mine even though none of us can ever causally affect one another, and that this correlation is supposed to give me an evidential reason, not a causal one, to act cooperatively toward the values of whichever subset of those agents would do the same for me. A friend I occasionally argue about this with insists the whole thing is just acausal trade dressed up in fancier language, which hasn't done much to make it click for me.

What I'd like from you is a description of the most important implications of this idea for someone in my position: an ordinary person who gives a modest amount to charity every year, tries to take cause prioritization seriously, and has no ability to formalize any of the underlying game theory. I don't just want the slogan "be nicer to value systems you don't hold," which is about as far as skimming forum posts has gotten me; I'd like the answer to actually engage with the mechanics. What work is the correlation doing, given that I can never identify which distant agents are relevantly similar to me or confirm that any of them reciprocate? Does the argument really recommend something like hedging my giving across causes I wouldn't otherwise rank highly, or is that too quick a gloss? And how much of its force depends on accepting evidential, or updateless, decision theory over a causal one — would a committed causal decision theorist have any real reason to be moved by this, or are they simply exempt from its conclusions?

I've seen a few posts connect all of this to AI alignment and what people call "macrostrategy," but I'd rather you leave that angle to one side and concentrate on what, if anything, should actually change about how an individual like me behaves.
