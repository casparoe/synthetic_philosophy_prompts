The question was: "Explain Galen Strawson's Basic Argument for the conclusion that no one is ever truly (he sometimes says 'ultimately') morally responsible for what they do. Your answer should (a) reconstruct the steps of the argument and show exactly how the regress is generated, (b) explain why Strawson thinks the argument goes through whether determinism or indeterminism is true, and (c) present one serious objection to the argument along with a brief assessment of whether it succeeds."

Here is my partner's answer, word for word:

"Strawson's Basic Argument tries to show that no one is ever truly or 'ultimately' morally responsible for anything they do, even though in everyday life we obviously do praise and blame people all the time. The argument starts from the claim that whatever you do in a given situation, you do it because of the way you are at that moment — your beliefs, desires, values, personality, and so on. From there, Strawson says that if you're going to be genuinely responsible for what you do, you have to be responsible for being the way you are, since your actions are just an expression of that. But he thinks you can't be responsible for the way you are, mainly because you never chose your own genes, your family, the culture you grew up in, or the experiences that shaped your personality before you were even old enough to have a say in any of it. Since none of that was ever up to you, and everything you do afterward is really just the working-out of a character you never picked out for yourself in the first place, you can never be the ultimate source of your own actions. That means you can never deserve credit or blame in the deep sense that people usually assume when they get angry at someone or admire them.

A key part of Strawson's argument is that it doesn't depend on determinism being true. Even if some of what happens in your brain is genuinely random instead of determined, that randomness isn't something you chose either, so it wouldn't give you any more control over who you are than determinism would. That's part of why the Basic Argument is supposed to be more radical than older hard-determinist arguments — it's meant to work no matter what turns out to be true about causation in the universe.

One obvious objection is that this proves too much, because it seems to rule out the kind of responsibility we clearly do hold each other to every day. Strawson's own reply to this, which he develops in 'Freedom and Resentment,' is that our ordinary practices of praising and blaming don't actually need this ultimate, cosmic kind of responsibility at all — they're really just expressions of natural human reactive attitudes like resentment, gratitude and indignation, and those attitudes would keep operating among us no matter what the deep metaphysics turns out to be. So ordinary responsibility can survive even if 'true' responsibility is impossible, because the two are answers to different questions."
