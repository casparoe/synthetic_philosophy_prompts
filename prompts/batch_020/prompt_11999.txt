For the past few months I've been designing a negotiation protocol that lets autonomous LLM agents inspect each other's complete policies -- system prompt, tool-use rules, everything -- before any deal is struck, on the hope that mutual transparency could substitute for costly enforcement. A colleague pointed out that this is just Tennenholtz's old "program equilibrium" setting, and that its more sophisticated descendant, the Löbian FairBot construction, faces a problem sharper than the usual complaints about unrealistic transparency: FairBot paired with itself is a program equilibrium sustaining cooperation, but DefectBot paired with itself is equally a program equilibrium, and so, for that matter, is almost any other individually rational outcome. On her view, nothing internal to the framework tells two transparent, rational agents to coordinate on the cooperative equilibrium rather than any of the others, so program equilibrium doesn't solve the cooperation problem -- it just re-describes it as a problem of equilibrium selection. I suspect FairBot is privileged in some way DefectBot is not, but I haven't managed to say why without smuggling in the conclusion I want. Argue that Löbian program equilibrium does pick out mutual cooperation as the outcome rational, transparent agents are bound to coordinate on, meeting the equilibrium-selection objection head-on rather than settling for the weaker claim that cooperation is merely one possible outcome among many.
