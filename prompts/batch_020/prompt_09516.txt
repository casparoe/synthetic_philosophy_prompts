I'm a PhD student in philosophy of law. After our jurisprudence seminar's session on the Hart–Fuller debate, two classmates kept arguing about the grudge informer case over coffee: in 1944 a German woman denounced her husband for anti-Hitler remarks made while home on leave; after the war she was prosecuted for unlawfully depriving him of his liberty, and the court is usually said to have declared the Nazi statute invalid as contrary to "the sound conscience and sense of justice of all decent human beings."

(1) Marcus took Fuller's side: the Nazi legal order had degraded so badly — secret decrees, retroactive statutes, courts turned into instruments of terror — that the anti-Hitler statute never qualified as law, so punishing the wife required no retroactivity at all; ordinary law against false imprisonment had simply stayed in force. (2) Naomi objected that this proves too much: plenty of Nazi-era contract, tax, and traffic law was mundane and clearly valid, so Fuller owes a reason why this particular statute fails, not just a gesture at systemic rot — and once he's judging statute by statute, isn't he just smuggling back Hart's own separation of validity from morality? (3) Marcus replied that Fuller's test is explicitly a matter of degree and can target a single enactment: this statute was substantively vicious and used as a tool of terror rather than as advertised, so it can fail while the traffic code doesn't. (4) Naomi answered that this just relabels Hart's point: it's the substantive moral judgment he wanted made openly, via a frank retroactive statute, rather than smuggled into a verdict about validity. She then added a wrinkle: she'd since read that the actual appellate court never invalidated the statute at all, but convicted the wife on the narrower ground that she had no duty to inform and acted from personal motives — so the case both sides treat as their shared battleground (as Hart himself later admitted, in a footnote) may not show what either side needs it to show. (5) Marcus answered that this is a fact about legal history, not about the theories: Hart and Fuller can debate a stylized hypothetical, and whether validity can depend on injustice doesn't hinge on what the Bamberg court actually said.

Could you go through this move by move and say who has the better of it at each stage — not just an overall winner — and flag anything both are missing? I'm especially curious whether Naomi's historical point in (4) should change how much weight either side's theory can really claim from this example, or whether Marcus is right in (5) that it's beside the point.
