I'm putting together a short piece for a graduate seminar on logical empiricism and its afterlife, built around A. J. Ayer's 1988 near-death experience. I'd like your comments before I circulate it. Specifically: (1) does the interpretive argument in the last two paragraphs actually hold up, or does it read too much philosophical weight into what might just be a hedge under public pressure? (2) is there a more defensible way to characterize what is really at stake in Ayer's distinction between "belief" and "attitude"? and (3) have I gotten any of the philosophical or historical details wrong? I would rather you disagree with the reading, if you think it mistaken, than simply wave it through.

Here is the draft, which I've been calling "Ayer's Ministers of the Universe":

Ayer's verification principle held that a sentence expresses a genuine factual claim only if it is either analytic or capable, at least in principle, of empirical confirmation or disconfirmation. Applied to talk of surviving bodily death, the effect was corrosive: claims about a soul or self persisting apart from any body were, for Ayer, not so much false as barely candidates for truth or falsity, since no observation could tell for or against them. His own public atheism about survival was correspondingly less an empirical finding than a polemical stance, meant to expose confusion in the beliefs of others rather than to compete with them on the same evidential ground.

In June 1988, while hospitalized for pneumonia, Ayer choked on a piece of smoked salmon and his heart stopped for four minutes. He described the experience in a Sunday Telegraph piece that ran under the title "What I Saw When I Was Dead" -- not, as he would have preferred, "That Undiscovered Country." He recalled being confronted with "a red light, exceedingly bright, and also very painful," which he took to be governing the universe, along with two beings "responsible for space," which he found was "out of joint" owing to their incompetence; he felt that setting it right had somehow become his job.

The essay closed with the line that the experience had "slightly weakened my conviction that my genuine death ... will be the end of me" -- a sentence that produced a minor press sensation, prompting speculation that a leading atheist philosopher had undergone a deathbed change of heart. Seven weeks later, in "Postscript to a Postmortem," Ayer partly retracted the remark, insisting that what had weakened was "not my belief that there is no life after death, but my inflexible attitude" toward it.

That distinction can look like backpedaling under pressure. I want to suggest it is something more interesting: a distinction Ayer's own system arguably required him to draw, whether he saw this himself or not. If disembodied survival was, by his own criterion, already too much a matter of unverifiable metaphysics to be a candidate for truth or falsity, then his lifelong "belief" that there is no such thing was never tested empirical knowledge to begin with; there was no genuine hypothesis there to confirm. What he actually held was closer to a practical orientation, useful for puncturing bad arguments but never itself answerable to evidence in the way ordinary beliefs are. On this reading, four minutes of cardiac arrest could not touch his belief, because nothing belief-like, in the strict Ayerian sense, was ever there to touch; only the confidence of his attitude could shift, and did.

But this may concede too much. Ayer also had a straightforwardly empirical commitment available to him: that conscious experience depends on functioning brain activity and should not survive its total interruption. It is arguably this ordinary, falsifiable belief, and not some free-floating attitude, whose grip he found loosened, however briefly, by an experience he could not, in the moment, help finding significant. If so, the episode is less a vindication of verificationism's way of drawing the belief/attitude line than a reminder of how uncertain even its architect could feel, in extremity, about the deliverances of his own physicalism.
