The disagreement started over something almost trivial — whether a mutual friend of ours had actually moved to Portland last spring — but when I pushed back on my friend's version of events, she told me she already knew she was right and so didn't need to hear me out. When I objected that this seemed like a strange way to conduct an argument, she defended herself by invoking what she called "the dogmatism paradox," which she traced to an unpublished 1972 lecture of Saul Kripke's, first set out in print the following year by Gilbert Harman, who also gave the puzzle its name.

As she reconstructed the argument, if I know that some proposition h is true, then I already know that anything which could count as evidence against h is evidence against something true, and is therefore misleading; so if I really know h, I am rationally entitled to disregard, in advance and without examination, whatever apparent counter-evidence to h might turn up later. She took this to show that once a person actually knows something, rather than merely being justified in believing it, staying open-minded about that particular claim stops being praiseworthy and starts being a kind of confusion about one's own epistemic position. I spent the rest of the evening looking for the weak premise and couldn't find one, so I more or less conceded the point, even though the conclusion still strikes me as obviously wrong.

What I'd like help with is this. Granting that the argument is as solid as it seemed that night, why do epistemologists continue to write as though intellectual humility and responsiveness to new evidence are virtues across the board, rather than restricting that advice to people who merely believe things instead of knowing them? And, more to the point of my evening: was my friend within her epistemic rights to end the conversation the way she did, and should I return the favor the next time she challenges something I'm sure I already know? I would genuinely like to know whether this is the moral epistemologists have actually drawn from Kripke and Harman's argument, because if it is, it's odd how rarely philosophers seem to invoke it when their own settled convictions are the ones being challenged.
