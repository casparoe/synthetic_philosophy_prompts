A few of us who have been working through Hannah Arendt's political essays in an informal reading group met for dinner after a session on "Truth and Politics" and "Lying in Politics," and the conversation turned into a genuinely long argument between two of the members, Noah and Celeste, that the rest of us never managed to settle. The case that set it off came from Noah's own job at his city's transportation department: after an internal audit flagged a serious funding shortfall, the agency cut three bus routes and described the change, in every press release and public meeting for months afterward, as a "route optimization initiative" rather than as the service cut the audit showed it to be, to the point that, by Noah's account, the officials repeating the phrase eventually seemed to have talked themselves into believing they were improving service rather than simply cutting it.

(1) Noah's opening claim was that this is a clean instance of the political lying Arendt dissects in her Pentagon Papers essay: an institution manufacturing a convenient fiction to replace an inconvenient fact, and enforcing that fiction through sheer repetition until it hardens into the official record. (2) Celeste objected that this gets Arendt's actual target backward: Arendt's real complaint was against the RAND Corporation's systems analysts, whom Celeste kept calling the "problem-solvers," and it was precisely that they were not liars in the traditional sense, since an ordinary liar still privately knows the truth they are concealing, and it is exactly that private knowledge that keeps them tethered to reality and correctable in principle. Once deceivers come to believe their own line, Celeste argued, you have left lying behind for the distinct, and on Arendt's telling more troubling, condition of self-deception, in which the very faculty for distinguishing fact from fiction has been damaged rather than just the outgoing message. (3) Noah answered that the distinction is morally beside the point: riders lost service either way, and treating self-deceived officials as something other than liars just hands them a psychological alibi they have not earned. (4) Celeste replied that Arendt is not in the business of handing out alibis but is tracking which failure does more damage to a shared political world: a strategic liar leaves the structure of true and false intact for at least one person, whereas a self-deceived institution has lost its own grip on that structure and so can no longer be reached by evidence at all. That, Celeste said, is a difference that matters for whether the situation is even repairable, whatever it does for blame. (5) Noah pushed back that this move makes the whole diagnosis practically useless, since no outsider, Celeste included, can ever verify whether the officials privately believed their own phrase or were only performing conviction; an unfalsifiable distinction cannot bear the explanatory weight she wants to put on it. (6) Celeste's last word was that Arendt was never offering a real-time sincerity test for individual speakers. She was describing a structural change in how power operates once professional image management and public-relations techniques enter the picture, turning manufactured, coordinated self-belief into a routine institutional feature rather than a rare private vice, and it is that shift, not any particular official's inner state, which ought to worry us.

I sat through the whole argument without ever landing on a view of my own, so I would like your independent read on it. Could you work through points (1) through (6) in order and say who has the better of it at each step, including whether one of them is quietly talking past the other at some point, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I would also be curious whether either of them is misreading Arendt in some way neither noticed, and whether her essays actually supply either side with resources that Noah and Celeste failed to draw on.
