Here's the question I gave them:

"In 'Of Money,' 'Of Interest,' and 'Of the Balance of Trade,' Hume argues against the mercantilist assumption that a nation's wealth grows in proportion to the gold and silver it accumulates. Explain (a) the price-specie-flow mechanism and how Hume uses it to argue that a permanently favorable balance of trade is self-defeating, and (b) Hume's account of what actually determines interest rates in a commercial society. Then discuss whether, for Hume, money is fundamentally 'neutral' with respect to real economic activity — and if your answer is 'it's complicated,' say exactly where the complication lies."

And here's what one of my stronger students (usually sits around A-minus) turned in, basically verbatim:

"Hume's move against the mercantilists is basically mechanical rather than moral. Suppose a country manages to run a permanent trade surplus and pulls in gold and silver from its neighbors — that very success sets up its own reversal. As specie flows in, the domestic money supply rises, and since Hume holds a quantity theory of money (prices track the amount of money chasing a given stock of goods), domestic prices rise too. But now that country's exports are dearer relative to its neighbors, whose money supply and price levels have fallen as bullion drained out of them, so foreigners buy less from it and it buys more from them, until specie flows back out and price levels re-equalize across countries. No nation can hoard a disproportionate share of the world's money for long — the balance of trade is self-correcting. It's basically the same insight Adam Smith and then Ricardo would later put on firmer footing with the theory of comparative advantage: hoarding bullion through a forced trade surplus is pointless because real wealth comes from specialization and exchange, not accumulated treasure.

Hume extends this logic to interest rates too. If the sheer quantity of money in a country doesn't determine its real wealth, you'd expect him to say something similar about interest — and he does, arguing that rates fall as a nation's stock of money grows, since lenders competing to place a bigger pool of specie bid the price of a loan down, and rise when specie is scarce. His go-to example is Spain: for all the talk of Spanish decline, Hume actually takes the flood of American silver to explain why money was for a time unusually cheap to borrow there, before prices and trade with the rest of Europe caught up.

Is money neutral for Hume, then? In the long run, yes — an increase in the money stock eventually just re-prices everything and changes nothing real, the way multiplying every price tag in the country by ten would leave relative prices, and so real trade, untouched. The more interesting wrinkle, and the one I think gets flattened when people call Hume a strict quantity theorist, is that a nation whose money supply is gradually increasing gets a genuine, if temporary, edge in industry and employment over one whose supply is flat, because prices don't adjust all at once — there's a lag in which merchants and workers are holding more money before they're facing higher prices, and that lag is where the stimulus lives. So money is neutral only 'in the limit'; on the way there, it isn't."
