I cover science and ideas for a living, and recently found myself at a dinner after a public panel on the solar system, where two of the panelists kept arguing about Pluto's demotion long after the Q&A had ended. Renata, an orbital dynamicist, and Dev, a planetary geologist who had worked on the New Horizons mission, went back and forth roughly like this:

(1) Renata: The International Astronomical Union wasn't just legislating a word in 2006. "Has cleared the neighborhood around its orbit" tracks something dynamically real — whether a body dominates its zone predicts how it formed and how stable that zone will stay. That's a fact about Pluto, not a preference.

(2) Dev: But "cleared its neighborhood" isn't a property of Pluto at all — it's a property of the whole region. Drop an Earth-mass body into Pluto's orbit and it would likely take far longer than the age of the solar system to clear that same neighborhood, simply because the outer disk is so much bigger and slower. What's actually intrinsic to Pluto — its roundness, its layered geology, its thin atmosphere — is what does the real explanatory work, and that's what people meant by "planet" long before 2006. So the whole fight over which definition is "correct" is merely verbal: once we agree Pluto is round, geologically active, and hasn't gravitationally cleared its zone, there's no further fact about whether it's "really" a planet — that's just a decision about where to draw a word's boundary, no different from arguing about whether a hot tub counts as a "pool."

(3) Renata: Too fast. Not just any predicate carves up the solar system equally well. "Cleared its orbital zone" supports generalizations about formation and long-term stability that mere roundness doesn't, so if one classification does more explanatory work, deciding which property our theories should track is substantive, not verbal.

(4) Dev: Roundness does comparable work — it predicts internal differentiation, geological activity, and the capacity to hold an atmosphere or subsurface ocean, which is exactly why geologists care about it. If both properties support real generalizations, they're equally entitled to count as "joint-carving," and the leftover question of which one gets the word "planet" is precisely the kind of question with no further fact of the matter.

By the time the check came, neither had conceded an inch, and I was no closer to a verdict myself. Could you go through moves (1) through (4) and say, for each, who has the stronger case and why — drawing on how philosophers actually distinguish verbal from substantive disputes (Hirsch and Chalmers are the obvious reference points, but bring in others if relevant)? In particular: does Renata's move (3) succeed in blocking Dev's "merely verbal" diagnosis, or does move (4) reinstate it — and is there a position, something like what Karen Bennett calls an "unhappy-faced" verdict, that both of them are missing? End with your overall verdict on its own line, in exactly this format: \verdict{verbal}, \verdict{substantive}, or \verdict{unresolved}.
