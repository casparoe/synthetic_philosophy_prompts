I'm a backend engineer at a small startup that builds a personal-assistant app, and part of my job involves a feature that infers what users actually want from how they behave in the app (what they linger on, what they dismiss, what they keep coming back to) rather than only from the preferences they explicitly set on a settings page; internally we just call this "learning the reward function," even though it's a pretty loose, applied cousin of actual inverse reinforcement learning. We also run a Friday lunch reading group on AI alignment papers, and last week's paper, on learning reward functions from behavior, kicked off an argument between two colleagues, Colin (one of our ML engineers) and Marta (a PM who studied philosophy before moving into product), that outlasted the meeting itself and that nobody present managed to settle. Since I don't have any formal philosophy background myself, I'd like an outside read on who actually had the better of it, because I could see something to both sides and don't want to just side with whoever argued louder.

The hypothetical that set it off: an assistant notices, from months of logs, that a user reliably keeps scrolling a particular feed late at night, well after dismissing a bedtime reminder, and on that basis infers a reward function under which surfacing more of that content at night scores highly, even though the same user, on a settings survey, said they wanted the app to help them spend less time on their phone before bed. (1) Colin said the system is doing exactly what it's supposed to: given a large, consistent body of choices, inferring the reward function that best explains them is the whole point of preference learning, and if we're just going to override that with the survey answer whenever the two conflict, there was no reason to build a behavior-based system instead of a settings page. (2) Marta said this begs the question, because it assumes that whatever a person consistently does late at night, under whatever pull happens to be operating on them at that moment, simply is what they want, which has the same shape as the classic philosophical case of an addict who takes the drug every single time despite not wanting, at some level, to be a person who takes it. If consistent behavior settles preference by definition, then acting against your own preferences becomes impossible by fiat, which can't be the right result. (3) Colin answered that the two readings (genuinely prefers scrolling, versus wishes to stop but keeps failing) produce more or less identical logs, so this isn't something the system is getting wrong, it's something nobody could get right from behavior alone; besides, systems like this don't assume perfect rationality in the first place, they assume something like a noisy or "Boltzmann-rational" agent, where actions get chosen with probability related to how good they are, which already leaves room for the person to slip sometimes. (4) Marta replied that the noise assumption doesn't rescue this, because as she understood it, that kind of noise is basically undirected (no more likely to push you toward one specific tempting option than toward any other equally costly mistake), whereas weakness of will is systematic and one-directional: the same feed, the same time of night, every time, which is exactly what you would also expect from someone who simply has a boring, stable preference for late-night scrolling. So the noise model doesn't discriminate between the two readings any better than the plain version did.

Neither of them budged, and by the end I suspect they were partly talking past each other.
