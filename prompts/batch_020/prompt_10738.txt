In my AP English class, we just finished the section of *The Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* where Huck writes a letter telling Miss Watson where to find Jim, the enslaved man who has been traveling downriver with him, and then, after a long inner struggle in which he genuinely believes that helping Jim escape is a sin that will send him to hell, tears the letter up anyway and decides, "All right, then, I'll go to hell." My teacher mentioned, almost in passing, that this exact scene is actually a well-known test case in academic philosophy for competing theories of what gives an action "moral worth," and I couldn't stop thinking about it, so I did some digging on my own.

On one side is Kant's view, from the *Groundwork*, that an action has genuine moral worth only when it is done from duty rather than merely in accordance with duty or out of inclination; he even argues that a person who has lost all natural sympathy for others but still helps them purely because it is right is, for the first time, acting with real moral worth, while the naturally warm-hearted philanthropist is not. On the other side, I found that Nomy Arpaly has argued, partly in dialogue with a much older paper by Jonathan Bennett that already used Huck's exact dilemma to explore the tension between "sympathy" and "conscience," that moral worth doesn't require acting from any explicit conception of duty at all: what matters is whether the agent is actually responsive to whatever features make the action right, something Huck clearly is even though his sincere, conscious judgment is that what he's doing is wrong. This all seems connected to a separate worry, which I've seen attributed to Michael Smith, that wanting to do the right thing "because it's the right thing" might itself be a kind of moral fetishism rather than a sign of good character.

Could you compare these two accounts of moral worth in some depth? I'm especially curious whether Kant's framework has any real way of crediting Huck's decision with moral worth, or whether it seems committed to denying this, since Huck is not acting from any conception of duty and is, if anything, acting against the one he consciously holds. More generally, which of the two accounts do you find more defensible as a theory of what moral worth actually consists in, and why?
