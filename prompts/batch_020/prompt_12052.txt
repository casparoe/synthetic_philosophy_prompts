Okay, need help with a speech, and this one's got a real constraint baked into it. My old high school history teacher, Mr. Doyle, is retiring after 38 years, and a bunch of his former students -- including me, and im fairly sure it's his fault I ended up a philosophy professor -- are throwing him a dinner. Here's the thing: without ever naming the guy, Doyle ran his entire classroom on Whitehead's "rhythm of education," the romance-precision-generalization cycle from The Aims of Education. Every unit started with something designed purely to hook you (romance), then he'd grind you through dates, sources, and the actual discipline of doing history (precision), then near the end he'd cut you loose on some open question and let you notice you could actually think with all of it (generalization). He also ran a whole campaign against what he called "dead facts" -- stuff you could memorize for a quiz but never do anything with -- which is basically just Whitehead's "inert ideas" with the terminology filed off, though I'd bet real money he's never read a page of Whitehead in his life.

I mentioned this to a colleague in the ed school and she said just quote Whitehead directly, but that feels like exactly the wrong move here. About half the room is teachers, plus a few of us who went into academia and will clock the structure instantly; the other half is family and former students who'd glaze over the second Whitehead's name came up. So: can you write the toast, aiming for about five minutes read aloud? It needs to actually move through the three stages as its structure, not just describe them, and somewhere in the middle I want one line that uses "inert ideas" as a joke rather than a citation -- something like, the one unforgivable move tonight would be a toast that's just a pile of inert facts about the guy's career. And ideally the toast itself doesn't end up being exactly that.
