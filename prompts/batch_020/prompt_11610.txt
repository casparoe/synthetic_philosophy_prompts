I've read a fair amount of Robin Hanson's writing on futarchy over the years, and one tension in the proposal has never quite sat right with me. Hanson pitches futarchy — vote on values, bet on beliefs — as an extension of the argument in Hayek's "The Use of Knowledge in Society" that markets aggregate dispersed knowledge better than any central planner could: legislatures fix a welfare metric, and conditional prediction markets settle which policy maximizes it. But Hayek's epistemic case for prices was inseparable from a broader Austrian subjectivism that doubted whether a society's ends could ever be validly compressed into a single index for anyone — planner or market — to maximize; that same doubt underlies his later attacks on the coherence of a "social welfare function." I'd like you to compare the two positions directly: does futarchy's institutional design actually follow from Hayek's epistemics, or does specifying a welfare metric for the ballot box smuggle back in exactly the kind of aggregation his epistemics were meant to rule out? I'm less interested in the standard objections about manipulation or Goodhart's law than in this specific tension between the two halves of Hanson's own inspiration.
