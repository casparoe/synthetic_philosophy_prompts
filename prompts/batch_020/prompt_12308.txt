Two months ago I left a philosophy PhD program for a quantitative research role at a small trading firm, and during my first week a senior colleague pulled up an old internal post-mortem as a kind of hazing ritual for new hires: an early version of one of the firm's multi-asset strategies turned out to have cyclical implied preferences over three correlated baskets of instruments, and a rival desk had worked out how to loop trades against it and bleed off money in exactly the shape of a textbook money pump before risk management caught it. I had encountered money pumps as an undergraduate purely as a slightly bloodless argument for why rational preference orderings must be transitive, and it was unsettling to watch a colleague sketch the same structure on a whiteboard as something that had actually happened to real capital. I mentioned this over dinner to a friend finishing her philosophy MA, and she sent me into the literature on money pumps and diachronic rationality with a seriousness I never had as a student — particularly the debate over whether so-called "resolute choice" genuinely rescues an agent with cyclical or incomplete preferences from exploitation, or merely relabels stubbornness as rationality, and whether the whole apparatus survives the move from tidy textbook cycles to the messier case of options that are merely on a par rather than strictly ranked.

I co-host a small podcast aimed at quantitatively literate listeners with no formal training in philosophy, and next month's guest is a decision theorist whose recent work defends resolute choice as the correct response to money-pump arguments, including their extension to agents with incomplete preferences. I don't want the episode to just re-derive the cyclical-preference case that everyone half-remembers from an intro decision-theory unit, so could you draft nine or ten interview questions that push past a restatement of her position? Her go-to move when pressed on the charge of irrationality is to say that a resolute chooser evaluates entire extended plans rather than isolated acts, so there is no synchronic irrationality at any single choice node — I'd like at least one question that makes that move progressively harder to lean on. I'd also like at least one question on how, if at all, resolute choice is supposed to extend from cyclical to merely incomplete preferences via sequential "sweetening" offers; at least one on what actually distinguishes resolute choice from an agent who has simply bound herself in advance, the way Ulysses had himself tied to the mast; and at least one asking whether a structurally similar resolute-choice move could be made on behalf of diachronic Dutch-book arguments for Bayesian conditionalization, or whether credences resist the analogy in a way preferences don't. For each of these four, please add a natural follow-up question keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so the conversation presses on the view rather than just collecting statements of it.
