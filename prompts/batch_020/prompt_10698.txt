I'm a novelist, and since my second book, I've made it a firm rule never to read anything written about my own work while I'm still drafting the next one — no reviews, no reader comments, nothing. It isn't really thin skin so much as self-preservation: I've noticed that even praise can subtly redirect what I think I'm supposed to be doing on the page. My third novel comes out this autumn, and my editor has just asked, as a matter of routine, whether I'd like the trade reviews (Kirkus, Publishers Weekly, and so on) forwarded to me as they come in over the next month, or held back until after publication as usual. Normally I wouldn't think twice about declining.

The complication is that I'm currently deciding how large a role to give a secondary character — an aunt who narrates several chapters of the first book — in the sequel I'm drafting now. I have two versions of the opening section ready: one that brings her back substantially, and one that barely does. It's entirely possible that the trade reviews of book one will say something about how that character landed with readers, which is exactly the kind of evidence I'd otherwise have no way of getting before I have to commit to one version or the other.

A friend of mine, who did a doctorate in statistics before moving into finance, tells me my usual policy is beside the point in this particular case. Reading the reviews costs me nothing, he points out, since I remain free to disregard anything unhelpful or unpleasant in them, while there's a genuine chance they contain something bearing on a decision I have to make either way; by his reckoning, I stand to gain and cannot possibly lose by looking. I can't identify the flaw in his reasoning, exactly, but it still feels like it leaves something out about what reading reviews actually does to a person in the middle of writing a book, as opposed to what it would do to the sort of idealized decision-maker he seems to have in mind. I'd genuinely like to know whether he's right, and if he isn't, where precisely his argument breaks down.
