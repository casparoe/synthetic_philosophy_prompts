I've just started teaching a graduate seminar that pairs Sextus Empiricus's Outlines of Pyrrhonism with the contemporary literature on the epistemic regress problem, and a question from Tuesday's session has been nagging at me since. Agrippa's trilemma, as Sextus transmits it, holds that any justification offered for a belief must terminate in an unargued assumption, loop back into circularity, or run on without end -- essentially the same menu that foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism still offer today. Sextus himself never picks a horn: he deploys the trilemma to induce suspension of judgment about all three, not to endorse one of them. A student asked me something I couldn't answer well on the spot: if Sextus were actually made to weigh Chisholm's foundationalism, BonJour's coherentism, and Klein's infinitism on their merits, rather than deploying them only to generate equipollence, would his own arguments and temperament favor one as least self-undermining -- or does his Pyrrhonism give him principled grounds to keep declining the choice? I'd like a serious, textually grounded answer I can bring back to the seminar, not another survey of the three positions.
