The question was:

"State David Lewis's Principal Principle, including the role of the admissibility proviso (5 pts). Explain why Lewis regarded some restriction on admissible evidence as necessary (5 pts). Describe how the problem of 'undermining futures' puts pressure on the Principle (5 pts). Briefly indicate how Lewis's later 'New Principle' is meant to respond to this problem (5 pts)."

Here is my classmate's answer:

"Lewis's Principal Principle says that your credence in a proposition A, conditional on the chance of A being x together with any admissible evidence E, should equal x: C(A | Ch(A)=x & E) = x. Admissible evidence is, roughly, historical information about the world up to the relevant time, together with the content of the chance theory itself; it excludes anything that would let you 'see past' the chance process to the actual outcome, such as a crystal ball or a reliable oracle. Lewis needed this restriction because without it the Principle would require a clairvoyant who somehow already knows tomorrow's coin toss to still set her credence in 'heads' to 0.5 just because the coin is fair, which is clearly wrong once she has that extra information.

This restriction becomes especially delicate once we ask where the chances themselves come from. On Lewis's Humean view, a chance theory is simply whichever theory best balances simplicity and strength in summarizing the entire pattern of events in history, including events after the time at which the chance is being ascribed. That means a run of outcomes that is wildly improbable given the chance theory can count as evidence against the theory itself, even though that run lies in the future relative to the ascription; this is the problem of 'undermining futures,' and it threatens to make the Principal Principle incoherent, since the very evidence the theory needs in order to be true is evidence that a rational agent should, on ordinary principles of confirmation, sometimes hold against it.

In effect, this is really the same worry that motivates van Fraassen's Reflection Principle: just as the Principal Principle tells us to defer to the objective chances, Reflection tells us to defer to our own future, better-informed credences, since by the time the coin has landed our future self is effectively in the position of an oracle relative to our present self. Lewis's 'New Principle,' introduced to fix the undermining problem, essentially builds this same idea in explicitly, by requiring E to be drawn only from propositions about the past, so that no future, potentially undermining information can ever count as admissible."
