I've served on the governing board of a smallish literary prize for a few years now (it's sort of my community-service project as a working novelist), and every year we have some version of the same fight about how the judging panel should be structured. Right now three judges read everything and hash it out across two long meetings until they land on a winner, but a newer board member -- an ex-quant who writes thrillers on the side -- keeps citing the Condorcet Jury Theorem, arguing that if we swapped those three judges for fifteen or twenty "community readers" each casting an independent vote, the aggregate pick would be more likely to land on the actual best book, the same way a big enough jury supposedly converges on the correct verdict, and he's convinced its basically settled math at this point. Several of us have a nagging feeling this doesn't map cleanly onto picking a novel the way it maps onto guilt or innocence, but nobody on the commitee, myself included, can say exactly why, and I don't want to walk into next month's vote armed with nothing but a bad feeling.

Could you draft the memo I'd circulate to the board before that meeting? I'd like it to lay out plainly what the theorem actually requires in order to do the work he wants it to do -- the competence and independence assumptions, and what happens to the result if either one is shaky -- and be honest about where the analogy to book-judging holds up and where it strains, especially once you're choosing among a whole shelf of books rather than a guilty/not-guilty binary. It should still take the real intuition behind his proposal seriously -- that more independent eyes should cancel out any one judge's blind spots -- rather than just dismissing it as math borrowed from the wrong context. End with an actual recommendation -- keep the panel at three, go big, some hybrid -- plus roughly how the voting would need to be structured so we're not just recreating the same problem with extra steps and a bigger spreadsheet.
