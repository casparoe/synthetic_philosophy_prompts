I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but over the past year I've worked my way through Jaegwon Kim's writing on mental causation on my own, mostly via the Stanford Encyclopedia and a couple of secondary sources rather than the original journal articles, so I may have some of the fine details slightly off. What pulled me into this was an argument with a friend finishing a neuroscience PhD, who insisted that talk of "reasons" and "beliefs" doing anything is just a convenient story laid over neurons obeying physics. Kim's causal exclusion argument seems to back her up more than I'd like: if every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause, and a single event can't have two distinct sufficient causes except as a rare, freak coincidence, then the physical realizer of any mental state already does all the causal work that state was supposed to do, and the mental property riding on top of it looks redundant.

The escape route I find most attractive is Sydney Shoemaker's idea that a realized property's causal powers are always a proper subset of its realizer's causal powers, so that citing the mental property isn't citing some extra cause in competition with the physical one, but is instead picking out the very same causal transaction at a coarser grain. What keeps nagging at me is that this seems to demand that exactly the same subset of powers show up in every physical system that realizes the property, whether that's a human brain, an octopus's very differently wired nervous system, or some hypothetical silicon system, and I can't see how you'd specify that fixed, realizer-independent subset in advance without quietly falling back on a functional definition of the mental property, powers and all, which would make the "subset" move look like a repackaging of plain functionalism rather than an independent answer to Kim. I've gone back and forth on whether this is a real problem or whether I'm missing an obvious reply, so I'd like you to argue, as rigorously as you can, that Shoemaker's strategy survives this multiple-realizability worry without collapsing into functionalism or smuggling in type-identity through the back door. Please engage with the strongest version of the objection rather than one that's easy to wave away.
