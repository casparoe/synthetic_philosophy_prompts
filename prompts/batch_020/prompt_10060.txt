For a while now I have been working through the literature on money-pump arguments on my own, without any formal training, mostly by following citation trails out of decision-theory blog posts. The standard argument runs as follows: if your preferences cycle — you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A — a clever exploiter can walk you through a sequence of trades and small side-payments that leaves you strictly poorer than when you started, so genuine rationality requires transitive, and on some formulations complete, preferences.

What I cannot work out is the "resolute choice" reply to this argument, on which an agent is allowed to keep the cyclical preferences and still avoid being pumped, simply by committing in advance to a plan and refusing to deviate later, even at the point where, taken in isolation, they would rather switch. This seems to turn on whether rationality is really a property of individual choices made at a time or of whole policies extended across time, and I cannot decide whether the resolute-choice move actually rescues the agent's rationality on the second reading, or just relocates the irrationality to the moment of execution: right when they are supposed to hold the line, their current preferences are telling them to switch, so refusing to do so seems just as much a departure from doing what one most prefers right now as the original cycling was.

Could you write a short dialogue between two equally capable characters — one who thinks the money pump shows that preferences must be transitive and complete, and one who defends resolute choice as a genuinely rational policy across time — in which they work through this specific worry about relocation rather than simply restating their opening positions?
