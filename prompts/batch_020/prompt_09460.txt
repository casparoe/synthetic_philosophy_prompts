The question was: "Explain the core of Patrick Devlin's argument for the legal enforcement of morality, as he formulated it in response to the Wolfenden Report, and summarize the main thrust of Hart's reply to him. Then, in a few sentences, explain how Dworkin's later essay pushes the critique of Devlin a step further than Hart's own argument does." Here's the answer I got stuck on:

"The Wolfenden Report (1957) recommended decriminalising private homosexual acts between consenting adults, on the view that there's a realm of private morality that is 'not the law's business.' Devlin rejected this in 'The Enforcement of Morals.' His central claim is that a shared morality is what holds a society together — the 'invisible bonds' argument — so a serious enough departure from that shared morality threatens society's existence, much like treason, and can legitimately be suppressed by law even where there's no harm to any identifiable victim. To work out which departures count, Devlin appealed to the reaction of the ordinary 'man in the jury box,' whose intolerance, indignation, and disgust are treated as evidence that a real moral boundary has been crossed.

Hart's reply, following Mill's harm principle closely, was that the only legitimate basis for criminalising conduct is that it harms someone other than the person doing it, so he rejected paternalistic laws right along with Devlin's moralism — and he also pointed out that Devlin never actually shows that tolerating homosexual acts would cause society to disintegrate, since social morality changes constantly (attitudes to usury, for instance) without society falling apart, so the 'disintegration thesis' is really just an assertion dressed up as an argument.

Dworkin's essay takes this a step further by basically restating Hart's point in a stronger form: because Devlin can't produce evidence of actual harm to society, the 'moral' reaction he leans on is exposed as nothing more than unreasoned prejudice, and prejudice can never legitimately ground a criminal statute."
