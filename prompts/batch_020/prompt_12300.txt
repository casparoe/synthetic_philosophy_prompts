A friend and I spent an evening arguing about Augustine's absolutism on lying. My friend thought that since he ranks lies by gravity — an "officious" lie told to help someone is far less serious to him than a malicious one — he leaves room for a well-intentioned lie to become permissible once enough is at stake. I argued the ranking does no such work: even his least grave lie remains a sin for him, and the only real latitude he grants is for silence, refusal to answer, or misleading-but-true statements that stop short of asserting a falsehood.

That argument turns out to bear on something more concrete. I sit on the policy committee of a domestic-violence shelter, and we're revising our script for staff who are asked point-blank, by phone or at the door, whether a specific person is currently staying with us. One faction wants to authorize an outright denial whenever staff judge the asker dangerous; another wants only a fixed refusal to confirm or deny anything, on the grounds that it protects residents just as reliably without anyone having to state something false.

Please draft the short memo I'll bring to next month's meeting. Recommend one policy (or a precisely specified hybrid, if the cases genuinely differ), give staff guidance for the hardest cases, and state fairly the best argument against whatever you recommend — one that engages Augustine's actual reasons for treating lying as categorically worse than concealment, not just his conclusion.
