I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of biology, and every year, before we work through Wright, Cummins, and Neander on theories of function, I put a made-up case to the students to see what they say before the reading has shaped their intuitions. This term, after finishing the reading, two of my students, Elena and Marcus, picked the same case back up during office hours and got into a real disagreement that neither would drop. I was refereeing rather than arguing, and I honestly could not settle it myself, so I would like an outside, independent judgment on their exchange.

The case itself is a pure thought experiment, and every empirical detail in it is stipulated: a hypothetical lizard species has a gene producing a bright blue skin pigment, which arose as a side effect of a biochemical pathway that also blocks UV radiation. For roughly 190,000 years, in a high-UV ancestral habitat, this gene was maintained in the population purely by its UV-protective effect. About 10,000 years ago the lineage moved into a shaded, low-UV habitat where UV exposure no longer poses any survival risk at all, so the protective effect became entirely irrelevant to fitness -- but it turns out females of the species have always strongly preferred bluer males, and this mate preference is now the only thing keeping the gene from drifting out of the population. Nothing about the underlying biochemistry has changed: transplant these lizards back to the ancestral habitat, and the pigment blocks UV exactly as well as it ever did.

Here is their disagreement, more or less as it happened. (1) Elena said the pigment's function now is mate attraction, full stop: on Neander's selected-effects theory, a trait's function is whatever effect currently explains its presence in the population, and that is attraction, since protection is not doing any selective work anymore. (2) Marcus objected that this is not what the theory actually says: it individuates function via the selection history that explains why the gene is still there, and 190,000 years of selection for protection is a far larger part of that history than 10,000 years of selection for attractiveness, so a genuinely history-tracking verdict should favor protection, if anything -- Elena, he said, was just picking the answer that felt current and attaching Neander's name to it. (3) Elena replied that this proves too much: if sheer accumulated duration really settled the matter, the theory would issue absurd verdicts whenever a trait's selective role shifted even slightly, since old pressures would always outvote new ones by weight of time, and no etiological theorist actually applies the theory that way -- in practice they ask what is doing the explanatory work now, which is exactly what she had done. (4) Marcus said that might be a fair description of how the theory gets applied in practice, but that is a concession, not a defense: it shows that the theory, as officially stated, does not hand you "attraction" for free, and needs some further, unprincipled rule about how much recent history counts as the operative selection episode; he then added that a Cummins-style causal-role theorist avoids the whole dispute, since relative to the capacity of evading UV damage the function is protection, relative to the capacity of attracting mates it is signaling, and no history or arbitrary cutoff is needed either way. (5) Elena answered that this does not resolve the dispute, it dodges it: a theory equally willing to call both effects "functions," with no way to rank one above the other, has not told us what the pigment is for, it has just relisted what the pigment does relative to whichever capacity happens to interest you.

Could you adjudicate this move by move? Please first give your verdict as a JSON object of the form {"1": <score>, "2": <score>, "3": <score>, "4": <score>, "5": <score>}, where each score is an integer from -2 (clearly mistaken) to 2 (clearly right); then briefly justify each score; and close with a short paragraph on whether Elena and Marcus, between them, are missing some further consideration that bears on how biological functions ought to be individuated.
