So this happened at a dinner thing with friends last week and it's been bugging me since. I'm drafting a fantasy novel I've been chipping away at forever, and there's a bound spirit in it -- basically an oracle enchanted so it physically cannot say anything false, more like a geas than a moral rule it's choosing to follow. I was moaning to two friends about a plot snag: what happens if my villain corners the oracle and forces it to say, out loud, "the sentence I am now speaking is false"? Does the enchantment just short out? I meant it as a throwaway, half-joking question, but two people at the table, Dana (finishing up a philosophy PhD, logic side of things) and Marcus (does semantics/linguistics), spent the whole main course going at each other over it and neither one gave an inch.

Dana's opening move was that the sentence is neither true nor false, a gap, so the oracle's fine either way, since it was never asserting anything truth-apt to begin with. Marcus shot back that "neither true nor false" just amounts to "not true," which is exactly what the sentence claims about itself, so now the claim "it's not true" looks true, and you're stuck in the identical loop one level up -- he called this "the revenge problem," like the paradox just regenerates no matter how you dodge it. Dana's comeback was to go Tarskian: the "false" inside the sentence and the "not true" we use to describe it from outside aren't the same predicate, they belong to different levels of language, and revenge only bites if you insist a language can contain one all-purpose truth predicate covering its own sentences, which you provably can't have anyway. Marcus wasnt buying it -- he said stacking up levels like that might satisfy a logic seminar, but it means the oracle's magic secretly has to encode an infinite tower of truth predicates just to survive one dumb sentence, which is cheating, not explaining, and that the more honest move is to just say the sentence is true and false at once and let the enchantment genuinely short-circuit as a plot beat -- bite the bullet on real contradictions. Dana's clapback was that letting in even one true contradiction blows up the whole logic, since classically a contradiction lets you derive literally anything, so he isnt patching one sentence, he's rebuilding the oracle's entire logic from the ground up, which is a way bigger price than a few extra levels of "true."

Neither of them budged, and I don't know enough logic to referee it myself, so: can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's got the better of it at each step, not just hand down an overall winner? Also flag anything you think they're both missing, other options neither considered, or assumptions either side is leaning on without noticing. And if you had to actually bet on one of them being more right overall, I'm curious who and why.
