I got pretty into forecasting over the last several months (Manifold, Metaculus, that whole prediction-market hobby scene) and started actually tracking my calibration instead of just vibing, which is how I noticed something annoying about myself: whenever I nail three or four forecasts in a row, my stated confidence on the next few creeps up for no real reason, then a week later, looking back, its obviously just a hot-hand illusion and not any actual improvement in my judgment. That sent me down a rabbit hole into how rational belief is supposed to behave over time (don't ask), which is how I first ran into van Fraassen's Reflection Principle -- roughly, the idea that your credence right now in something should match what you currently expect your own future, still-rational credence in it to be, on pain of being Dutch-booked by someone clever enough to bet against your own future self.

I mentioned the hot-hand thing in our group chat mostly as a joke, but it turned into a real fight between two other people in the group, Dana and Marcus, and I'd love a neutral read on it since it's literally my bias they were fighting over and I don't trust myself to referee fairly. Dana's position was that I should already be discounting my own future confidence numbers right now, before any of it even happens, the same way you'd handle a thermometer you know reads two degrees high -- you don't throw the reading out, you subtract the known error and use that as your estimate. Marcus said that's not how Reflection works: van Fraassen's principle only binds you to a future self you have no specific reason to think will be irrational, and knowing about the hot-hand pattern in advance is exactly such a reason, so the principle doesn't tell you to discount anything, it just switches off for that future self entirely -- there's nothing left to defer to, correctly or otherwise. Dana said that's too fast: a bias you can name and roughly size in advance isn't the same as pure noise or manipulation, it's structured error, and structured error is exactly the kind of thing you can and should fold into a current estimate. Marcus's comeback was that Dana was quietly smuggling in a completely different kind of reasoning and mislabeling it -- once you're correcting a reading using an independent story about why the instrument is unreliable, that's just ordinary calibration/reliability analysis, not deference to a future credence, so "Reflection" doesn't actually apply on either of their readings, Dana just likes how the word sounds.

Neither of them backed down and it died there. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's actually right at each point, instead of just picking an overall winner? I'm especially curious whether Marcus's last move -- that this was never really about Reflection at all -- is a genuine gotcha or a bit too convenient, and whether there's some way of applying the Reflection Principle to a known, structured bias like mine that both of them are missing entirely.
