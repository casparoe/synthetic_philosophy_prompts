So my friend and I spent like two hours last night arguing about the St. Petersburg paradox — the coin-flip game where your prize doubles every time it comes up heads, and you get paid out whenever it finally lands tails. Expected value of that game is infinite, but obviously nobody would pay more than like $20 to play it. I said the whole puzzle basically dissolves once you admit money has diminishing marginal utility, so the *expected utility* is finite even though the expected value isn't. My friend wouldnt let it go though — she insisted that trick doesn't actually work in general, that you can always cook up some other version of the game that still has infinite expected utility no matter what curve you use, as long as the utility function is unbounded. Neither of us could actually produce that example on the spot, so it kind of stalled out there. Can you construct a St. Petersburg-style game like that — one that breaks the diminishing-marginal-utility fix for basically any unbounded utility function? And be straight with me: does that mean the fix is basically dead unless utility is bounded, or is there some other way to rescue it?
