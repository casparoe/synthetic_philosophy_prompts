A friend and I had a long-running argument the other night, sparked by a piece she had read defending the longtermist practice of taking vanishingly unlikely but potentially catastrophic or transformative scenarios seriously. The disagreement quickly got snagged on terminology rather than substance: she kept describing the whole approach as essentially Pascal's Wager -- betting on the scenario because the stakes are so large that they swamp the low probability -- while I insisted that what she actually had in mind was Pascal's Mugging, and that treating the two names as interchangeable shorthand for "sketchy reasoning about tiny probabilities" erases a real difference between the arguments.

She thinks I was splitting hairs. On her view, any argument that lets an enormous payoff override an agent's ordinary probability judgments falls under one general and already-answered worry, so that the standard replies to the Wager -- she brought up the many-gods objection, and the broader complaint that expected-value reasoning breaks down once infinite payoffs enter the sums -- dispose of the Mugging as well. I don't think that's right, but by the time we called it a night I couldn't articulate cleanly why the Mugging struck me as a separate, and if anything more stubborn, problem for decision theory.

I would like to get this straight rather than simply win the rematch. Could you compare Pascal's Wager with Bostrom's Pascal's Mugging carefully: where do their argumentative structures genuinely coincide, and where do they come apart? I am especially interested in whether the Mugging depends on anything like the infinite utilities that expose the Wager to objections such as the many-gods problem and the charge of ill-defined expectations, or whether it is built to work with merely finite, if astronomically large, payoffs -- in which case the usual replies to the Wager would leave it untouched. I would also welcome your judgment on whether "Pascal's Mugging" is an apt name for what looks like a fairly general problem about unbounded utility functions, or whether calling it "Pascal's" anything obscures more than it reveals, given that it involves no god, no faith, and none of the specific dilemma Pascal was addressing. I know the Mugging mainly through Bostrom's paper and the discussion surrounding it; my sense of the Wager goes back only to an undergraduate philosophy of religion course, so please don't assume more background on either side than that.
