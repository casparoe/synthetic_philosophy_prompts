Ok so I'm on the board of a small literary nonprofit that runs an annual personal-essay prize, and we've landed ourselves in a mess I can't stop thinking about (also might steal it for a book, don't tell anyone). Two months ago we announced a winner -- an unflinching memoir about growing up with a mother who had severe schizophrenia -- paid out the prize money, and its slotted for the print anthology in six weeks. Now the mother, very much alive, has written to us: she's easily identifiable in their small town, never consented to any of this, and says it'll out her psychiatric history to people who don't currently know. The author refuses to anonymize anything (and honestly, given how small-town and specific it all is, a fake name probably wouldn't cover much anyway), and says she needs the credit for her career -- an agent's already circling. Our contest rules say nothing about consent from people who show up in the essays, so there's no contractual out, and the board's split between 'we made a promise and this is what memoir is' and 'we now know exactly who we'd be hurting and can still stop it.' I keep coming back to that old question of whether real moral dilemmas -- cases where you're doing wrong no matter which way you jump -- actually exist, or wether it's always secretly resolvable, so that whoever loses isn't owed anything once we've made the 'right' call. Can you draft the memo I'd bring to the board: an actual recommendation with a timeline, that takes the losing side seriously instead of just gesturing at it?
