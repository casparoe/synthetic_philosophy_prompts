I'm at work on a novel built largely around hearsay: a family saga in which almost everything the characters believe about one another they've received at second or third hand, culminating in a courtroom subplot where the only evidence anyone has is testimony nobody can independently verify. One of my central characters trained for years in classical Nyāya logic before moving into a Western analytic philosophy doctorate, and I want to write a scene in which he argues with a colleague from that later training over something that has quietly divided them for years: whether hearing something from a reliable person is really its own basic way of knowing things, or only ever a disguised piece of inference.

His Nyāya training commits him to the view that śabda — testimony from a trustworthy speaker, an āpta — is a pramāṇa (a basic source of knowledge) in its own right, standing alongside perception and inference rather than resting on them: once you have understood the sentence and the speaker is genuinely reliable, you know what you have been told as directly as you know a color by seeing it, with no further inferential step required. His colleague, trained in the reductionist line that runs back through Hume, thinks this concedes too much: on her view, whatever justifies the belief is always an implicit inference from "this is testimony of the reliable kind" to "so it is true," and nothing is left over once that inference is subtracted out — which is exactly why we rightly stop believing someone the moment we learn they have lied before, and why a second, independent witness makes a report so much more convincing than the first one alone.

Could you write out their argument as a real exchange, something like eight to twelve turns, in which each has to answer the other's best objection rather than simply restating their own position? I'm especially interested in how the Nyāya side would answer the charge that treating testimony as basic makes belief too cheap, and how the reductionist would answer the objection that small children, and plenty of ordinary adults, clearly gain knowledge from being told things without performing anything a logician would recognize as an inference. I would rather the scene ended without a tidy winner, since that unresolved friction is exactly what I need for the rest of the book.
