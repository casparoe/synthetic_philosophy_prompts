A friend of mine and I spent most of an evening arguing about a decision-theoretic puzzle that turned out to be far less tidy than either of us expected: Gibbard and Harper's "Death in Damascus" case. As we were discussing it, the setup runs roughly like this: a man in Damascus is confronted by Death, who is such a reliable predictor that we may as well treat him as infallible for the purposes of the example. Death has already fixed on a city, Damascus or Aleppo, in which to look for the man tomorrow, and the man dies if and only if he ends up in whichever city Death has settled on. My friend, defending evidential decision theory, argued that whichever city the man currently feels drawn to must be the one Death predicted, so he should switch — except that the moment he settles on switching, the very same reasoning tells him to switch back, so evidential decision theory seems to recommend an endless oscillation rather than a choice. I defended causal decision theory, on which the man should reason from his unconditional credence about Death's already-fixed decision rather than from credence conditional on his own act, but I had to admit that this leaves the two acts perfectly tied, and worse, that neither comes out "ratifiable" in Richard Jeffrey's sense, unlike in Newcomb's original problem, where ratifiability at least picks out two-boxing as the stable option: whichever city the man settles on here, coming to expect that he'll settle on it makes that very city look like the dangerous one. Neither of us could say whether this total absence of a ratifiable act is a distinctive embarrassment for causal decision theory, a problem that any theory faces once a sufficiently good predictor is involved, or something defused by moves like Eells's tickle defense (the idea that a rational agent's introspected inclinations screen off any correlation between act and prediction) or Skyrms's picture of deliberation as a dynamic process that may or may not settle into equilibrium.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between a causal decision theorist and an evidential decision theorist who run into each other the morning after, somewhere in Damascus, and try to work out what the man should actually have done. Please have them engage with each other's strongest arguments rather than talk past one another: the evidential theorist should press the causal theorist on whether "there is no ratifiable act" is a tolerable thing for a normative theory to say, and the causal theorist should press back on whether "keep switching forever" amounts to genuine practical advice or is simply a symptom that something has gone wrong with evidential decision theory. I'd also like at least one of them to bring the tickle defense or Skyrms-style deliberational dynamics into the exchange, with the other responding to it substantively rather than waving it away. I don't want either character to simply win by default — I'm more interested in seeing exactly where the disagreement becomes genuinely hard to adjudicate than in a dialogue that quietly hands the victory to one side.
