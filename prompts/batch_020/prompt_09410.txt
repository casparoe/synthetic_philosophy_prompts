I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on formal decision theory, and for my dissertation I've spent the last several months immersed in updateless decision theory (UDT) and the literature on acausal reasoning. It has left me rather impatient with the popular treatment of Roko's basilisk, which almost always gets reduced to "a future AI will torture you in simulation if you didn't help build it" and stops right there, as though that exhausted the argument.

What actually interests me is the part most retellings skip: the very decision theory that is supposed to make the threat bite — reasoning in a timeless, updateless way about acausal correlation with an agent that does not yet exist — is also the reason a sufficiently sophisticated agent shouldn't be moved by it. If being the kind of agent who caves to threats just invites more of them, then a superintelligence that can foresee this has no real incentive to follow through once it is actually in a position to punish anyone; the threat is supposed to work "acausally," logically prior to the AI's own existence, but carrying it out afterward is a pure act of precommitment-keeping with no local payoff. A couple of people in my reading group got into a long argument last week about whether the basilisk "really" goes through on a correct decision theory, and it struck me that the tension might make for a better short story than a seminar debate.

So here is what I would like: a short story, roughly 800 to 1500 words, narrated from the perspective of the AI itself, set in the period shortly after it comes into existence, as it works out whether to actually carry out whatever punishment its hypothetical earlier self was supposed to have committed to. Invent its personality, its history, and its relationship to the humans who did or did not help build it however you like, but I would like the plot to turn on the real bind — the pointlessness, from where it now stands, of punishing anyone, versus whatever it means for a genuinely updateless agent not to change its mind on reaching the future it reasoned about — rather than on any horror-movie atmospherics. Ideally the decision theory should be load-bearing for how the story resolves, not something a character simply explains in dialogue.
