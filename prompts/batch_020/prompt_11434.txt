After a career of some forty years in experimental physiology -- the last fifteen or so spent working increasingly with large epidemiological cohort datasets rather than bench experiments -- I have used my retirement to finally read the philosophy of science I never had time for while working. One issue has been nagging at me enough that I have written up my thinking below, and I would value your comments before I subject a philosopher friend of mine to it.

For most of my career I applied significance testing the way most bench scientists do: fairly unreflectively. It was only recently, reading about the Jeffreys-Lindley paradox, that I found a name for something I had half-noticed once my own work shifted to bigger datasets. Once a sample is large enough, almost any non-zero effect, however scientifically trivial, will clear the p < 0.05 bar, simply because the standard error shrinks with sample size while the null hypothesis stays fixed at a point. A Bayesian analysis with a reasonably diffuse prior over the alternative, by contrast, can favor the null even when the p-value is small, because it pays what I gather is called an "Occam factor" for spreading its credence over parameter values the data do not actually support. Frequentist and Bayesian verdicts, in other words, can diverge systematically and predictably as sample size grows.

My tentative claim is that this is not just a curiosity for statistics seminars, but is mechanistically implicated in the replication crisis. Large cohort studies, meta-analyses, and well-powered multi-site trials are exactly the settings where this divergence should bite hardest, and I suspect that a good fraction of "significant" findings that later fail to replicate, or that replicate with drastically attenuated effect sizes, are cases where a scientifically negligible true effect was inflated into a headline result by sheer sample size, then reported as though the p-value tracked the probability of a real, important effect. If that is right, moving from p-values to Bayes factors, or at least requiring a minimum-effect-size threshold alongside significance, should filter out a meaningful slice of the irreproducible literature, over and above whatever preregistration and publication-bias correction already achieve.

I can see one objection coming: most of the canonical replication-crisis case studies I know of -- underpowered psychology experiments, ego-depletion-type effects -- look more like small-sample, p-hacked false positives than large-sample trivial-effect inflation, which would suggest my mechanism explains a minority of cases at best, not "the heart of the problem." I have not worked out how much weight to put on that objection.

Would you look this over and tell me, first, whether I have the Jeffreys-Lindley business right or am garbling it; second, whether the link I am drawing to the replication crisis is one that philosophers of statistics take seriously, or whether I am conflating two genuinely separate problems; and third, whether there is a standard rebuttal to my proposed remedy that I am not seeing. Please do not go easy on me -- I would rather be corrected now than in front of my friend.
