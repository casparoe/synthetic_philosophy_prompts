I'm a second-year PhD student working on mathematical reasoning in large language models, and a conversation at last week's lab meeting has left me more unsettled than I expected. A labmate brought up DeepMind's AlphaProof and AlphaGeometry 2 systems, which reached silver-medal standard at the 2024 International Mathematical Olympiad by producing formal proofs that a verifier could check step by step. My advisor, who trained as a mathematical logician before moving into machine learning, responded by walking us through the argument, originating with J. R. Lucas and later sharpened by Roger Penrose in The Emperor's New Mind and Shadows of the Mind, that no such formal system could ever fully capture human mathematical understanding. As she laid it out: any computer program is equivalent to some formal axiomatic system, and Gödel's incompleteness theorems guarantee that for any such system, provided it is consistent and strong enough to encode arithmetic, there is a sentence the system cannot prove even though we, reasoning about the system from the outside, can see that it is true. Since this move can apparently be repeated against whatever formal system is proposed as a model of a given machine's reasoning, she concluded that human mathematicians must have some insight into mathematical truth that no formal system, and therefore no computer, could ever fully replicate — and since the underlying incompleteness theorems are beyond dispute, she took the anti-mechanist conclusion to be just as rigorously established.

This isn't a purely abstract worry for me, since my dissertation proposal takes for granted that scaling up formal, algorithmic reasoning systems could in principle close the remaining gap with human mathematical creativity. When I raised this afterward with two more senior students, both dismissed the Lucas-Penrose argument as "a famous mistake" that philosophers settled long ago, but neither could tell me exactly where the reasoning fails, and I'd rather understand the issue properly before bringing it up with my advisor again. Given that Gödel's theorems themselves are not in question, how much weight should I really put on this as a mathematical case against machine reasoning ever equaling human mathematical insight, and where, precisely, does the inference from the theorems to that conclusion break down?
