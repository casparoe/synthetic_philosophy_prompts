I'm a software engineer, and next month I'm giving the toast at the retirement dinner for Renata, the most senior engineer on my team, who has been at the company for nineteen years and is leaving to spend her time on what she calls "toy projects nobody asked for." Renata's husband, Tom, taught philosophy before he retired, and years ago he got her reading Moritz Schlick — a founding figure of the Vienna Circle, I gather, though I only know this secondhand — in particular an essay called "On the Meaning of Life." As Renata once explained it to me during a late-night deploy (I have never read the essay myself), Schlick's argument is that almost everything we call "purposeful" activity only borrows its value from some further goal down the line, and that this chain of means and ends never actually bottoms out in anything valuable for its own sake; the only moments of real, self-contained meaning, on his view, are the ones that are their own point, which he calls play, with a child absorbed in a game as the clearest case of it. Renata has spent something like two decades quietly trying to write code the way that child plays, and she has said, more than once and not entirely as a joke, that if anyone at her dinner stands up and praises her "impact," her "legacy," or the systems she "shipped," she will get up and leave the room — because that framing is precisely the mistake Schlick is diagnosing.

This leaves me with a genuine problem. Almost everyone at the dinner is an engineer who has only ever known Renata through the systems she built and will expect, and enjoy, exactly that kind of toast. Tom will also be there, and he will know immediately whether I have actually understood Schlick's point or am just using "play" as a nicer-sounding synonym for "she loved her job." Could you write me the toast — about four or five minutes long — that takes Schlick's actual reasoning about why purpose-chasing is hollow seriously enough to satisfy Tom, uses it to say something specific and affectionate about Renata rather than generic praise, and still works as a toast, warm and funny in places, for a room of engineers who have never heard of Moritz Schlick and shouldn't need to in order to enjoy it?
