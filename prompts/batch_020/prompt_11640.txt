I'm taking an upper-level seminar on formal epistemology this term, and this week we spent two sessions on Bas van Fraassen's Reflection Principle. As I understand it, the principle says that a rational agent's current credence in some proposition A, conditional on the supposition that her credence in A at a later time will equal some specific value r, should itself equal r -- effectively treating one's own anticipated future opinion the way one would treat a recognized expert's testimony, provided there's no specific reason to distrust the rationality of that future self. Van Fraassen motivates this with a diachronic Dutch book argument, but our professor spent most of one class on the standard objections: cases where an agent knows in advance that her future credence will shift for reasons that have nothing to do with rational updating on evidence -- memory loss, emotional manipulation, indoctrination, or ordinary cognitive decline. In those cases, Reflection seems to recommend deferring to a future self who, by the agent's own current lights, is likely to be less reliable rather than more.

After class, a few of us got into an argument about how much practical weight any of this carries outside of formal epistemology. One classmate, who is also taking a bioethics elective, brought up psychiatric advance directives -- sometimes called "Ulysses contracts" -- in which a person currently in a stable, competent state pre-authorizes treatment for a future manic or psychotic episode, explicitly instructing doctors to override whatever her future self says in the moment. Another countered with a much lower-stakes case: he's about to spend the summer canvassing for a political campaign known for its high-pressure, insular culture, and half-joked that he wasn't sure whether he should trust his own post-summer opinions of the candidate more or less than his current ones. We went back and forth without resolving whether the Reflection Principle and its standard exceptions actually give useful guidance in cases like these, or whether the principle is too idealized -- built for agents who only ever change their minds through rational updating -- to say much about belief change driven by persuasion, coercion, or psychological drift.

Could you lay out what you see as the most important implications of the Reflection Principle, including both the Dutch book rationale behind it and the recognized exceptions to it, for how much epistemic authority someone should grant to their own anticipated future opinions in situations like these, where there's real reason to suspect that the coming shift in belief won't be a matter of rational updating at all?
