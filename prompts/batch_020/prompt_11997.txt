I volunteer on the grants committee of a small community foundation, and we are divided over this year's $500,000 discretionary 'innovation' fund. Proposal A, a maternal-health program, is about 95 percent likely to avert roughly 250 deaths. Proposal B, a one-off advocacy campaign for an air-quality regulation abroad, has a 10 percent chance of averting about 4,000 deaths and a 90 percent chance of accomplishing nothing. B wins on expected value (400 versus 250), and three of the five of us want to fund it for that reason alone. I have no formal background in economics or philosophy, only what I have puzzled out on my own, and the most I can say is that a 90 percent chance of total failure ought to count for something beyond expected value — especially since we will not get to repeat this choice until the odds average out. Please draft the memo I will circulate before our vote: recommend a specific split between A and B, suggest a rule the committee could apply to similar choices later, and take the expected-value members' reasoning seriously rather than dismissing our hesitation as mere bias.
