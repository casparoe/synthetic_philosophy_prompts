I work as a backend engineer on the risk team at a small consumer-lending startup, and last month I sat in on a model-review meeting that turned into a genuine argument between two of our data scientists, Grace and Raf, about whether our underwriting model is "fair." Some background: the model outputs a predicted default probability that determines whether an applicant is auto-approved, sent to manual review, or offered a worse rate. An internal audit split applicants into two cohorts (compliance insisted the writeup just call them Group A and Group B rather than naming the actual demographic split) and found two things: the score is well-calibrated within each group — applicants rated a 15% default risk default about 15% of the time whether they're in Group A or Group B — but among applicants who go on to repay in full, Group A members get flagged high-risk, and bumped to manual review or a worse rate, considerably more often than equivalent Group B members. Group A also has a somewhat higher overall default rate in the historical data than Group B.

The argument went roughly like this. (1) Grace said the model is fine, because calibration is the right fairness bar: the score means the same thing regardless of group, so nobody is being lied to. (2) Raf said that's compatible with exactly the harm applicants experience — Group A borrowers who would have repaid in full get the extra scrutiny far more often — and calibration is silent on that. (3) Grace granted the false-positive gap but said it isn't a modeling choice, it's forced: given the base-rate difference, you can't have calibration plus equal false-positive and false-negative rates across both groups at once except in degenerate cases, something she half-remembered from a fairness training as an actual impossibility theorem, not a matter of opinion. (4) Raf said "forced by the base rates" is doing too much unexamined work, since the base-rate gap is itself a fact about a historical outcome variable shaped by things like who quietly got offered a hardship extension versus got coded as delinquent — so calling the resulting error-rate gap mathematically inevitable launders a historical pattern through a theorem. (5) Grace replied that even granting the base rate is partly an artifact, that doesn't tell you which error rate to equalize instead, and the obvious fix — different score thresholds per group — means explicitly sorting people by group membership, which she thought was closer to what fair-lending law exists to forbid than to require. (6) Raf said that conflates two different objections — "equalizing false-positive rates isn't obviously the right target" and "doing it this way might be illegal or impractical" — and that Grace had quietly retreated from the first to the second without acknowledging it.

Neither of them budged, and I don't have the statistics or philosophy background to referee this myself. Then close with your own overall verdict: is there a defensible sense in which this model simply is or isn't "fair," or does the impossibility result mean that question has no real answer until someone supplies a value judgment first?
