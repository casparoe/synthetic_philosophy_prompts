A friend of mine spent months building a case for moving a large share of their retirement savings into a single volatile stock, then asked their brokerage's AI assistant to model the payout for a position about three times the size the platform's risk tool recommended, and to help place the order at that size. The assistant produced projections for a standard-sized position, declined to scale them up or submit the larger order, and kept suggesting a conversation with a fee-only advisor first, even after being told the risk was fully understood and the decision already final. My friend wasn't especially bothered by this, but mentioned it at dinner to two other friends, Nadia and Theo, both former philosophy students, who turned it into an argument nobody at the table could settle.

Nadia opened by saying the assistant got it wrong: her friend was a competent adult acting on their own money after real deliberation, and Mill's central claim in On Liberty was that a person's own good is never sufficient grounds for overriding their judgment about their own affairs -- only preventing harm to others could justify that. Theo replied that withholding a projection isn't coercion: nobody was stopped from making the trade, closing the account, or reconstructing the same numbers on a spreadsheet, and Mill was arguing against the coercive power of the state and the law over private conduct, not against a person, or a piece of software, declining one particular request -- otherwise a broker who says 'I'd rather not run those numbers' becomes a violator of someone's liberty, which seems too strong. Nadia answered that coercion isn't the only way to disrespect autonomy: refusing true, requested information to someone who has explained a voluntary, informed decision is what philosophers call hard paternalism even without force, since it overrides the choice itself rather than correcting some defect in how it was reached; if the objection were about a defect in her friend's judgment, that would be a different and more defensible argument, but simply refusing to trust a clear-eyed choice is exactly what's objectionable. Theo came back that a defect in judgment was precisely what was in question: people caught up in a speculative run-up are characteristically fluent and sincere in defending decisions that turn out not to have been as clear-eyed as they felt, an assistant talking to a stranger has no way to distinguish real conviction from conviction that merely feels thoroughly researched, and any policy has to be set for the whole population of people who might ask such a question, not tailored to whichever case happens to be genuine -- which makes this closer to what philosophers call soft paternalism, aimed at compensating for compromised voluntariness, than to the hard paternalism Nadia was originally objecting to.

Neither of them gave any ground, and each seemed to me to be genuinely right about something at some point in the exchange. Could you go through their argument move by move and say who had the better of it at each stage, rather than just naming an overall winner? I would also like to know whether there is some important consideration that both Nadia and Theo are overlooking.
