After forty years as an experimental physicist, I have spent much of my retirement reading through the history of philosophy, and by way of Pascal's work on hydrostatics and the arithmetical triangle I eventually arrived at the Pensées, a text I find by turns dazzling and exasperating. These days I sit in, mostly as an observer, on an upper-level undergraduate seminar on early modern political thought at the local university; its instructor is short-staffed this term and has asked a few of us auditors with some background in formal argument to help mark a batch of short take-home responses. I am glad to help, but since I have no formal training in philosophy myself, I would like an independent, careful assessment of one particular answer before I settle on my own mark, so that I have something to compare my impressions against.

The question set to the students was: "Explain Pascal's account of the relationship between justice and force in the Pensées. Your answer should address (a) why Pascal thinks force, rather than justice, is what political communities actually recognize and obey; (b) how this claim connects to his broader account of human reason after the Fall; and (c) whether Pascal's position amounts to a form of relativism about justice, or to something else. You need not cite fragment numbers, but you should engage with specific material from the text."

Here is the student's answer in full: "Pascal's starting point is his famous observation that justice without force is powerless, while force without justice is tyrannical, and that since people have never managed to make what is just strong, they have instead made what is strong be called just. On his view this is not mere cynicism: it follows from his account of fallen reason. Because concupiscence corrupts human judgment, especially wherever self-interest is at stake, people cannot converge on a shared, rational picture of what justice actually requires — everyone's sense of justice is bent by their own self-love. Force, by contrast, is a plain fact that nobody seriously disputes: everyone can see who holds the army and the throne. So political order stabilizes around force and the customs it protects, rather than around any rational consensus about justice. Pascal reinforces this with the Montaignean observation that laws vary arbitrarily from one place to the next, so that the same act counts as a crime on one side of a border and is lawful on the other — evidence that positive law tracks local custom rather than any single objective standard. This is also why Pascal is not really offering his own version of Hobbes's social contract: for Hobbes, sovereignty is authorized by an act of collective rational agreement, whereas for Pascal there is no such rational agreement to be had, only the historical fact of force and habit hardening into custom. Taken together, these points commit Pascal to a fairly thoroughgoing relativism about justice: since no one can reason their way to a justice that is not simply their own interest in disguise, and since actual legal practice everywhere reflects custom rather than any universal standard, 'justice' just names whatever a given community's force and custom happen to sanction. This is also, I think, the point of Pascal's idea of the 'reason of effects' (raison des effets): the wise response to this situation, on his view, is to explain the true, force-based origin of law and rank to ordinary people, rather than let them go on believing, wrongly, that the great deserve their status by nature — a public that can see the real reason behind the effect is better placed to consent to the arrangement freely."
