Bradley's regress is usually introduced as a problem about relations in general: if a relation R is needed to unite two terms a and b into a single fact, it looks as though some further relation is needed to unite R with a and with b, and so on without end. The same regress resurfaces in sharper form once the question becomes what makes a proposition -- say, that Desdemona loves Cassio -- a genuine unity with a truth value, rather than a mere list or set of its constituents, {Desdemona, loving, Cassio}, which represents nothing and is neither true nor false. Historically, the standard ways of buying this unity have come at a cost. Frege's appeal to the "unsaturatedness" of concepts explains why a concept and an object combine into a unity instead of sitting side by side unconnected, but it also generates the notorious puzzle that the phrase "the concept horse," by Frege's own lights, cannot denote a concept. Russell's insistence that a relation must occur "relatingly," and not merely as one more term, together with his later multiple relation theory of judgment, ran into Wittgenstein's objection that the theory cannot explain why "Othello judges that Desdemona loves Cassio" is a different judgment from "Othello judges that Cassio loves Desdemona," given that both involve exactly the same objects and relation.

A reading group I'm part of has spent the last few sessions on more recent attempts to solve this problem, and it's left us divided on how much progress has actually been made. Write an essay that stakes out and defends a position on the following question: does Bradley's regress show that no account of propositions as structured, constituent-based entities can explain their own unity, or can the regress be defused? In building your answer, engage specifically with at least one contemporary "act-based" theory of propositions -- of the kind associated with Scott Soames and Peter Hanks, on which a proposition's unity derives from the performance of a predicative or cognitive act rather than from any further relation among its constituents -- and with at least one rival proposal, such as Jeffrey King's identification of propositions with facts about the semantic relations that obtain among the parts of an interpreted sentence in a context. For each theory you discuss, assess whether it genuinely dissolves Bradley's regress or merely relocates it -- for instance, into an unexplained unity between an act and its content, or between a context and the fact it is supposed to constitute. Reach a definite verdict rather than concluding that the matter is simply unsettled. A qualified verdict is acceptable (for instance, that the regress sinks constituent theories generally but that act-based theories escape it, or the reverse), but it must be argued for rather than merely asserted, and the essay should directly address the strongest reason to prefer the opposite verdict.
