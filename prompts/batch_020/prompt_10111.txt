I'm working on a long-form feature about the philosophical underpinnings of debates over redistribution and social insurance, and I've become stuck on a puzzle that popular treatments of "the veil of ignorance" tend to paper over. John Rawls famously argues that parties choosing principles of justice from behind a veil of ignorance, not knowing which position in society they will occupy, would reason according to the maximin rule and would therefore settle on the difference principle, permitting social and economic inequalities only when they work to the benefit of the least advantaged. John Harsanyi, however, constructs a formally similar thought experiment, his "impartial observer" argument, in which a person behind the veil is assumed to face an ordinary problem of choice under uncertainty, assigning equal probability to becoming each member of society and maximizing expected utility, and from this he derives something close to average utilitarianism. He went on to argue directly, in a 1975 essay pointedly titled "Can the Maximin Principle Serve as a Basis for Morality?", that Rawls's maximin rule is simply not a rational way to choose among gambles once probabilities are available.

I'd like to understand, and eventually explain to readers, how essentially the same imaginative device can be made to yield such different theories of distributive justice. Could you compare Rawls's route to the difference principle with Harsanyi's route to utilitarianism, focusing on the specific assumptions about rational choice under uncertainty, such as whether determinate probabilities are available behind the veil, how risk-averse a rational chooser should be, and what Rawls calls the "strains of commitment" involved in actually living under principles agreed to in advance, that pull the two thinkers apart despite their shared starting point? I would also welcome your own judgment: whose account of reasoning behind the veil do you find more defensible, and does the fact that the same device can be steered toward such opposed conclusions suggest that the veil of ignorance is too indeterminate to do the foundational work Rawls wants it to do?
