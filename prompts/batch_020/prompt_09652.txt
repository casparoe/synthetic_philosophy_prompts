A friend of mine and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about Benjamin Franklin's "art of virtue" project from the Autobiography, and neither of us managed to convince the other. As you'll recall, Franklin drew up a list of thirteen virtues, running from Temperance and Silence through Order, Resolution, and Industry, and ending with Humility, which he tells us he added only after a Quaker acquaintance informed him that he was generally reputed proud. Each virtue received a short precept, and Franklin tracked his daily lapses in a little ruled book: a grid with the days of the week across the top and the thirteen virtues down the side, working through one virtue at a time so as to complete the whole course four times a year. He is disarmingly honest that the project never delivered the perfection he was after, comparing his own shortfall to a man who wants his axe polished all over but gives up partway through turning the grindstone and settles for one that is merely speckled - and yet he insists the attempt still left him a better and happier man than he would otherwise have been.

The argument started when my friend claimed that this whole scheme is really just a primitive habit-tracker, so naturally Franklin, if transported to the present, would be an enthusiastic adopter of "nudge" techniques - the choice-architecture, smart-defaults, friction-reducing interventions that Thaler and Sunstein popularized under the banner of "libertarian paternalism" - since they achieve the same behavioral results while sparing people the wear and tear of willpower. I objected that this can't be the whole story, since Franklin's own precept for the virtue of Order ("let all your things have their places; let each part of your business have its time") is already a piece of self-imposed choice architecture, and yet he keeps it firmly inside a system whose other twelve virtues locate the moral point of the exercise in deliberate, effortful self-governance rather than in re-engineering one's surroundings so that good behavior costs nothing to sustain. If a nudge produces identical outward conduct without any inward struggle, it isn't obvious that anything Franklin himself would count as progress toward virtue has actually occurred.

I would like your help settling this. Reasoning as much as possible from what Franklin actually wrote about the project, rather than from his general reputation as an apostle of self-improvement, what do you think his considered verdict on nudges and choice architecture would be? I would rather you commit to a specific answer than simply lay out considerations on both sides, and in doing so, please address whether his own treatment of the virtue of Order, together with his willingness to settle for a "speckled" result instead of genuine perfection, tells for or against the idea that he would have welcomed offloading self-control onto a designed environment.
