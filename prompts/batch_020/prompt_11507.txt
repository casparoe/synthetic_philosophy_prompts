Last weekend a friend and I spent nearly two hours arguing about her bathroom scale, of all things. She had stepped on it three mornings running, cross-checked each reading against how loosely or tightly her jeans fit that day, found that the two always agreed, and concluded the scale must be accurate. When I objected that "how my jeans fit" isn't really an independent check on a scale, since that method has never itself been calibrated against anything outside the very impression she was trying to verify, she accused me of being stubborn about what "independent" even means. The argument escalated once I realized this is more or less the structure of what epistemologists call the bootstrapping problem, or, in Stewart Cohen's phrase, the problem of easy knowledge: with an ordinary car gas gauge, you can look at the needle, infer the tank is full, separately note that the needle reads "full," log the two as agreeing, and repeat this over enough trips to apparently earn inductive support for the claim that the gauge is reliable — without ever stepping outside the gauge's own deliverances to check it against anything else. I pointed out that the same recipe would seem to certify a gauge that is simply stuck on "full," as long as you never happen to let the tank run low enough to catch it out. My friend answered that demanding some further, source-external check before you're entitled to trust a basic faculty like perception or memory just generates a regress or lands you in skepticism, and that she'd rather side with something like Crispin Wright's idea that we are simply entitled, without earning it through any inference, to certain "cornerstone" assumptions. I could not decide whether that was a genuine answer to the asymmetry I wanted explained, or just a relabeling of it.

We never settled it, so I would like you to write the dialogue we should have had instead. Set it between two epistemologists, distinguished by position rather than name: one who defends the legitimacy of bootstrapping, drawing on something in the spirit of Wright's entitlement move or a dogmatist line closer to James Pryor's, to explain why no antecedent, source-external check on reliability should be required before a basic source's outputs can accumulate into justified belief that the source itself is reliable; and one who holds that the gas-gauge pattern shows that basic knowledge structures generate illegitimate "easy knowledge," and that some genuine independence requirement on evidence is missing, even if it's hard to state precisely. Neither should have the easier end of the argument: have the skeptic press the bootstrapper on how the view is supposed to distinguish the stuck gauge from the genuinely reliable one, given that both would pass the bootstrap test identically from the inside, and have the bootstrapper press back on whether "independent check" can be spelled out at all without either collapsing into wholesale skepticism about every basic source or quietly presupposing the very reliability that's in question. Somewhere in the exchange, I would like the bootstrapper to be pushed on whether the same trouble reappears for an ordinary Bayesian who conditionalizes on a source's apparent track record without ever calibrating it against anything external — that's the point at which I suspect my friend's confidence would actually start to waver, and I want to see whether I'm right about that. Let the dialogue end at a genuine impasse rather than handing either side a clean win.
