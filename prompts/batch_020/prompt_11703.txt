I'm a PhD student in epistemology, currently working through Timothy Williamson's discussion of self-knowledge for my qualifying exam reading list. I've spent the last couple of weeks going carefully through the anti-luminosity argument in *Knowledge and Its Limits*, and I think I understand its basic mechanics: given that our discriminatory powers are never infinitely fine-grained, no non-trivial condition — something like feeling cold, or being in some perceptual state that shifts gradually across a run of borderline cases — can be "luminous" in Williamson's technical sense, i.e., such that whenever the condition obtains, the subject is thereby in a position to know that it obtains. Where I keep getting tripped up is exactly how this general result is supposed to bear on the KK principle specifically, the thesis that whenever one knows that p, one also knows that one knows that p.

A few of us got into an argument about this after our reading group last week. One person claimed that the failure of KK is really nothing more than a restatement of the anti-luminosity argument once you plug in "knowing that p" for the relevant condition, so that refuting luminosity in general automatically refutes KK; someone else pointed out that luminosity is officially defined in terms of merely being "in a position to know," which is weaker than actually knowing, and wondered whether that gap leaves room for KK and luminosity to come apart — for instance, whether one could deny luminosity quite generally while still salvaging some restricted version of KK, or the reverse. Nobody at the table could settle it convincingly on the spot.

Could you compare the luminosity condition, as Williamson defines and then argues against it, with the KK principle? I'm especially interested in the precise logical relationship between the two: is KK best understood as a special instance of the more general luminosity framework, and if so, is it the stronger or the weaker claim of the two; does the failure of luminosity for the state of knowing suffice on its own to establish the failure of KK; and is there any coherent position on which the two theses could diverge in truth-value? Please justify your answer rather than simply asserting the relationship.
