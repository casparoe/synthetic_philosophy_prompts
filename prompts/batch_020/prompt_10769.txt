Over the past couple of months I've been taking an evening course on the history of decision theory, where Frank Ramsey keeps turning up as the source of half the field's foundations: the Ramsey–de Finetti treatment of subjective probability, the Ramsey model of optimal saving, and so on. Almost as an aside, our instructor mentioned that Ramsey once gave an after-dinner talk to his Cambridge secret society, the Apostles, in February 1925, in which he laid out his personal philosophy of life at the age of twenty-two — not quite five years before he died unexpectedly, at twenty-six, following complications from an operation for jaundice. That detail landed oddly with me, since I turned forty myself last month and have been in an uncharacteristically reflective mood about mortality and scale ever since. I tracked down the talk, usually printed as the closing section ("Epilogue") of Ramsey's *Foundations of Mathematics* (1931) and reprinted in D. H. Mellor's edition of the *Philosophical Papers* (1990). I've now reread it more times than I can count, and while the tone is bracing, I can't quite reconstruct the argument, if there is one, as opposed to just a mood or a piece of temperament. Here it is, as I have it:

"Where I seem to differ from some of my friends is in attaching little importance to physical size. I don't feel the least humble before the vastness of the heavens. The stars may be large, but they cannot think or love; and these are qualities which impress me far more than size does. I take no credit for weighing nearly seventeen stone.

My picture of the world is drawn in perspective, and not like a model to scale. The foreground is occupied by human beings and the stars are all as small as threepenny bits. I don't really believe in astronomy, except as a complicated description of part of the course of human and possibly animal sensation. I apply my perspective not merely to space but also to time. In time the world will cool and everything will die; but that is a long time off still, and its present value at compound discount is almost nothing.

Nor is the present less valuable because the future will be blank. Humanity, which fills the foreground of my picture, I find interesting and on the whole admirable. I find, just now at least, the world a pleasant and exciting place. You may find it depressing; I am sorry for you, and you despise me. But I have reason and you have none; you would only have a reason for despising me if your feeling corresponded to the fact in a way mine didn't. But neither can correspond to the fact. The fact is not in itself good or bad; it is just that it thrills me but depresses you. On the other hand, I pity you with reason, because it is pleasanter to be thrilled than to be depressed, and not merely pleasanter but better for all one's activities."

Three aspects of this have resisted my own attempts at interpretation. First, the "present value at compound discount" line: is Ramsey just borrowing a vivid phrase from his own economics for rhetorical color, or is he making a substantive claim — one that would genuinely license discounting the heat death of the universe the same way we discount a distant financial liability? Second, the final paragraph seems to want it both ways: he claims to have "reason" on his side while his gloomier interlocutor has "none," yet in almost the same breath says the underlying fact is "not in itself good or bad," which sounds like it should make both attitudes equally reasonable, or equally arbitrary. Is there a consistent position underneath this — something like an early emotivism about value bolted onto a purely instrumental, non-evaluative sense of "reason" — or is Ramsey just not being careful? Third, and more broadly: does this "perspectival" picture actually rebut someone in the grip of Pascal-style dread at the silence of infinite spaces, or does it just relocate the felt problem without resolving it? I'd be grateful for a close reading of the text itself, rather than a general survey of Ramsey's biography or reputation, both of which I've picked up plenty of by now.
