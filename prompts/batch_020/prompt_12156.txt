I'm taking a seminar this term on the philosophy of evolutionary biology, and we recently spent a week on Gould and Lewontin's 1979 paper "The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm" before moving to what our professor called its most notorious application: the evolutionary status of the female orgasm. We read Gould's 1987 Natural History column on the subject (later reprinted as "Male Nipples and Clitoral Ripples"), where he extends the spandrel logic to argue that female orgasm, like the male nipple, is a developmental side effect of a trait that does real work in the other sex -- in this case, riding along on the anatomical and neural machinery that makes male orgasm, and hence ejaculation, reproductively indispensable. Alongside this we read excerpts from Elisabeth Lloyd's survey of the roughly twenty rival adaptationist hypotheses on offer -- including the "upsuck" account of orgasm-timed sperm retention and the fluctuating-asymmetry studies linking orgasm frequency to a partner's bodily symmetry -- in which she concludes that none of them clears a reasonable evidentiary bar, and that the byproduct account should stand as the default until one does.

What's been nagging at me since that class is whether this is really a disagreement about evidence, or a disagreement about where the burden of proof should sit by default for a trait like this. Could you write a dialogue between two evolutionary biologists who are equally sharp and genuinely responsive to one another -- one defending a specific adaptationist account of the female orgasm, the other defending the developmental byproduct view -- rather than one side simply listing hypotheses and the other knocking them down? I'd like the adaptationist to press the byproduct theorist on why a shared embryological origin should count as evidence against later selection having refined or repurposed the trait, and I'd like the byproduct theorist to push back by asking what finding could possibly count against that move, since "selection got to it afterward" starts to look unfalsifiable in exactly the way Gould and Lewontin accused adaptationist storytelling of being. If it fits naturally, I'd also like the two of them to surface that irony explicitly: that the byproduct camp, in trying to avoid just-so stories, may be leaning on an unfalsifiable move of its own.
