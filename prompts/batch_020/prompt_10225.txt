I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist and have only taken up philosophy seriously since retiring, so I would value a more practiced eye on an argument I witnessed between two old friends from graduate school, Marek (still a physicist) and Odile (now a biostatistician), over dinner last month.

It began when Odile mentioned that her hospital's imaging software reports things such as "this mass has a 34% probability of being malignant." (1) Marek called that meaningless: probability, at bottom, is a long-run relative frequency, and one mass in one patient is not a repeatable trial, so the 34% is either a disguised claim about some reference class of similar cases (which one, exactly?) or not really a probability at all. (2) Odile replied that this proves too much, since physics is full of respectable single-case probabilities: a given nucleus has a genuine propensity to decay within the hour, and that propensity belongs to that nucleus, not to some class of similar nuclei chosen after the fact. (3) Marek granted this for genuinely indeterministic systems, but maintained that the tumor is already determinately malignant or not, so the 34% cannot be a propensity of anything — it is Odile's ignorance dressed up as a number, categorically unlike the nucleus's propensity even though the arithmetic looks identical. (4) Odile answered that this is no cost at all: "probability" legitimately names more than one thing, physical propensity in one case and rational credence in the other, and philosophers of probability have long made peace with exactly this kind of pluralism. (5) Marek's closing point was that both numbers obey the same axioms, are conditionalized in the same way, and license the same bets, so it would be a remarkable coincidence for two supposedly unrelated concepts to be mathematically indistinguishable in every respect that matters for action.
