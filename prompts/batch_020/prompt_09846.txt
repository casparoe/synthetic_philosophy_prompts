I'm a PhD student working mainly on modal metaphysics, though this term I've been auditing a seminar on philosophical theology that spent two sessions on Gödel's ontological argument, and the discussion has stayed with me since. By the second session everyone in the room had accepted that the proof is formally valid given its axioms, so the conversation turned almost entirely to Jordan Howard Sobel's modal collapse objection: the result, derivable from the very axioms of Dana Scott's version of Gödel's notes, that every actually true proposition turns out to be necessarily true. Nobody wanted a proof of God's existence to come bundled with the death of contingency, so we moved on to the emendations designed to block that specific derivation, above all C. Anthony Anderson's 1990 revision of the positive-property axioms, which our instructor presented, a little too quickly for my taste, as having restored the argument to something close to its original standing, especially now that the underlying logic has been checked as valid in automated proof assistants once the axioms are patched.

What I'd like from you is not the generic verdict that Gödel's proof fails; I already know, and don't need rehearsed, the standard complaint that any modal ontological argument just smuggles its conclusion into a possibility premise. I want you to argue for a narrower and, I think, more interesting claim: that blocking Sobel's derivation of full-blown modal collapse, whether through Anderson's route or some comparable patch, does not restore whatever dialectical force the argument might once have had against a reasonable non-theist. My suspicion is that the very features of the system that generate the collapse in the first place -- closing positive properties under strict implication, and then requiring that all positive properties be jointly compossible in a single individual -- are exactly the features doing the real work of turning mere possible God-likeness into necessary God-likeness, so that any axiom set strong enough to reach the conclusion Gödel wants will always be exactly strong enough to threaten collapse somewhere else, if not in Sobel's precise form then in some structurally similar one. Please build the case in a way that actually engages the machinery of the proof -- the positive-property axioms, the definition of essence, and the role of the S5 axiom -- rather than resting on the possibility-premise complaint alone.
