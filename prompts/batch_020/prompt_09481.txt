I'm a junior, and in my ceramics elective this semester, we're allowed to reclaim clay from any piece we've decided not to keep by squashing it back into a plain lump so it can be reused for something else. Last week this turned into a genuine argument between two of my friends. Owen had spent most of the period shaping a small clay bust of his dog, decided partway through that it wasn't working, and squashed it back into a ball so he could start over. Nadia, working at the next table, said — half-joking at first — that he'd just killed his sculpture, and when he brushed this off she kept pressing until it turned into a real disagreement. I was glazing a bowl at the table between them and ended up hearing the whole thing.

It went roughly like this. (1) Owen said nothing had actually been destroyed, since the lump he ended up with is made of exactly the same clay the bust was made of; the "bust" and the "lump," he said, were never two different things to begin with, just one piece of clay described two different ways at two different times. (2) Nadia said that can't be right, because the lump could have survived being squashed flat and the bust couldn't have: squash a lump and you still have a lump, but squash a bust and there is no bust left at all. Since the bust and the lump don't share that property, she said, they can't be one and the same object by Leibniz's Law — there must have been two coinciding things sitting on that table the whole time. (3) Owen replied that this was circular: right up until he actually squashed it, the bust and the lump were indistinguishable in every observable respect, and "can survive being squashed" wasn't a property either one of them actually had yet, only a fact about what would happen — Nadia was assuming they were different kinds of things in order to conclude that they were different kinds of things. (4) Nadia answered that dispositions don't need to be manifested to be real: a wineglass is fragile long before anyone drops it, and nobody calls that circular reasoning; the bust and the lump, she said, can differ in exactly that way right now, prior to any squashing, simply because "statue" and "lump of clay" come bundled with different built-in limits on how much change something can undergo and still count as the same thing.

Neither of them budged, and I honestly couldn't tell who had the better of it. Could you go through moves (1) through (4) and say, for each one, who's right, who's wrong, or whether it's more complicated than that, rather than just declaring an overall winner? And if you think they're both circling around some further issue that neither of them actually put into words, I would genuinely like to know what that is.
