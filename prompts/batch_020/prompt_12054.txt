Question 4 (15 points). Classical (total) utilitarianism is committed to what Parfit called the Repugnant Conclusion: for any population of ten billion people, all with a very high quality of life, there is some much larger population whose existence would be better, even though its members have lives that are barely worth living.
(a) (4 points) Briefly explain, via the Mere Addition Paradox, how total utilitarianism is driven to this conclusion.
(b) (6 points) Explain how critical-level utilitarianism modifies total utilitarianism so as to avoid the Repugnant Conclusion.
(c) (5 points) State one well-known cost of the critical-level solution, and explain why it arises from the very feature of the theory that lets it avoid the Repugnant Conclusion.

Student's answer:

(a) Total utilitarianism ranks populations by the sum of welfare across everyone who ever lives. The Mere Addition Paradox shows how this generates the Repugnant Conclusion in stages. Start with population A: a modest number of people, all flourishing. Adding a further group whose lives are worth living, without changing the welfare of anyone already in A, seems to yield a population A+ that is not worse than A, since we haven't hurt anyone and we've added some good lives "for free." But a population B, of the same size as A+, in which welfare is spread more equally and whose total welfare is at least as high, seems better than A+, since it has at least as much good spread more fairly. So B is not worse than A. Now repeat the whole process starting from B instead of A: get B+, then a more equal C that's not worse than B+, and so on. After enough repetitions you reach a population Z containing a vast number of people whose lives are all barely worth living, and by the transitivity of "not worse than," Z is not worse than A. That is the Repugnant Conclusion.

(b) Critical-level utilitarianism blocks this by changing how individual lives are counted. Instead of adding up raw welfare levels, the theory subtracts a fixed positive constant — the critical level — from each person's welfare before summing. A person's welfare has to clear this bar before their life counts as a good addition to the population; below the critical level, a life is not worth living, so adding such lives drags the total value down rather than up. This is exactly what happens to population Z: because everyone in Z has welfare only barely above zero, which is well under the critical level, Z's critical-level-adjusted total is hugely negative, so Z comes out far worse than A rather than better. The Repugnant Conclusion is avoided because huge numbers of barely-worth-living lives can no longer outweigh a smaller number of excellent ones.

(c) The well-known cost is the Sadistic Conclusion, which Parfit identified as the price of blocking the Repugnant Conclusion this way. Because lives below the critical level count as net-negative contributions to total value, there are cases in which the theory says it is better to add a smaller number of people with negative welfare — lives of suffering — than a much larger number of people with positive welfare below the critical level, since the extra positive-but-substandard lives can drag the total down by more than the negative-welfare lives do. This is a direct consequence of the same subtraction move that let critical-level utilitarianism avoid the Repugnant Conclusion in part (b): once a life can count against the total despite being worth living, a worse-off life can end up counting as the lesser evil. So critical-level utilitarianism trades the Repugnant Conclusion for the Sadistic Conclusion, which is exactly the kind of trade-off Arrhenius's impossibility theorem says we're stuck making no matter which population axiology we choose.
