I've been reading about the history of set theory and keep coming back to the controversy that erupted after Ernst Zermelo published his 1904 proof that every set can be well-ordered. The proof rested on a principle he was the first to state explicitly as a general axiom — the Axiom of Choice — and it scandalized several leading French analysts: within a year, Baire, Borel, and Lebesgue were airing their doubts, with Hadamard defending Zermelo, in a public exchange of letters published in the Bulletin de la Société Mathématique de France under the title "Cinq lettres sur la théorie des ensembles." Mathematicians have been arguing about the axiom's legitimacy ever since, especially once it emerged, two decades later, that this same axiom licenses the Banach–Tarski decomposition of a solid ball into finitely many pieces that can be reassembled, using nothing but rotations and translations, into two balls each the size of the original.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two mathematicians rehashing this dispute in modern terms, say in the corridor after a graduate seminar. One character defends the Axiom of Choice as intuitively obvious, in the spirit of Zermelo's own defense and of Russell's later illustration involving infinitely many pairs of shoes versus infinitely many pairs of socks: just as we can always pick an item from each of finitely many nonempty sets, we should be able to do so from any collection of nonempty sets, no matter how large. The other channels the old skepticism of Borel and Lebesgue, insisting that a proof which can never exhibit or define the object it posits has not really established that object's existence, so that the two of them turn out to mean rather different things by "existence" without quite noticing it. Have the two genuinely engage each other's strongest points, rather than talk past one another, on at least the following: whether the self-evidence of choice for finite (or countable) collections really survives the jump to arbitrary, possibly uncountable ones; whether Banach–Tarski should be read as a reductio of the axiom or as a harmless consequence involving sets that have no volume to begin with; and what, concretely, the skeptic is willing to give up mathematically — the unconditional existence of a basis for every vector space, Tychonoff's theorem, and the like — in exchange for constructive scruples. The dialogue need not resolve the dispute, but it should end with both characters agreeing on exactly where it bottoms out: is this ultimately a disagreement within mathematics, or one about the nature of mathematical existence and infinity themselves?
