(1) He said Aquinas's actual view narrows over his career: in the Sentences commentary Aquinas says flatly that whoever kills a usurping tyrant to free the country "is praised and rewarded," yet by the time he writes the homicide article in the Summa, the rule has become simply that killing evildoers belongs to whoever has public charge of the community, with no usurper exception restated, and by De Regimine Principum he is actively warning readers off private action against tyrants of any kind.

(2) She countered that there's no narrowing, only one distinction applied consistently throughout: a tyrant with no legitimate title at all (a usurper) versus a tyrant who holds legitimate title but rules badly. The praise in the Sentences concerns only the usurper, who is closer to a violent aggressor than to a settled evildoer awaiting judgment, and the same permissive seed reappears in the sedition article, where resisting a regime ordered only to the ruler's private good isn't really sedition at all, so long as the backlash doesn't do more damage than the tyranny did.

(3) He replied that this can't be the whole story, since the homicide article is exactly where a surviving usurper-exception would need restating, and it simply isn't there.

(4) She answered that the homicide article was never the natural home for that exception, since resisting an ongoing usurpation is more like self-defense against a live aggressor than like retrospectively punishing a settled evildoer, and that the caution in De Regimine Principum is best read as a claim about how rarely the further conditions for exercising the permission (a real chance of success, no worse successor waiting in the wings) are met in practice, not as a claim that the permission itself has been withdrawn.

(5) He rejoined that a permission Aquinas himself suggests can almost never be safely exercised is doing no real work, and that the "praised and rewarded" line is more plausibly read as a young commentator glossing Cicero's defense of Brutus than as Aquinas's own considered position.
