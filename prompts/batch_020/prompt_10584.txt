I serve on my university's General Education Committee, which certifies courses for our "Critical Reasoning" requirement and periodically reviews them for renewal. One popular course, cross-listed with the School of Education, builds its second half around Wittgenstein's On Certainty: students are introduced to the idea of a "hinge proposition" -- a certainty such as "the earth has existed for a long time," which isn't itself justified by evidence but instead forms part of the fixed framework within which ordinary doubting and evidence-weighing become possible -- and are then asked to apply this to why people cling to beliefs about, say, vaccine safety or election outcomes in the face of what looks like decisive counter-evidence. The instructor's stated aim is epistemic humility: if such beliefs function as hinges for the people who hold them, dismissing those people as simply irrational misses what is actually going on. Several committee members, with the Dean's backing, want the course's certification revoked, arguing that whatever its merits as Wittgenstein scholarship, teaching undergraduates that a belief can be an unfalsifiable "hinge" just immunizes bad beliefs from criticism and defeats the point of a critical-reasoning requirement. The instructor answers that this objection collapses a distinction her syllabus is careful to draw: genuine Wittgensteinian hinges are maximally general and effectively common ground between believer and skeptic alike (that the earth is old, that one has two hands), whereas something like vaccine safety is a specific, revisable empirical claim actually held for identifiable, if bad, reasons -- so her course diagnoses a confusion rather than excusing one. What nags at me, and what nobody on the committee has raised, is that Wittgenstein's own discussion, late in the book, of a community that consults an oracle rather than physicists suggests he was willing to let something hinge-like vary across whole "world-pictures," which sits uneasily with the instructor's insistence that real hinges are universal common ground rather than the sort of thing that could set one community's bedrock against another's.

As the philosopher on the committee, I've been asked to draft the short decision memo we'll vote on next week, recommending that the course be certified, decertified, or certified with conditions. Please write that memo. I want an actual recommendation, defended with reasons the instructor could engage with rather than wave away as a misreading, and I'd especially like you to address whether her generality-and-universality criterion for genuine hinges survives the oracle passage -- since her whole defense of the course seems to depend on that distinction holding up.
