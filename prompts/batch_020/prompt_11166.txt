I am several drafts into a novel centred on a fictional Knightbridge Professor of Moral Philosophy at Cambridge in the 1880s — an earnest, faintly haunted utilitarian who has begun to suspect that the ethical theory to which he has devoted his career might be true, and also unpublishable in its full rigour. To get his interior life right, I have been working through Henry Sidgwick's "The Methods of Ethics" (my professor is not meant to be Sidgwick himself, though he is unmistakably a creature of that milieu), and I keep returning to a passage late in the book, in the chapter on the practical "Method of Utilitarianism," where Sidgwick asks whether a Utilitarian may sometimes be justified in privately acting, or even privately advising, on principles he would not be willing to teach or advocate openly. I can follow the sentences individually, but I do not feel I have grasped what exactly Sidgwick is committing himself to, and I would like some help before I put any version of the argument into my professor's mouth — or, more plausibly, only into his private notebook.

Here is the passage, which comes just after Sidgwick has discussed a couple of cases (lying about how one voted under the secret ballot; a personal preference for celibacy over marriage) in which he thinks utilitarian calculation might license a departure from an otherwise sound rule of Common-Sense morality:

"Thus, on Utilitarian principles, it may be right to do and privately recommend, under certain circumstances, what it would not be right to advocate openly; it may be right to teach openly to one set of persons what it would be wrong to teach to others; it may be conceivably right to do, if it can be done with comparative secrecy, what it would be wrong to do in the face of the world; and even, if perfect secrecy can be reasonably expected, what it would be wrong to recommend by private advice or example. These conclusions are all of a paradoxical character: there is no doubt that the moral consciousness of a plain man broadly repudiates the general notion of an esoteric morality, differing from that popularly taught; and it would be commonly agreed that an action which would be bad if done openly is not rendered good by secrecy. We may observe, however, that there are strong utilitarian reasons for maintaining generally this latter common opinion; for it is obviously advantageous, generally speaking, that acts which it is expedient to repress by social disapprobation should become known, as otherwise the disapprobation cannot operate; so that it seems inexpedient to support by any moral encouragement the natural disposition of men in general to conceal their wrong doings; besides that the concealment would in most cases have importantly injurious effects on the agent's habits of veracity. Thus the Utilitarian conclusion, carefully stated, would seem to be this; that the opinion that secrecy may render an action right which would not otherwise be so should itself be kept comparatively secret; and similarly it seems expedient that the doctrine that esoteric morality is expedient should itself be kept esoteric. Or if this concealment be difficult to maintain, it may be desirable that Common Sense should repudiate the doctrines which it is expedient to confine to an enlightened few. And thus a Utilitarian may reasonably desire, on Utilitarian principles, that some of his conclusions should be rejected by mankind generally; or even that the vulgar should keep aloof from his system as a whole, in so far as the inevitable indefiniteness and complexity of its calculations render it likely to lead to bad results in their hands."

Three things would help me. First, could you disentangle, in plainer terms, how many distinct and distinctly strange claims are actually stacked up in this one passage? It reads to me as though there is a first-order claim about permissible secret exceptions, a second-order claim about keeping the very doctrine of secret exceptions secret, and something like a third-order recommendation that "the vulgar" be kept from the whole system — and I can't tell whether Sidgwick means these to stand or fall together, or whether he is sliding between genuinely different arguments. Second, in some other reading around this topic I have come across the claim that this sort of passage is really Sidgwick's way of coping with the notorious over-demandingness of utilitarian ethics — the worry that a theory of strict, impartial benevolence asks more of ordinary agents than they can or should be asked to give, so that the "true" doctrine has to be quarantined from general practice. Is that a fair account of what is driving Sidgwick's argument here, or is the esoteric-morality manoeuvre really answering a different worry (something more like a worry about competence and the misuse of exceptions, rather than about the sheer weight of what the theory demands)? Third, is there not a tension between this passage and Sidgwick's evident commitment elsewhere in the book to impartial benevolence as a rationally self-evident first principle — does counselling that most people be kept from the full theory itself sit uneasily with the very impartiality the theory is supposed to enshrine, or is there a way of reconciling the two that I'm missing? I don't need a verdict on whether Sidgwick is ultimately right about any of this — I just want a clear enough map of the argument's structure to decide how much of it my professor should be willing to defend at high table, and how much he should only ever confess to a diary he assumes no one else will read.
