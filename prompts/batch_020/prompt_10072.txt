For my Chinese philosophy seminar, I've been working through the Zhuangzi's treatment of death and keep running into a tension I can't resolve on my own. In the "Qiwulun" (chapter 2), Zhuangzi only raises doubts about whether we're right to prefer life over death, comparing that preference to the confusion of someone who left home young and can no longer remember the way back - a suspension of judgment, posed as a question rather than asserted outright. In "Zhile" (chapter 18), though, he stops mourning his wife by reasoning that her death is simply the next phase of one continuous transformation, on a par with the turning of the seasons, which sounds like a settled metaphysical picture rather than an open question. Later in that same chapter, the skull he meets on the road goes further still, insisting death is straightforwardly better than life and refusing his offer of revival, while Zhuangzi himself voices only mild incredulity rather than real objection. So within a fairly short stretch of text we get withheld judgment, equanimous neutrality, and outright preference for death, with nothing flagging which one, if any, is meant to be Zhuangzi's actual view. Is this simply an inconsistency the text leaves unresolved, or is there a defensible way of reading these three moments as expressions of one coherent position? I'd like you to work through the tension itself rather than just cataloguing the possible readings.
