And here is the student's answer, word for word:

"The grudge informer case is the postwar German case where a woman, wanting rid of her husband, denounced him to the Nazi authorities in 1944 for remarks he'd made against Hitler while home on leave; he was arrested and convicted under a 1934 statute that made such remarks a crime, though in the end he wasn't executed. After the war a German court convicted the wife of unlawfully depriving her husband of his liberty, reasoning that the 1934 statute was so contrary to the sound conscience and sense of justice of all decent people that it had never been valid law, so she couldn't invoke it as a defence.

Hart thinks this reasoning is confused. Rather than pretending the wicked statute was void from the beginning, he thinks the court (or the legislature) should have openly admitted that the statute was valid law under the Nazi system and passed a frankly retroactive law to punish the wife, accepting that this sacrifices the principle against retroactivity for a greater moral good. For Hart, that's what the separation of law and morals actually buys you: it lets you see the moral price you're paying instead of hiding it behind the claim that immoral rules were never really law in the first place.

Fuller thinks this framing lets Hart off too easy. His idea of an internal morality of law — what Hart elsewhere calls the 'minimum content of natural law', the requirement that a legal system meet certain minimum moral standards, here protecting citizens' basic security and welfare against arbitrary power, before it counts as a functioning legal order at all — is exactly what the Nazi regime had abandoned: laws were kept secret, applied retroactively, and enforced inconsistently depending on who the target was. So for Fuller the 1934 statute isn't just one bad rule sitting inside an otherwise healthy system; it's a symptom of a regime that had ceased, in large part, to be a legal order in his sense at all. And Fuller can fairly point out that Hart's own fix — a retroactive statute — itself breaks one of the internal-morality requirements, which suggests that even Hart can't avoid treating some moral qualities as conditions on legality rather than as an optional extra."
