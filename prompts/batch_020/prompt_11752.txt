And here's the student's answer:

"Gibbard's case asks us to imagine a lump of clay, Lumpl, that gets shaped at t1 into a statue, Goliath, and both are squashed out of existence at exactly the same later moment, t2. Because Lumpl and Goliath come into being and go out of being together, and are made of exactly the same matter throughout, it looks like there is really just one object there, not two.

But Lumpl and Goliath seem to differ modally: Lumpl could have survived being squashed into a ball at t2 (it would just become a squashed, non-statue-shaped lump), while Goliath could not have survived being squashed and remained Goliath, since a statue can't survive losing its shape entirely. So Lumpl has the property 'could survive squashing' and Goliath lacks it.

This matters because of Leibniz's Law, which says that if two things share all of their properties, they count as one and the same thing. Since Lumpl and Goliath differ over this modal property, Leibniz's Law tells us they are not identical, even though they occupy exactly the same space and are made of exactly the same matter at every moment either one exists.

The 'constitution is not identity' response, associated with Lewis, grants that Lumpl and Goliath really are numerically distinct, but says this is less strange than it sounds, because Lumpl constitutes Goliath: constitution is a real relation, weaker than identity but much stronger than mere spatial overlap -- the same relation that holds between a piece of cloth and the flag it makes up. Because the two were never identical to begin with, Leibniz's Law is never actually violated; the puzzle only looks threatening if you assume there's a single object answering to both names. Lewis's other main proposal treats the statue and the lump as made of overlapping temporal parts that coincide exactly while Goliath exists, which avoids multiplying objects in quite the same way but instead relativizes identity to a choice of counterpart relation."
