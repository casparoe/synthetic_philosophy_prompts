Since retiring four years ago from a career as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, I have been reading my way through moral and political philosophy, and I recently fell down a rabbit hole that connects rather satisfyingly to my old quantitative habits: David Gauthier's contractarian ethics, and in particular his claim in "Morals by Agreement" that rational bargainers converge on what he calls "minimax relative concession" — which I now understand to be, in effect, the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution to the bargaining problem, rather than the more famous Nash solution most economists reach for by default. This would be a purely academic interest were it not for the fact that I still sit, as an emeritus member, on my university's Joint Instrumentation Committee, which oversees a cryo-electron microscopy suite jointly funded some years ago by the Department of Structural Biology and the Department of Materials Science and Engineering — and the committee has just stumbled into precisely this dispute in earnest.

The suite's roughly 1,600 usable hours per year have always been split about 55/45 in Structural Biology's favor, reflecting the departments' original capital contributions, on the understanding that if the arrangement ever collapsed, Structural Biology would fall back on limited off-site access to an older instrument at a national laboratory and Materials Science on a slower shared instrument at a neighboring campus — these fallback positions functioning, in effect, as our disagreement point. Last spring a donor-funded detector upgrade expanded the instrument's capabilities in ways that mainly benefit Structural Biology's high-resolution work while leaving Materials Science's own ceiling more or less where it was; when a subcommittee re-ran the numbers using the Nash bargaining solution — maximizing the product of each side's gain over its disagreement payoff — Structural Biology's recommended share of hours actually went down relative to the pre-upgrade split. Several of us found this the wrong answer for an upgrade justified almost entirely by Structural Biology's needs, and it turns out this is a known failure of the Nash solution that the Kalai–Smorodinsky alternative, built around each side's best-case or "ideal" point, is specifically designed to rule out. Two committee members are now pressing to switch to the Kalai–Smorodinsky rule; three others want to keep Nash, partly because it is written into our bylaws and partly because they suspect that pegging shares to a department's "ideal point" invites inflated claims about how much time it could hypothetically use.

Because I am the one who raised the issue, the chair has asked me to circulate a short written recommendation before next month's meeting, and I would welcome your help drafting it. I would like it to take a clear position on which bargaining rule the committee should adopt as standing policy — or to argue that the choice has been framed wrongly, if you think that is the case — to render the underlying axioms in terms my colleagues without a mathematical background can actually follow, to specify what the resulting split of hours should be for the coming allocation cycle and how long the policy should remain in force before it is reviewed, and, most importantly, to engage honestly with the strongest thing that can be said for whichever side the memo does not end up favoring, rather than dispatching it in a token sentence.
