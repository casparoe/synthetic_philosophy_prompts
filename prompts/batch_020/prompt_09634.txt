I've been reading about Otto Neurath's Isotype project from the 1920s and 30s, and what strikes me most is how strict its internal rules were. Each symbol was meant to carry one fixed, publicly agreed meaning; a larger quantity had to be shown by repeating a same-sized icon rather than enlarging it, since Neurath argued people cannot reliably judge area or volume by eye; and the whole system was pitched as a supplement to ordinary language for facts about quantity and comparison, not a substitute for verbal argument, negation, or nuance. Behind these rules sat his broader Vienna Circle commitment to putting social and economic facts in front of ordinary people, regardless of language or schooling, without relying on rhetoric.

This leaves me wondering what Neurath would make of emoji, arguably the closest thing we now have to a genuinely global picture vocabulary. Emoji derive most of their value from exactly the ambiguity, irony, and cultural inflection his rules were designed to eliminate, and people reach for them to convey tone rather than checkable facts. Working only from what you know of Neurath's actual commitments, rather than a generic sense that he simply "liked pictures," reconstruct his likely verdict: is emoji's success a vindication of his bet on pictures over words, or does it work only by abandoning what made Isotype trustworthy to him? Even if the honest answer is mixed, tell me which way it tips on balance, and why.
