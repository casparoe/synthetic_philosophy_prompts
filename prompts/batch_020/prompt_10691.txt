I'm on my university's academic integrity board, and this week's case has turned into a logic puzzle I can't shake. A student was referred to us on five seperate cheating counts from the same seminar -- unauthorized notes on five different quizzes across the semester -- each one investigated independently with its own evidence (proctor logs, seating charts, a couple of photos). Our investigator's office has this new pilot practice of attaching a rough confidence percentage to each count instead of a flat yes/no finding (still not sure how I feel about it), and all five counts came back around 90%. Our bylaws say a "responsible" finding on any count needs at least 85% confidence, and our sanction guide calls for expulsion when a student is found responsible on all five counts of a single case, so on paper this looks like an easy five-for-five.

Except one of our members ran the numbers and pointed out that if the five counts are roughly independent, which seems fair given the different dates, quizzes, and evidence, the odds the student is actually guilty of all five -- which is the thing an expulsion would be punishing -- come out to around 0.9 to the fifth power, just under 59%, way under our 85% bar. Ask instead whether they're guilty of at least one count and it's comfortably above 99.9%. So the exact same evidence and the exact same per-count threshold makes this student either an obvious expulsion or nowhere close, depending only on how we phrase the question we're voting on. My nephew, a philosophy major, informs me this is basically the lottery paradox and the preface paradox having a baby, which is a fun thing to say and does not tell me what to actually do about Thursday's vote.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate to the board beforehand? I need an actual recommendation, not just a restatement of the puzzle: whether "responsible" should be decided count-by-count with sanctions keyed to each count separately, whether the panel needs some different, explicit standard for the conjunction when there are multiple counts, or something else entirely -- plus a specific call on this student's case and sanction. It also needs to seriously engage the member who thinks aggregating across counts at all is the mistake and that each count should simply be judged, and sanctioned, on its own, rather than just noting her objection and moving past it.
