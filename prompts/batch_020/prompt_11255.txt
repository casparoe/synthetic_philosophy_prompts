**Question (20 points).** Williamson argues against the luminosity of mental states using the example of a subject whose sense of coldness changes gradually over the course of an evening as the temperature steadily drops.
(a) [8 points] Reconstruct the argument, stating clearly what luminosity is and what work the margin-for-error principle does in the reductio.
(b) [6 points] Explain how this argument, if sound, bears on the KK principle.
(c) [6 points] A classmate claims that, if sound, the argument shows that "first-person authority" is an illusion, and that we are in no better position to know our own minds than an outside observer would be. Assess this claim.

**Student's answer:**

(a) A condition C is luminous just in case, whenever it obtains, one is thereby in a position to know that it obtains. Williamson asks us to track a subject across an evening as the room cools, from an initial instant 0, when she isn't cold at all, through many further instants each just a moment later than the last, down to a final instant n, when she is freezing. Because her internal sense of temperature isn't perfectly fine-grained, she can't discriminate her state at instant i from her state at instant i+1. The margin-for-error principle says that a belief produced by a discriminative capacity of limited resolution only counts as knowledge if it would still be true in the nearest cases the capacity can't distinguish from the actual one — so if she knows at instant i that she feels cold, she must also actually feel cold at instant i-1. Now suppose, for reductio, that feeling cold is luminous. At instant n she is freezing, so by luminosity she knows she feels cold. By the margin-for-error principle, she must then have felt cold at n-1, and iterating this step all the way back, she must have felt cold at instant 0. But by hypothesis she felt nothing of the kind at instant 0. Contradiction — so luminosity fails for this condition.

(b) The same style of reasoning generalises to the special case where the condition in question is itself a state of knowledge. If I know that p, am I always thereby in a position to know that I know that p? That's exactly the KK principle, and knowing that p is just as capable of being gained or lost by degrees as feeling cold is: my evidence for p can accumulate or erode gradually, and my grip on whether I've cleared the bar for knowledge is exactly as coarse-grained as my grip on how cold I feel. So the same margin-for-error argument shows that the KK principle is simply false, for any subject and any proposition: however good one's evidence, there will always be some proposition one knows without being in a position to know that one knows it.

(c) I think this is broadly right. If luminosity fails, there's no guarantee that being in a mental state suffices for knowing that one is in it — which is exactly the special access Descartes thought introspection gave us into our own minds. Once we grant that a subject can feel cold without being in a position to know that she feels cold, the traditional idea of privileged first-person access collapses: our knowledge of our own current states turns out to rest on the very same fallible, margin-for-error-laden capacities that give us knowledge of anything else, so there is no principled asymmetry left between knowing our own mind and knowing someone else's.
