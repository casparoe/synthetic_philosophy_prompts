I'm TAing an upper-level jurisprudence seminar this term and would like a second, independent read on a student's take-home answer before I finalize marks. Here is the question we set:

Patrick Devlin argued, against the Wolfenden Committee's view that there is a realm of private morality that is "not the law's business," that English criminal law is entitled to enforce a society's shared morality independently of any showing of harm to others. Reconstruct Devlin's argument for this conclusion, including his "disintegration thesis" and the limiting conditions he places on it. Then explain the two main objections H.L.A. Hart raises against Devlin in Law, Liberty, and Morality, and assess whether either objection succeeds, or whether Devlin has a plausible reply available.

Here is the student's answer:

Devlin's argument starts from the Wolfenden Committee's 1957 recommendation to decriminalize private homosexual acts between consenting adults, on the ground that there must be a realm of private morality that is not the law's business. Devlin, himself a judge, rejected this separation. He held that every society is held together by a shared morality, and that this shared morality is as necessary to a society's existence as its shared institutions of government: if enough of its members stopped sharing it, the society would disintegrate, in the way it might if enough people stopped obeying the law. This is Devlin's disintegration thesis. Because a state may legitimately act to prevent its own destruction, it may use the criminal law to shore up the shared morality even where no identifiable individual is harmed. Devlin limits this considerably: the moral reaction in question must be a genuine, widely and deeply held one of intolerance, indignation, and disgust, not mere dislike, and lawmakers should still leave the maximum degree of individual freedom compatible with the integrity of society.

Hart raises two main objections. First, he argues that the disintegration thesis is an empirical claim for which Devlin supplies no evidence: many societies have altered their sexual or religious morality substantially, and sometimes quickly, without disintegrating, so there is no particular reason to expect toleration of private homosexual conduct to be any different. Second, and more fundamentally, Hart accuses Devlin of conflating positive and critical morality. On Hart's distinction, positive morality is the morality actually embedded in a society's institutions and laws at a given time, while critical morality is the set of general moral principles used to ask whether those institutions and laws are good ones. Devlin's mistake, on this diagnosis, is to treat the fact that a practice offends a society's positive morality as if that already showed, by critical-moral standards, that suppressing the practice was justified, when the positive morality in question might simply encode inherited prejudice.

Hart's own view follows Mill's harm principle strictly: the only legitimate ground for criminalizing conduct is that it harms someone other than the agent, so purely self-regarding immorality is never a proper object of the criminal law, however widely it is condemned. Devlin could reply that the disintegration thesis was never meant to predict a visible collapse on any particular timetable, only that a society's identity is partly constituted by its shared morality, so that sufficient moral change simply produces a different society rather than the destruction of the old one; but since Devlin himself couches the danger in terms of actual social cohesion, this looks like a retreat from his original claim. On balance, both of Hart's objections stand, and Devlin's argument fails.
