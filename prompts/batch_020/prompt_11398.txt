ok so I've been chewing on something from Bostrom's Superintelligence and I can't fully untangle it. The orthogonality thesis says intelligence and final goals are basically independent axes — a paperclip maximizer can in principle be just as smart as a fully aligned AI, since "smart" is about optimization power, not about what you're optimizing for. But sitting right next to that, in the same book, is the instrumental convergence thesis, which says that almost *any* sufficiently smart agent, no matter what its final goal is, will converge on the same short list of instrumental subgoals — self-preservation, resource acquisition, not letting its goal content get altered, etc. That second thesis feels like its quietly taking back a lot of what the first one gives you: if you can predict most of an agent's actual behavior just from "it's very intelligent," without knowing anything about its terminal goal, then intelligence isn't really orthogonal to the stuff we actually care about (behavior), it's just orthogonal to the one variable that turns out to matter least for prediction purposes. Can you help me think through whether these two theses are genuinely in tension, or whether there's a clean way to show they're compatible? And if they are compatible, is there a sharper version of my worry that doesn't just collapse into a definitional dodge like "well, final goals are still technically unconstrained"?
