I spent thirty-two years running a molecular genetics lab before retiring, and for the past year I have been trying to backfill the philosophical foundations of a concept I used constantly in that lab without ever examining it closely: causation. A few former colleagues and I now meet every other Sunday over video call to work through the literature together; we got through Lewis's counterfactual analysis in the spring and have spent the summer on Woodward's and Pearl's interventionist (or "manipulationist," as Woodward sometimes calls it) alternative. Last session ended in a disagreement between two of the group, Henrik, a retired immunologist, and Naomi, a retired biostatistician, that none of us could settle, and I would value an outside verdict.

The case that started it is drawn from my own former field, rather than the philosophers' usual assassins and short-circuits. Picture two paralogous transcription factors, A and B, either of which is independently sufficient to switch on a downstream protein P; in the wild-type cell both are expressed and P appears at normal levels. Knock out A alone and B fully compensates, so P is unaffected; knock out B alone and A compensates; only the double knockout eliminates P. The question was whether A's expression counts as a cause of P's presence in the ordinary wild-type cell, and the exchange went four rounds. (1) Henrik pointed out that on Lewis's counterfactual test A fails outright: had A not been expressed, P would still be there via B, so there is no counterfactual dependence; and the obvious interventionist test gives the same answer, since intervening on A while holding B fixed at its actual, expressed value leaves P unchanged. On the letter of both theories, he said, A is not a cause; at most the disjunction "A or B" is. (2) Naomi objected that this is absurd: A's promoter is active, and some real share of the P molecules in that cell were transcribed via A's own machinery rather than B's, so Henrik was confusing whether the outcome-type depends on A with whether A actually helped produce the outcome. She said the interventionist framework can recover the right answer if you test A's effect against a counterfactual, rather than actual, value of B — say, B knocked out — since then moving A clearly moves P. (3) Henrik granted that this is exactly the move licensed by Halpern and Pearl's structural-equations account of actual causation, which tests a candidate cause's difference-making against a "witness" setting of other variables that may depart from their actual values. But he said the field has never settled which departures are admissible, revising the official definition repeatedly in response to new counterexamples, so invoking the move just means choosing whichever witness delivers the verdict one already wanted. (4) Naomi replied that unsettled boundary conditions do not disqualify a research program, and that the objection is not even special to interventionism: the only serious attempts to make a broadly counterfactual theory handle redundant causes at all — Lewis's later talk of degrees of "influence," and the structural-equations rewrites of dependence proposed by writers such as Hitchcock — reach for exactly the same hold-fixed, contingency-based apparatus that Henrik was objecting to. So if the complaint lands, she said, it lands against counterfactual dependence as such, not against interventionism in particular.

I can see something right in each of these four moves and cannot rank them myself. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move?
