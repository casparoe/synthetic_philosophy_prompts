For years I have followed rationalist-adjacent blogs and forums, where "satisficing" tends to get invoked in two quite different senses that I have rarely seen anyone carefully distinguish. The first is Herbert Simon's original notion from his work on bounded rationality: satisficing as the rational response of a resource-limited agent — a firm, a manager, an organism — to costly search, whereby the agent stops at the first option that clears some aspiration-level threshold instead of continuing to hunt for the optimum. The second is the "maximizer versus satisficer" distinction from personality psychology, popularized by Barry Schwartz's work on the paradox of choice, in which satisficing and maximizing are treated as comparatively stable individual dispositions, and in which maximizers have reliably been found to report more regret and lower subjective well-being despite often reaching objectively better outcomes.

Please compare these two notions of satisficing in some depth. Are they genuinely the same underlying idea appearing at two different levels of description — a computational theory of rational choice versus a trait theory of individual differences — or does something essential to Simon's original point (that satisficing is the economically rational response to a fixed cost of search, not a fixed temperament) get altered, or quietly lost, as the term migrates into the psychology of everyday decisions? And given the well-being findings on the psychological side, what should we conclude about the normative question Simon actually cared about — whether satisficing or maximizing is the more rational way to decide — and justify your answer by engaging with the actual mechanics of both literatures rather than resolving the question by definitional fiat.
