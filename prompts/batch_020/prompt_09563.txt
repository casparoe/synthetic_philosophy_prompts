I never studied philosophy in school, but a few years ago I read Schopenhauer's *The World as Will and Representation* almost by accident, and the section on music -- the one where he argues that music isn't like the other arts at all, that it skips over the Platonic Ideas entirely and gives us a direct copy of the will itself, rather than a copy of a copy -- has stuck with me ever since. I co-host a tiny philosophy podcast with a friend, recorded in my living room, and next week we're interviewing a philosopher who has built her career defending that theory: her whole position is that it deserves to be taken seriously as actual metaphysics of music, not waved away as nineteenth-century enthusiasm for Rossini dressed up in jargon.

I'd like eight to ten questions that keep her inside the mechanics of the view rather than letting her just restate it elegantly, which I suspect she's very practiced at doing. I want at least one question built around this puzzle: Schopenhauer holds that space and time are merely forms our minds impose on appearances, so that the will in itself is outside time altogether -- yet music, unlike a building or a statue, cannot exist except as one note following another. Why would the most obviously time-bound of all the arts be the best candidate for copying something that isn't in time at all, rather than the worst? I also want one question on his mapping of the bass line onto brute, unorganized nature, the middle voices onto the plant and animal kingdoms, and the melody onto conscious human striving -- I'd like her pressed on whether that mapping is doing real metaphysical work or is just a suggestive analogy dressed up as an argument, and what would even distinguish those two possibilities. And I want one question on what looks to me like a real tension: he says aesthetic experience of any kind quiets the will and gives us a kind of peace, but he also says music, uniquely among the arts, hands us the will directly, with no Idea and no image standing between it and us -- so why wouldn't hearing the most direct possible expression of the very thing that makes us restless and needy stir the will up rather than settle it? For each of these three, please add a short, pointed follow-up already tailored to whatever answer she's most likely to reach for, so we're not stuck if she deflects with something well-rehearsed. Fill out the rest of the list however you think best, but skip any warm-up question about how she first got interested in Schopenhauer -- I'd rather spend the whole segment inside the argument -- and maybe close with something asking what kind of musical experience, if any, would actually count as evidence against the theory rather than just another illustration of it.
