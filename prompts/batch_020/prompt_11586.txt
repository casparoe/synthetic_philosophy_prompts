A friend and I got into a long argument after watching a nature documentary together last night. The trigger was a segment on parasitoid wasps — the ones that paralyze a caterpillar and lay their eggs inside it so the larvae can eat it alive from within — which my friend raised as a modern-day rejoinder to Leibniz's claim that ours is the best of all possible worlds. Their point wasn't the familiar Voltaire-style "but earthquakes and massacres happen" objection; it was that Leibniz's optimism looks far worse once you factor in what Darwin and modern ecology revealed about nature as a whole — that in most species the statistically normal fate of an individual is to be born, suffer, and die as food for something else within days, and that this is not an occasional malfunction but the ordinary operating logic of the biological world. Since Leibniz died in 1716, well over a century before the publication of On the Origin of Species, he never had to reckon with this picture directly, and we couldn't agree on whether his own system had the resources to absorb it or would eventually have to give way.

I'd like your help settling this, or at least sharpening the disagreement. Rather than restating the general "best of all possible worlds" slogan, or the Pangloss caricature of it, could you reconstruct how Leibniz's actual metaphysics would have to handle the objection? I'm especially interested in how the following pieces of his system bear on it: the principle of sufficient reason together with the notion of compossibility, on which God selects the most varied world compatible with the simplest laws; his three-way distinction between metaphysical, physical, and moral evil; and his account of the hierarchy of monads, on which animals possess genuine sentience and memory but not reason, so that he cannot simply borrow Descartes' move of denying that animals suffer at all. Given all this, what do you think would have been Leibniz's most honest response to the Darwinian picture, and is there a point at which the framework would genuinely strain under the objection rather than merely absorb it rhetorically?
