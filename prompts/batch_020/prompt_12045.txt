I'm working on a novel with a secondary character - a brilliant, unconventional Victorian woman novelist who is obviously (though never named as such) modelled on George Eliot - and I keep getting stuck on a scene where she has to defend her private religious views to a clergyman who's courting her. I don't want to write the lazy "she lost her faith and became a vague humanist" version of this that shows up in every stuffy biopic. I remember reading that before she wrote a word of fiction, Eliot actually translated both Feuerbach's The Essence of Christianity and Spinoza's Ethics, and that these two books apparently left pretty different fingerprints on her mature outlook - Feuerbach basically saying religion is just humanity worshipping it's own best qualities projected outward onto a fake sky-God, Spinoza offering this whole necessitarian "God-or-Nature" system where individual free will is more or less an illusion and virtue means understanding your place in that causal order. A friend from my writing group who did her thesis on the Victorians insists it's really all just Comte and the "Religion of Humanity," but that feels like it's skating past the more interesting, weirder stuff underneath.

So here's what I actually need: can you compare Feuerbach's and Spinoza's positions here in some real depth (not a two-line summary), and then make a case for which of the two frameworks does more actual work in Eliot's mature fiction - I'm thinking of things like the ending of Middlemarch, or the whole arc of Silas Marner - versus whether she's really just holding both in an uneasy, never fully reconciled tension. I'd like enough substance that I can put a genuinely specific, well-argued version of this into my clergyman-courtship scene, rather than something generic that could apply to any 19th century freethinker.
