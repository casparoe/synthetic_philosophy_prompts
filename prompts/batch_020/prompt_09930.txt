I'm plotting a novel in which a wellness startup builds an app called Ledger that revives Benjamin Franklin's "bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection" for the smartphone age: users pick from his thirteen virtues, dictate a nightly confession, and an AI scores their conduct against each one, tallies weekly streaks, and posts everyone's charts to a shared leaderboard among friends — a nod to the fact that Franklin himself treated the whole enterprise as a kind of moral bookkeeping. I need to draft the scene where the founder demos this for a version of Franklin himself, and I want his reaction to feel like something the actual man would say, not a generic "technology bad" complaint.

Drawing on the specifics of how he really ran the project — his week-by-week rotation through the virtues, his admission that Order alone defeated him, the joke he made about being proud of his own humility, and his wry, eventual acceptance that a speckled axe beats one that's impossibly bright — tell me whether he'd recognize Ledger as a fulfillment of his method, or object to something in it on its own terms: the outsourcing of judgment to a machine, the leaderboard replacing the privacy of his little book, or something else you think matters more. I want a clear verdict, not a balanced survey of considerations.
