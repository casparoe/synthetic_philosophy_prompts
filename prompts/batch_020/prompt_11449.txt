I spent about thirty-five years doing statistical mechanics before I retired, and these days I audit philosophy courses at the university near me just for fun. Last week's seminar was on methodological individualism versus holism in social science, and afterward two of the grad students, Nadia and Gareth, kept going at it over coffee while I mostly just listened. It reminded me a lot of the "more is different" arguments we used to have about emergence in condensed matter, except nobody in physics get this heated about it. Neither of them would budge, and I didn't want to referee on the spot, so I said I'd think it over -- except it's been several days now and I still can't decide who actually had the better of it.

Here's roughly how the exchange went, as best I can reconstruct it. (1) Nadia opened by saying that no explanation of a social phenomenon -- a bank run, a riot, a fashion trend -- really explains anything until its cashed out in terms of individual people's beliefs, desires and actions; saying "the market did X" or "society believes Y" is just shorthand, and if you can't unpack the shorthand you've redescribed the phenomenon rather than explained it. She invoked Weber's insistence on verstehen and the old Popper/Watkins idea that individuals, with their situations and dispositions, are the only real raw material the social world is built out of. (2) Gareth said that proves too much: Durkheim's suicide-rate data holds up as a stable pattern across people with totally different personalities and personal histories, which suggests the explanation lives at the level of religious affiliation and social integration, not individual psychology -- and besides, the "individual-level" facts Nadia wants to reduce everything to (a person's role, the going price, the local norm) are already social facts through and through, so it's not a reduction to bare individuals at all, just social facts wearing a disguise. (3) Nadia came back with something like Coleman's boat: fine, the macro pattern is realized by all sorts of different individual psychologies, but that only shows you need a mechanism connecting macro conditions to individual incentives and back up to outcomes again -- a bare correlation between two macro-level facts (religion, suicide rate) isn't an explanation until somebody supplies that mechanism, and mechanisms bottom out in individuals. She pointed to Schelling's checkerboard segregation model as the template: mild individual preferences, aggregated, produce a stark macro pattern that nobody intended, which is exactly the kind of story holism can't tell on its own. (4) Gareth's comeback was that some social explanations are functional through and through and don't reduce to that sort of mechanism at all -- he brought up G.A. Cohen's defense of functional explanation in Marx, where a set of production relations persists because of its effect on developing productive capacity, and said no amount of individual-incentive-hunting rescues that story without either begging the question or turning it into a different and much weaker explanation than the one on offer. (5) Nadia's last word was that unaccompanied functional explanations are exactly what Elster taught everybody to distrust, and that Cohen still owes some kind of selection mechanism before the functional story stops being mysterious -- which just drags us back to individuals again.

Could you go through this move by move and tell me who's got the stronger argument at each of the five steps, not just an overall winner -- and flag anything you think both Nadia and Gareth are quietly assuming or missing? I'd also be curious what you think as a general matter: does this dispute have an actual fact of the matter, or are individualism and holism more like two legitimate research strategies that were never going to fully settle it, the way you can do perfectly good physics at the level of thermodynamics or at the level of statistical mechanics without either side "winning"?
