In my seminar on moral responsibility, we've spent the last two weeks working through the recent literature on hypocrisy and the standing to blame, including Wallace's article on hypocrisy and equal standing and Cohen's essay on whether hypocritical Western governments have any standing to condemn terrorists. Both readings converge on the claim that a hypocrite forfeits the standing to blame others for an offense they themselves commit without justification. As I understand it, this is essentially because the hypocrite's accusation turns out not to be accurate after all: once it becomes clear that the accuser has no standing, the charge against the target effectively collapses, in more or less the same way any other unsupported accusation would.

This question felt more urgent after a local news story broke: a city council member who built her whole campaign around attacking corporate tax avoidance was just revealed to have routed her own consulting income through an offshore shell company for years. A friend of mine argued in our class group chat that this revelation doesn't just show she personally has no right to raise the issue anymore — it actually discredits her original claim that this kind of avoidance is wrong, since it's the hypocrisy itself that reveals the accusation to have been baseless all along. Given that the standing-to-blame literature seems to support this kind of reasoning, I would appreciate help generalizing it properly: should I treat a critic's hypocrisy as itself evidence that their underlying accusation was mistaken, and does this mean that pointing out someone's hypocrisy is a legitimate way of rebutting the substance of their criticism, rather than merely a way of discrediting the critic personally?
