Three weeks ago I suffered a cardiac arrest during a routine gallbladder operation and was, according to the surgical team, without a pulse for nearly two minutes. While unconscious, I underwent an extraordinarily vivid experience: a tunnel of shifting light, a sensation of being drawn away from my own body, and an overwhelming impression that something was rearranging the room around me. Since returning home, I have not been able to stop reading about near-death experiences, partly to make sense of what happened to me and partly out of genuine anxiety about what it might mean for what happens after we die. In the course of this reading, a friend who studied philosophy directed me to the case of A.J. Ayer, the famous twentieth-century logical positivist who spent his career arguing, in books such as Language, Truth and Logic, that talk of God, souls, or an afterlife was strictly meaningless. Ayer underwent a strikingly similar experience in 1988, after a choking incident during a hospital stay: he later wrote of seeing a painful red light and encountering beings who seemed to be responsible for administering space and time, and he reportedly told his own doctor afterward that he had seen a "Divine Being" and would have to revise his life's work accordingly.

What strikes me about this case is that Ayer was about as far from a credulous person as one could find: his entire philosophical reputation rested on dismissing exactly this kind of talk as nonsense. It seems telling that a single firsthand brush with death was apparently enough to make even so rigorous and lifelong a materialist recant decades of committed atheism once he actually lived through it. Given that even Ayer, of all people, could not hold the line on physicalism after his own experience, how much weight should I now be putting on my own episode when deciding whether to revise the atheist, materialist views I held before any of this happened?
