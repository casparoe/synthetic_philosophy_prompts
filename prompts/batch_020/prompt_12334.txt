Student answer: "The regress problem arises once we ask what justifies a belief and insist that the answer always cite some further belief. This generates a chain of justifying beliefs that must either terminate somewhere, loop back on itself, or continue without end. Foundationalists take the first option: the chain terminates in basic beliefs, which are justified non-inferentially, for example by direct perceptual experience, rather than by appeal to further beliefs. Coherentists deny that there is any privileged terminus at all; a belief is justified by how well it fits with the rest of one's belief system, an image often captured by Neurath's raft, which sailors must rebuild plank by plank while already at sea, with no dry dock to return to. Infinitism, defended by Peter Klein, takes the regress itself to be unobjectionable: a belief is justified just in case there is an infinite, non-repeating series of further reasons available for it. In effect, Klein's infinitism agrees with the coherentist that no belief is basic, and simply straightens coherentism's circle of mutual support out into an endless line, so that nothing is ever left unsupported.

The strongest objection to infinitism is that finite creatures cannot traverse an infinite series of reasons, so that no human belief could ever be justified on this view. Klein's answer is that ordinary justification never actually calls for infinitely many reasons: in conversation, we simply keep supplying reasons until our interlocutor is satisfied, at which point the chain can rest. Because justification is therefore relative to a conversational context, our inability to complete an infinite series is beside the point, and the finite minds objection fails to touch infinitism."
