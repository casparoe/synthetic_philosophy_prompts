Been down a Bentham rabbit hole for a piece and thought this one's for you. Everyone knows him as the greatest-happiness-principle, panopticon guy, but way fewer people know he also wrote a whole unpublished manuscript back in the 1780s (usually referred to as "Offences Against One's Self") arguing on strictly utilitarian grounds that the sodomy laws of his day were indefensible — his point being that an act between consenting people that causes no pain to anyone just isn't the law's business, no matter how much disgust it provokes in bystanders. He knew going public with it would wreck him, so he sat on it, and it didn't actually surface until the 20th century. Given that essay, plus the rest of his apperatus — the felicific calculus, and his jab that a lot of morals legislation is really just "antipathy" dressed up as a principle — how do you think he'd land on the current fight over fully decriminalizing sex work: full decrim (like New Zealand) vs. the "Nordic model" that only criminalizes the buyer? Don't just hand me a one-line verdict, actually reconstruct the argument the way he'd have built it, and tell me where his own framework starts to strain (trafficking, third-party harms, whatever) instead of pointing neatly at one policy.
