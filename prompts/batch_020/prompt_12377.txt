A few months ago, a friend and I got into a long-running argument about trolley problems — specifically, why it seems acceptable to divert a runaway trolley onto a track with one person on it, but monstrous to push someone into its path in order to stop it from killing five others, even though both choices trade one death for five lives saved. I don't have any background in philosophy, but the disagreement sent me down a rabbit hole of forum threads and encyclopedia articles, and that's how I learned that most modern discussions of this issue, and of what's now called the doctrine of double effect, trace back to the same short passage in Aquinas's Summa Theologica, on whether it is lawful to kill a man in self-defense. Rather than rely on other people's summaries of it, I tracked down the passage itself (Part II-II, Question 64, Article 7, in the old English translation by the Fathers of the English Dominican Province) and tried to work through it directly. Here is the key paragraph, the "I answer that" section where Aquinas lays out his reasoning:

"Nothing hinders one act from having two effects, only one of which is intended, while the other is beside the intention. Now moral acts take their species according to what is intended, and not according to what is beside the intention, since this is accidental. Accordingly the act of self-defense may have two effects, one is the saving of one's life, the other is the slaying of the aggressor. Therefore this act, since one's intention is to save one's own life, is not unlawful, seeing that it is natural to everything to keep itself in 'being,' as far as possible. And yet, though proceeding from a good intention, an act may be rendered unlawful, if it be out of proportion to the end. Wherefore if a man, in self-defense, uses more than necessary violence, it will be unlawful: whereas if he repel force with moderation his defense will be lawful, because according to the jurists, 'it is lawful to repel force by force, provided one does not exceed the limits of a blameless defense.' Nor is it necessary for salvation that a man omit the act of moderate self-defense in order to avoid killing the other man, since one is bound to take more care of one's own life than of another's. But as it is unlawful to take a man's life, except for the public authority acting for the common good, it is not lawful for a man to intend killing a man in self-defense, except for such as have public authority, who while intending to kill a man in self-defense, refer this to the public good, as in the case of a soldier fighting against the foe, and in the minister of the judge struggling with robbers, although even these sin if they be moved by private animosity."

I've now read this maybe a dozen times, and three things still trip me up. First, I can't pin down what work the distinction between "intended" and "beside the intention" is actually doing: Aquinas says the two effects come from a single act and that the act's moral kind depends only on the intended one, but the aggressor's death is fully foreseen and the entire action is built around bringing it about, so I can't tell whether "beside the intention" means anything more precise than "the part I didn't want." Second, and this is what confuses me most: right after stating what sounds like a general principle about foreseen-but-unintended effects, Aquinas seems to switch to something narrower — that a private person may never actually intend to kill even in self-defense, only intend to save himself, whereas a soldier or "the minister of the judge" is allowed to intend the killing itself, provided he refers it to the common good. That reads to me like a second, different justification rather than an application of the first one, and it isn't anything I've seen mentioned in the various modern summaries of "the four conditions of double effect" that I've come across online. Third, I don't understand how the requirement of proportion — not using more force than necessary — is supposed to connect to the intention-based argument at all: if the killing is only a side effect of an act aimed at self-preservation, why would the amount of force used change whether that description still holds? Could you help me work through these three difficulties in the order I've raised them, and then say something about whether, once they're sorted out, this passage actually supports the tidy four-condition version of double effect that people attribute to it, or whether Aquinas's own argument is doing something looser, or different, than that?
