I have been asked to give the toast at the retirement dinner of a colleague who spent four decades running the conservation-science lab at a major museum, using pigment analysis, X-radiography, and archival research to tell genuine paintings from forgeries. She is also known, among the art historians and philosophers of art in the room, for a contrarian position she has defended for years: that a sufficiently good forgery has exactly the same aesthetic value as the work it copies, so that her lab was never really protecting beauty, only provenance, the market, and the credit owed to the real artist — a view she sums up as "the eye can't convict a fake; only the paint chip can." Everyone else at the dinner — family, junior staff, a trustee or two — will be expecting a simpler and more flattering story, in which she spent her career catching fakes because she had an eye for beauty that no fake could fool. Write the toast: it should run about five minutes, turn on at least one line or story that plays on the gap between aesthetic value and the historical-and-market value her work actually protected, be genuinely funny and not merely sentimental, and never — not even as a joke — credit her with the eye-for-beauty story the second group is expecting.
