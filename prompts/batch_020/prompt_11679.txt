At work last week, a colleague and I got into a surprisingly heated code-review argument that I haven't quite been able to let go of. He had written a caching function that, thanks to a side effect nobody had planned for, also ended up deduplicating a queue elsewhere in the pipeline. His position was that the function's real "function" is whatever it currently does for the system, deduplication included, regardless of what he originally had in mind when he wrote it. I argued that its function is caching, full stop, and that the deduplication is just a happy accident, because caching is what the function was actually designed and written to do. Neither of us backed down, and afterward, out of curiosity, I went looking online and found out that philosophers of biology have apparently been having a version of this exact argument for decades, under the heading of "etiological" versus "causal-role" theories of biological function.

I'd like you to write me a comparison of these two theories, anchored around Larry Wright's etiological account and Robert Cummins's causal-role account, since those are the two names that keep coming up in what I've read (feel free to bring in refinements like Ruth Millikan's "proper function" view too, if it's useful). Could you work through how each theory handles the standard hard cases people raise in this debate: vestigial organs, exaptations (a trait like feathers, which under one common hypothesis were originally selected for insulation long before they were ever co-opted for flight), malfunction (a heart that fails to pump blood is still described as malfunctioning, which seems to require some notion of what it's "supposed" to do), and freak or brand-new traits with no selective history at all behind them (the "Swampman" thought experiment -- a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a person that springs into existence with no evolutionary history whatsoever -- seems relevant here). I'd like to know which of these cases end up being easy for one theory and hard for the other, and why. Finally, please don't leave me on the fence: tell me which of the two theories gives the more defensible general account of biological function, and also whether the disagreement my colleague and I had actually maps onto this philosophical debate, or whether the fact that software is intentionally designed breaks the analogy in some important way.
