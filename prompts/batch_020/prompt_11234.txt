For the past three years I've been working on a novel—a long, slow-moving family drama set across one summer at a lake house—and last month I finally let a woman who mentored me back in graduate school read the finished manuscript. Her verdict has stayed with me. She said the prose was accomplished and the structure sound, and admitted she admired several individual scenes on a purely technical level, but that reading the whole thing she never once felt she was in the presence of something I had actually lived through myself; it read, she said, like a very well-made object rather than anything closer to a confession, and that this, more than any flaw of craft, was why she wouldn't call it real art.

When I described this to my writing group afterward, they split down the middle: half thought she'd put her finger on something genuine and important, the other half thought she was smuggling in a private, almost mystical standard with no real bearing on whether a novel works for its readers. I pushed back on her a little myself, pointing out that some of my favorite novels are visibly, even ostentatiously engineered and still move me enormously, and she replied that being moved by skillful engineering isn't the same thing as being moved by a feeling that genuinely belonged to someone else first. I haven't been able to settle this in my own mind since. Is there a real, defensible distinction to be drawn between a novel that is merely well made and one that succeeds as art in whatever further sense my old mentor has in mind, or is she chasing an idea that sounds profound but doesn't actually survive scrutiny once you try to pin it down?
