A friend of mine, Daniel, spent several years as a junior officer in the armed forces of a small country whose government, a few years ago, launched an unprovoked invasion of a neighboring state on what later turned out to be fabricated pretexts. Daniel had no hand in that decision; he was a career officer who was mobilized along with his entire cohort and sent to the front within weeks of the invasion being announced. By his own account, which matches the little I've since read about that stretch of the war, he never targeted civilians and generally held himself to the same rules of engagement that soldiers on the other, defending side were also supposed to observe: he shot at people who were shooting at him and his unit, and not much else. When I asked him recently, half-joking, whether the war weighed on his conscience, he said no. The decision to invade was his government's crime, not his, and since he only ever killed enemy combatants under the same battlefield rules that a soldier on the just side would also have been following, he doesn't see himself as any more morally implicated than the soldiers on the other side who were shooting back at him.

That answer has stayed with me, because it sounds reasonable on first pass, and I've genuinely tried to steelman it. But here's the disanalogy that keeps nagging at me: if I attack someone without provocation and they fight back in proportionate self-defense, and I then strike them again "because they were attacking me," nobody would say my second blow is just as innocent as their first. Yet Daniel's argument seems to imply that this ordinary asymmetry between aggressor and defender simply washes out once the same structure is scaled up to a war, as long as combatants on both sides are playing by the same rules. I tried rescuing his position by pointing out that which country's army you happen to be born into is itself a matter of luck, and it seems unfair to hold someone fully responsible for facts about a war he had no hand in starting -- but that move seems to prove too much, since it would also excuse a lot of individual wrongdoing that has nothing to do with war. I put the puzzle to a Discord server of people I know mostly through a handful of rationalist-adjacent blogs, and after an hour of back-and-forth we had mostly just restated our starting intuitions at each other in more words.

I would like to actually get to the bottom of this rather than trade intuitions. Was Daniel right that he did nothing wrong, provided he really did abide by the same in-battle rules as everyone else? And more generally, is there a genuine moral difference between fighting on the unjust side of a war and fighting on the just side when both combatants follow the same rules of conduct in battle -- and if there is such a difference, why are our ordinary legal and moral practices so reluctant to recognize it?
