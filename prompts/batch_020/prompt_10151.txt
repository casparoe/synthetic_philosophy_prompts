I'm about a third of the way into a novel about two evolutionary biologists, Marguerite and Owen, who did their doctorates together in the 80s and had a spectacular falling-out over -- basically -- the Gould/Lewontin spandrels fight. Owen still runs a hardcore adaptationist lab (everything's for something, you just haven't found the function yet), Marguerite writes popular-science books these days that are essentially book-length versions of the "Panglossian paradigm" critique, and the plot puts them next to each other at their old advisors retirement dinner, first time they've spoken in decades. I actually went and read the 1979 San Marco paper for this (plus a good chunk of the argument it set off), and fell down a rabbit hole that kind of broke my brain: architecture historians apparently pointed out that the shapes in San Marco aren't technically spandrels at all, they're pendentives, and then Dennett went a step further and argued even the pendentives weren't pure architectural byproducts, because the builders could've used squinches or corbels instead and picked pendentives partly for aesthetic reasons -- so the "necessary byproduct" at the very center of the metaphor turns out to itself be a choice among alternatives, one level up. Which is delicious, and definately the texture I want for the dinner scene.

What I don't want is two people trading Wikipedia talking points at each other, so here's my actual ask: give me one biological trait -- real organism, or something biologically plausible you invent, doesn't matter -- that has that same two-level structure. Meaning: something that looks like a more-or-less inevitable side effect of building some other trait (you can't really avoid having SOME version of it), but where the specific form that side effect takes looks like it's been further tuned by selection for a use of its own, so that a careful biologist genuinely can't sort it cleanly into "spandrel," "exaptation," or straight adaptation. And don't just assert that it's ambiguous -- walk me through why it resists the sorting, what test would settle it, and why nobody's got that test. One request: please don't reach for the chin, the panda's thumb, or the San Marco spandrels themselves -- those are exactly the examples I'm trying to get away from. I need this to sound like two people who've spent their whole careers on it, not two people reciting the same Gould essay collection everybody already owns.
