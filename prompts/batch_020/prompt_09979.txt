Last weekend a friend and I argued for nearly two hours about brain death, and neither of us budged. She's a hospice nurse and insisted that the whole-brain standard is little more than a legal convenience for organ procurement, citing Alan Shewmon's documented cases of patients who met whole-brain-death criteria yet survived for months or years on ventilators — growing, healing wounds, fighting off infections — which she took as evidence that the body remains a genuinely integrated organism rather than a corpse. I countered that mechanically sustained integration of that kind shows only that a collection of organs can be kept functioning together, not that the person is still alive, and that some non-arbitrary line has to separate a living human being from a warm body being kept oxygenated for someone else's benefit.

Write an essay that adjudicates this dispute. Focus specifically on whether Shewmon's cases really refute the "integrative unity" rationale for equating whole-brain death with the death of the organism as a whole, and on whether any rival rationale — such as the President's Council on Bioethics's 2008 attempt to ground brain death in the organism's fundamental drive to engage with its environment (paradigmatically, the drive to breathe) rather than in integration — can rescue the whole-brain standard. Weigh these considerations against the alternative of simply adopting a circulatory-respiratory criterion instead, and close with a definite, justified verdict rather than a mere survey of the positions.
