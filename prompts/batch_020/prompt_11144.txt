So this is going to sound morbid, but I've been working on a longer feature about organ donation, and the whole thing kicked off because I read about the Jahi McMath case -- the California teenager a hospital declared brain dead after a tonsillectomy, whose family then kept her on a ventilator in New Jersey for years (that state lets you object to a brain death diagnosis on religious grounds) before she was finally pronounced dead again, this time by cardiac criteria. Going down that rabbit hole made me realize the definition of death is way less settled then I assumed. Next week I'm interviewing a bioethicist for the piece -- I won't name her here, but she's one of the more prominent defenders of the standard "whole-brain" criterion, the view that irreversible loss of all brain function just IS death of the organism, not a convenient fiction we adopted so transplant teams have organs to work with -- and I want to come at her with sharper questions than she's probably used to fielding.

Can you draft me 8-10 interview questions that actually engage with the substance of her view instead of letting her restate it? I want at least one question that makes her deal with Alan Shewmon's "chronic brain death" cases -- patients who kept healing wounds, fighting off infections, growing, even gestating pregnancies for months after being declared brain dead, which seems like a real problem for the "integrative unity" argument that's supposed to explain why losing your brain means your body stops being one organism rather than just suffering a catastrophic injury. I want one on the newer fight over normothermic regional perfusion in donation-after-circulatory-death cases, where surgeons clamp the vessels feeding the brain and then restart the donor's circulation to the rest of the body mere minutes after declaring death by "permanent" cessation of circulation, which sure looks like un-declaring death so the organs stay fresh. And I want one that pins her down on the gap between "permanent" and "irreversible," since a lot of actual death-determination law leans on that distinction more than most people realize. For each question, tack on a quick follow-up keyed to whatever deflection she's likely to reach for -- my guess is something like "that's a measurement problem, not a conceptual one," or "the brain is special because it's the integrator, period." Please skip the throat-clearing stuff like how she got into bioethics, I can write that part myself.
