I have, somewhat to my surprise, spent this past year serving on my university's laboratory safety review board, which has turned out to be more philosophically interesting than I anticipated. We are currently drafting findings on a fire that destroyed roughly two years of a doctoral student's experimental data. The fire marshal's report identifies two independent conditions, either of which, it states, would on its own have been fully sufficient to produce a fire of exactly the kind and severity that occurred: wiring that facilities services had been warned about during two prior inspections, and a solvent that the lab's own safety officer had allowed to be stored in quantities well beyond what protocol permitted. Because neither condition was counterfactually necessary for the fire—removing either one would simply have left the other fully capable of producing the very same outcome—the board has split into a faction that wants to hold both facilities and the safety officer jointly responsible, and a faction that wants to say that, strictly speaking, neither one caused anything, since the fire would have occurred regardless of what either party did. Reading through the draft report, it struck me—rather on the nose, given that theories of causation are my actual area of research—that we are really just re-running the old dispute between Lewis's counterfactual-dependence theory of causation and the interventionist alternative developed by Woodward and Pearl, except that now there are real budgetary and disciplinary consequences attached, and the case is one of symmetric overdetermination rather than the preemption scenarios that tend to dominate seminar-room discussion.

Before I raise this with my colleagues on the board, I would like to think it through more carefully than our meetings generally allow. Could you set out the most important implications of the disagreement between counterfactual and interventionist theories of causation for how a body like ours should reason about cases of this kind—and, in particular, for whether either framework gives us principled grounds for assigning responsibility to both parties, to neither, or to one but not the other?
