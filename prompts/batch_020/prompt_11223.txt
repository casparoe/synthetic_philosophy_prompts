I've recently been reading up on Émilie du Châtelet, whom I'd only ever known as "the physicist who translated Newton's Principia into French." I was surprised to learn that not long before her death in 1749 she also wrote a systematic essay on happiness, usually translated as the Discourse on Happiness, which apparently wasn't published in any accurate form until long afterward. What struck me most is that she doesn't moralize against gambling the way I'd have expected from an Enlightenment thinker: she lists it right alongside theater-going and fine dining as one of the legitimate recreational pleasures of life. More generally, she argues that being susceptible to illusion is one of five necessary ingredients of happiness, alongside freedom from prejudice, virtue, health, and passions, and she's careful to distinguish these useful illusions from mere errors, which she says are always harmful. Her test case is that illusion works like an optical illusion: it doesn't show us things exactly as they are, but it isn't a lie either, since it just adjusts things to our nature in a way that's harmless, even beneficial, whereas an error, like a religious prejudice or a false scientific belief, gets no such pass. Amusingly, for someone so mathematically gifted, she was apparently also a genuinely reckless card player who ran up large gambling debts at the French court, seemingly enjoying in practice the very illusion — that luck was running her way — that she later defended in print.
