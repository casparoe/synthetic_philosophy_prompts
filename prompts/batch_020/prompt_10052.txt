Two friends of mine, Sam and Lena, made a small bet on a sports-betting exchange: Sam sold Lena a contract that would pay out only if a particular team won the championship, at odds he set himself, and she bought it. Afterward, they argued about whether the trade had actually been rational for either of them.

Sam said he doubted it: if they were both reasonably rational and had followed the same coverage all season, his willingness to sell at those odds should have told Lena the odds favored him, and her willingness to buy should, by the same logic, have told him they favored her — so neither should have traded unless one of them was wrong about the other's rationality. He added that this is basically the logic behind the "no-trade theorems" economists use to argue that purely speculative trade between equally informed, equally rational people shouldn't happen at all.

Lena said that can't be right, since matched voluntary bets like theirs happen constantly between people who mostly aren't fools; an argument that rules out their bet rules out most of what such markets actually do.

Sam answered that most of that trading isn't the "pure" case his argument targets — much of it is people hedging other risks, needing liquidity, or starting from different priors, not two equally informed people speculating against each other. Their bet, he said, really was the pure case, since they'd followed the same stats together all season and neither needed the money — so if the argument ever applies to real people, it should apply here.

Lena's final reply was that this proves too much: if he'd really believed all that beforehand, he'd never have offered her the contract, so invoking it now, after losing, looks like a rationalization.

I couldn't tell who had the better of it at each stage. Please go through the exchange move by move — Sam's opening claim, Lena's volume objection, Sam's refinement, and Lena's closing rejoinder — and assess how much each one gets right. In particular, does Lena's closing point actually undercut Sam's argument, or just his sincerity? And what, if anything, are both of them missing about what has to be true for reasoning like this to apply to an actual bet?
