A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about van Fraassen's Reflection Principle -- the requirement that one's current credence in a proposition, conditional on the supposition that one's credence in it at some later time will be r, must itself be r. My friend maintained that the principle survives its usual counterexamples (anticipated memory loss, anticipated manipulation of one's beliefs, and the like) once it is restricted to cases where the agent has no specific reason to think the future credence will arise from anything other than ordinary rational updating on evidence. I argued that this restriction quietly empties the principle of content: once "no reason to distrust the update" becomes the operative condition, Reflection collapses into the truism that one should defer to opinions one already has no reason to doubt, and the diachronic Dutch-book argument that was meant to justify the principle stops doing any real work. Neither of us was persuaded. Please construct the strongest version of my side: argue that the restricted Reflection Principle is either trivial or covertly circular, and that no non-question-begging line can separate the anticipated belief changes the restriction is meant to exclude from the ones it is meant to protect.
