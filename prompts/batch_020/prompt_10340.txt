I'm reporting a longer feature on scientists and mathematicians who spent their later years trying to put questions of mortality and religion on a rigorous footing, and Kurt Gödel keeps turning out to be the richest case study I have. Piecing the story together from his biographers and his published correspondence, the trajectory looks strikingly continuous: having shown with the incompleteness theorems that any sufficiently powerful formal system must contain truths it cannot prove from within itself, Gödel spent much of his later life extending that same insight into modal logic, arriving at the argument -- built on his axioms about "positive properties" -- that he showed to Dana Scott around 1970 and that surfaced publicly only after his death as his now-famous ontological proof. As far as I can tell, it's this same piece of reasoning that also underlies the letters in which he reassures his mother that a rational, law-governed universe of the sort his own theorems presuppose couldn't possibly allow something as significant as a fully developed personality simply to be annihilated at death.

Given that, I'm trying to work out how to frame this for readers who aren't logicians: why don't philosophers of logic treat the survival of consciousness after death as a serious, load-bearing corollary of Gödel's mathematical legacy, the way the incompleteness theorems themselves are treated as settled? Could you walk me through where exactly the reasoning is generally thought to break down once you move from the formal proof to the claim about an afterlife?
