I'm a PhD student in political theory, currently drafting a dissertation chapter on early modern theories of democracy, and I've run into a problem I can't quite resolve about how to handle Spinoza. He is routinely presented as one of the first thinkers to defend democracy on principled rather than merely pragmatic grounds: in the Theological-Political Treatise, he argues that democracy is the most natural of regimes, because citizens there transfer their right into the collective body of which each remains a part, rather than into a private will set over them, so that they remain as nearly equal and free as they were in the state of nature. Yet the Political Treatise's own chapter on democracy -- the eleventh and final chapter, unfinished, breaking off only a few pages in -- is precisely the chapter in which Spinoza sets out to explain why women should be permanently barred from citizenship. And unlike the other groups he excludes from full political standing, whose exclusion he ties to a dependent status that could in principle change, he presents the disqualification of women as grounded in nature itself, reasoning from the premise that no nation has ever been governed jointly by men and women, or by women alone.

My reading group is split on how much weight this passage should carry against the standard picture of Spinoza as a theorist of democratic equality. Some of us think it is an unprincipled empirical mistake that could be excised without disturbing anything else in his political theory. Others think his own defining move -- identifying natural right strictly with power (potentia), rather than with reason or moral status -- makes some such exclusion close to unavoidable once his false beliefs about the relative power of the sexes are fed into the argument, so that the trouble runs deeper than a mistake he could simply have corrected. I would like to see this second, less comfortable view argued as rigorously as possible before I decide whether to defend it in the chapter itself. Please argue, engaging the actual structure of his theory of natural right and sovereignty rather than just his general enthusiasm for democracy, that Spinoza's exclusion of women is the considered consequence of his naturalism about right rather than an anomaly grafted onto it -- and that, consequently, anyone who wants to preserve his argument for democracy while extending it to women owes us a revision of his metaphysics of right, not merely of his empirical assumptions about women.
