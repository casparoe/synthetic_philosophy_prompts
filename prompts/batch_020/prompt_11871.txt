I sit on my university's honorary degrees committee, and we have reached an impasse that our statute does not obviously resolve. The nominee is the retired president of a nuclear-armed country who, some thirty years ago, while serving as defense minister during a border war his country appeared to be losing, ordered a single nuclear strike on an enemy city. The strike's purpose, which he confirms in a memoir published a decade later, was to shock the enemy government into immediate and unconditional surrender, thereby avoiding a projected year of continued conventional fighting that the general staff estimated would kill far more people, soldiers and civilians combined, than the strike itself. He has spent the decades since helping build his country's postwar democratic institutions, which is the stated occasion for the nomination. One member of the committee, a philosopher by training, has circulated Elizabeth Anscombe's 1956 pamphlet opposing Oxford's decision to give Truman an honorary degree, and argues that her reasoning transfers without remainder: whatever lives the strike may have saved on net, its point was to kill civilians as the means of coercing surrender, and on Anscombe's view, choosing to kill the innocent as a means is murder regardless of the arithmetic, a verdict no "necessity" defense can touch. Other members do not dispute that the act may have been wrong, or even murder in her sense, but think this concedes less than she assumes: they want to distinguish honoring a life's subsequent work from certifying the whole of it, and some would place a statesman coerced by catastrophe into a terrible choice in a different moral category from the private killer her argument was built to capture, closer to what later writers on political ethics have called the problem of "dirty hands."

Our statute allows the committee to withdraw a nomination already forwarded to the Senate only for conduct that would bring the university "into disrepute," a phrase nobody anticipated having to interpret for a case like this one. I need a short written opinion to circulate before the vote. It should recommend one specific outcome, whether that is to proceed, withdraw, or something short of either, and it should engage Anscombe's actual argument on its own terms rather than collapsing it into a generic debate about collateral damage, while giving the losing side's strongest version a genuine hearing rather than a token one.
