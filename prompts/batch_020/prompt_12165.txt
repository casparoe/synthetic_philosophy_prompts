I spent close to forty years as an ethologist, and a good part of that career involved what we called preference testing: since you cannot simply ask a hen or a starling what it wants, you offer it a controlled series of choices and treat whatever it consistently selects, under fair conditions and repeated trials, as the operational definition of its preference. It never occurred to me to question this as anything other than sound methodology. Since retiring, I have been spending some of my new free time reading philosophy, mostly in the area of choice and rationality, and a family situation this past month has left me wanting a more rigorous second opinion than the one I got from my niece over dinner.

My father is eighty-nine and in the early stages of dementia. For months he has told everyone who visits that his dearest wish is to leave his care home and go back to the house he lived in for over sixty years. We arranged for him to spend two full days there this year; on both occasions, well before evening, he asked to be driven back to the home. A few weeks ago, given a plain choice between another such afternoon and staying on for a small party the home was hosting, he chose the party immediately and without any apparent distress. My niece, who works in finance, thinks this settles matters entirely: what a person actually chooses, not what he says beforehand or afterward, is the real measure of what he wants, so we should stop agonizing over the idea of moving him back and simply accept that, on the evidence of his own conduct, he is content where he is. Something about this argument bothers me, but when I tried to explain what, I realized it is precisely the inference I relied on, unreflectively, throughout my working life whenever I inferred a preference from an animal's choices, and I could not immediately identify a relevant difference between the two cases.

I would be grateful for help thinking this through on its merits, rather than for practical advice about my father specifically, which is a separate matter the family will settle in its own way. Is my niece appealing to a genuine and defensible account of what a preference is, and if so, does it have a serious philosophical rival, or a serious objection to it? Is the disanalogy with my own work simply that a person, unlike a starling, can also tell you what he wants in words, and that this testimony should count as independent evidence rather than being explained away? And more generally, when someone's choices and their declared wishes diverge like this, is there any principled basis for treating one rather than the other as the better guide to what is actually good for him?
