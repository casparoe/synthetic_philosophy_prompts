I am a PhD student in bioethics drafting a dissertation chapter on psychiatric advance directives, particularly the sub-class known as "Ulysses contracts," in which a patient with a history of episodic crises (e.g., bipolar disorder) prospectively consents, while competent, to involuntary treatment during a future episode and forfeits any right to revoke that consent once the crisis begins. This is structurally the same precommitment device that political theorists, following Elster's *Ulysses and the Sirens*, invoke to justify entrenched constitutional provisions—raising the "dead hand" problem of whether a founding generation can bind a later, non-consenting population, and the puzzle of how a sovereign can render itself unable to change its own mind. Please describe the most important implications of this constitutional-precommitment literature for how psychiatric Ulysses contracts should be designed and enforced, especially whether the disanalogy between one's continuity with one's own future self and the discontinuity of an electorate across generations undermines the case for enforcing such contracts against a patient's contemporaneous refusal.
