I'm a novelist, and the book I'm currently drafting follows five strangers whose paths cross for roughly ninety seconds on a subway platform when an elderly man collapses: two of them stop to help, one hesitates and then walks on, and two seem barely to register that anything happened at all. The rest of the novel moves backward and forward through each of their private histories, daring the reader to decide whether those ninety seconds revealed something stable and true about who each person is, or whether the platform did essentially all the causal work and the "character" we're tempted to read into it afterward is really a retrospective illusion. Since that's essentially the situationist challenge to virtue ethics wearing the costume of a plot, I've started recording a short interview series to accompany the book's publication, and my next guest is a philosopher who has spent much of her career defending the reality and moral significance of character traits against exactly that challenge.

A few early readers of the manuscript have pushed back hard on the "character" side of things, so I want this interview to show I've actually sat with the strongest version of their objection rather than waved at it. Could you draft eight to ten questions for me? I want at least one question that engages a specific piece of evidence rather than "the studies" in general: something built around a design like Darley and Batson's experiment with seminarians who were induced to hurry before passing someone in apparent distress, asking exactly what a single result of that kind is supposed to license us to conclude about a person's traits -- especially given how often it gets paired with Mischel's finding that trait-behavior correlations across situations rarely climb past .3. I want at least one question that asks whether retreating to narrower, if-this-situation-then-that-behavior "local" traits, in the spirit of Mischel and Shoda's later work, saves virtue talk only by quietly abandoning the cross-situational stability that Aristotle's account of virtue was supposed to have in the first place. And I want at least one that asks whether her own emphasis on practical wisdom -- virtue as the capacity to respond well to the particulars of a situation, rather than a disposition that fires identically regardless of context -- means the situationist literature was attacking a caricature of character that no careful Aristotelian ever held, and if so, why that objection was received as a serious threat to virtue ethics rather than dismissed as a misreading of the view. For each of these three, please add a follow-up question keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so the exchange doesn't simply stop at the first parry. Close the list with one question, leaving her no room to hedge, that asks her to name a specific empirical finding that would actually count as evidence against her position.
