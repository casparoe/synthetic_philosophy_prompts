I'm putting together a session for my grad seminar on death across philosophical traditions — we're pairing Epicurus and the Stoics against a chunk of the Zhuangzi — and there's one passage I've now reread about six times and still can't tell whether I'm confused because of Zhuangzi or because of the translation, so I could use a second pair of eyes before class tomorrow. I'm using the old Legge version (1891, so it's out of copyright and easy to hand out), but the Wade-Giles-ish names take a second to place (Dze-lâi is Zilai, Dze-lî is Zili), and Legge is constantly stuffing his own bracketed words into the sentences to smooth the syntax, so it's genuinely hard to tell where his glossing stops and Zhuangzi starts. Here's the bit, from Book VI, "The Great and Most Honoured Master," right after the story of the hunchback who marvels that the "Creator" made him so deformed:

Before long Dze-lâi fell ill, and lay gasping at the point of death, while his wife and children stood around him wailing. Dze-lî went to ask for him, and said to them, 'Hush! Get out of the way! Do not disturb him as he is passing through his change.' Then, leaning against the door, he said (to the dying man), 'Great indeed is the Creator! What will He now make you to become? Where will He take you to? Will He make you the liver of a rat, or the arm of an insect?'

Dze-lâi replied, 'Wherever a parent tells a son to go, east, west, south, or north, he simply follows the command. The Yin and Yang are more to a man than his parents are. If they are hastening my death, and I do not quietly submit to them, I shall be obstinate and rebellious. There is the great Mass (of nature);—I find the support of my body in it; my life is spent in toil on it; my old age seeks ease on it; at death I find rest on it:—what has made my life a good will make my death also a good.

'Here now is a great founder, casting his metal. If the metal were to leap up (in the pot), and say, "I must be made into a (sword like the) Mo-yeh," the great founder would be sure to regard it as uncanny. So, again, when a form is being fashioned in the mould of the womb, if it were to say, "I must become a man; I must become a man," the Creator would be sure to regard it as uncanny. When we once understand that heaven and earth are a great melting-pot, and the Creator a great founder, where can we have to go to that shall not be right for us? We are born as from a quiet sleep, and we die to a calm awaking.'

Two things about this are tripping me up. First, this "Creator"/"great founder" who looks at the half-cast metal and judges it "uncanny" is doing alot of personal, opinionated work for a Daoist universe — a few paragraphs earlier in this same chapter Legge has Zhuangzi describe the Tao itself as a thing that "does nothing and has no bodily form," which is about as un-agential as you can get. So is the foundry-master just a colorful stand-in for that impersonal Tao, or is Zhuangzi genuinely trading on the idea of something that judges and prefers — and if it's the former, what does the personification actually buy him that a less agential image wouldn't? Second, the whole metal/fetus bit is supposed to show that clinging to your current shape is presumptuous, but it only works as an example if the unformed ore or the unformed fetus already has a preference to be presumptuous about, which is exactly what it doesn't have yet. So is Zhuangzi saying Zilai's fear of losing his form is exactly as confused as ore insisting on becoming a named sword, or is that too fast, given that Zilai — unlike the ore — already has a settled identity that the ore never had to begin with? I'd love a reading that makes those two peices hang together, rather than just telling my students that old texts are sometimes gorgeously inconsistent and leaving it at that.
