I'm at work on a novel about Simone Weil's last year in London, and I've reached the chapter I've been circling for months: the scene where she actually makes her case against political parties, dramatized rather than summarized. Historically, she wrote a policy memo that spring urging the Free French leadership to bar political parties altogether from any future French constitution, at a moment when the exile government itself was weighing whether to formally recognize party organizations within its own ranks. She resigned her post that summer on a matter of principle and died within weeks, apparently without ever learning what had become of the note.

Below is the scene as it stands, between Weil and a fictional colleague of mine, Delorme, who has been asked to route her memo upward. Nothing in quotation marks is an actual line from her essay -- I don't trust my own grasp of the English translation enough to lift from it, so I've paraphrased her reasoning throughout in my own words.

London, March 1943. The Free French offices smelled of damp wool and tinned coffee. Delorme had read the note through twice before he set it down.

"You want us to tell the General to recommend banning parties. In writing. While we're still trying to convince Washington that a government without them can even call itself democratic."

"I want us to tell him what parties are," Weil said, "not what we would prefer to believe they are."

"They're how a people governs itself."

"They're machines. You feed them subscriptions and evenings and small loyalties, and what comes out the far end isn't the people's will. It's a passion, manufactured, sold back to people as their own opinion." She didn't raise her voice; she rarely did. "Ask a man what his party believes and he'll answer at once. Ask him to defend a single plank of it from first principles, and watch him discover he can't. He was never asked to think it. Only to hold it."

"That's human laziness. You can't legislate against laziness."

"No. But left alone, one man's errors and the next man's cancel out, the way Rousseau said they would, if each is truly left to judge for himself. A party doesn't ask two hundred thousand members to judge. It hands them one passion to carry together, and calls the carrying unity."

Delorme almost smiled. "You make it sound like a disease."

"I make it sound like what it does. Watch what any party actually wants, once you strip away what it says it wants on a given Tuesday. Not to be right about this question or that one. More: more branches, more subscriptions, more seats -- with no idea, ever, of when enough would be enough. Find me the party secretary who has stood up at a congress and said, we have grown as far as we should." She capped her pen. "Ask your own colleagues, the ones drafting position papers this week for questions they privately doubt. Ask them what they're protecting when they hold the line: the truth of the matter, or their section's standing at the next congress. There's no crime in choosing the second. The lie is in calling it the first."

He did not send the note upward that week. Nor, so far as she would ever learn, the week after.
