So I had a long back-and-forth with a friend last night about Jeffrey conditionalization -- the rule for updating when evidence doesn't come in as a clean, certain fact but just shifts your confidence in some proposition. She argued that once you fix your new probability for the evidence proposition, the rule pins down everything else, full stop, no wiggle room. I said that's only true one update at a time: if two seperate pieces of iffy evidence come in one after another, the order you process them in can change what you end up believing, which seems bad if your final beliefs are supposed to be independent of which piece you happened to learn about first. She said that's nuts, and basically accused me of misremembering how conditionalization works, since ordinary Bayesian updating obviously doesn't care about order. Neither of us could actually produce numbers on the spot, so: can you build a concrete toy example -- a prior over a handful of possibilities, plus two separate uncertain-evidence updates -- where doing the two Jeffrey updates in one order gives a different final distribution than doing them in the other order? Show the actual numbers, then explain in plain terms what feature of the setup is doing the work.
