Last month I tried a sensory-deprivation float tank at a wellness centre, having heard that some writers and athletes use them to unwind and think more clearly. You lie back in a shallow pool of water saturated with Epsom salts and warmed to skin temperature, sealed inside a completely dark, soundproof pod; within a few minutes almost every external reference point disappears. There is no light and no sound, and because the water holds you perfectly buoyant at body temperature, you gradually stop being able to tell where your skin ends and the water begins, or where your arms and legs are in relation to one another.

About halfway through the session I had a genuinely strange experience: it felt as though my body had simply dropped out of the picture altogether. I had no impression of its shape, weight, temperature, or position, and yet I was unmistakably still there, thinking my own thoughts and entirely certain that I — whatever that "I" amounted to — existed. When I mentioned this afterward to a friend who is finishing a doctorate in neuroscience, she was not impressed: she said all it showed was that my brain's threshold for registering faint, ambiguous signals had briefly been exceeded, and that nothing about the episode suggests my mind is, or could be, anything other than my body and brain doing certain things — the feeling of bodilessness was just a temporary gap in processing, not a discovery about what I really am.

I find her explanation reasonable as far as it goes, but it doesn't seem to fully engage with what struck me about the experience in the first place: if I could be completely certain of my own existence while being aware of nothing whatsoever about my body, doesn't that at least suggest that my awareness of myself is not the same thing as, and doesn't depend on, my awareness of my body? I'd like to know whether my friend's response really disposes of this thought, or whether it is missing a more basic point that no amount of neuroscience could, by itself, settle.
