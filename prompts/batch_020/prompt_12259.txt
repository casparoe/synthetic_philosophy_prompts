So this is a weird one but it's genuinely splitting our committee down the middle. I'm a philosophy PhD student (decision theory/formal epistemology-ish) and on the side I volunteer on the grants committee for a small longtermist fund that mostly moves five- and six-figure amounts to x-risk and s-risk adjacent projects. I've been TAing a decision theory seminar this term, which is probably why I got roped into writing this up. Last round we got a proposal that finally forces us to take an actual position on evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL) instead of just gesturing at it over drinks at EAG.

Quick context in case it's not your corner of the literature: the idea, which traces back to Oesterheld's original write-up and is now mostly developed out of the Center on Long-Term Risk, is that if the universe (or multiverse) is large or infinite, there are causally disconnected agents out there whose decision procedures correlate with ours even though no signal or bargaining is possible -- so under evidential rather than causal decision theory, acting as if we're implementing a "compromise" utility function that also serves very alien value systems is itself evidence that our distant correlates are reciprocating for ours. The applicant wants two things: (1) a year of salary to keep formalizing how you'd even estimate a compromise utility function over a totally unobserved distribution of distant agents' values, and (2) for the fund to earmark something like 10-15% of next year's grantmaking as a standing "cooperative portfolio" steered by ECL reasoning instead of our normal cause-prio process, starting this round.

About half the committee thinks this is exactly the kind of update a longtermist fund should be willing to make, since we already fund plenty of stuff on much flimsier expected-value chains; the other half thinks it stacks contestable premise on contestable premise -- EDT over CDT, a specific cosmology, a value-distribution nobody can check -- into something unfalsifiable in principle, and worries it's a foot in the door for justifying literally any allocation as "acausal cooperation." We owe the board our recommendation by next week. Could you draft the committee's actual decision memo -- seperate calls on the salary line and the portfolio earmark, whether that's fund, decline, or fund-with-conditions -- with the concrete reasoning behind each and a realistic timeframe or review point? Whichever way you land, don't just wave at "more research needed": engage seriously with the strongest version of whichever side you end up not recommending, since that's the half of the committee I actually have to go persuade.
