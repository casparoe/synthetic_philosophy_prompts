I have spent the better part of a decade reading LessWrong, the EA Forum, and their surrounding blogs, so my default reaction to questions of the form "whom do you save when you cannot save everyone" has always been the scope-sensitive, shut-up-and-multiply one: more lives saved is better, full stop, and any hesitation is probably scope insensitivity in disguise. That default got unsettled a few months ago when an EA Forum thread on pandemic ventilator-triage protocols resurfaced Taurek's 1977 paper "Should the Numbers Count?", and now, inconveniently, my own volunteer organization has run into a real-life version of exactly the dilemma he poses.

I serve on the steering committee of a small volunteer backcountry medical response network that covers a stretch of terrain with no reliable helicopter access, and which keeps its own limited stock of antivenom for the one locally venomous snake whose bite is reliably fatal within hours if untreated. Three weeks ago, two incidents were radioed in almost simultaneously: a solo hiker bitten badly enough that saving him would require our entire remaining stock, and, on the far side of the ridge, a group of six volunteers on a trail crew who had disturbed a den and were each bitten badly enough to die untreated, but who could collectively be saved by that same total quantity of antivenom split six ways. There was no way to get more in time and no way to treat both incidents. The on-call medic diverted everything to the group of six, reasoning that six lives obviously outweigh one, and the hiker died. Nobody doubts she acted in good faith, but the episode revealed that we have no actual protocol for this class of case, and the committee is now split: most members treat the six-over-one call as simply correct, while a minority -- myself included, having gone back and reread Taurek -- think we have never really confronted his argument that treating the larger group's stake as simply "more" in the relevant sense is a substantive and contestable move, not a free inference from the arithmetic, and that his own proposed alternative for cases where everyone stands to lose the same thing (he suggests some kind of chance procedure rather than an automatic rule that favors the larger group) deserves genuine engagement rather than dismissal as reckless.

The committee meets in ten days, and I have been asked to circulate a memo beforehand recommending a standing protocol -- to be adopted provisionally for the coming season and revisited afterward -- that on-call medics could actually apply in the field without relitigating moral philosophy under pressure. Please draft that memo. It should commit to one specific rule (straightforward numbers-maximizing, some form of lottery, weighted or unweighted, or something else) rather than staying neutral between options; it should explain Taurek's argument against the aggregative default in terms the committee's majority will recognize as a real objection rather than a debating trick; and it should then give the numbers-count side its strongest hearing in turn. In particular, I would like it to address how a contractualist argument for the moral relevance of numbers -- of the kind Scanlon offers in response to exactly this challenge -- differs from the cruder "just add up the lives saved" reasoning that Taurek was originally attacking, since I suspect that distinction is where our committee's actual disagreement lives.
