I'm a cognitive science major whose seminar on theories of well-being has just covered idealized-preference accounts, and I sit on my university's committee overseeing the registrar's new AI advising assistant. Its most contested feature delays any course-drop request by 48 hours when it conflicts with goals the student stated in earlier chats, showing him those past statements before the drop goes through. The case forcing our review: a pre-med sophomore tried to drop organic chemistry at 1 a.m. before the deadline, and the tool quoted back his own words from advising sessions about keeping his pre-med track open 'no matter how bad a semester gets.' He didn't drop the class and later thanked the tool in a survey, but also filed a complaint calling the delay patronizing and denying the tool's right to decide which version of him is real. The committee is split: some say this just holds him to commitments in his own words; others say deciding which past statements count as his 'real' preference is a judgment call the designers are quietly making for him. Draft the policy memo for next week's meeting — keep the feature, drop it, or restrict it to opt-in or irreversible decisions — and give the losing side's strongest version its due, not a token concession.
