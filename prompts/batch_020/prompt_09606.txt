I'm a retired marine biologist who only got properly into philosophy the last couple of years, since I stopped working full time. I've been going to a little Kierkegaard reading group that meets at my library, and last session, on his stuff about the press and "the public," turned into a real argument between two of the regulars that's been bugging me since.

Frank's line: Kierkegaard spent years hammering the press and "the public" for letting people snipe and pass judgment anonymously with zero skin in the game, but for most of the 1840s he published his own big books -- Either/Or, Fear and Trembling, the Postscript -- under a pile of made-up names, and only admitted he'd written them years later, in that declaration tacked onto the end of the Postscript. Same move, Frank says: hide behind a mask, cash in the credit once its safe.

Miriam said thats not the same thing at all. The pseudonyms weren't cover, they were more like characters, each one living out a different way of existing -- aesthetic, ethical, whatever -- and when he finally stepped forward he practically begged readers to quote the pseudonym and not him. Compare that to the anonymous reviewers he was actually angry at, who wanted to take shots with no name attached, ever. And when the Corsaren paper went after him by name, he didn't hide from that either -- by her account he more or less provoked it, then stood there and took the public mockery for a couple of years.

Frank came back with: fine, but he still got years of Copenhagen gossiping about who "Victor Eremita" really was, which is exactly the kind of idle chatter about a phantom public figure he later says is corrosive -- he was feeding the very machine he condemned, and only owned up once the books had already made their mark.

Miriam's answer was that the whole point of what she called "indirect communication" was to stop readers from just deferring to, or dismissing, an idea because of whose name was on it -- so the uncertainty was doing real philosophical work, not covering his backside.

I honestly can't referee this one. Could you go through it move by move and say who's got the better of each exchange, not just who wins overall? Is there something about Kierkegaard's actual view of the press and the public that they're both missing, which would settle this more cleanly than either of their positions? I'd like to settle it at next week's meeting, so just end with one line, in exactly this format: \verdict{Frank} or \verdict{Miriam}, whichever one comes out ahead overall.
