The question the students were given reads: "Gregory Kavka's toxin puzzle is often read as posing a problem for the thesis that a rational agent can intend, now, to perform some future action only if she will still have sufficient reason to perform it when the time comes. (a) Reconstruct the puzzle and state precisely where the difficulty lies: is it a puzzle about the rationality of drinking the toxin, about the rationality of intending to drink it, or about something else? (b) Explain how Edward McClennen's notion of resolute choice is meant to address the puzzle. (c) Say briefly whether Michael Bratman's planning theory of intention, on its own, supplies the resources to explain how the intention could be both formed and sustained." Here is the student's answer in full:

"Kavka's toxin puzzle asks us to imagine being offered a large reward for forming, tonight, the intention to drink a mild but unpleasant toxin tomorrow at noon; the reward is paid out solely on the basis of whether the intention was formed, and by the time noon arrives the money is already settled one way or the other, so drinking the toxin at that point would serve no further purpose. The puzzle is not really about whether it is rational to drink the toxin -- everyone agrees that once the reward is secured, drinking would be pointless self-harm. The puzzle is about whether, tonight, you can actually form the intention to drink it, given that you can already foresee you will have no reason to comply tomorrow. In this respect the case is structurally close to Newcomb's Problem: both present a situation where causal decision theorists and evidential decision theorists come apart on what the rational choice is, and both trade on a tension between what is rational to do 'in the moment' and what is rational to have committed to beforehand.

McClennen's resolute choice model is meant to dissolve the puzzle by having the agent look ahead at the outset, recognize that her later self will have no incentive to go through with drinking, and build that anticipated fact into the plan she adopts now; the resolute chooser is 'resolute' in the sense that she takes full account, in advance, of how her future self will actually behave, and picks the best plan achievable given that constraint, rather than naively assuming she can bind her future self to anything at all. On McClennen's view, this lets us see that Kavka's agent is not irrational for failing to drink the toxin -- she was rational all along in having anticipated exactly that outcome.

Bratman's planning theory of intention adds a further resource: because intentions function as relatively stable 'plan states' that resist constant reconsideration, an agent who has formed the intention to drink will simply carry it out without re-opening the question of its merits at noon, and this by itself is enough to show that the intention can be both formed and sustained, which resolves the puzzle Kavka raises."
