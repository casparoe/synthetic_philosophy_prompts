I've been a longtime lurker on LessWrong and the Alignment Forum, and I've noticed that a lot of the discourse around mesa-optimization and inner alignment quietly presupposes an answer to an old problem in philosophy of mind: what makes an internal state of a system "about" one thing in the world rather than another, when that state was shaped by a selection process (evolution, gradient descent, operant conditioning) that couldn't itself distinguish between the candidate targets during training? This is a version of what philosophers call the disjunction problem, usually illustrated with a frog whose bug-detecting neuron fires just as readily for a fly as for a BB pellet flicked across its visual field: does the neuron's firing represent "fly," the disjunctive "fly-or-BB-pellet," or something coarser like "small dark moving thing"? I went back through the main naturalistic proposals for resolving this and came away unable to tell which of them actually earn their solution rather than just relocating the indeterminacy somewhere less obvious, so I would like a second, more systematic opinion.

Please rank the following theories by how well each solves the disjunction problem on its own terms: without covertly reintroducing the very intentional vocabulary the theory is meant to naturalize, and without making "misrepresentation" so permissive that the theory can no longer explain why one reading of the disjunction is correct and the others are errors. The theories are: Millikan's biosemantics, which fixes content via the historically selected "Normal explanation" of a mechanism's proper function; Papineau's success-based teleosemantics, which fixes content via whatever needs to be true for the consuming mechanisms to succeed; Dretske's informational teleosemantics, which fixes content at the moment a learned or evolved indicator function is first acquired; Neander's teleosemantics, which locates content in the proximate triggering condition that a perceptual response mechanism was selected to track; Fodor's asymmetric-dependence theory, which drops the appeal to selection history in favor of a purely causal story about which correlations are law-like and which are merely parasitic on them; and Cummins's ahistorical, causal-role notion of function, offered as a rival foundation for "function" that doesn't depend on evolutionary history at all. Feel free to invoke Davidson's Swampman as a stress test wherever it helps you decide between two placements.
