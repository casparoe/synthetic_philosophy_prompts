So, I did condensed matter physics for about thirty years before retiring, and lately I've fallen down a philosophy of science rabbit hole. Here's what's bugging me: people keep pointing to renormalization group universality as the killer case for "real" emergence -- the fact that a fluid right at its liquid-gas critical point and a magnet at its Curie point end up with exactly the same critical exponents even though the microscopic physics (jostling molecules vs. spins) has nothing in common. Fine, I buy that, and I even buy the RG explanation for why it happens: everything flows toward the same fixed point, and the microscopic details become "irrelevant" in the technical sense. What I can't get past is that strictly speaking none of this is true of any actual, finite hunk of matter -- real non-analytic singularities only show up in the infinite-system, thermodynamic limit, so the "sharp" universal behavior everyone points to as proof of emergence is a feature of an idealized limit no real system ever reaches. Doesnt that cut the other way, like a frictionless plane -- a handy fiction rather than evidence something ontologically new is going on? I read a philosophy book, "The Devil in the Details" I think it was called, that leans on that very limit as proof of genuine emergence rather than a reason to be suspicious of it, and I couldn't follow the move at all. Can you walk me through, in plain terms, why leaning on a mathematical idealization is supposed to earn you real metaphysical novelty instead of just being a convenient shortcut?
