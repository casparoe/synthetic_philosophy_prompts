Over the last year or so I've fallen down a bit of a rabbit hole reading everything I can find, on my own time and with no formal training in either philosophy or computer science, about how we should even talk about what a large language model is doing when it produces text. The framing that has stuck with me hardest is the idea, which I first ran into on some AI forums, that a model like GPT isn't a single agent with beliefs and goals at all, but something closer to a "simulator" that can generate any number of different characters, or "simulacra," the way a novelist can voice a dozen different characters without being any one of them. I mentioned this to a couple of friends who write software for a living, and while they had plenty to say about transformers and training runs, neither of them had really sat with the question of whether "agent" is even the right kind of word to reach for in the first place, which only made me want to think the whole thing through more carefully.

I run a small interview podcast as a hobby, and next month I'm recording an episode with a researcher who has become known for defending this simulator picture quite rigorously, including a stock answer she gives whenever someone asks whether the model "wants" to keep a conversation going or "wants" to avoid saying something harmful: she says wanting is only ever a property of the characters being simulated, never of the simulator doing the simulating. I don't want the episode to just be her repeating that line, since I've now heard some version of it several times online. Could you draft eight or nine interview questions that push on the places where I suspect this picture gets strained -- for instance, whether that simulator/simulacrum distinction still does real work once we remember that simulacra are the only thing anyone ever actually talks to; how the picture is supposed to survive the shift from a raw base model, which does seem to behave like an open-ended predictor of any plausible continuation, to a model fine-tuned with human feedback into having one fairly consistent voice and set of preferences; and what she would say to someone who has wrapped such a model in a loop with tools and persistent memory so that it chains actions together toward a goal over many steps, since that starts to look less like "simulating a character" and more like simply acting. For each question, could you also add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so the conversation doesn't just stop at her first line of defense?
