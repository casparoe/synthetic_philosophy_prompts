We'd just finished the exercise where you show that excluded middle, double-negation elimination, and Peirce's law are all classically equivalent but Coq won't hand you any of them for free without importing an axiom, and two people in the group, Marcus and Deb, got into it about what that actually *means*. I wrote down the gist right after because it was more interesting then I expected, but I honestly can't tell who came out ahead, and I don't have any philosophy background to fall back on here.

(1) Marcus said the whole thing is way less deep then people make it sound: intuitionistic logic is basically typed functional programming in a trenchcoat. Via Curry-Howard, proofs are programs and propositions are types, so rejecting excluded middle isn't some belief about the nature of mathematical objects -- it's just a fact about proof terms, since there's no general program that decides an arbitrary proposition. Once you see that, he said, there's no separate philosophical argument left to have.

(2) Deb pushed back: Curry-Howard is a correspondence Howard wrote up as an unpublished manuscript in 1969, decades after Brouwer and Heyting were already doing this. If you actually read Brouwer, she said, he was suspicious of formal systems capturing mathematics at all -- he rejected excluded middle because he treated math as a mental construction unfolding over time, so a universal claim over an infinite domain just isn't true-or-false until somebody has a construction settling it. That's a claim about the nature of mathematical objects, not a fact about compilers, and it predates the concept of a "proof term" by decades.

(3) Marcus's comeback: sure, but nobody doing type theory today believes in Brouwer's "creating subject" stuff. Heyting formalized the logic and Kleene gave it rigorous computational semantics not too long after, and that's the version everybody actually uses in Coq and Agda. Treating Brouwer's original motivation as the "real" meaning of intuitionistic logic, he said, is like treating Newton's fluxions as the "real" meaning of calculus just because Newton got there first.

(4) Deb's closer: that's a genetic fallacy running backwards -- a formal system outliving its original motivation doesn't make that motivation irrelevant to why the rules are shaped the way they are in the first place. She brought up Dummett, who argued for intuitionistic logic on totally different grounds than computation: a theory-of-meaning argument that understanding a statement has to be manifestable in how we use or verify it, rather than resting on possibly-unknowable truth conditions, which independently pushes you toward dropping excluded middle. That argument has nothing to do with programs and stands or falls on its own.
