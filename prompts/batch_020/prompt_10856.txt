I spent my working life as an evolutionary biologist, and only since retiring have I started reading seriously in philosophy of science, particularly work on anthropic reasoning. One argument I cannot stop turning over is the physicist Brandon Carter's old "hard-steps" case bearing on the Fermi paradox: because we happen to find ourselves roughly halfway through the Sun's main-sequence lifetime rather than early in it, Carter argued that the major transitions behind our own existence -- the origin of life, the evolution of eukaryotes, multicellularity, intelligence -- must each be intrinsically rare, slow events, individually unlikely to complete within any given star's habitable window. If he is right, intelligent life is exceedingly rare, and the "Great Filter" lies safely behind us rather than ahead. I have also read the objection that this argument, however elegant, illegitimately extracts a stable rate from Earth's single history, since we have no second case against which to check whether our history was typical -- which strikes me as a cousin of the "inverse gambler's fallacy" raised against other anthropic arguments. Please write a dialogue between two equally sharp former colleagues of mine -- one trained, like Carter, as a physicist before moving into astrobiology and persuaded by his reasoning, the other a career evolutionary biologist convinced it smuggles in far more than the evidence can support -- who stay behind after a seminar to argue this out at the whiteboard. Have them press on the specific weak points in each other's positions, until it becomes clear whether they actually disagree about the evidence or about something deeper: what anthropic reasoning from a single case can ever be expected to establish.
