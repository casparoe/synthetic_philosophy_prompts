My interest in this started when a coworker mentioned using the Kelly criterion for position sizing in trading; digging further, I found that some people extend it into a much larger claim — that rational choice under uncertainty should generally be grounded in geometric rather than arithmetic expectation, with Kelly-optimal betting as just the cleanest illustration. Write a dialogue between a Kelly bettor and an expected-value maximizer who are repeatedly offered the same favorable bet — a coin with a known edge, where each round both may stake any fraction of their current bankroll. The Kelly bettor stakes the Kelly fraction every round and argues that geometric expectation is the truer general standard of rationality; the expected-value maximizer instead bets to maximize the expected value of their bankroll — which, in this setup, means betting everything every round — and argues that Kelly's growth-optimality is merely a special fact about logarithmic utility, with no claim on rationality more broadly. Have each press the other on the hardest version of their position: does the fact that the Kelly strategy wins "almost surely" in the long run establish anything a single-shot expected-value calculation should care about, and is the ensemble-average/time-average distinction a genuine, utility-independent argument for logarithmic growth, or does it just relabel an ordinary preference for lower variance? Neither character should land an easy knockout; each must answer the other's strongest objection rather than simply restating their own position.
