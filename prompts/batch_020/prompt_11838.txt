Long time lurker on LW/ACX, first time actually asking about something instead of just reading. This came out of our meetup's decision theory night and its been rattling around in my head since, because neither person backed down and I honestly can't tell who's right.

We'd just gone through Parfit's Hitchhiker (from Reasons and Persons): you're dying of thirst in the desert, a driver with a perfect lie detector says she'll take you to town but only if she predicts you'll pay her $1000 once you're safely there, and the question everyone argues about is whether you actually pay once you arrive. Two people in the group, Wes and Nora, got into it afterward and wouldn't let it go:

(1) Wes said obviously you pay -- the whole point of the case is that "rational" has to mean "has the disposition that gets good outcomes," not "compute the act that's optimal given a fixed past," since CDT agents get left to die and that alone shows CDT is just wrong about what rationality even is.

(2) Nora said that's too quick: sure, the disposition to pay is the one you want walking into the desert, but once you're standing in town and the driver's long gone, what reason do you actually have to hand over the money? The good outcome already happened, and it was caused by her prediction, not by any future payment -- answering "what should I do now" with "whatever disposition would've been good to have" doesn't actually follow.

(3) Wes replied that's a false seperation: with a truly perfect predictor there's no possible world where you have the paying-disposition and then don't pay, so "the disposition already worked, now do the locally smart thing" secretly assumes you can peel the act apart from the disposition, which by hypothesis you can't.

(4) Nora said the actual Parfit move is subtler than either of their positions: he grants the disposition to pay is the one you have most reason to cause yourself to have, while still calling the resulting payment irrational by ordinary act-based standards -- that's the "rational irrationality" thing, and it's not the same as Wes's "rationality just means winning."

(5) Wes said fine, but then Nora's view is unstable: if, once in town, he could secretly swallow a pill that turned him back into a pure self-interested calculator with zero chance of anyone finding out, wouldn't Nora's "rationally irrational" agent take it and keep the money? If yes, the disposition was never real to begin with. If no, she owes an explanation that isn't just "because that disposition wins," which is his view with extra steps.

(6) Nora said wanting the pill doesn't show the disposition is fake, it shows genuine trustworthiness has to survive exactly that kind of temptation -- a driver who could tell you were the "pay-unless-I-can-secretly-weasel-out" type wouldn't pick you up either, so needing robustness against the pill is just a further fact about how thorough the disposition has to be, not a collapse into Wes's view.

They just went in circles after that and nobody else in the group wanted to touch it. Can you go through the six moves and say who's got the better of it at each one (doesn't have to be one-sided at every step), and flag anything substantive you think both of them are missing?
