As a novelist, I'm drawn to philosophical thought experiments that already read like parables, and I keep returning to the "wrong kind of reasons" problem that besets buck-passing accounts of value -- T. M. Scanlon's idea that to be good is just to have properties that provide reasons for certain favorable attitudes or responses. The trouble, pressed by critics such as Rabinowicz and Rønnow-Rasmussen, is that a demon's threat to harm you unless you manage to feel real admiration for something contemptible gives you a reason to admire it without making it one bit more admirable. Write me a short story, roughly 1,200 words, that takes this structure seriously as narrative rather than puzzle: a character is offered something they desperately want, on condition that they actually come to hold some attitude -- love, reverence, moral approval -- toward a person or object that plainly doesn't merit it. Stay with the mechanics of manufacturing the attitude on command: whether it can be done at all, what it costs if it can, and whether the gap between feeling and object stays visible from inside even after apparent success. Don't let any character state the philosophical distinction outright; the dilemma should live in what they do, not in what they say.
