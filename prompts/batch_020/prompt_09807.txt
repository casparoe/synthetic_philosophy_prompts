I spent my working life as a physicist, and only started properly reading philosophy after retiring, which is how I've wound up down a rabbit hole on decision theory. Hofstadter's old idea of 'superrationality' won't leave me alone: two agents in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, if they reason identically and know it, should both cooperate, since each one's choice is evidence of what the other will choose. It smells like a symmetry argument out of physics -- identical systems in identical situations do identical things -- which is the kind of move I instinctively trust. But then I recalled Aumann's correlated equilibrium, where strategies get correlated through a shared signal with no causal arrow between the players, and now I can't tell if their just the same trick wearing different clothes. Could you compare the two properly: is mutual cooperation in the one-shot PD actually achievable as a correlated equilibrium, or does superrationality quietly require dropping some assumption that correlated equilibrium (and game theory more generally) still relies on? Tell me which of the two notions comes out more defensible once you line them up side by side, and justify your verdict.
