I've been part of a small Bayesian-epistemology reading group for a few years now, mostly people I first came across through the usual rationalist blogs and forums, and this month we read Frank Ramsey's "Truth and Probability" back to back with the relevant chapter of Richard Jeffrey's *The Logic of Decision*. It's an odd pairing, since Ramsey died in 1930 at twenty-six, some thirty-five years before Jeffrey's book was even published, but it produced more disagreement in the group than anything we've covered in months, so I'd like an outside opinion.

Ramsey's whole case for the probability axioms rests on cashing out degrees of belief in betting behavior: your credence in a proposition just is whatever is revealed by the odds you'd accept, and incoherent credences can be turned into a guaranteed loss by a sufficiently clever bookmaker. Crucially, though, this is a claim about coherence at a single moment; Ramsey never really supplies, or defends, a rule for how credences should move as evidence comes in — that job was left to later writers, for both plain conditionalization and its generalizations. Jeffrey conditionalization (or "probability kinematics") addresses a harder version of that gap: cases where you never become certain of anything at all, such as Jeffrey's own example of examining a piece of cloth in poor light and coming away more confident, though not certain, that it is green. The rule takes your new confidence over some partition of possibilities as given and propagates it through the rest of your beliefs on the assumption that your credence in everything else, conditional on each cell of that partition, stays fixed (the "rigidity" condition), rather than conditionalizing on any single proposition you now believe outright. Its standard formal defense is itself a Dutch book argument, but a "dynamic" one, extending Ramsey's synchronic machinery to bets made across time. Yet the rule has features Ramsey's own argument never had to face: successive updates of this kind generally don't commute the way strict conditionalization does, and the whole procedure depends on privileging one particular partition, rather than some more finely or coarsely grained alternative, as the true shape of the experience — a choice with no obvious betting-behavior fact standing behind it.

So, reasoning strictly from what Ramsey actually committed himself to, rather than from what a generic modern Bayesian would say: would he have welcomed Jeffrey conditionalization, and the dynamic Dutch book used to justify it, as the natural completion of his own project, or would his operationalism about belief have made him suspicious of a procedure whose inputs — a privileged partition, a pattern of confidence with no single proposition behind it — can't themselves be cashed out in betting dispositions? And if the latter, is there anything in his actual framework that could handle uncertain evidence at all, or would he simply have to concede the gap? Jeffrey eventually called the broader picture "radical probabilism"; I'm curious whether Ramsey's own premises make him an unwitting ancestor of that view, or whether they rule it out.
