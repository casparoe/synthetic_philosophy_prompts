I was recently appointed to the root-cause-analysis committee at the hospital where I work, following a serious medication error. Our meetings keep circling one disagreement in particular, which a colleague with a philosophy background recognized as a version of the old methodological individualism versus holism debate in social science: some members want the report to center on the specific decisions of the clinicians involved, while others insist that "the unit's culture" was itself a cause, and that focusing on individuals leaves the real cause unaddressed. Trying to get my bearings before we reconvene, I traced the classic statement of the individualist side back to Mill's *A System of Logic* (1843, Book VI, Chapter VII), and found myself stuck on the paragraph where he actually makes the case:

"Men, however, in a state of society, are still men; their actions and passions are obedient to the laws of individual human nature. Men are not, when brought together, converted into another kind of substance, with different properties; as hydrogen and oxygen are different from water, or as hydrogen, oxygen, carbon, and azote are different from nerves, muscles, and tendons. Human beings in society have no properties but those which are derived from, and may be resolved into, the laws of the nature of individual man. In social phenomena the Composition of Causes is the universal law. Now, the method of philosophising which may be termed chemical overlooks this fact, and proceeds as if the nature of man as an individual were not concerned at all, or were concerned in a very inferior degree, in the operations of human beings in society."

I can follow the general shape of the argument -- he is rejecting a "chemical" model of social causation in favor of a "mechanical" one -- but I would like help with the details. Why exactly does he think the hydrogen-and-water case is the wrong analogy for society and plain addition the right one, and how much work is "Composition of Causes" actually doing to earn that conclusion, rather than just naming it? More importantly for my committee: if the argument goes through, does it actually rule out treating something like "unit culture" as a genuine cause in its own right, or does it only rule out one particular bad way of defending that claim, leaving room for my systems-minded colleagues to be right, just not for the reason they would usually give?
