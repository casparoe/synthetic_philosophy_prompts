A friend and I spent much of last weekend arguing over whether to make an offer on a house immediately or to first commission a second round of inspections, and neither of us persuaded the other. I maintained that this was not really a close call: a result in Bayesian decision theory known as I. J. Good's theorem on the value of information shows that a rational agent's expected utility from deciding after gathering further evidence is never lower than from deciding right away, so choosing to act before gathering further evidence that could still be obtained is, strictly speaking, irrational. My friend suspected there was something wrong with using the theorem this way but couldn't say exactly what it was. Since this is a genuine, well-established theorem rather than a mere rule of thumb, was I right that her eagerness to make the offer immediately was the irrational position, or is there a flaw in how I've extended the theorem to a case like ours?
