In my decision theory seminar this term, we recently worked through Alan Hájek and Harris Nover's Pasadena game: a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads, and if that happens on toss n, you receive (-1)^(n+1) × (2^n / n) dollars. Weighting each payoff by its probability, 2^(-n), collapses the expectation into the alternating harmonic series, which sums to ln 2 in the order set by the tosses themselves — but because that series is only conditionally, not absolutely, convergent, the Riemann rearrangement theorem guarantees it can be reordered to converge to any real number, to diverge to positive or negative infinity, or to oscillate without settling on any value. Hájek and Nover press the point further: the indeterminacy survives even if you refuse to reorder anything, since other sequences of finite truncations of the very same game — each just as natural as truncating by number of tosses — converge to different limits, or to none at all. This is supposed to be a deeper problem than the St. Petersburg paradox, whose expectation is merely infinite, a value that is invariant under rearrangement because every term in that sum is positive.

Write a dialogue between two decision theorists disputing what to conclude from this. One should argue that the Pasadena game is a genuine counterexample showing that "maximize expected value" cannot serve as the general criterion of rational choice, and that decision theory needs either a principled restriction on where that criterion applies or a real replacement for it. The other should argue that expected-value theory comes through intact once we're careful about which operations on infinite gambles are actually decision-theoretically legitimate. Have them engage directly with the truncation-sequence point and not just the easier rearrangement point, make sure each responds to the strongest version of the other's argument, and make sure neither side concedes more than it should — I want genuine disagreement, not two people taking turns lecturing.
