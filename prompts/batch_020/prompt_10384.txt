I cover the future-of-work beat for an online magazine, and about a year ago I ended up joining the governance council of a small worker-owned bike courier co-op in my city — partly for a story, partly because the idea appealed to me personally. We're now stuck on a decision that's split the council for weeks. A mission-driven investment fund has offered real money: enough for e-cargo bikes, insurance, and a dispatch system that actually works. The catch is that new couriers hired to handle the growth would come on as regular employees for their first three years, rather than member-owners with a vote and an equity share, the way everyone already on the roster got to join. Existing members keep everything they have, and the new hires would be paid more than they'd make at a gig-economy competitor — just without the ownership piece, for a while.

Two of our members have started citing Marx at meetings, arguing this quietly reintroduces exploitation and alienation into a co-op that exists specifically to avoid both, wage bump or not. Others say that's a category error dressed up as principle — nobody's surplus is being siphoned off to a shareholder who contributes nothing, and the realistic alternative to taking the deal is folding within a year, which is its own kind of alienation. I want to bring an actual memo to the next meeting instead of just my own muddled opinion (the vote is in three weeks), so could you draft it? I dont want a "capitalism bad" gloss — I want it to use Marx's own distinction between exploitation, which is a pretty technical claim about surplus labor and not really about whether a wage feels fair, and alienation, which is about your relation to the product, the process, and your co-workers, to actually work out whether this specific deal brings either one back in. And I need it to land somewhere concrete: accept, reject, or accept only with specific changes to the terms, and if so, which ones.
