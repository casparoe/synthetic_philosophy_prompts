I'm teaching a graduate seminar this term on practical reason, and this week's session sets Bernard Williams's "Internal and External Reasons" alongside Book 2 of Hume's Treatise of Human Nature, on the standard assumption that Williams's internalism — an agent has a reason to act only if there is a "sound deliberative route" from that agent's existing "subjective motivational set" to the action — is essentially a descendant of Hume's claim that reason alone cannot move the will. While drafting my lecture notes I reread the relevant stretch of the Treatise more slowly than usual, and I've argued myself into a difficulty about it that I'd rather sort out with you than discover live in front of twelve second-year students.

Here is the passage (Treatise 2.3.3.4, 6–7; I've cut one clause for length, marked with [...]):

"Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them.

"What may at first occur on this head, is, that as nothing can be contrary to truth or reason, except what has a reference to it, and as the judgments of our understanding only have this reference, it must follow, that passions can be contrary to reason only so far as they are accompany'd with some judgment or opinion. According to this principle, which is so obvious and natural, 'tis only in two senses, that any affection can be call'd unreasonable. First, When a passion, such as hope or fear, grief or joy, despair or security, is founded on the supposition of the existence of objects, which really do not exist. Secondly, When in exerting any passion in action, we chuse means insufficient for the design'd end, and deceive ourselves in our judgment of causes and effects. Where a passion is neither founded on false suppositions, nor chuses means insufficient for the end, the understanding can neither justify nor condemn it. [...] In short, a passion must be accompany'd with some false judgment, in order to its being unreasonable; and even then 'tis not the passion, properly speaking, which is unreasonable, but the judgment.

"The consequences are evident. Since a passion can never, in any sense, be call'd unreasonable, but when founded on a false supposition, or when it chuses means insufficient for the design'd end, 'tis impossible, that reason and passion can ever oppose each other, or dispute for the government of the will and actions. The moment we perceive the falshood of any supposition, or the insufficiency of any means our passions yield to our reason without any opposition. I may desire any fruit as of an excellent relish; but whenever you convince me of my mistake, my longing ceases."

My difficulty is this. A few lines before this passage, Hume's stated thesis is unconditional: reason "can never oppose passion in the direction of the will." Yet what he goes on to describe looks like exactly that kind of opposition — a passion that would otherwise have issued in action is extinguished once the understanding corrects the belief it depended on, and the fruit example reads like reason redirecting the will, not like a passion that remains fully in command while reason merely ministers to it. So I'd like your help with two connected points. First, is there a way of construing "yield to our reason without any opposition" that preserves the letter of the slave thesis — reason having, in itself, no power to alter what we want or do — or does the passage in fact concede real, if narrowly circumscribed, motivational efficacy to reason, contrary to Hume's own announced conclusion? Second, whichever reading you favor, what should we make of the fact that Williams's internalism relies on this same manoeuvre — an internal reason claim can be defeated or revised by correcting the agent's factual or instrumental beliefs, without appeal to any external reason — to answer the standard complaint that internalism can't make sense of an agent having reason to revise a deeply mistaken end? Does Hume's example show that this belief-correcting move was internalist-respectable all along, or does it show that the move smuggles in more than a strict internalist is entitled to?
