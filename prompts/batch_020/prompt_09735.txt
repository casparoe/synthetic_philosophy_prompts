A friend and I spent much of an evening arguing about whether Frankfurt-style cases really succeed in refuting the Principle of Alternative Possibilities, or whether the strategy just relocates the difficulty it was meant to dissolve. We never quite converged. As it happens, I am now grading problem sets for the free will unit of a seminar I TA, and one student's answer bears directly on the dispute we were having the night before. I would rather have it evaluated strictly on its own merits than let my priors from that conversation color the grade, so I would like you to act as an independent grader.

Here is the question (worth 25 points):

Frankfurt's case involving Black and Jones is meant as a counterexample to the Principle of Alternative Possibilities (PAP): the claim that an agent is morally responsible for an action only if she could have done otherwise.
(a) [8 pts] Reconstruct the Black/Jones case and state precisely what Frankfurt takes it to establish.
(b) [10 pts] Present the "dilemma defense" against Frankfurt-style cases associated with Widerker and Ginet, and explain how Pereboom's "buffer" cases are meant to escape it.
(c) [7 pts] In your own judgment, does the buffer-case strategy succeed in restoring Frankfurt's original conclusion? Defend your answer in a few sentences.

Here is the student's answer:

(a) Black wants Jones to vote for a certain candidate. If Jones shows any sign that he is about to decide against voting that way, Black's device intervenes and makes him do it anyway; but if Jones is going to decide on his own to vote that way, Black never has to act, and that is exactly what happens: Jones votes for the candidate entirely on his own, and Black stays out of the picture. Jones seems just as responsible for his vote as he would be if the device did not exist, and yet, because Black would have stepped in, Jones could not have done otherwise. Frankfurt concludes that moral responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise, so PAP is false. What the case really shows, then, is that moral responsibility is compatible with determinism: Jones is responsible even though, given Black's disposition, there was never any way for him to vote differently.

(b) Widerker and Ginet's dilemma targets the "prior sign" Black's device monitors for. Either that sign deterministically guarantees Jones's decision, or it is merely a reliable-but-fallible indicator of it. If the former, an incompatibilist can simply reply that Jones's decision, being causally determined, was never going to be free regardless of Black's presence, so the case has smuggled in the very thing it needed to argue for rather than isolating the lack of alternatives as the operative factor. If the latter, Jones retains some possibility of deciding against the sign, which hands PAP's defenders back exactly the alternative possibility Frankfurt needed to eliminate. Pereboom's buffer cases escape this dilemma by tightening the timing: a temporal buffer is inserted between the sign and the action, and the sign-to-decision link is stipulated to be deterministic during that buffer window, which is precisely what lets Pereboom avoid Widerker and Ginet's worry that indeterminism reopens a genuine alternative possibility for Jones.

(c) I think the buffer strategy succeeds: it closes off the specific loophole Widerker and Ginet pointed to, and nothing else about the case looks ad hoc. If there is a residual worry, it is just that the buffer window may have to be implausibly short to do its job, which is a cost in realism rather than in the argument's validity.
