I serve on my university's research-integrity appeals panel, a five-member faculty committee that hears the final internal review before a misconduct finding is forwarded to the funding agency. Under the policy we operate by -- in a structure common to many research-misconduct frameworks -- a finding against a respondent requires the panel to be satisfied of two distinct elements: first, that the respondent fabricated or falsified data, and second, that they did so knowingly or recklessly rather than through honest error or ordinary carelessness. I have sat on this panel for two years without this procedural question ever actually mattering to an outcome.

That changed with the case we are now closing out: a biologist accused of manipulating gel images in a grant renewal application. An informal straw poll among the panel has yielded an awkward pattern. On the falsification question, panelists A, B, and C are convinced the images were altered, while D and E are not. On the intent question, A, D, and E are convinced any alteration would have been reckless, while B and C are not persuaded. So only A judges both elements to be satisfied; B and C accept falsification but not recklessness, D and E accept recklessness but not falsification, and everyone agrees both elements are required for a finding. Our bylaws leave open two ways to tally this, and they point in opposite directions. Vote element-by-element and apply the definition mechanically, and both elements clear their 3-2 majority, sustaining the finding. Ask each panelist instead for a single up-or-down vote on the charge itself, and only A votes to sustain, so the finding fails four to one. Some on the panel insist on the element-by-element vote, since only that keeps the verdict tied to the shared definition rather than to five unstated private theories of guilt; others insist on the direct vote on the charge, since we were convened to judge this named colleague rather than to certify sub-findings nobody asked us to rule on separately, and manufacturing a finding by splitting the question into pieces strikes them as a due-process problem in its own right.

Please draft the short memo I will circulate to the panel ahead of next week's meeting. It should recommend a specific way of resolving this case -- including which procedure to use and the resulting verdict -- and it should engage the losing side's argument seriously enough that a colleague who holds that view would recognize their own reasoning in it rather than a caricature. If you think the better answer is a standing rule for how we vote in future cases rather than an ad hoc call on this one, say so and explain why, but either way I need something the panel can act on.
