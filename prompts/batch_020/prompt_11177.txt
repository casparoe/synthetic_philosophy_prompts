For my epistemology seminar, we've been working through the "Meno problem" in its contemporary guise as the swamping problem: if all that ultimately matters is having a true belief, why should knowledge — true belief produced by a reliable process or by the knower's own cognitive ability — count for more than a true belief arrived at in some other way? Credit (or achievement) theories of knowledge, of the sort defended by philosophers like Greco and Riggs, answer this by holding that knowledge is true belief for which the subject deserves cognitive credit, on analogy with an archer who hits the target through skill rather than luck. My worry is that this move seems to break down for "easy" cases of knowledge — for instance, seeing an unobstructed mug on a well-lit table — where nothing that looks like a special cognitive achievement is on display, even though this is obviously still a case of knowledge rather than a merely lucky true belief. Please argue that credit theories can survive this "no achievement in easy cases" worry and so still solve the swamping problem in full generality, ideally by developing some difficulty-independent notion of cognitive achievement, and then identify the strongest objection a critic could raise against the defense you've given.
