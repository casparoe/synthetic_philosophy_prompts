I'm writing my IB History Extended Essay on Isaac Newton's unpublished theological manuscripts, and thanks to the Newton Project's online transcriptions I've been able to go a little deeper into the primary material than most of the secondary sources I've found, which tend to mention in passing that "Newton secretly rejected the Trinity" and leave it there. What actually strikes me is that Newton was doing careful, technical textual criticism of his own: in the paper he sent to John Locke around 1690, later published as "Two Notable Corruptions of Scripture," he argued from manuscript evidence that the Comma Johanneum (1 John 5:7) and the "God was manifest in the flesh" reading of 1 Timothy 3:16 were both later insertions manufactured to supply Trinitarian theology with explicit proof-texts — and on both readings, modern critical editions of the Greek New Testament have essentially come down on his side (Bart Ehrman would later build an entire scholarly reputation on exactly this kind of argument). At the same time, Newton spent decades on his "Observations upon the Prophecies of Daniel and the Apocalypse of St. John," building a whole chronological system — including his famously hedged calculation that the world would not end before 2060 — that only works if Daniel is exactly what it presents itself as: a genuine sixth-century-BCE prophet whose visions of successive empires can be cautiously decoded into real information about the future.

My supervisor thinks I'm chasing a footnote and wants me to keep the essay narrowly focused on the New Testament material, but I think there's a real problem here worth taking seriously first. Mainstream historical-critical scholarship today dates the final form of Daniel to the 160s BCE, under the persecution of Antiochus IV Epiphanes, precisely because the "predictions" are strikingly accurate up to about that point and then go wrong — the standard internal signature of a vaticinium ex eventu, a prophecy written after the fact. That seems like a fundamentally different kind of argument from the one Newton himself made about the New Testament: identifying a scribal insertion prunes a corruption off an otherwise sound text, while redating Daniel's authorship undercuts the very premise that makes the prophetic project meaningful in the first place. So, reasoning from what Newton actually wrote about scriptural corruption, prophecy, and the "true religion" he believed had been progressively obscured, how do you think he would have responded if confronted with the Maccabean dating argument on its own terms? Would his own standards of textual evidence give him any principled way to resist it or absorb it into his existing corruption narrative, or does it cut in a way his position on the New Testament passages simply doesn't — and either way, what would that mean for a prophetic chronology that assumed Daniel's authenticity from its very first step?
