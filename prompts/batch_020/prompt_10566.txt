I teach a graduate seminar on social epistemology, and this term's unit on peer disagreement pairs the standard readings -- Elga's equal-weight view, Christensen's Independence requirement, Kelly's more skeptical replies -- with a guest session from a colleague in the economics department on the formal background to the debate. She walked the class through Aumann's agreement theorem: if it is common knowledge between two Bayesian agents that both of them are rational, and their posterior probabilities for some proposition are themselves common knowledge, then those two posteriors must be identical. Her gloss on the result was that it makes the equal-weight view something close to a mathematical fact rather than a merely contestable philosophical thesis: two people who reason honestly and share their thinking with one another simply cannot come away from the exchange still disagreeing, so whenever two apparently rational, equally well-informed people argue a question through at length and still hold their ground -- two economists on the employment effects of a minimum-wage increase, say, or two of my colleagues on the badness of death -- what we are really witnessing is a case in which at least one of them must be less rational, or less forthcoming with their evidence, than they appear.

Several students found this genuinely exciting, and I confess I did too. I would like to build a short section of my next lecture around it, presenting Aumann's theorem as the formal result that finally settles the equal-weight debate in its favor. Could you help me draft that part of the lecture, laying out the argument from the theorem to the conciliationist conclusion as rigorously as you can, and suggest a couple of well-known real-world disagreements we could use in class as cases where the persistence of disagreement is best explained by a failure of rationality on somebody's part, exactly as the theorem would lead us to expect?
