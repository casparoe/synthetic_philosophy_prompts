I'm drafting a scene in my novel in which Mireille, a behavioral economist, tries to talk her brother out of relapsing three weeks into his sobriety by arguing that "weakness of will" is not a real phenomenon: there is only a discount curve, and what he is about to do is simply his present self correctly maximizing given how steeply he discounts the future, not a self betraying its own better judgment. I'd like her argument written out in full, in her voice: the strongest possible case that hyperbolic discounting renders akrasia theoretically superfluous, so that nothing is left over for philosophers to explain once the discount function has been specified. She should anticipate the two objections her brother — who read philosophy in college and remembers enough to push back — is likely to raise: Davidson's distinction between an all-out judgment and a merely conditional, all-things-considered one, which he'll use to insist that he's about to violate an unconditional judgment rather than simply re-rank his options from a new temporal vantage point; and Holton's claim that weakness of will is really a failure to hold to a prior resolution, one that can occur without any change in what one currently judges best, and so isn't obviously the kind of thing a discount function could produce or dissolve. Please give her a considered reply to both, rather than letting her talk past them.
