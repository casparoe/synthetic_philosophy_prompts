Okay so for my Formal Epistemology seminar we got assigned the Garrabrant et al logical induction paper (the MIRI one from 2016) as background reading, and I have to give a short presentation on "implications" next week, which is stressing me out a bit. The setup is familiar from earlier in the course -- standard Bayesian epistemology basically assumes logical omniscience, so on paper you're supposed to already have credence 1 in every theorem and 0 in every contradiction the second you understand the sentence, but obviously real reasoners (us, mathematicians, whoever) spend literal years not knowing if something like the twin prime conjecture is true. Logical induction is presented as a fix for this: you get a computable sequence of probability assignments to arithmetic sentences, evolving over what the paper calls something like logical time, that satisfies a criterion where no trader running an efficiently computable strategy can make unbounded profit off the market of sentence-prices. From that you're supposed to get a bunch of nice properties for free, like eventually trusting provable things, using pattern recognition on cases you've checked by hand, and not getting suckered by weird self-referential sentences.

Here's my problem though: me and my study group basically followed the math (mostly) but we're all blanking on what to actually say the implications ARE for, like, a person, as opposed to an idealized computable reasoner that nobody will ever actually run. My professors hint was that its supposed to change how we think about confidence in open conjectures and even kind of mundane stuff like trusting a proof sketch you havent fully verified line by line, but none of us can turn that into real talking points beyond "the math is cool." Could you walk me through what you think are the most important implications of logical induction for how an ordinary, non-omniscient, finite-compute person should reason about their own uncertainty in mathematical or logical claims? Like concretely: if I've hand-checked a conjecture for the first 500 cases and it's held every single time, or I skimmed a proof and it feels right but haven't gone through every line, what does this framework actually license me to say about my own credence in those situations, versus what would just be me making stuff up?
