Next month I am giving the toast at the retirement dinner of a colleague I have known since we were graduate students together in philosophy some thirty years ago, and who is now the senior figure in our department's ancient philosophy group. She is locally famous for a fortnightly reading group, always held over wine, that generations of her students have simply called "the Symposium." One piece of department lore is that she is invariably the last person at the table still counting glasses -- she pours generously for everyone else and barely touches her own. She has also long maintained, half as a joke and half in earnest, that the Republic's wariness of Dionysiac loosening and the Laws' far more permissive treatment of regulated wine-drinking cannot both be Plato's last word, and that her whole teaching practice has been a standing bet on the Athenian Stranger over Socrates' guardians. The dinner will be a genuinely mixed room: a dozen or so specialists in ancient philosophy who will notice immediately if I misstate the actual argument, and a much larger number of relatives, administrators, and former advisees from unrelated fields who know Plato only in outline and will disengage the moment the toast turns into a lecture.

I would like you to draft this toast for me. It should put real weight on the specific position the Laws defends in Books One and Two -- that a carefully supervised drinking party can function as a controlled test and training-ground for self-mastery, on the same principle by which manageable fear is used to train courage; that the whole arrangement depends on having a sober overseer who keeps the gathering from tipping into license; and that the license Plato is willing to permit increases with age, easing the rigidity of the old rather than indulging the impulsiveness of the young. Please put those specifics to work, including the detail about who at that table has always stayed sober, to say something true and affectionate about why she ran her seminar exactly as she did and what the department is actually losing. Don't let the toast collapse into either "philosophers disapprove of pleasure" or "even Plato liked a drink" -- it has to survive contact with anyone in the room who has actually read the text. Aim for something that runs about five minutes read aloud and closes on an actual toast, glasses raised, rather than trailing off into sentiment.
