I cover cognitive science and philosophy of mind as a science journalist, and next week I'm interviewing a theoretical neuroscientist for a profile piece on the free energy principle, which she champions as a grand unifying theory of the brain and, increasingly, of life itself. Her go-to move when pressed on objections is to say critics are demanding the wrong kind of falsifiability from the wrong kind of theory. Draft eight to ten interview questions that don't just let her restate the framework. Include one that presses the "dark room problem" — why organisms seek novelty rather than retreat to static, fully predictable environments, if minimizing prediction error is the brain's whole purpose — and pushes past the standard appeal to expected free energy and epistemic value; one that forces her to say plainly whether the principle is a tautological description that any self-organizing system satisfies by definition, or a falsifiable claim about how the brain specifically is built and wired; and one asking whether applying the same formalism to cells, immune systems, and whole societies leaves it anything specific to say about cognition at all. For each question, add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give.
