A friend and I spent much of last night debating how much weight Good's 1967 theorem on the value of information can really bear. I defended the standard line: a rational, expected-utility-maximizing agent should never decline cost-free, relevant evidence before acting. My friend answered with case after case from the literature that supposedly undermines this guarantee, and by the end we could not agree, case by case, whether each one revealed a genuine problem for that slogan or merely relied on an assumption that no one seriously wants to keep anyway.

Below are six of the cases she raised. Please rank them by how seriously each one damages the slogan that a rational agent should always accept free, relevant information before choosing an action, from most damaging (tier S) to least damaging (tier C):

1. An agent who is certain she is a Bayesian conditionalizer in principle, but uncertain which updating rule she will actually apply once the evidence arrives.
2. A case in which the very act of looking is entangled with the state one is uncertain about, so that the state's probability is not independent of the act, as in Newcomb-style setups.
3. A maxmin-expected-utility agent whose set of credences "dilates" upon receiving a signal, so that her worst-case expected utility over that set decreases.
4. Hirshleifer's example, in which one party's access to information destroys a mutually beneficial insurance arrangement, leaving someone worse off even though no one involved acted irrationally and the information was both true and free.
5. A dynamically inconsistent agent, whose plan today for what to do upon seeing the evidence is not the plan her future, post-evidence self will actually carry out.
6. Improper or merely finitely additive priors, under which the expectation used to define the value of the experiment fails to behave as required.
