This term I'm taking a small seminar on decision theory and the philosophical foundations of longtermism, and we spent the first few weeks on Douglas Hofstadter's notion of superrationality, first laid out in his "Dilemmas for Superrational Thinkers, Leading Up to a Luring Lottery" column for Scientific American and later collected, along with a follow-up piece, in Metamagical Themas. The core move is familiar from the two-player prisoner's dilemma: a superrational agent notices that any line of reasoning that would lead it to defect is equally available to an identically-reasoning opponent, and concludes that both should cooperate, since a superrational thinker's calculations must include the fact that it belongs to a whole class of thinkers who will all reach the same verdict. Hofstadter pushes the idea further with his "platonia dilemma," in which an eccentric trillionaire offers a billion dollars to exactly one of twenty people who each independently, and without communicating, decide whether to send in a claim; superrationality there requires all twenty identical reasoners to converge, unaided by coordination, on the very same mixed strategy.

We've now moved on to Caspar Oesterheld's 2017 report for the Center on Long-Term Risk, which he originally titled "Multiverse-wide Cooperation via Correlated Decision Making" and which is more often discussed today under the label "evidential cooperation in large worlds" (ECL). As I understand the argument, because the universe or multiverse is very probably vast, it almost certainly contains enormous numbers of agents who reason similarly to us, and this licenses acting so as to benefit that whole population — via evidential rather than causal decision theory — even though we could never causally interact with nearly any of them. Our professor described this in passing as "superrationality at cosmic scale," but we ran out of time before working out how much of Hofstadter's original argument actually survives once you make two changes at once: moving from a handful of literally identical reasoners to a vast, possibly unbounded population, and moving from agents with identical goals and evidence to agents who may hold quite different values and be in quite different empirical situations from our own.

Could you write me a comparison of these two ideas that engages seriously with that transition? I would like you to address, in particular: which of the two generalizations — scale or heterogeneity — is doing more of the philosophical work in making ECL a harder case to defend than Hofstadter's original scenarios; whether standard objections to ECL, such as disagreement over who counts as similar enough to be correlated with, or the difficulty of settling on what Oesterheld calls a "compromise utility function" across differing value systems, have any real counterpart in the superrationality literature, or instead only arise once you generalize in this way; and, in the end, whether ECL is best understood as basically an extension of superrationality that inherits its justification wholesale, or as an argument that requires enough additional philosophical machinery that it ought to stand or fall independently of whether superrationality itself is sound.
