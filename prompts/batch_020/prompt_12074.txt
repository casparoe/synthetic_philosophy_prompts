The question (worth 10 points) reads:

"Consider two choice problems. Problem 1: choose between L1 ($1,000,000 for certain) and L2 ($5,000,000 with probability .10, $1,000,000 with probability .89, $0 with probability .01). Problem 2: choose between L3 ($1,000,000 with probability .11, $0 with probability .89) and L4 ($5,000,000 with probability .10, $0 with probability .90). Most people choose L1 over L2, and L4 over L3. Explain precisely which axiom of expected-utility theory this pattern violates, and comment on what Allais himself took the example to establish."

My classmate's answer:

"The trick is to see that all four lotteries are built from the same two ingredients. L1 and L2 share a 0.89 chance of $1,000,000; strip that common piece away and L1 is left offering the remaining $1,000,000 outright, while L2 is left offering a 10/11 chance of $5,000,000 and a 1/11 chance of nothing. L3 and L4 are the very same residual bet, just paired with a 0.89 chance of $0 instead of a 0.89 chance of $1,000,000. Under expected utility, preferences are linear in the probabilities, so a rational agent's ranking of the residual bet should not depend on what it happens to be mixed with. Since most people flip from preferring certainty in Problem 1 to preferring the long shot in Problem 2, they are ranking the same residual bet differently depending on the common piece attached to it — a straightforward violation of the independence axiom, which Savage's sure-thing principle was formulated to rule out. Allais intended the example to reinforce the same conclusion Savage and von Neumann and Morgenstern had already reached by axiomatic means: that such reversals are a form of genuine error in probabilistic reasoning, and that a sound normative theory of rational choice is not obliged to bend itself to accommodate them."
