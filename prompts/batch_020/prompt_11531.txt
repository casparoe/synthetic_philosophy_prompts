Three weeks ago, a single line of dialogue from my third novel -- spoken by a character the book quite clearly does not endorse -- was screenshotted out of context and made the rounds on BookTok and then on Twitter, and for about ten days I was the subject of a proper pile-on: several thousand quote-posts, a wave of one-star Goodreads reviews from people who plainly hadn't read past the first page, and one lengthy video essay diagnosing my personal politics from that single sentence. It has since burned itself out, as these things do, but a friend of mine who teaches philosophy heard the whole saga and told me, half-joking, that I had just lived through a miniature version of the Corsair affair: Kierkegaard goading Copenhagen's satirical press into attacking him personally, and then spending several later writings working out what he took the episode to reveal about the press, "the public," and what he called levelling. I went back and read the relevant pages of The Present Age myself afterward, mostly out of self-interested curiosity, and the comparison struck closer to home than I expected it to.

I'd like your help thinking it through properly rather than just venting about it, though. Could you lay out the most important implications of Kierkegaard's specific diagnosis of the press and the public -- as he distinguishes both from a genuine community, from a mere crowd, and from ordinary unpopularity -- for how I should actually handle my situation: whether his account gives any principled reason to engage with readers online at all, rather than simply publishing and staying quiet; how I ought to interpret a pile-on like mine, given that it was in no ordinary sense a judgment reached by anyone in particular; and whether his own conduct during and after the affair, which he later reflected on with evident ambivalence, is a model worth imitating or mainly a cautionary tale. I have no real use for generic advice about ignoring my mentions; I want to know what actually follows, if anything does, from his particular argument.
