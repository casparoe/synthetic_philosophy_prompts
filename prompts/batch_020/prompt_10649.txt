I've spent a long time reading rationalist and EA-adjacent blogs and forums, where classical political economy occasionally gets relitigated in a particular contrarian register: posts pointing out, say, that "nation of shopkeepers" is usually misattributed to Napoleon when it is actually Adam Smith's own barb about British colonial policy. Partly because of that background, I offered to help a friend grade take-home midterms for the history-of-economic-thought seminar she teaches, and she's asked me to give her an independent read on a few answers before she finalizes grades, rather than just rubber-stamping them.

The question was: "In Book IV, chapter vii of An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations, Smith discusses at length Great Britain's economic relationship with her colonies, especially those in North America. Drawing on this material: (a) explain why Smith thought the monopoly of colonial trade was harmful to Great Britain's interest even though colonial trade itself was valuable; (b) explain what Smith meant by calling the project of empire 'a project fit only for a nation of shopkeepers'; and (c) explain the alternative(s) Smith proposed for Britain's relationship with her colonies, and how they follow from the diagnosis in (a)."

The student's answer, verbatim, was:

Smith's argument for why the monopoly of colonial trade hurt Britain, even though trade with the colonies was itself worth having, turns on where British capital ended up being invested. The Navigation Acts and related laws confined the colonial trade to British ships and merchants, and required colonial produce to be shipped to Britain rather than sold directly to the rest of Europe. Smith argues that this forced part of Britain's capital out of nearer, faster-turnover trades -- particularly the trade with Europe and the Mediterranean -- and into the more distant American and West Indian trade, where returns come more slowly. Since a given amount of capital employed in a distant trade sets less productive labour in motion per year than the same capital would in a nearby trade, this reallocation reduced the total amount of industry Britain's capital stock could support, even as it raised profits in the branches of trade left short of competition for capital. So monopoly did not simply fail to help Britain; it actively made the country's capital less productive than it would have been under "a natural and free" pattern of trade, while still allowing certain merchants to do very well out of the arrangement.

This is the background for the "nation of shopkeepers" line. Smith's point is not a swipe at commerce as such -- he spends most of the book celebrating commercial society -- but a specific irony about the empire. Building an empire purely to manufacture a captive set of customers might look like the kind of scheme a shopkeeping nation would hatch, but Smith says it is actually the opposite: no sensible shopkeeper would spend his own money buying an estate for a customer just to keep that customer's business, so a nation of actual shopkeepers, thinking as shopkeepers do about costs and benefits, would never have approved this project either. It only goes forward because the government is "influenced by shopkeepers" in the narrower sense of merchants with a stake in the monopoly, who capture the gains while the costs of defending and administering the empire are spread across all taxpayers. Because those costs were spread so thin, the arrangement was politically sustainable despite being, on the whole, close to a wash for Britain -- a large transfer to a small politically connected class rather than a large net loss to the nation.

Given this diagnosis, Smith's proposed remedy is to give up the monopoly and grant the American colonies their independence. If Britain no longer tried to hold a political empire, it could still trade with an independent America freely, the way it already traded with other foreign nations, and it would no longer need to pay for the colonies' defense and civil administration -- costs which, under the monopoly system, were being paid by the British public so that the benefits could accrue to the favored merchants. Cutting the political tie while keeping the commercial one, Smith thought, would leave Britain at least as well off commercially and considerably better off fiscally.
