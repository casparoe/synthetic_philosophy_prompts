I've been part of a small reading group that grew out of a local LessWrong meetup, and last week's session was on informational cascades, working through the classic Bikhchandani–Hirshleifer–Welch setup: a sequence of diners choose, one at a time, between two outwardly identical restaurants, A and B, where each diner has picked up one noisy private hint about which restaurant is actually better (a review skimmed that morning, say) and can see where everyone before them chose to sit, but never their hints. Afterward, two people from the group, Nora and Theo, got into a disagreement over dinner that neither would concede, and I'd like an outside read on it.

Nora's position was that once a handful of diners in a row have piled into A, even though the group's hints were roughly balanced, every diner after that point is being straightforwardly irrational to keep piling into A regardless of what their own hint says: they have a piece of evidence and they're discarding it, which she said is close to the textbook definition of a cognitive bias. Theo objected that this gets things backwards: once the running imbalance in the crowd's choices is large enough, Bayesian updating implies that a single bounded private hint simply cannot outweigh it, so ignoring your hint at that point is exactly what an ideally rational agent should do, and calling that irrational just misapplies the word. Nora conceded the individual-level math but held her ground at a different level: the entire reason each diner had a private hint in the first place was so the group could pool information and land on the better restaurant, and here that pooling has visibly failed, so by her lights something has clearly gone wrong even though no single diner erred. Theo agreed that something goes wrong, but said Nora was still mislabeling it: from that point on, a diner's choice stops carrying any of their private information, so a crowd of fifty diners sitting in A looks like fifty pieces of evidence for A when it may really be two or three, plus a lot of copying — a problem of information loss and fragility, on his view, not of anyone reasoning badly.

Neither of them budged, so I said I'd ask someone less invested in being right. Could you go through Nora and Theo's exchange move by move and say who has the better of each specific point, rather than just picking an overall winner, and flag anything important the two of them end up talking past each other on? I'd also be curious whether the exact thing Theo is pointing at in his last move — the gap between how much evidence a cascade looks like it contains and how much it actually does — has a standard name in the literature, since neither of us could pin one down.
