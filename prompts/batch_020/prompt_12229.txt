I never studied philosophy in school, but a couple of years ago I went down a rabbit hole reading about the ethics of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and that's how I came across G.E.M. Anscombe's pamphlet "Mr. Truman's Degree," where she argued that Oxford disgraced itself in 1956 by giving Harry Truman an honorary degree, on the grounds that deliberately killing that many civilians to force a surrender was murder, full stop, regardless of how many lives it supposedly saved by shortening the war. The argument stuck with me, and it feels suddenly relevant again now that I've ended up, to my own surprise, on the honorary-degrees subcommittee of the board of trustees at the small college I graduated from (I run an HVAC business and have no other connection to academia).

We've been asked to recommend an honorary doctorate for a retired four-star general who has spent the last two decades running a well-regarded refugee-resettlement foundation. The complication is his earlier record: as theater commander in a war a little over twenty years ago, he ordered the destruction of the enemy capital's power grid and water-treatment plants, reasoning — correctly, as it turned out — that the resulting collapse in civilian living conditions would break the enemy government's will to fight within weeks rather than years, especially given the coalition's declared policy of accepting nothing short of the regime's total collapse, and so would save far more lives than a prolonged ground campaign. Half my subcommittee calls this ordinary dual-use-infrastructure targeting that shouldn't count against an honor meant for his later humanitarian work; the other half says that once the point of hitting a target is to break civilians' capacity to survive, as the actual mechanism for forcing surrender rather than a side effect of degrading something of independent military value, the general did exactly what Anscombe accused Truman of doing, and that honoring him now implicates the college.

I have to circulate a written recommendation to the full board before they vote at next month's meeting, and I want it to actually take a side rather than just presenting both views: approve, reject, or approve with a specific condition, such as how the citation is worded. Please engage with Anscombe's argument on its own terms, not the cartoon version where she just says consequences never matter — I'm especially interested in what her point about the assumed "conditions" behind the "it saved more lives" defense would say here, and in her idea that honoring someone can make an institution complicit — but give the dual-use-infrastructure side of my subcommittee a fair hearing too before you come down on one side.
