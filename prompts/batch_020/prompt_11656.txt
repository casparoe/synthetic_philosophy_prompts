I am leading a graduate seminar this term on Henry Sidgwick, and one session is devoted to a puzzle that strikes me as more interesting than it first appears: why did a philosopher famous for his scrupulous, almost paralyzing rigor, and by all accounts personally skeptical of the supernatural, devote the better part of three decades to the Society for Psychical Research, serving as its first president and remaining active in its work until his death in 1900? My own inclination, shaped by Schultz's intellectual biography of Sidgwick, is to read this against the dualism of practical reason left unresolved at the close of the Methods of Ethics: having resigned his Trinity fellowship in 1869 rather than continue subscribing to doctrine he no longer believed, Sidgwick was left without any settled guarantee that duty and self-interest ultimately coincide, and I suspect the Society was in part an attempt to recover empirically the assurance that theology could no longer supply him. When I tried this reading out on colleagues at a recent workshop, though, several offered competing or complementary explanations, and before I build a lecture around my own preferred story I would like an outside judgment.

- "dualism": an attempt to empirically ground the reconciliation of rational egoism and utilitarian benevolence that pure philosophical argument had failed to secure
- "faith_vacuum": filling, in a manner compatible with his intellectual honesty, the void left by his loss of orthodox Christian belief
- "circle": loyalty and intellectual entanglement with friends and family already committed to the project, especially Myers, Gurney, and his wife Eleanor
- "method": a general epistemic scruple that no evidence bearing on questions of ultimate significance may be dismissed without investigation, quite apart from any personal stake in the result
- "debunking": a chiefly critical motive, using the Society's resources and prestige to expose fraudulent mediums and sloppy inference
- "zeitgeist": a broader late-Victorian response to the sense that Darwinism and biblical criticism had left no scientific foundation for a moral world-order
