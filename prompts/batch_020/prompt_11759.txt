For the past couple of months I've been working through an online philosophy course in my spare time, mostly because a friend talked me into it, and, somewhat to my surprise, I've been enjoying it far more than I expected given that I have no formal background in the subject (I'm a backend software engineer by trade). The course is structured around peer review: after submitting your own short-answer response each week, you're assigned a classmate's submission to grade against a point breakdown the instructor supplies, and part of your own grade depends on how carefully you did the grading. This week's assigned reading was Moritz Schlick's 1927 essay "On the Meaning of Life," and I've been handed the response quoted below. Since I don't fully trust my own instincts on material like this, I'd like a more expert opinion before I submit a grade, so that I neither shortchange nor oversell whoever wrote it.

The prompt everyone answered was: "In his 1927 essay 'On the Meaning of Life,' Moritz Schlick rejects a purposive, means-end conception of meaning in favor of an account centered on play. (a) Reconstruct Schlick's argument for why an activity's meaningfulness cannot simply consist in its being a means to some further end. (b) Explain why he takes play, and by extension youth, to be the paradigm case of meaningful activity, and say whether this commits him to holding that only children can live meaningfully. (c) Is the position defended in this essay in tension with, or a natural extension of, Schlick's broader commitments as a founding figure of logical positivism?"

Here is the submission I've been assigned, quoted in full: "Schlick's worry is that if we say some activity A is meaningful because it brings about a further good B, we haven't yet located value in A itself — we've just pushed the question back to whether B is valuable, and if B is valuable only because it brings about C, we're off on a regress that never bottoms out. Since a chain of that kind can never terminate in something self-justifying, treating meaning as a matter of purposiveness, of always being 'for the sake of' something else, cannot be the ultimate source of value; some activity has to be valuable in itself. Schlick finds this self-sufficient value in play: a child playing isn't playing in order to reach some later payoff, the playing simply is its own point, so means and end coincide. Because children are most disposed to approach the world this way, Schlick treats childhood as the closest thing we have to a model of meaningful existence, though he doesn't think meaning is closed off to adults — mature pursuits like art, invention, and discovery can be meaningful too, precisely to the extent that they keep this playful, autotelic quality instead of turning into pure drudgery performed only for a wage or a distant goal. This dovetails with Schlick's hedonism: since he holds elsewhere that pleasure is the only intrinsically good thing, any pleasant activity — eating a good meal, sunbathing, idly relaxing — turns out to be exactly as meaning-conferring as creative play, since all that ultimately matters on his view is the presence of pleasant feeling, whatever its source. Finally, I'd say the essay fits comfortably with Schlick's logical positivism: given that the Vienna Circle held that metaphysical claims lacking empirical verification conditions are strictly meaningless pseudo-problems, Schlick treats 'what is the meaning of life' the same way here, dissolving the question by showing it rests on a confused, purposive picture of value rather than answering it head-on."
