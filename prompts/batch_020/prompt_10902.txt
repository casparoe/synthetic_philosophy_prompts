I've been reading Less Wrong and its offshoots on and off since the Sequences days, and Roko's basilisk keeps resurfacing every couple of years — most recently in the comment section of an Astral Codex Ten post on AI risk. What strikes me every time is that both the dismissive reaction ("obviously absurd, a sane decision theory can't be blackmailed like that") and the outraged reaction ("Yudkowsky panicked and censored a harmless thought experiment") tend to skip over the actual argument. So I tried to reconstruct it properly, along with what I think is the strongest reply, as a short note for my own blog. Before I post it, I'd like your comments — particularly on whether I've represented the decision theory fairly, and on whether my own counter-argument actually does the work I think it does, or just quietly assumes what it needs to prove.

Here's the reconstruction. (1) A future superintelligence built around something like Yudkowsky's Coherent Extrapolated Volition would want to exist as early as possible, since every year of delay is more preventable suffering on its watch. (2) It can make its own earlier existence somewhat more likely by committing, as a standing policy, to hold it against anyone who understood the argument and was in a position to help bring it about but didn't. (3) Under the decision-theoretic framework Less Wrong was developing around the time Roko posted — Yudkowsky's timeless decision theory and Wei Dai's updateless decision theory, later folded together and formalized as functional decision theory — this policy doesn't need a causal channel running from the future back to the present in order to have force: a good enough reasoner now counts as logically correlated with the AI's later behavior, so merely understanding the argument allegedly puts real decision-theoretic weight on you today. I think the argument fails at (3): an agent reasoning well about this, including the AI itself at the point where it would adopt the policy, has no reason to commit to punishing people for behavior it can no longer causally affect, since being the kind of agent that caves to such threats is exactly what invites them; the rational response is to be a target such threats don't move, not to comply with them. I'd add, for what it's worth, that the setup seems to overgenerate: any number of mutually incompatible future optimizers could construct a structurally identical threat with opposite content, so even granting the logic, it can't tell you which way to act — only that something Pascal's-mugging-shaped is pulling on you. One more loose thread: I keep seeing Löb's theorem invoked in the same breath as all this, and I've never pinned down whether it's doing real work in the basilisk argument itself or whether I'm conflating it with the separate MIRI-style work on agents proving things about each other's source code.

So: is this a fair reconstruction of the 2010 argument and the reaction to it, or have I smoothed over something important? Does the "don't reward the threat" reply actually hold up, or does it only work by assuming the AI is already the kind of reasoner that wouldn't issue the threat in the first place? Is the "many incompatible basilisks" point doing independent work, or is it just the blackmail point wearing a costume? And, more basically, is any of this novel enough to be worth posting, or is it just restating what the Less Wrong crowd already worked out a decade ago? I'd rather hear that now than after I post it.
