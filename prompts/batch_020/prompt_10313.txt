I've been a longtime lurker on the rationalist-adjacent parts of the internet -- LessWrong, Astral Codex Ten, and the occasional dive into the Overcoming Bias archives -- where human enhancement keeps coming up, usually tangled together with embryo selection and nootropics. Wanting more grounding than blog-comment-section philosophy, I went and read up on how bioethicists actually draw the therapy/enhancement distinction. The core of it seems to be Christopher Boorse's biostatistical account of health as statistically normal functioning relative to an age-and-sex reference class, combined with Norman Daniels's extension of that idea into a theory of just health care: restoring someone to species-typical functioning is something health systems owe people, because it protects the normal range of opportunity that a Rawlsian theory of justice cares about, while pushing someone past species-typical functioning is not owed in the same way. The example that keeps recurring in the literature is growth hormone: giving it to a child whose short stature is caused by GH deficiency counts as treatment, while giving the same drug at the same dose to a child who is just constitutionally short counts as enhancement, even though the two children can end up exactly as tall and neither did anything to deserve their starting height. Critics -- Julian Savulescu is probably the best-known -- have argued that the distinction can't bear the moral weight Daniels wants it to bear, and that what should actually matter is something more like expected effect on well-being or opportunity, treatment/enhancement labels notwithstanding.

This isn't purely academic for me: my partner and I are going through IVF, and the clinic's embryo report includes both disease-risk polygenic scores and "trait" polygenic scores for things like height and educational attainment, so we're going to have to decide, in a very unphilosophical spreadsheet, whether ranking an embryo down for a high disease-risk score is really the same kind of act as ranking one up for a high cognitive-ability score. Rather than just summarizing the Boorse/Daniels view and the welfarist objections to it, I'd like you to work out what you take to be the most important implications of that debate for a decision like ours. Does the fact that the line between therapy and enhancement is so hard to draw in principle mean it isn't doing any real moral work, so that we should just think in terms of expected welfare across the board? Or is there a way to rescue something in the vicinity of Daniels's distinction that would still give us a principled reason to treat disease-avoidance and trait-selection differently? I'd rather you reason it through than just report back the state of the debate.
