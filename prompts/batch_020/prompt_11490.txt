For an independent-study project on Nietzsche, I've spent the last few weeks working through Ecce Homo, and the section called "Why I Am So Clever" really surprised me: he spends pages on nutrition, climate, and what he calls recreation, treating them as almost as important to philosophy as logic or argument. He complains that heavy German food and, especially, beer-drinking blunt the German spirit, contrasts this unfavorably with the lighter fare he came to prefer once he started wintering in Italy and the south of France, and is oddly dismissive of the vegetarian, back-to-nature health campaigns of the German "Lebensreform" movement that were fashionable at the time — even though he was constantly experimenting with his own diet because of his chronic migraines and digestive problems. My teacher pointed out that this fits a larger pattern: in Beyond Good and Evil, Nietzsche denies that self-preservation is life's basic drive at all, treating it as at most a byproduct of the more fundamental "will to power," and in Twilight of the Idols and the "On Free Death" chapter of Zarathustra he goes so far as to praise a well-timed death over an anxiously prolonged one.

Around the same time, a friend of mine got really into the biohacking and longevity-optimization scene that's become huge online — identical meals measured out to the gram, constant blood panels and glucose monitors, elaborate supplement stacks, all aimed less at treating any particular illness than at indefinitely postponing death itself. It struck me that this looks a lot like a high-tech version of the fussy self-experimentation Nietzsche practiced on himself, except organized around exactly the goal — pure self-preservation — that he treats as secondary at best and decadent at worst. So here's my question: if Nietzsche could see today's longevity-optimization culture, would he recognize it as continuous with his own disciplined attention to diet and what he calls "the great health," or would he see the specific goal behind it as a betrayal of everything he actually valued in self-discipline and self-overcoming? I'd like an answer that really engages with that tension rather than just picking whichever side sounds more dramatic.
