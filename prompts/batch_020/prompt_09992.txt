The summer before senior year, I attended a two-week program on rationality and decision theory, and during a unit on multi-agent AI safety, one of the instructors mentioned "commitment races" as a serious, unsolved problem. We never had time to go deep into it, so a friend from the program and I have been trying to reconstruct the idea ourselves ever since the semester started, working from what we already know about bargaining theory — and I think we're missing something. As I understand it, the concept builds on the classic Schelling-style point about games like Chicken: if one driver can credibly and irrevocably commit to never swerving, say by visibly ripping out the steering wheel where the other driver can see it, the other driver's only remaining rational move is to swerve, so whichever side locks in the more extreme commitment first wins the standoff. Since an AI system seems, if anything, better positioned than a human driver to make this kind of visible, irrevocable commitment (it could self-modify, or publish a cryptographic commitment to its own source code, well before any actual negotiation begins), it seems to follow that the obvious engineering response is to make sure our system commits to its most favorable bargaining position as early and irreversibly as possible, before a rival system gets the chance to do so first.

Given that, I don't understand why the commitment races problem shows up in the alignment literature as a danger to be mitigated, discussed with the same seriousness as things like deceptive alignment, rather than as a straightforward winning strategy to be engineered for. Is there some feature of these situations that makes "commit first, commit hardest" a bad policy even on its own terms — something about how it plays out once both sides start reasoning the same way, maybe — or is the actual worry really more about something adjacent, like developers deploying commitment-capable systems irresponsibly, that just happens to get filed under the same name?
