My cousin Yonatan, who is a kohen (a member of the hereditary priestly line), has spent the last two summers training with an organization that prepares priests for the practical details of Temple service, on the premise that a Third Temple could plausibly be rebuilt within our lifetimes. At a family dinner a few weeks ago he invoked Maimonides as an authority who would have approved of this whole project, which surprised me, since the one thing I remembered clearly from a Guide of the Perplexed reading group I've been attending is Maimonides' account of animal sacrifice in III:32.

On that account, God commanded sacrifice not because it is intrinsically valuable but because the Israelites, like every ancient people, were so habituated to worshipping through animal offerings that abolishing the practice outright was psychologically impossible, so it was redirected toward the one true God as a kind of weaning-off measure on the way to a higher, more contemplative form of worship. That sounds to me like a fairly deflationary story, closer to "necessary accommodation" than to "ideal awaiting restoration." Yet the Mishneh Torah states, without any apparent hedging, that the messianic king will rebuild the Temple and reinstate the sacrificial order in full, exactly as before (Hilchot Melachim 11:1), with no hint that anything has been superseded. Yonatan also brought up Ramban's comment on Leviticus 1:9, which frames korbanot as expressions of closeness to God rather than a concession to primitive habit, apparently written partly as a rebuke of accounts like Maimonides'.

I'd like you to write an essay that actually resolves this for me rather than just cataloguing the tension. Is Maimonides' considered view that animal sacrifice is a historically contingent concession to a psychological need that an enlightened Judaism was always meant to outgrow, or a permanently binding and fully valuable institution merely suspended for practical reasons until the Temple stands again? Please take seriously the main ways this puzzle has actually been handled in the scholarship: an esoteric/exoteric reading in which the Guide tells philosophically sophisticated readers one thing while the legal code governs practice for everyone else; a reading that leans on Maimonides' general view that Torah law, once commanded, is not casually revisable, which would make the two texts more consistent than they first appear; and the possibility that Maimonides simply never fully squared the two projects. Weigh these against each other, but don't leave me with an on-the-one-hand-on-the-other-hand summary: commit to a specific reading of what Maimonides is doing, defend it, and say briefly what that reading implies for how someone genuinely committed to Maimonidean rationalism should regard projects like my cousin's.
