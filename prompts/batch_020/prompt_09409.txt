A friend of mine who works in behavioral economics and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about the Allais paradox. She insisted that anyone who takes a certain $1 million over a 10% chance of $5 million, an 89% chance of $1 million, and a 1% chance of nothing, but then turns around and prefers a 10% chance of $5 million to an 11% chance of $1 million, is simply violating the independence axiom, full stop -- and that there is nothing more philosophically interesting to say beyond "people are inconsistent." I pushed back that this treats independence as though it were obviously constitutive of rational preference, which begs the question against approaches such as rank-dependent utility, Machina's generalized expected utility analysis, and risk-weighted models of choice, all of which reject independence as a requirement from the outset and can rationalize both Allais's original common-consequence pattern and the separate common-ratio pattern without attributing any error to the chooser. Neither of us budged.

I run a small decision-theory reading group that occasionally records its sessions as a podcast, and next month I'm interviewing a decision theorist known for developing a non-expected-utility theory in which an agent's global attitude toward the "shape" of risk in a gamble is itself a legitimate object of rational preference. She has argued in print that the classic Allais pattern is not a mistake for expected utility theory to explain away, but a rationally permissible preference that expected utility was wrong to exclude. Whenever pressed on the standard money-pump argument for independence, she tends to reply that the pump only threatens a chooser who re-evaluates "naively" or "sophisticatedly" at each node of a decision tree, not one who resolutely commits to a plan in advance. Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for me, sequenced so that each builds on the last and makes that reply progressively harder to lean on? I would like at least one question that pins down exactly how her framework handles the common-ratio effect rather than just the common-consequence case Allais originally used, at least one that presses on whether her appeal to resolute choice quietly imports a rationality constraint that her framework otherwise claims to do without, and a closing question asking what empirical or formal result would actually count as evidence against her view. For three of the questions, please add a short parenthetical noting the deflection she is likely to attempt and a sharper follow-up in case she takes it.
