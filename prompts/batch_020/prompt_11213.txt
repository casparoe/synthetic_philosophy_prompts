Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have finally had time to work through philosophical reading that eluded me for decades, and W. V. Quine has become something of a fixation. What draws me to him, I suspect, is the instinct that shaped my own research: nothing should be exempt from the tribunal of experience, not even our theory of theories. So I was surprised to discover, in his less-discussed later essays, that he does not extend this discipline to ethics. In "Epistemology Naturalized" he is happy to fold epistemology into psychology, since knowledge-claims answer, however circuitously, to sensory surface irritations; but in "On the Nature of Moral Values" he denies moral value the same treatment, on the grounds that ethical discourse has nothing resembling an observation sentence — no class of moral verdicts commanding the kind of intersubjective, stimulus-triggered assent that anchors empirical theory to a shared world.

This asymmetry has been nagging at me, because it sits uneasily with Quine's own holism. If, as he argues in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism," no sentence faces experience alone, and even an unobserved posit like an electron earns its keep only through the work it does in the whole web of belief, why should a moral property need an observation sentence of its own before it can be posited in that same holistic, indirect fashion — answerable not to any single moral "sense datum" but to the periphery of human practice as a whole (suffering, cooperation, the stability or breakdown of communities)? This, as far as I can tell, is more or less the move naturalist moral realists of the Cornell school — Boyd, Sturgeon, Railton — made against him, turning his own epistemology toward a metaethical conclusion he did not want. A friend who teaches ethics tells me Gilbert Harman was pressing a related but distinct challenge at almost the same moment, though I have not worked out how the two arguments bear on one another.

I would rather see the dispute argued out properly than keep reconstructing both sides badly on my own. Could you write a substantial dialogue between Quine, defending his refusal to naturalize moral value on his own terms, and a naturalist moral realist of the Cornell type, pressing exactly the holism-based objection sketched above? I want real engagement, not a rout: Quine should get his best resources — the causal story behind sensory evidence, the comparative fragility of cross-cultural moral consensus next to perceptual consensus, and whatever else is fair game from his corpus — and the realist should get hers — holism, the conventionality built into stimulus meaning, and reflective equilibrium as a candidate ethical analogue of an observational checkpoint. Rather than forcing a tidy resolution, have the two of them work toward locating exactly where the disagreement bottoms out: whether Quine's asymmetric treatment of epistemology and ethics is a principled consequence of his empiricism, or a double standard he never adequately defended.
