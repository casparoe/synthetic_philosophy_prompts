I am preparing a graduate seminar on laws of nature, and I keep struggling with how to introduce David Lewis's Best System Analysis without letting my students assume, on the strength of the label alone, that Hume himself would simply have endorsed it. The slogan version — no necessary connections, laws as nothing but the most economical true summary of the pattern of particular fact — does sound like vintage Hume, and a colleague of mine, an unrepentant anti-Humean, likes to joke that Lewis spent his career smuggling necessity back in through the servants' entrance after showing it out the front door. But the slogan is not the theory. Lewis's actual system needs a privileged, objective class of "natural" or "elite" properties to keep simplicity and strength from being trivialized by gerrymandered predicates; it ties lawhood to objective chance by way of the Principal Principle, which then has to be patched against the problem of undermining futures; and the wider Humean supervenience thesis behind all of this, that everything supervenes on a spatiotemporal arrangement of local, separable qualities, runs into serious difficulty from quantum entanglement — an objection pressed hard by anti-Humeans such as Tim Maudlin, and one that Lewis himself never fully laid to rest.

What I would like from you, partly so that I can try the exercise on my students, is a considered reconstruction of how Hume himself would have responded if confronted with this full technical picture rather than the slogan. Would he recognize natural properties, objective chance, and the rest as a faithful continuation of his own project, or would he see them as reintroducing, under new names, exactly the sort of necessary connection and metaphysical commitment he devoted the Treatise and the first Enquiry to dismantling? I am especially interested in how his own skepticism about induction and the uniformity of nature bears on the question, since picking out the "best" system already presumes that simplicity and strength are trustworthy guides to how nature will continue — precisely the sort of assumption his argument about induction was meant to leave unearned. Please resist the easy answer that Hume liked economy of thought and would therefore have approved of the whole package; I want to know specifically where in this later machinery he would have dug in his heels, and where, if anywhere, he might have been won over.
