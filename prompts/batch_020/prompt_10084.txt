So my friend Priya (we did our MA together, now we're both ABD in the same philosophy program) is getting married in three weeks and asked me to do the toast at the reception. Here's the wrinkle: her whole comps portfolio, and now her dissertation prospectus, is built around the "Ideal" chapter of Moore's Principia Ethica -- the part where he argues the greatest goods anyone can point to are personal affection and the enjoyment of beauty, and that almost nothing else survives once you run it through his isolation test (imagine the thing totally on its own, stripped of everything else, and ask if its bare existence is still worth much). For like four years the running bit in our cohort has been Priya screening potential partners against "the two great goods," and everyone in the department has heard her do the heap-of-filth thought experiment at parties -- the one where Moore asks you to compare a gorgeous world and an ugly one that no one will ever, ever see, to argue beauty has to have some value independent of anyone enjoying it. So I really want to build the toast around this stuff, it would feel weird not to at this point.

Problem is the room's split right down the middle. Half is our department, including two professors who will clock it instantly if I flatten Moore's actual view into generic "love and beauty matter" greeting-card sentiment, or just get the isolation test wrong. The other half is Wren's family and coworkers, who don't know G.E. Moore from Thomas More and need the thing to land as a love story, full stop, no footnotes required.

And theres a real trap hiding in the material itself: Moore's account of personal affection is weirdly clinical once you look right at it -- its admiration of someone's actual, particular good qualities plus whatever emotion is "appropriate" to them -- and said baldly at a wedding that can come out sounding like "I ran the numbers and you cleared the bar," which is exactly the opposite of what a toast is supposed to do. Can you write me something like a 4-5 minute toast that uses Moore's real view as the actual spine of the thing (the isolation test, the organic-unity bit about the whole being worth more than the sum of the parts, whatever earns its place) and includes one joke that the philosophers in the room get instantly while everyone else still laughs at without needing the reference explained? If I have to choose, I'd rather it undersell the philosophy and oversell the affection than the other way around.
