So I got picked for jury duty last month, first time ever, and we ended up on a criminal case that went to deliberation for like two full days before we finally convicted the guy. It was split 9-3 for most of the first day and I could not stop thinking about whether we'd actually gotten it right, so afterward I went down kind of a rabbit hole reading about the statistics of group decisions, which is how I found Condorcet's jury theorem. As I understand it, the theorem basically proves that if you get a bunch of people voting yes/no on something and each person is even a little bit better than a coin flip at getting it right, the majority verdict becomes almost guaranteed to be correct once the group is big enough - like, mathematically guaranteed, not just "probably." That part seems pretty solid to me since it's really just probability at the end of the day, not some soft social-science claim.

So now I dont get why courts are so conservative about jury design. If the theorem is right, shouldnt we be empaneling like 40 or 50 people per trial instead of 12, and requiring something like 90% agreement instead of either simple majority or full unanimity, since that would obviously get us way closer to the "guaranteed correct" limit the theorem describes? My cousin, who's a public defender, told me it's "not that simple" because deliberating jurors aren't voting independently the way the theorem needs them to, but I don't really buy that objection - they still each cast one individual vote at the end, so I don't see why the underlying math would stop applying. To make things more confusing, I also stumbled on an old political-science paper arguing that requiring unanimity can actually make wrongful convictions MORE likely rather than less, which sounds completely backwards to me and I can't figure out how that's even consistent with the jury theorem in the first place. Is there something wrong with how i'm reading the theorem here, or is this really just a case of the justice system not having caught up with two-hundred-year-old math?
