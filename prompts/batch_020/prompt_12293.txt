I'm a PhD student in information science, and this semester I got voluntold onto my university's new "algorithmic oversight" committee, set up after some bad press about a totally different tool last year. Right now we're deciding whether to renew the contract for the early-alert system our advising office uses to flag students as high-risk for non-completion -- getting flagged triggers a mandatory advising hold, so it's a real burden, not just a label. The vendors annual audit shows the score is nicely calibrated by first-gen status: among students the tool rates as high-risk, the actual non-completion rate ends up basically the same for first-gen and continuing-gen students. But a sociologist on the committee pulled the confusion matrices herself and found the false-positive rate is way higher for first-gen students (a lot of them get flagged and turn out totally fine), while the false-negative rate skews the opposite way for continuing-gen students. She wants the contract killed over that gap; the vendor's rep just keeps repeating that calibration is the "gold standard" for fair scoring, and I can tell most of the non-technical members on the committee are going to side with whoever sounds most confident on Thursday.

My hunch, from what I know of the fairness-in-ML literature, is that this isn't a bug the vendor can quietly patch -- if the base rates genuinely differ between first-gen and continuing-gen students, you structurally can't have calibration and equal false-positive/false-negative rates at the same time (it's basically the Kleinberg/Chouldechova impossibility result playing out on my own campus), so re-thresholding to close the sociologist's gap would just break calibration somewhere else. Not that the memo itself should namedrop any of that -- what I actually need is the memo, the thing I'd circulate before Thursday's meeting: pick renew-as-is, renew-with-conditions, or terminate, attach an actual timeframe, and make the case in a way that treats the sociologist's objection as a genuine tradeoff rather than a math misunderstanding, without just letting "calibration is industry standard" win by default becuase it sounds more technical.
