I spent my working life in experimental solid-state physics, and only in retirement have I begun reading philosophy in earnest. Eugene Wigner's essay on the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics has stuck with me more than almost anything else I've read this year, mainly because of the pattern behind his examples: complex numbers, non-Euclidean geometry, Hilbert spaces, and the rest were all worked out by mathematicians chasing internal elegance and logical consistency, with no thought of physical application, and yet each was later found to be exactly the tool nature required. On reflection this pattern looks close to universal -- give any sufficiently developed branch of pure mathematics enough time and physics eventually puts it to use -- and if that generalization holds, it seems to me that mathematical Platonism becomes almost unavoidable, since no rival account, whether formalism, fictionalism, or anything else that treats mathematics as a free human invention, can explain why invented symbols should be so reliably pre-fitted to a world that was never consulted when they were invented. Have I understood Wigner's argument correctly, and does it really leave Platonism as the only view left standing?
