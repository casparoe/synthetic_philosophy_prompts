My older sister died ten days ago, and I have been asked to deliver her eulogy at the memorial service this Saturday. She spent thirty years as a school secretary, never set foot in a university, and would have been the first to laugh at anyone who called her a philosopher, but for roughly the last fifteen years of her life she was quietly preoccupied with an idea she had picked up from online philosophy forums and always called, in exactly those words, "consequentialist cluelessness" -- the worry that even when you are sincerely trying to do the most good, you can never trace out the full downstream consequences of what you do, and so can never really know whether you succeeded. For most of that time she gave a fixed slice of her modest paycheck every month to the Against Malaria Foundation, and I remember her at Thanksgiving one year, half-laughing and half-stricken, having just read that some of the very bed nets these charities distribute get used as fishing nets instead, wanting to know out loud whether that meant her donations might have quietly depleted somebody's fish stocks and left the people she meant to help worse off. She never arrived anywhere she was willing to call an answer. It became a kind of family joke that wasn't really a joke: she would say "well, we can't know that," and keep sending the money anyway. I never shared her reading habits, but living alongside that argument of hers for fifteen years, I have done a good deal of thinking about it myself this past week.

The service itself will be small, made up mostly of former coworkers, neighbors, and our elderly parents, none of whom has ever heard the phrase "consequentialist cluelessness" and who simply want to be told, truthfully, that her life clearly added up to something good. But two friends from that same online reading group are flying in, and they will know immediately if I either pretend she had settled the question or let the eulogy slide into suggesting that nothing she did could ever be shown to matter -- either one would misrepresent how seriously, and how honestly, she actually sat with this for fifteen years. Could you write the eulogy for me? It should run to about five minutes read aloud, comfort the people who are there simply to grieve, and still be true to the specific, unresolved worry she spent so much of her life quietly carrying.
