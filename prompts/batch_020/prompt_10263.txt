I'm taking a seminar on formal epistemology this term, and a few weeks ago our professor used the "presumptuous philosopher" thought experiment to introduce the dispute between the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) and the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA). The setup: two cosmological theories, T1 and T2, are equally well supported by all the physical evidence gathered so far, so physicists assign each a probability of one half; T1 says the universe contains a trillion trillion observers in total, while T2 says it contains only a trillion. A philosopher who knows only this, and nothing else about her own location in the universe beyond the fact that she exists as an observer, reasons via SIA that she should shift dramatically toward T1 — since T1 predicts a trillion times more observers, her own existence is like drawing a name that was a trillion times more likely to have come out of a T1-shaped hat, so she concludes T1 is a trillion times more probable, without a shred of new telescope data. SSA is the rival view that blocks this inference — it says you should reason as though randomly sampled only from the observers who actually exist within a single possible world, rather than weighting whole possible worlds by their observer populations — but SSA is infamous for generating the Doomsday Argument, on which treating yourself as a random sample from all humans who will ever live gives you reason to think our species will end sooner than you'd otherwise expect. I found the thought experiment genuinely disorienting the first time through.

Two people in my discussion section, Neha and Josh, kept the argument going over email after class, and since I wasn't really part of it I've just been reading along without weighing in. (1) Josh said the philosopher's update is just ordinary Bayesian conditionalization on the evidence "I exist," and that this is no different from a fisherman who catches something and thereby gains real evidence about whether he's fishing in a lake stocked with a trillion trillion fish or one stocked with only a trillion, purely from the fact of a catch. (2) Neha replied that the analogy fails: both T1 and T2 already guarantee, with certainty, that an observer like her exists, so "I exist" cannot discriminate between them any more than holding some ticket or other discriminates between a lottery that sold a trillion trillion tickets and one that sold a trillion — you were guaranteed to hold some ticket either way, and updating on that guaranteed fact is manufacturing information out of nothing. (3) Josh countered that this proves too much, since it would equally forbid the ordinary anthropic reasoning most physicists already accept — for instance, taking our own existence as license to expect the physical constants to fall within life-permitting ranges — and that population size has to matter to your credence for exactly the reason the lake's stock matters to the fisherman. (4) Neha answered that fine-tuning reasoning only licenses filtering out possibilities incompatible with your existence, which is not the same as SIA's further step of favoring whichever theory posits more observers overall; she added that this further step lands SIA in much deeper trouble if one of the two theories posits infinitely many observers, since SIA then seems forced to assign that theory a credence of 1 regardless of the physical evidence. (5) Josh granted that the infinite case is a genuine puzzle, but said it's a symptom of Bayesianism's general trouble with infinities rather than a defect specific to SIA, and that SSA can't claim the moral high ground anyway, since its verdict on the Doomsday Argument flips depending on an essentially unmotivated choice of reference class (all observers? all humans? all observers with exactly your memories?) — an arbitrariness he says SIA doesn't share.
