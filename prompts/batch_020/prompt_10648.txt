In my school's philosophy club, our sponsor told us about an old decision-theory puzzle called "Death in Damascus": a man meets Death in Damascus, and Death tells him he'll be coming for him tomorrow, so that night the man flees to Aleppo instead. The twist is that Death is essentially never wrong about where the person it's after will be, so wherever the man actually decides to spend the night is exactly where Death will show up. Afterward, two people in the club, Theo and Ada, got into an argument about what the puzzle actually shows, and I wrote down their exchange because neither one backed down.

(1) Theo said the lesson is simple: since Death tracks you perfectly no matter what, staying and fleeing are equally dangerous, so the man might as well stay in Damascus and skip the trip.

(2) Ada said that's too quick. If Death really is that good a predictor, then the moment the man settles on "I'll stay," that very resolve is what makes Damascus look dangerous, and the moment he settles on "I'll flee," that resolve makes Aleppo look dangerous instead. So there's no option he can settle on that doesn't look worse right after he's settled on it. The puzzle isn't showing that the two options are equally safe -- it's showing that "figure out which option is safer and pick it" breaks down completely here, since the danger of an option only shows up once he's already leaning toward it.

(3) Theo answered that this is a fact about the man's state of mind while he's deliberating, not about what he should actually do, since nothing he decides tonight can reach backward and move Death to a different city -- Death has already gone wherever Death has gone. Once you remember that his decision can't relocate Death, the two options really are on a par, and what Ada is calling an "instability" is just a symptom of reasoning badly, not a real feature of the situation.

(4) Ada replied that this gets it backward: it's precisely because his choice can't move Death around that the instability is a genuine problem and not just sloppy thinking. The story the man tells himself for fleeing -- that Death is probably in Damascus -- is undercut by his own act of fleeing, and that has nothing to do with backward causation; it's about what his choice is evidence of. If pointing out that there's no backward causation here were enough to make the problem disappear, she said, decision theorists would have stopped arguing about cases like this a long time ago.

I'd also like to know whether what either of them is describing has a real name in the decision theory literature, and whether there's a way of resolving the puzzle that both of them are missing.
