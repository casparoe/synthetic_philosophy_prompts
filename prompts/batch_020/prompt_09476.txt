For thirty-one years I ran an experimental physics laboratory, and it is only since retiring that I have begun reading philosophy with any seriousness, mostly through a small discussion circle that meets monthly at our local university's continuing-education center. Last month one of the group's younger members shamed me into rereading Turing's 1950 paper, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," past the famous opening pages that everyone quotes, and I was startled to find that one of the last objections he takes up, shortly before turning to learning machines, is what he calls the argument from extra-sensory perception. He treats the statistical case for telepathy as close to overwhelming, and rather than waving the objection away, he proposes making the physical arrangements of the imitation game "telepathy-proof."

Our circle has invited a historian of science to next month's meeting who has written on precisely this neglected section and argues that it is continuous with, rather than an embarrassment to, the rest of Turing's method. I have been asked to prepare the questions, and would rather not embarrass myself with anything a first-year student could ask. Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for this guest? I would like at least one question on the apparent tension between Turing's insistence elsewhere in the paper on purely behavioral, operational criteria for thought and his willingness to entertain a phenomenon that, on its face, would operate outside any behavioral channel; one that tests whether a "telepathy-proof" setting is even a coherent fix, given that it seems to presuppose the same kind of physical, distance-sensitive channel that psychical researchers of that era insisted telepathy was not; and one asking what, if anything, a reader who is confident that ESP is not real should still take away from this part of the paper. For each of these, please add a brief follow-up question keyed to the answer you expect the guest actually to give, in the manner of an interviewer who does not let a comfortable answer pass unchallenged, and please keep the whole list free of warm-up questions of the "what first drew you to this topic" variety.
