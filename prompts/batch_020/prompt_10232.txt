I spent my working life as a field entomologist, running host-plant surveys for a group of leaf beetles whose larvae are notoriously fussy eaters, and since retiring I have enrolled in a local philosophy of science course, partly to fill the time and partly out of a nagging sense that I never examined the logic behind methods I used unreflectively for forty years. This week's session was on Hempel's raven paradox, and it has stayed with me more than anything else so far. The setup, as our instructor presented it, is this: 'All ravens are black' is logically equivalent to 'All non-black things are non-ravens' (she called this the Equivalence Condition), and if one accepts Nicod's criterion — that an object satisfying both the antecedent and the consequent of a conditional hypothesis confirms it, while anything failing to satisfy the antecedent is simply irrelevant to it — then a red apple or a white shoe is a positive instance of the second formulation, and therefore, by equivalence, confirms the first as well. Hempel himself accepted this conclusion, arguing that our discomfort comes from smuggling in background knowledge about the world (how many non-black things there are, how many ravens), not from any defect in the logic itself.

What has kept me turning this over is how directly it bears on my own old fieldwork, and on a citizen-recording scheme I still help run. Our working hypothesis for years was of exactly the raven's form: 'this beetle occurs only on knotweed.' On the Nicod story, every non-knotweed plant a volunteer checked and found beetle-free was just as much a confirming instance as every infested knotweed plant we found — the two only come apart once you bring in something like relative class sizes, which is the usual way people rescue our intuition that they shouldn't count equally: since non-knotweed vegetation vastly outnumbers knotweed in any survey area, the clean non-host plant confirms the hypothesis too, just by an amount so small as to be negligible. That much I had more or less made peace with. But I have also come across a counterexample, which I believe is due to I.J. Good, showing that under certain background assumptions about the space of alternative hypotheses, even finding an infested knotweed plant — an uncontroversial positive instance, not some contrapositive trick — can fail to confirm the hypothesis, or even disconfirm it. That seems to threaten Nicod's criterion on its home ground, not just at the paradoxical margins.

I would be grateful if you could set out what you take to be the most important implications of the raven paradox, and of these attempted resolutions, for how a group of volunteer recorders like ours should actually handle non-detections in the field. Concretely: should 'checked, beetle-free' on a non-host plant be logged as data at all? Does the relative-class-size story give us a principled way to weight it against positive sightings, rather than just a way to reassure ourselves that we needn't worry about it? And does Good's counterexample mean that even our positive sightings cannot be interpreted independently of background assumptions about rival hypotheses that we, as working naturalists rather than statisticians, have generally never bothered to state?
