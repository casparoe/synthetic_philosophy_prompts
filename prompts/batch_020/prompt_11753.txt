Okay, this one's a bit personal -- my uncle, who was a semi-retired math professor, passed away this past fall, and while we were clearing out his office I found his copy of Hao Wang's "A Logical Journey" absolutely destroyed with marginalia, but almost all of it clustered on the chapters about Gödel's private philosophical views rather than the incompleteness stuff everyone actually cites him for. I had no idea that alongside the logic, Gödel apparently worked out this whole rationalist case for an afterlife -- something like: if the world is genuinely rational (in that strong Leibnizian sense he was obsessed with, where everything has a sufficient reason), then it would be absurd for a mind to spend a lifetime developing itself only to be snuffed out just as its really getting somewhere, so there must be something after. Apparently this shows up both in letters he wrote to his mother defending the idea and in his private notebooks (I think they're called the "Max Phil" notebooks -- written in this old German shorthand, Gabelsberger, that people are only now finishing transcribing). Finding all that right when I did messed with me a little, and now I'm kind of obsessed with the topic.

Anyway, I co-run a small history-of-mathematics podcast, and next month we're recording an episode with a historian of logic who's spent years working through the Gödel Nachlass and knows this material cold. I really don't want the episode to just rehash the standard biography beats (Einstein walks, the poisoning fears, starving himself at the end) -- I want to actually pressure-test the afterlife argument as philosophy. Could you draft me 8-10 interview questions that dig into: the actual logical shape of the "rational world" argument and whether the conclusion even follows from the premises he'd grant; how it connects to his fixation on Leibniz's monadology and his belief that Leibniz's own papers had been suppressed; whether it sits comfortably with, or actually strains against, his Platonism about mathematical objects; the "well he was also paranoid and died refusing food, so how seriously can we take any of this" dismissal that skeptics reach for; and what, if anything, a rigorous logician's private theology tells us about the relationship between formal rationality and religious belief more generally. For each question, add a short follow-up that anticipates the careful, hedging answer a scholar would probably give, so the conversation doesn't just stall out there.
