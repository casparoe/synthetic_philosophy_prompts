I'm about two-thirds through a novel where the framing gimmick is that David Hume gets yanked out of 1760s Edinburgh and dropped into the present day for about a week (don't ask how, it's not that kind of book so I'm keeping the mechanism deliberately vague). Most of it is him reacting to the obvious stuff - evolution, feminism, the NHS - but theres a dinner party scene I keep rewriting where he gets cornered by a crypto guy going on about how Bitcoin is "sound money" that will finally save us from central banks printing money out of thin air and from a national debt thats spiraling completely out of control. My writing group likes the setup but keeps telling me Hume's actual response reads as generic 18th-century-guy-is-baffled-by-modern-thing, and I think they're right - I've basically just been putting "how delightful, free markets" in his mouth instead of anything he'd really think.

So here's what I want from you: based on what Hume actually argued in essays like Of Money, Of the Balance of Trade, and Of Public Credit, what would his real verdict be? Two things I'm trying to work out - would he look at Bitcoin's fixed supply and see it as basically the second coming of specie, i.e. exactly the kind of "hard money" he'd approve of, or does that comparison miss something he thought was actually important about what money does in an economy? I don't need a wishy-washy "on the one hand, on the other hand" - I need him to actually land somewhere on both counts, just so long as it's a landing the real Hume would sign off on and not whatever's most dramatically convenient for my scene.
