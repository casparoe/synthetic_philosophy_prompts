I've recently been reading about multi-agent perspectives on AI safety, and I keep running into references to the "commitment races problem" as a distinctive obstacle to safe bargaining among sufficiently advanced agents. The summaries I've found so far gesture at the idea in a sentence or two -- roughly, that agents capable of making credible commitments have an incentive to commit as early as possible, and that this can end badly -- but I haven't found an explanation that spells out the underlying mechanism clearly enough for me to reconstruct it myself. I follow the basic Schelling-style intuition that in bargaining, the party who visibly and irrevocably commits first to an aggressive demand (the classic image is someone disabling their own steering wheel in a game of chicken) can force a rational opponent to concede; what I don't yet understand is why extending this logic to more sophisticated agents turns into a "race" with genuinely catastrophic downside, rather than just an ordinary first-mover advantage that far-sighted parties could simply contract around in advance.

Could you help me reconstruct the argument in more detail? I'm especially interested in three things: first, why there is supposed to be a real tension between committing early, in order to gain bargaining leverage, and committing wisely, only once an agent has adequately reflected on its values and on the structure of the game it is in, and why this tension resists being dissolved by some prior meta-level agreement to commit only after joint deliberation; second, how this problem is supposed to interact with updateless or functional decision theories, which already build in something commitment-like by having an agent optimize its policy from a position "prior" to conditioning on certain information -- does leaning on this kind of decision theory make the commitment race better, worse, or basically orthogonal to it; and third, whether any of the mitigations proposed in this literature, such as norms around transparency, restrictions on which kinds of commitment devices are permissible, or delegating negotiation to a more reflective surrogate, actually resolve the underlying dilemma or merely push the same race back a level. I would rather come away with a clear sense of the game-theoretic logic that makes this problem hard to dissolve than with a survey of who has written about it.
