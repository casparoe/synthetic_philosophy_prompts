I sit on my state's Species Status Review Panel, which rules on legal protection for wildlife populations. We must decide on the Blue Hollow springfish, confined to three spring pools that feed into a river where the common, secure Piedmont springfish is otherwise abundant. Nuclear DNA shows the Blue Hollow fish form a sharply distinct cluster, with their own coloration and adaptations to spring life, but their mitochondrial DNA is nested inside the common species' diversity, a signature of old introgression, and in wet years a culvert lets the two interbreed and produce fertile offspring. A landowner coalition opposing the listing, which would block a groundwater permit that two of the three remaining pools need to survive, argues the fish are a hybridizing local variant, not a genuine evolutionary lineage, and the genetics genuinely support that reading, not merely their economic interest. Our statute also lets us list, short of full species status, a vertebrate "distinct population segment" markedly separate from other populations and significant to the taxon, without settling what the fish really are. The panel votes in three weeks, before the permit is finalized. Draft the memo I'll circulate beforehand: it should recommend a specific course of action with a timeline, and it should take the landowners' scientific objection seriously rather than dismiss it as bad faith.
