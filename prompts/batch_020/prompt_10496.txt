I spend a fair amount of my spare time reading AI-safety and decision-theory forums -- I'm a software engineer by training, not a philosopher, so I come at all this as an interested outsider rather than someone who has studied it formally. Recently I went down a rabbit hole on "program equilibrium": the idea that if two agents in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma submit literal source code instead of an action, and each program is allowed to reason about, or try to prove things about, the other's code before it moves, mutual cooperation becomes achievable in a way it never is for ordinary pure or mixed strategies, where defection strictly dominates.

The construction that struck me most is the so-called "FairBot": a program that cooperates with an opponent's code just in case it can find a formal proof that the opponent cooperates with it. What's strange is that the justification for why two FairBots don't just chase each other in an infinite regress -- "A can only prove B cooperates if B can prove A cooperates, which in turn requires..." -- rests on Löb's theorem from provability logic, not on anything like trust, communication, or repeated play. Talking this over with a couple of people who read the same material, I noticed a real disagreement about how much weight the result can actually bear, and I'd rather see that disagreement played out than have it summarized for me.

Could you write a dialogue between two people on opposite sides of this? One should hold that Löbian program equilibrium is a genuinely load-bearing result -- a real escape from one-shot-dilemma pessimism with concrete implications for how transparent, source-code-legible AI systems might cooperate without external enforcement. The other should hold that it is an elegant piece of sleight of hand: it works only by assuming away the actually hard part of cooperation (exact, mutual code transparency plus unbounded proof search that no real agent has); it grows fragile once you bound the proof search -- in ways that have been discussed as producing a kind of chicken game over how long each side is willing to keep searching before giving up and defecting; plain FairBot cooperates rather indiscriminately, which is part of why the literature moved on to more careful variants like "PrudentBot"; and even in its cleanest form, the whole apparatus just relocates the old question of why anyone would build a cooperative disposition in the first place, rather than answering it. Please make both sides equally sharp -- no strawman skeptic who is simply confused about the logic, and no strawman enthusiast who waves away the fragility results -- and have them respond to each other's actual objections as the conversation develops rather than talk past one another.
