So this week in my seminar on effective altruism and longtermism (officially it's called something like "Ethics and the Far Future," I forget) we spent basically a whole class on this objection to consequentialism called "cluelessness," and it's been stuck in my head since. The idea is that consequentialism tells you to do whatever produces the best outcomes, but we're actually clueless about almost all the downstream effects of anything we do, since causal chains ripple outward forever -- the professor's example was donating a bednet: sure it saves a kid, but that kid grows up, has their own kids, uses resources, maybe becomes a teacher or a warlord, and there's no principled way to fold any of that into the calculation. She drew a distinction that I guess comes from Greaves (and apparently there's a much older, darker version of basically the same worry from a paper by Lenman, something from the 90s or 2000s) between "simple" cluelessness, where the effects you can't foresee are as likely to be good as bad so they wash out and you can basically ignore them, versus "complex" cluelessness, where you DO have some reason to think the unforeseeable effects skew one way, but nowhere near enough to say by how much or weigh it against what you can see. Apparently it's the second one that's supposed to be the actual threat to consequentialism as something you could live your life by, not just a fun thought experiment.

I definately want to see this done as a story instead of an argument, because reading about it made me feel kind of ill in a way a philosophy reading isn't really supposed to. Could you write me a short story, maybe 1200-1800 words, that's built around complex cluelessness specifically -- not a generic "tiny actions have huge unforseen consequences" butterfly-effect story, since honestly that's just the simple version and kind of misses the whole point. Any genre or setting is fine, but I'd actually prefer it stay mundane and present-day (someone deciding on a donation, a job, whether to report something, whatever) rather than leaning on time travel or some device that literally shows the character the future, since that sort of cheats the epistemic problem away. I just want a character who ends up genuinely stuck in that specific complex-cluelessness way, where thinking harder makes the decision worse instead of better, and the story finds a way to make that feeling land without anyone stopping to monologue the theory at anyone else.
