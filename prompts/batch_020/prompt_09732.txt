I'm not a historian or philosopher by training, just someone who reads a lot on my own, and I've been turning something over in my head for a few weeks now. I got interested in John von Neumann after reading about the Manhattan Project and realizing he's also one of the people credited with building the first real computers, and digging around from there led me to a short essay he wrote for Fortune magazine in 1955 called "Can We Survive Technology?" The core argument that stuck with me is that humanity had always been cushioned from the worst effects of new technology by having more geographical and political room to expand into, and that by the 1950s this cushion was basically used up, since the earth itself is finite. He works through nuclear energy, automation, global communications, and climate control as examples, and at one point almost casually notes that carbon dioxide from burning coal and oil might already be measurably warming the planet. His conclusion is fairly bleak: he says outright that the danger can't be solved by singling out and banning whichever technology looks most dangerous at the moment, that trying to cleanly separate the "good" uses of a technology from the "bad" ones never holds up for long, and that no permanent fix is coming, only a long string of small, opportunistic, case-by-case decisions, backed by nothing sturdier than ordinary human patience, flexibility, and intelligence.

Here's the part I can't quite square. Separately, I read that his friend Stanislaw Ulam, writing a tribute not long after von Neumann's death, recalled that von Neumann used to talk in private conversation about technological change accelerating toward some kind of "essential singularity," beyond which human affairs as we currently understand them could no longer continue - and this, apparently, is the actual origin of how people use the word "singularity" today when they talk about AI. That sounds like a far more dramatic, discontinuous idea than "just keep muddling through, one small decision at a time," and I haven't been able to work out whether these are really two sides of one coherent outlook or just two different moods he happened to be in on different occasions.

So here's my question, assuming both of these really do reflect his considered views rather than just being flattened by whoever was reporting them: if von Neumann were alive today and looking at the current arguments over whether advanced AI should be paused, tightly regulated, or brought under some binding international treaty, which of these two impulses do you think would win out in him? Would he treat calls for a pause or treaty as exactly the kind of naive search for a "cure-all" he warned against in his own essay, or would he decide that AI is precisely the sort of technology that crosses the line he gestured at with Ulam, and therefore deserves the drastic new political invention his essay otherwise refused to spell out? I'd like you to actually work through the tension rather than just picking a side, using what these two sources suggest about how his mind worked rather than a generic impression of what a brilliant Cold War-era scientist would probably say about artificial intelligence.
