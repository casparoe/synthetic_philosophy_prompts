Long-time lurker on LW/ACX (and the old SSC archives before that), never really posted much myself, but this stuff has finally landed in my actual job. Molyneux's problem comes up on the rationalist-adjacent internet every couple of years -- the old thought experiment about whether someone born blind, who can already tell a cube from a sphere by touch, would recognize them by sight alone the instant they gained vision -- and I remember threads tying it to real cases: Project Prakash and similar programs that restore sight to people (often kids, sometimes adults) who've been blind since infancy from treatable stuff like dense cataracts, plus older material like Oliver Sacks' essay about the patient he calls Virgil, whose cataract surgery as an adult left him disoriented and honestly pretty miserable instead of delighted. I always found that stuff fascinating in a purely abstract way, so its a little surreal that it's now landed on my desk for real.

Here's the situation: I sit on the IRB (human-subjects ethics board) at a hospital that's about to start a similar sight-restoration program, and the surgical team wants to bolt on a reserach protocol -- right after the bandages come off, before the patient's allowed to touch anything, show them an object they already know by feel and see if they can pick it out by sight alone, then repeat over the following weeks. Half the committee wants the consent form to say, explicitly, that patients will probably fail this at first, that the early weeks of vision might be confusing or even distressing rather than a straightforward gift (citing exactly the Virgil-type outcomes), and that this is a genuinely open question about how perception works, not a formality to get through. The other half thinks that's paternalistic overkill dressed up as rigor, worries it could talk desperate patients out of sight-restoring surgery over what's basically old philosophy trivia to them, and -- more seriously -- thinks priming subjects with detailed expectations of failure could bias the very data the researchers are trying to collect. I need to write the actual memo the committee votes on Monday: a specific recommendation for what informed consent has to include (or a recommendation to drop the testing protocol entirely, if that's your honest conclusion) -- not "let's form a subcommittee" -- and it has to genuinely engage the bias/paternalism worry instead of just steamrolling it, since a couple of the researchers on our board will be in the room and won't be satisfied with hand-waving.
