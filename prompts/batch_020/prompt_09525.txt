So I'm on my state's new algorithmic accountability board, and next month we vote on whether to keep using a fraud-detection model that flags unemployment claims for manual review -- if you're flagged, your benefits just sit there for weeks while a human reviews it. The vendor says the model is fair because its calibrated by claimant subgroup, including primary language: within any given risk score, the same fraction of flagged claims are confirmed fraud no matter what subgroup your in. A legal-aid board member ran the actual numbers and says that because confirmed-fraud base rates differ by subgroup in the historical data, that same calibration mathematically guarantees unequal error rates -- non-English-primary claimants get flagged (and their money held up) way more relative to how much fraud they actually commit, while real fraud among some other subgroup slips through more often. I don't think this is a bug the vendor can quietly patch -- pretty sure it's the actual fairness-impossibility-theorem thing I keep half remembering from some Scott Alexander post, or maybe it was the Kleinberg/Chouldechova papers themselves -- you can't get calibration and equalized error rates at once unless the base rates already match. Half the board thinks calibration is the only fairness notion that's even mathematically coherent here, and that "fixing" the error rates means explicit subgroup-specific thresholds, which they really don't want to touch legally. The other half thinks a tool that predictably shafts one group for weeks at a stretch isn't fair just because it's calibrated. Can you draft the actual memo I'd bring to the board -- pick approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions, give it a timeframe, and actually engage with whichever side you don't take instead of just gesturing at it?
