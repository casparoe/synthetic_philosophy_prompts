Before I retired I spent three decades in geophysics, much of it on seismic inversion -- reconstructing subsurface structure from surface-recorded waveforms -- so I have a professional wariness of inverse problems: the map from underlying cause to observed data is rarely invertible without smuggling in extra assumptions to break the ties. Since retiring I've been reading fairly widely in philosophy and its neighboring corners of AI research, and I recently came across inverse reinforcement learning as a way of inferring human preferences from observed behavior. It strikes me as the same trouble with an extra twist: in seismic inversion everyone at least agrees on the wave equation, whereas here there is no fixed 'forward model' linking preferences to behavior, since you don't independently know how rational or noisy the person's choices are. So any given stretch of behavior seems as though it could be explained equally well by a rational agent with one reward function, or a somewhat irrational agent with quite a different one, with nothing in the data alone able to adjudicate between them. Could you explain, without leaning too heavily on the mathematics, how deep this indeterminacy really runs, and whether the standard remedies -- assuming a particular noise or rationality model, or placing a joint prior over reward and rationality together -- yield genuine identifiability, or merely a principled way of favoring one story over another?
