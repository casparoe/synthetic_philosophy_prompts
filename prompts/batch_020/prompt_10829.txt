Dr. Ferris has taught the only philosophy elective at my school for more than twenty years, and she's retiring the same week my class graduates. By a small tradition, a student from her last class speaks about her at the ceremony, and this year that's me. Every spring she ends the course with Moritz Schlick's essay "On the Meaning of Life," and she genuinely believes he got it right: that meaning isn't in the purposive activity filling most of adult life — doing one thing for the sake of another — but in play, whatever is done for its own sake, with childhood as the clearest case and art as the nearest adult equivalent. She's asked me, half-joking, not to let the tribute collapse into the usual commencement move of praising her for preparing us for our futures or giving us purpose, since she thinks that's exactly the mistake her favorite essay diagnoses. The difficulty is that the ceremony isn't a philosophy seminar: her former students will catch every reference, but my classmates who never took her class, the parents, and the rest of the faculty have no idea who Schlick is and expect the usual uplifting, forward-looking send-off. Please write the three-minute tribute I'll actually deliver — true to what she really thinks made her life and teaching meaningful, and still moving for a room that has never heard his name.
