In 1615, Cardinal Robert Bellarmine wrote to the Carmelite theologian Paolo Antonio Foscarini that astronomers were free to treat the Copernican hypothesis as a convenient device for calculating the planets' positions, but that treating it as physically true, and reinterpreting scripture to fit it, would be reckless unless someone could produce a strict demonstration that the earth actually moves; he added that he did not think any such demonstration had been given, and doubted one ever would be. That same year, Galileo was drafting his letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, arguing that because nature and scripture share a single divine author, an apparent conflict between the two can only be apparent, so once a natural conclusion is rigorously established, any scriptural passage that seems to contradict it must simply be read non-literally -- following the accommodation principle he traces to Augustine -- and that churchmen have no standing to dictate answers to questions that belong to observation and demonstration rather than exegesis.

I've been working through both letters for a seminar and keep going back and forth on who actually comes out ahead. Bellarmine's caution looks methodologically sound on narrow grounds: by 1615 Galileo's telescope had already ruled out the pure Ptolemaic system (the phases of Venus alone did that), but the same observations fit Tycho Brahe's geo-heliocentric alternative equally well, and Galileo's own eventual physical argument for the earth's motion, from the tides, turned out to be mistaken -- so no strict demonstration existed yet, and in fact none would exist until stellar parallax was finally measured more than two centuries later. Yet Galileo's general interpretive policy, that literal scriptural readings should yield to rigorously demonstrated natural knowledge rather than the other way around, still seems like the more defensible standing rule for handling this kind of conflict, more or less regardless of how solid his own evidence happened to be in 1615.

Write an essay that reconstructs the strongest version of each man's argument, considers whether the question of who was "right" should be evaluated at the level of the specific 1615 evidence or at the level of the general interpretive policy each was defending, and then reaches and defends a definite verdict about which side had the more justified position. Don't settle for a "both sides had a point" non-conclusion: commit to an answer -- a qualified one is fine (for instance, a verdict that scores the two men differently at the two levels just distinguished) -- and defend it against the strongest objection available to the losing side.
