For my elective on the History of Economic Thought, we just finished two weeks on George Berkeley — not the Berkeley from last year's intro-to-philosophy unit, who argued that objects are just collections of ideas and that to be is to be perceived, but the Berkeley of The Querist, his odd little series of numbered questions from the 1730s about Irish poverty and banking. We read the queries where he denies that gold and silver have any "intrinsic value" and insists that the "true idea of money" is nothing more than a ticket or counter for recording power to command other people's labor, along with the long stretch where he lays out, in surprising institutional detail, his proposed national bank: publicly owned rather than held in private shares, run by directors barred from sitting in Parliament and checked by a rotating audit committee, and required to keep its notes "at par" by only issuing more of them as trade itself grows.

Our teacher's essay prompt asks us to use this material to guess at Berkeley's reaction to something he obviously never saw coming: cryptocurrency. She was upfront that she doesn't want the easy answer — "he was an immaterialist, so of course he'd be fine with money that isn't physical" — and wants us to actually reason from the specific arguments in The Querist instead. That's where I'm stuck. Berkeley's whole case for paper credit is that it functions as a ticket for circulating real industry, but he's just as insistent, pointing to the Mississippi and South Sea bubbles, that credit cut loose from industry collapses into mere "gaming." Which side of that line would something like Bitcoin fall on for him? And separately, he clearly cares a great deal about who can be held accountable for a currency's soundness — his whole bank proposal is basically an exercise in making sure neither private shareholders nor an unchecked government can abuse it. What do you think he'd make of a currency answerable to no institution at all?

If those two strands of his thinking actually pull in different directions here, I'd rather you say so and weigh them against each other than force a tidy conclusion. I'm mainly trying to figure out, as best as it can be reconstructed from what he actually wrote, where he would most plausibly have landed.
