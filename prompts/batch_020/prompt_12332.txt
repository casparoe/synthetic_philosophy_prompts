My father spent almost forty years as an actuary, was a lifelong agnostic, and had no patience whatsoever for talk of heaven — and yet sometime in his last decade he became quietly convinced that death was not simply the end. It didn't come from religion. As far as I can reconstruct from the books on his nightstand, it started with Rebecca Goldstein's biography of Kurt Gödel, which he apparently picked up on a whim at an airport, and it ended with a heavily underlined copy of Hao Wang's "A Logical Journey," a much harder book, left open on his desk when he died. From his margin notes, I've pieced together that he had fixed on an argument Gödel apparently made, in letters to his own mother, to the effect that — as best I can tell — whether the world is fundamentally rational or not, either way points toward there being more than one life. He was scrupulous, in notes to himself, about keeping this apart from two things he considered nonsense: the popular claim that the incompleteness theorems themselves prove the existence of God or an afterlife, and ordinary religious comfort about souls and heaven. He'd actually written "NOT THIS" next to a magazine clipping making the first claim.

I'm giving his eulogy this weekend, to a room that will include several of his old colleagues from the actuarial firm and a niece with a philosophy minor, any of whom would notice if I got the shape of his actual reasoning wrong, sitting alongside grandchildren and neighbors who have never heard of Gödel and don't need to. I'm not a philosopher or a mathematician myself, and I'm working from marginalia rather than any real understanding of my own, but I want to honor what he actually seems to have believed rather than fall back on something soft and generic. Could you help me write a short eulogy, seven or eight minutes long, that gets his reasoning right enough to satisfy the people who will be listening for it, while still sounding like grief and love rather than a philosophy lecture to everyone else in the room?
