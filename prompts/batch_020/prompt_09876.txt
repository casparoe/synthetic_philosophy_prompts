Here's the student's answer:

"Tegmark's thought experiment imagines a 'quantum gun' rigged to fire based on a genuinely random quantum event, with a 50% chance of killing you instantly. Since many-worlds is now the mainstream interpretation among physicists, we should take its implications seriously: after you pull the trigger the world branches, and in half the resulting branches you're dead while in the other half you're fine. Do this many times and after N trials there are 2^N branches, but every single observer who's still around to ask 'am I alive?' will always answer yes, since dead people don't get to observe anything. So a many-worlder should expect to always find themselves alive no matter how many times they pull the trigger -- this is what Tegmark calls quantum immortality.

The obvious worry is that this seems to license insane risk-taking. I think the immortality conclusion is too quick, for two reasons. First, always finding yourself alive is just a selection effect, similar to how everyone who's ever wondered 'why wasn't I born in the Stone Age' finds themselves in the modern era -- it doesn't mean the universe is rigged in your favor, just that dead or unborn people don't get to wonder anything. Second and more importantly, the fraction of the wavefunction, or 'measure,' belonging to your surviving branch gets cut in half each round, so after enough trials there's only a thin, faint sliver of you left -- you become less and less real each time, even though the version that remains still feels totally normal from the inside. So 'quantum immortality' overstates what's on offer: you don't keep your full, robust existence, you just get an ever-thinner shred of it, and cashing that in for money or thrills seems like a bad trade."
