Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been auditing philosophy courses at a local university, and this term the instructor of the undergraduate decision theory course has enlisted my help marking problem sets, on the theory that a physicist ought to be comfortable with formal argument. As this is my first term doing this kind of grading, I would welcome a second, independent assessment of my marking on the item below before I submit it.

Here is the question as set for the students:

Consider an agent whose preferences over three goods, A, B, and C, are strict and cyclical: A ≻ B ≻ C ≻ A. Assume her preferences remain stable throughout the exercise and that she is willing to pay a small sum ε to trade a less-preferred good for a more-preferred one.

(a) Explain how a sequence of trades could turn these preferences into a "money pump," extracting an arbitrary amount of money from the agent while leaving her with the same good she started with.
(b) Distinguish the "naive" (myopic) and "sophisticated" strategies that Strotz introduced for agents facing a sequence of choices, and explain why Edward McClennen thought a third strategy, "resolute choice," was needed in addition to these two.
(c) Explain how a resolute chooser is supposed to avoid the money pump from part (a) even though her pairwise preferences over A, B, and C remain cyclical throughout.
(d) In a short paragraph, defend a view on the following question: does the availability of resolute choice show that cyclical preferences can be rational after all, or does it merely paper over an independent requirement that preferences be transitive?

And here, verbatim, is the answer submitted by one of the students:

(a) Since C is less preferred than B, the agent will pay ε to trade C for B. Since B is less preferred than A, she will then pay ε to trade B for A. Since A is less preferred than C, she will pay a further ε to trade A back down to C. She now holds C again, exactly what she started with, but is 3ε poorer, and because nothing stops the trader from running the same cycle again, he can extract as much money as he likes. This works only because the cyclical ranking is exploited pairwise and in sequence rather than all at once; if the agent compared A, B, and C simultaneously she could not be pumped this way. In effect this is just an instance of the classical Dutch book argument: in each case, a bookie constructs a sequence of individually acceptable transactions that show the agent's credence that her current holding is the best available option to be probabilistically incoherent, guaranteeing her a sure loss.

(b) Strotz distinguished two strategies for an agent who anticipates a sequence of choices. The naive (myopic) chooser simply does whatever looks best to her at each node without correcting for the fact that her future self will choose differently, and so keeps having her plans undone later on. McClennen then introduced the sophisticated strategy: the agent works out by backward induction what she will actually do at every later node and, at the very first node, commits herself to the single overall plan that is best given those predictions, carrying it out even at a node where a different action would look locally preferable. He argued a third label, resolute choice, was needed because in certain sequential problems -- for instance, versions of the Allais paradox recast as a multi-stage choice -- being sophisticated in this sense still does not secure the outcome the agent would herself judge best in advance.

(c) A resolute agent fixes, before the first trade, on the plan of refusing every trade in the cycle, and then simply carries out that plan at each later node regardless of the fact that, taken on its own, each single trade still looks attractive given her cyclical preferences. Since she never accepts the first trade, the trader never gets the chance to run her around the cycle, so she keeps her ε's and her original good. Her pairwise preferences among A, B, and C are never revised; what changes is only her disposition to act on them one link of the cycle at a time.

(d) I think the resolute-choice response basically succeeds: it shows that an agent can be fully rational, in the sense of never being exploitable for sure, while still ranking A over B, B over C, and C over A, provided she is resolute enough to treat the whole trading sequence, rather than each trade, as the object of choice. Transitivity turns out to be a requirement on naive choosers only; anyone able to bind herself to a plan has no further need of it.
