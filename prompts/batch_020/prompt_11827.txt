As a novelist, I earn my living asserting things about people who have never existed — that Emma Bovary is unhappy in her marriage, that Sherlock Holmes lodges at 221B Baker Street — so I have grown curious whether Russell's theory of descriptions can really make sense of this practice. The theory is prized for handling negative existentials cleanly: "Holmes does not exist" comes out true without forcing us, as Meinong recommends, to posit some object called Holmes that merely lacks existence. Yet the same machinery strains on an ordinary positive claim like "Holmes is a detective," which strikes almost everyone as true — truer, certainly, than "Holmes is a chartered accountant" — even though it should be false on exactly the grounds that make the negative existential true: nothing uniquely satisfies the description packed into the name "Holmes." Write an essay that works through this tension in detail. Consider whether it can be resolved from within a broadly Russellian, descriptivist treatment of names — for instance, by reading fiction-internal claims as governed by an implicit "according to the story" operator — or whether it instead shows that some rival account of fictional reference, such as Meinongian object theory or a pretense-based fictionalism, is doing the real explanatory work whenever we talk about invented characters. Don't simply catalogue the options; reach a verdict and defend it.
