One of my oldest friends, Ilse, is a sculptor. She spent months on a clay bust of her mother, then one night, unhappy with the likeness, pressed it back into a shapeless lump to reuse the clay. At dinner afterward, two other friends, Renata and Marcus, argued for an hour about what she'd actually done, and neither would give ground.

(1) Marcus said Ilse had destroyed the bust: whatever became of the clay, "the bust" no longer exists. (2) Renata said nothing was destroyed, since every bit of clay remained; destruction means losing matter, not rearranging it. (3) Marcus said this proves too much: melt down a wedding ring and by Renata's logic no ring was ever destroyed, since the gold persists -- yet the ring is clearly gone. (4) Renata granted that the ring stops functioning as a ring, but said that's a fact about naming habits, not about some further object vanishing -- there was never anything there but the gold. (5) Marcus replied that the bust had properties the clay itself never had: it could be insured as an artwork worth a specific sum, and could not have survived being squashed, while the lump plainly could. Since they never shared every property, he said, they were never one and the same thing, so squashing it really did destroy something. (6) Renata answered that "couldn't have survived being squashed" names no real property at all, just a restatement of "we'd call it destroyed then" -- which makes his whole argument circular.

I'd like to dramatize this dispute in a novel and want to understand its logic before giving it to a character.
