"Street's argument is aimed at anyone who thinks moral or evaluative facts hold independently of what any evaluator thinks or feels about them. The problem is that our whole capacity for making evaluative judgements - the sense that some things matter and others dont - is a product of natural selection acting on our ancestors long before anyone had worked out any ethical theory. So the realist needs some account of how the forces that shaped our evaluative attitudes relate to the independent truths they believe in. If there's no relation at all, it would be a wild coincidence if the attitudes natural selection left us with happened to line up with the independent truth, so the realist has to be sceptical about most of our substantive evaluative beliefs.

Street distinguishes two ways the realist could instead say there is a relation. On the tracking account, selection favoured ancestors with accurate evaluative beliefs because the accuracy itself was advantageous - e.g. correctly perceiving that helping your kin is good made you more likely to actually help them. On the adaptive link account, selection favoured ancestors who simply had certain evaluative attitudes (like valuing their own children) because having those attitudes caused fitness-enhancing behaviour, regardless of whether the attitudes were true. Street thinks it's really the tracking account that gives the more modest, scientifically credible picture here, since it only requires selection to favour whatever attitudes caused fitness-enhancing behaviour without those attitudes needing to be true - so realists are left with no reason to think their attitudes are accurate.

Street's own way out is to point to a third factor that explains why our fitness-promoting attitudes and the independent evaluative truths line up without either causing the other, so the coincidence worry doesn't touch her own view. I think this mostly works, since it lets her keep saying some things are really good or bad without owing evolution any explanatory debt."
