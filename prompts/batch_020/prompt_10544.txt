I work as a backend engineer, and this whole question started from an argument with a colleague about how to compare two imperfect machine-learning models against ground truth when neither one is actually correct: he insisted that one model was clearly "closer to the truth" than the other even though both produced plenty of wrong outputs, and I got curious whether philosophers had ever tried to make that intuition rigorous. That sent me down a rabbit hole into Karl Popper's notion of "verisimilitude," which he introduced so a fallibilist could say that some false scientific theories are nevertheless closer to the truth than other false theories are. From there I landed on what looks like a genuinely thorny technical dispute in philosophy of science that I'd like help sorting through.

As I understand it, Popper's original definition from the 1960s cashes out "closer to the truth" in terms of a theory's truth-content and falsity-content, roughly the sets of true and false statements it logically implies, and says one theory is more verisimilar than another when its true consequences include the other's without adding any new false ones (and a mirror-image condition governing falsity-content). The catch, which I've seen credited to Pavel Tichý and David Miller writing independently in 1974, is that this definition apparently makes it impossible for one false theory ever to count as closer to the truth than another false theory: it only ever delivers a verdict when one of the two competitors is actually true, which seems to defeat the purpose, since the comparisons anyone actually cares about (Newton's mechanics versus Einstein's, say) are precisely false-versus-false ones. My understanding is that this pushed later writers, Ilkka Niiniluoto and Graham Oddie among them, toward "similarity" or "likeness" accounts that measure closeness to the truth through some kind of distance between the states of affairs a theory permits and the actual state of affairs, rather than through set-inclusion among logical consequences.

Could you compare these two approaches properly for me? I'd like: (1) an explanation, with enough of the actual reasoning that I could reconstruct it myself, of why Popper's content-based definition collapses specifically on false-versus-false comparisons; (2) a walk-through of how either Niiniluoto's or Oddie's similarity-based machinery (please specify which one you're drawing on) is built so as to avoid that exact failure; and (3) your honest assessment of whether the fix is one I should find satisfying, or whether it merely trades the old problem for new ones that are just as damaging, such as the ranking depending on which descriptive language is used to carve up the space of possibilities, or the measure requiring access to the complete truth in order to gauge "distance" from it. Please end with your own considered verdict on whether either camp has actually rescued the pretheoretic idea that later theories can be closer to the truth than earlier ones, rather than a neutral summary of both sides.
