I sit on my state's consumer-lending oversight board, which has statutory authority to prohibit any term or practice by state-licensed short-term lenders that it finds to be unfair, deceptive, or abusive, though the enabling statute leaves "abusive" otherwise undefined. We are about to vote on a rule governing loan rollovers: under current practice, a borrower who cannot repay a two-week loan on its due date may instead pay a fee to extend it, unchanged in principal, for another two weeks, and nothing currently limits how many times this can be repeated. Our own complaint and lending data show that although most borrowers roll a loan over once or not at all, a small minority who roll the same loan over many times in a row account for a disproportionately large share of all rollover-fee revenue collected statewide, routinely paying, in fees alone, several times what they originally borrowed before the underlying debt is ever retired.

The board is divided about what, if anything, to do about this before we vote next Wednesday. One camp wants to cap rollovers at two, after which the lender must move the borrower onto a no-additional-fee installment plan; they argue that a product whose economics depend on borrowers repeatedly re-choosing, every two weeks, to defer rather than resolve the same debt is built around exploiting the gap between what a borrower most wants in that moment and what they would have chosen to commit to at the outset, and that the revenue data show this to be the business model rather than an edge case. The other camp opposes any hard cap; they point to exit interviews in which most heavy-rollover borrowers say they were glad to have had the option rather than face an overdraft, a shut-off notice, or an unlicensed lender, and they argue that an adult's repeated choice, at each two-week renewal, to extend rather than default is still their own reasoned response to the options actually available to them, not evidence that the board needs to override their judgment. Please draft the decision memo the board will vote on: it should recommend a specific rollover limit (if any) and what should trigger a mandatory installment plan, address how the rule applies to loans already mid-sequence when it takes effect, set a compliance timeline for lenders, and take the second camp's argument seriously on its own terms rather than treating it as cover for the industry's profit motive.
