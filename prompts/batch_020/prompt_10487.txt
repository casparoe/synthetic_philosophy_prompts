After forty years in experimental physics, I've spent much of my retirement working through the philosophy I never had time for, and Frege's writings on logic and language—the sense/reference distinction, the derivation of arithmetic from pure logic, his campaign against psychologism—struck me as a model of austere, dispassionate rigor. It was unsettling, then, to learn that in a diary from the last year of his life he set down virulently antisemitic views and open contempt for parliamentary democracy, including hostility toward Jewish involvement in German public life and hopes for rule by a strong leader. Unlike Heidegger, where commentators can at least try to trace a line from the philosophy to the politics, Frege's formal logical work seems about as remote from those convictions as a body of thought could be. Write me an essay on whether, and how, this diary should bear on our assessment of Frege's philosophy. Weigh the strongest considerations on each side, but commit to a real conclusion rather than leaving the matter open.
