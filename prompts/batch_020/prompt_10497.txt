I have spent a good deal of time on my own working through Luis de Molina's account of scientia media — his attempt to explain how God can know, logically prior to any decision about which world to create, exactly what a free creature would do in any circumstance whatsoever (what the literature calls a counterfactual of creaturely freedom). Molina's own answer was that God's intellect supercomprehends each possible creature's nature so perfectly that He simply knows the truth of such a counterfactual, with nothing further needed to ground it. What I cannot get past is a much later worry, now usually called the grounding objection, pressed by Robert Adams and William Hasker among others: that on a genuinely libertarian picture of freedom, nothing about a creature's nature or circumstances settles in advance what it would do, so there is nothing, even for an infinitely comprehending mind, in virtue of which such a counterfactual could be true before the creature actually chooses. Molina, naturally, never had to answer this particular, sharpened version of the worry. Working only with the concepts and moves that were actually available to him in the sixteenth century, how do you think he would try to respond to it, and would that response really succeed, or would it just relocate the same mystery one level up, into an unexplained fact about what God's mind already contains?
