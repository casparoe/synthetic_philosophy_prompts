I am writing a scene, set at an interdisciplinary conference, in which a veteran cosmologist finds herself cornered by a brash outsider who claims to have settled a live debate between two rival cosmological models—one implying a comparatively modest population of observers, the other an almost inconceivably vast one—without running a single additional experiment. His entire argument rests on the bare fact that he exists. Before I commit the scene to paper, I want the exchange to hold up under real philosophical scrutiny rather than merely sound clever. Compare the Self-Sampling Assumption and the Self-Indication Assumption as competing accounts of what, if anything, the outsider is entitled to conclude, taking Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher scenario as your guide. Then tell me which assumption you think the cosmologist should ultimately endorse, defending your verdict against the strongest objection it faces.
