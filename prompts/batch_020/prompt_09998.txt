So this has been the topic of a slightly unhinged group chat all week and I could use an outside opinion. At our biweekly game night a few of us invented this dumb little betting game based on the prisoner's dilemma, basically a homemade 'split or steal': two people each put in $20, then secretly write 'share' or 'steal' on a scrap of paper. Both share -> you each just get your $20 back. Both steal -> the money's gone (into the charity jar, so it's not a total waste). One steals while the other shares -> the stealer walks with the full $40 and the other person gets nothing. Last round, my friends Owen and Farah played each other: Owen stole, Farah shared, so he walked off with $40 and she got nothing. It turned into an actual argument over dessert about whether Owen had made the 'rational' choice, and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning. (I've also got a personal stake in this: I'm paired against my partner in the next round, so I'd love to know who's actually right before I have to decide anything myself.)

Owen's position was that stealing was just obviously correct: it's one-shot, no repeat rounds, no way to punish or reward each other afterwards, and stealing gets you at least as much as sharing no matter what the other person does (in game theory terms, it weakly dominates) -- so sharing is just being a sucker, end of story. Farah pushed back with this idea she says she picked up from an old Hofstadter essay, something called 'superrationality': since she and Owen are both people who actually reason things through carefully, and they both know that about each other, the real question isn't 'what should I do given that her choice is already fixed,' it's 'what happens when two people running the exact same reasoning both run it' -- and if that reasoning spits out 'steal,' it spits out 'steal' for both of them and they both walk away with nothing, which is worse for everyone than if it had spit out 'share' for both. So the actually rational move, she said, is to pick the reasoning that doesn't leave both of you worse off, and Owen only 'won' because she isn't as ruthless as he is. Owen's comeback was that this proves too much: even granting that two identical reasoners would ideally land on 'share,' he still personally does better by stealing no matter what Farah does -- so if she's really going to share 'because she's superrational,' isn't that just extra reason for him to steal? Doesn't her own argument hand him a better excuse to defect, not a worse one? Farah said that move only works if you quietly assume her choice is fixed independently of his reasoning, which is exactly what the symmetry point denies -- if they're truly running the same algorithm, 'I steal while she shares' isn't a real option on the table, any more than two copies of the same program could give different outputs on the same input. She did admit this all depends on how much two clever friends actually share a 'reasoning algorithm' versus just both being smart in their own seperate ways, and she wasn't fully sure that bar was cleared -- but she thinks it's a lot closer to being cleared between two old friends than between two random strangers.

Could you go through this move by move and say who's actually got the better of the argument at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? And if you think there's some bigger point about what superrationality can and can't actually deliver that both of them (and the rest of us at the table) are missing, I'd really like to hear it -- ideally something that would actually help me figure out what to do against my partner next week.
