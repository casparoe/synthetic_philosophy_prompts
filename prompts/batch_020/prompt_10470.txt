After nearly forty years as an analytical chemist, I have spent my retirement turning that same appetite for precision toward philosophy, chiefly by attending a continuing-education seminar on decision theory at the local university. A discussion there last week on the foundations of expected-utility theory left me turning over a decision of my own that none of us in the seminar could quite agree on how to think about, and I would value an outside opinion.

I am presently deciding how to divide a portion of my retirement savings between two funds. The first has an unbroken forty-year record, ample enough that its own actuaries can quote me a precise expected return together with a precise, well-characterized spread around it. The second is built around a trading strategy barely five years old; the handful of independent analyses I have managed to find suggest its expected return may be just as good as the older fund's, quite possibly better, though the analysts are candid that they cannot pin down the true odds with any real confidence, the strategy being too young and too untested for anyone to characterize its behavior the way one can for the older fund. When I am honest with myself, I notice a strong pull toward the older fund even once I stipulate, purely for the sake of argument, that the two expected returns are identical. Oddly, were the older fund not available for comparison, and the newer one my only option, I doubt I would feel nearly so uneasy about it. I recognize in this something close to a distinction I drew constantly at the bench: between a method's known, quantified error and the mere absence of any validated error estimate for a method not yet tested. In the laboratory, however, that distinction never once inclined me to prefer one method over another once their expected performance was judged equal; here, it seems to be doing exactly that.

My training inclines me to think that a rational person simply goes wherever the expected value points, and that any further hesitation is a bias in need of correction. Yet that diagnosis feels too quick to me. Is my reluctance toward the newer fund simply irrational, given that I have stipulated the expected returns to be equal, or is there a genuine, principled rationale for treating known and unknown odds differently, one that a strict expected-value framework fails to capture? And should the fact that my unease seems to depend on the older fund's being there for comparison change my answer to that question?
