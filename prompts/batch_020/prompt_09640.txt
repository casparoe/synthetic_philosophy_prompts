I'm a novelist working on a book set largely in Manchester in the early 1950s, one thread of which follows a fictional science correspondent who talks her way into a long interview with Alan Turing not long after "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" comes out in Mind. The scene I keep rewriting turns on the strangest few pages of that paper: tucked among his replies to objections to the imitation game, Turing pauses to say that the statistical case for telepathy strikes him as compelling, lists it alongside clairvoyance, precognition, and psychokinesis as one of the four recognized varieties of extra-sensory perception, and then has to patch his own thought experiment against the possibility that a human contestant might use telepathy to give the game away -- his fix being to keep everyone in what he calls a 'telepathy-proof room.' My early readers keep assuming I invented this bit for comic effect, so I want the interview scene to make it unmistakable that it's really in the paper, and I want my correspondent character to press on it rather than simply let Turing restate it for her.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions I could adapt for that scene, each accompanied by a short note on the answer Turing would most plausibly give and a sharper follow-up keyed to that answer? Among them I'd want at least one question on why he treats telepathy as evidentially special rather than lumping it in with the other three phenomena on his list; one on whether the 'telepathy-proof room' is a sensible safeguard or a tacit admission that the imitation game is more fragile than the rest of the paper lets on; one that asks him to square this apparent openness with the far brisker, more dismissive tone he takes toward the theological and 'heads in the sand' objections only a page or two earlier in the same section; and one that presses on whether, by his own lights, a machine could ever be credited with the same kind of correlation he is willing to grant a telepathic person, and what that would do to his criterion for thinking. I would like the sequence to close on a question that puts to him directly whether the whole passage is sincere or a deliberate stress-test of his own proposal -- I have seen it read both ways by people who have written about the paper, and I would rather the exchange preserve that ambiguity than resolve it for her.
