And here's what Jordan wrote:

"Objection #9 is probably the strangest thing in the whole paper. Turing's basically worried about ESP — telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition, and psychokinesis. His point is that if any of that is real, then in the imitation game a human contestant might be able to do things a machine physically can't, like correctly guess a card or number way above chance, and the interrogator could use that as a giveaway that has nothing to do with actual thinking. What's wild is Turing doesn't dismiss ESP as pseudoscience — he actually says the statistical evidence for telepathy is strong enough that he can't just ignore it, which is a pretty surprising thing for a mathematician to admit. For his fix, he basically falls back on the same solipsism point he used against the Argument from Consciousness: since none of us can ever really prove another mind is thinking (or reading minds) instead of just faking it, we shouldn't demand a higher standard of proof from the machine than we'd accept from a person. Because he can't rule ESP out, Turing more or less admits the imitation game loses its power to tell man from machine, which is a bigger retreat than he makes anywhere else in section 6."
