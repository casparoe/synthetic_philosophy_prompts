I'm working on a feature about how mid-century analytic philosophy still underwrites contemporary arguments against redistribution, and last week I sat in on a small reading group that meets monthly to work through classics of political philosophy — that particular session was devoted to Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument from Anarchy, State, and Utopia. For context: Nozick asks us to start from any distribution of holdings people are willing to call just, call it D1 — say, a perfectly equal one — and then imagine that Wilt Chamberlain, an exceptional basketball player, signs a contract giving him twenty-five cents from every ticket sold to watch him play. Over a season, a million fans happily pay the extra quarter, so Wilt ends up with $250,000 while everyone else has a little less than they started with; call this new distribution D2. Nozick's claim is that D2 is exactly as just as D1 was, since it was reached from D1 through a long chain of transfers nobody thinks was individually wrongful, and from this he draws a general moral: no "patterned" principle of justice — one that fixes everyone's share by some formula, like strict equality — can be kept in force without either continuously confiscating people's holdings or continuously stopping them from transacting as they please. Liberty, as he puts it, upsets patterns.

Two people in the group, Marissa and Theo, went several rounds on this that I still haven't managed to settle in my own head. (1) Theo opened by defending Nozick's inference more or less as stated above. (2) Marissa objected that maintaining the pattern needn't require anyone to chase Wilt down after the season and confiscate his earnings: a society could instead announce, before a single ticket is sold, a fixed rule redistributing earnings above some threshold, so that every fan's "free" decision to pay the extra quarter happens against a baseline everyone already knows, the way a sales tax doesn't stop you from buying a coffee even though it changes what you end up paying — nobody's freedom to transact is touched, only what's left over afterward. (3) Theo replied that this only relocates the interference rather than removing it: if the whole point of the rule is to keep clawing back whatever inequalities voluntary exchange keeps generating, it has to be continually re-tuned to people's shifting talents, tastes, and luck, which is just confiscation wearing a disguise. (4) Marissa answered that Nozick's own minimal state also continually "re-tunes" its enforcement of property and contract law as circumstances change, so if that doesn't count as ongoing interference with liberty for him, he owes us a principled line between the two kinds of continuous rule-enforcement — and "whatever preserves people's existing holdings" is not some neutral, pattern-free baseline, it already assumes the very entitlement conclusion the example is supposed to establish.
