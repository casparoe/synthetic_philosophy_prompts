I spent about thirty-five years doing observational astronomy before I retired, and since then I've been working my way through the philosophy of science shelf at the library, which is how Popper ended up on my kitchen table most mornings this spring. His demarcation idea seems clean enough on the surface: a theory counts as scientific only if it's falsifiable, and the honest move when the data contradicts it is to drop the theory, whereas pseudosciences supposedly always have some excuse ready to survive the bad results instead. What's been nagging at me is that this doesn't sit well with the two most famous episodes from my own field, which happen to involve the same nineteenth century astronomer. When Uranus's orbit wouldn't match Newtonian predictions, Le Verrier didn't drop Newtonian gravity, he patched it by proposing an unseen extra planet, and that patch turned out to be Neptune, still one of the great triumphs in the history of physics. A little over a decade later he ran the exact same play on the leftover wobble in Mercury's orbit, proposing an unseen little planet called Vulcan close in to the sun — except this time the planet wasn't there, and the wobble turned out to be one of the first confirmations of general relativity instead.

So the move itself, "rescue the theory with a new unobserved entity rather than let the data sink it," was identical both times, right down to the same guy doing it, and the only difference between his shining moment and his dead end is which one turned out to be true, something nobody could possibly have known at the time either paper was published. Doesn't that mean Popper's criterion can only ever sort good science from bad in hindsight, after you already know who won, which seems to gut the whole point of having a criterion your meant to apply to a theory while its still live? My neighbour taught high school physics for thirty years and swears I'm missing something obvious here, but she couldn't actually tell me what it was, so I'm hoping you can settle it properly.
