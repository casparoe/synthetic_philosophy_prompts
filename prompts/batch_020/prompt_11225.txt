I am assembling a small cycle of short fiction in which each story is built around one precisely stated puzzle from analytic metaphysics, and the entry I am missing concerns mereology. Peter van Inwagen's Special Composition Question asks under what conditions some objects (the "xs") compose a further single object (a "y") rather than merely lying side by side; his own answer, often called organicism, holds that composition occurs only when the activity of the xs constitutes a life, so that living organisms are the sole genuine composite objects, while chairs, ships, and mountains are strictly nothing but simples arranged chairwise, shipwise, or mountainwise. That restrictive answer competes with universalism, on which any objects whatever compose something, and with nihilism, on which composition never occurs at all. Please write a story of roughly 1,000 to 1,500 words whose plot or central image genuinely turns on this question, ideally by dramatizing a hard case for the "life" criterion — a slime mould, a coral colony, conjoined twins, a lichen — so that what is strange or at stake in organicism becomes vivid rather than merely asserted. I would like it in the vein of Borges or Calvino, where the engine of the story is the abstract puzzle itself rather than psychology or incident, and I would rather it not become another tale about a robot or an AI discovering its own mind: the puzzle here concerns ordinary physical objects and their parts, not consciousness.
