So this happened at my nephew's school "family math night" last week and it's been rattling around in my head since. A few of the older kids ran a booth where you could earn a sticker for every card you correctly called "true" or "false." Most of the cards were harmless number trivia, but two of them were, without anyone at the booth realizing it: "8,051 is a prime number" and "8,051 is the product of two whole numbers, each bigger than 1." I watched several kids cheerfully call "true" on both and walk off happy with their stickers. Of course those two claims can't both be true — any whole number over 1 is either prime or it isn't, there's no third option — but nobody there had any real way of knowing that without sitting down and grinding through the division (it took me a few minutes with a pencil that night to find out 8,051 is 83 times 97). So every kid who called both cards "true" was guaranteed, before the fact, to be wrong about at least one of them, with no way to tell which.

I mentioned this to a colleague in the computer science department, half joking, and he got weirdly insistant that this is just a bog-standard case of irrationality: if what you're willing to assert can't all be true at once, you're being incoherent, full stop, and it doesn't matter one bit how hard the incoherence was to notice. I teach intro logic and would normally just tell my students "well, don't hold inconsistent beliefs" and leave it there — but now that's feeling like a cop-out. It seems different from a kid who says "I love broccoli" and "I hate all vegetables" in the same breath, where the contradiction just sits there on the surface. Here you'd need actual work, arithmetic or in general something proof-shaped, before the contradiction is even visible. Is my colleague right that it's the exact same phenomenon and the kids were simply being irrational? Or does the amount of work it takes to notice an inconsistency matter to whether holding it counts as irrational in the first place?
