So I TA a philosophy of mind seminar, and we just did the week on the frame problem, going through Dennett's "Cognitive Wheels" piece with poor R1 blowing itself up because it pulls the wagon out of the room without noticing the bomb sitting on top of it, and then its sequels R1D1 and R2D1 both getting stuck too -- one of them drowns in irrelevant deductions about wall colors, the other just sits there frozen trying to figure out which deductions even count as relevant before its allowed to start reasoning about them. One of my students raised something in section afterward that I couldn't fully rebut, and its been bugging me since. Their argument was basically: the frame problem was only ever a problem for GOFAI-style systems that represent the world with explicit logical axioms and have to write out "frame axioms" for everything an action doesn't change. Since pretty much nobody builds AI that way anymore -- modern systems (LLMs, end to end trained robot policies, whatever) just learn statistical structure from data instead of doing explicit deduction over hand-coded facts -- doesn't that basically mean the frame problem got solved by switching paradigms, rather than by anyone actually cracking it, and is now mostly a historical curiosity?

I wasn't satisfied with that but couldn't quite articulate why, beyond a vague sense that Dennett was pointing at something deeper than "writing frame axioms by hand is tedious." So: is my student basically right, or is there some version of the problem that survives the move to connectionist/statistical architectures? And if it does survive, what would that even look like concretely -- is it the same problem showing up in new clothes, or something that only superficially shares a name with what McCarthy and Hayes were originally worried about?
