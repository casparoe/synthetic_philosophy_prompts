A friend and I spent much of an evening arguing about Maimonides on animal sacrifice, and neither of us gave up any ground. I was defending his position in Guide of the Perplexed III:32: that the sacrificial system was a concession to the idolatrous habits the Israelites had absorbed in Egypt, redirected toward God rather than abolished outright, because human nature cannot bear a sudden reversal of ingrained practice. My friend pressed Ramban's objection, from his commentary on Leviticus 1:9, that this cannot be the whole story, since Cain, Abel, Noah, and the patriarchs were already offering sacrifices -- described using exactly the "pleasing odor" phrase that Leviticus itself uses -- long before there existed any surrounding idolatry from which anyone needed weaning. I countered that those earlier sacrifices were voluntary rather than commanded, but I am not convinced that distinction bears the weight it needs to, and I never got to raise what seems to me the best objection to Ramban's own alternative: that grounding sacrifice's efficacy in some real effect on God -- in his more kabbalistic register, even a need to repair a rupture between the sefirot of Shekhinah and Tif'eret -- reintroduces exactly the sort of anthropomorphism Maimonides spends the rest of the Guide trying to dismantle.

Could you write a dialogue between Maimonides and Ramban that picks up this disagreement and takes it further than we managed to? Give each figure their strongest moves and have them actually answer one another rather than deliver parallel speeches: Maimonides needs a real response to the historical counterexamples and to the sacrificial language, and Ramban needs a real response to the charge that his own account purchases its explanatory power at the cost of compromising divine transcendence.
