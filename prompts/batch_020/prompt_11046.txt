Stuck on a passage from Aquinas's commentary on the Sentences (book 2, distinction 44, question 2, article 2 -- the article asking whether Christians have to obey secular rulers, tyrants included). Didn't have a published translation handy so this is my own rough stab at the Latin, sorry if it's clunky in spots:

"Authority can fail to come from God in two ways: because of how it was acquired, or because of how it's used.

On the acquisition side, there are two ways this can go wrong: a defect in the person himself, if he's unworthy, or a defect in the very way power was acquired -- say, through violence, through simony, or some other unlawful means. That first kind of defect doesn't block a genuine right to rule from being established -- authority, formally speaking, always traces back to God, which is exactly what grounds the obligation to obey -- so subjects still owe obedience to rulers of this sort no matter how unworthy they personally are. The second kind of defect is different: it blocks any right to rule from arising in the first place, because someone who seizes power by force never actually becomes a true ruler or master. So when the chance arises, people are free to just throw off that kind of rule -- unless it's later made legitimate, say by the subjects' own consent or by a higher authority stepping in and sanctioning it.

Abuse of authority splits into two kinds as well. One is where the ruler orders something that works against the very purpose authority exists for in the first place -- commanding some sinful act, say, when authority's whole job is supposed to be building up virtue, not tearing it down. Here you're not just off the hook for obeying, you're required to refuse, the way the martyrs went to their deaths rather than carry out a tyrant's impious orders. The other kind is where the ruler demands something that's simply outside what his authority actually covers -- like a lord billing a servant for some due the servant was never on the hook for. Here the subject can go either way, obey or refuse, without either being required."

Here's what's bugging me: he splits "defective title" into two cases -- the guy himself being unworthy, vs. power grabbed through force or fraud -- and says the first still generates a real duty to obey while the second doesnt, full stop, no matter how well either one actually governs once he's in charge. Seems backwards to me, shouldn't conduct in office matter more than the paperwork on how someone got there? And then there's this totally seperate abuse-of-power axis (commands against virtue vs. commands that just overstep the ruler's proper scope) that doesn't depend at all on which of the first two buckets the ruler falls into. Is there some unifying idea of what "authority" even amounts to for Aquinas that makes those particular lines fall where they do, or is this basically an ad-hoc taxonomy stitched together to answer objections? Would love a walkthrough of the actual logic.
