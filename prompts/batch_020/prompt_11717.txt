So I'm reporting a piece on how large language models seem to figure out what we mean from context even though they've never "met" us and share none of our history, and one of the philosophers I interviewed for background kept steering things back to Donald Davidson's radical interpretation stuff from the 70s and 80s. I get the basic setup: a "radical interpreter" has to work out simultaneously what a speaker believes and what her sentences mean, with nothing to go on but her behavior, and the Principle of Charity is the trick that's supposed to make this tractable, by assuming she's mostly rational and mostly right about her surroundings. Fine so far. But then, almost as an aside, he mentioned that Davidson tried to turn this exact machinery into a full refutation of skepticism about the external world -- something involving an "omniscient interpreter" who'd apply that same charity-based method to us and would therefore end up finding our beliefs mostly true, and since truth isn't supposed to depend on who's doing the interpreting, that apparently shows we really are mostly right about the world, so the classic Cartesian "what if almost everything I believe is false" worry never even gets off the ground.

I went and read around this a bit afterward, and it's definately a real move Davidson makes, not something my source invented -- but every summary I found just says "many have objected to this" and moves on, without ever saying what the objection actually is, which drives me a little nuts. Could you build the strongest argument you can that this omniscient-interpreter move doesn't actually defeat skepticism, even granting Davidson the entire radical interpretation set-up and the Principle of Charity going in? I don't want the cheap version of the objection ("well, what if the interpreter's theory just happens to be wrong"), since stipulating omniscience is precisely meant to block that -- I want to know whether there's a real problem in the inference from "any interpreter, however situated, would have to interpret us as mostly right" to "we are, in fact, mostly right."
