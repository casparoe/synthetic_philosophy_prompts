Good's theorem holds that, for a Bayesian decision-maker who updates by conditionalization, the expected value of cost-free information is never negative: optimally using an extra piece of evidence before acting cannot leave you worse off, in expectation, than deciding without it. Compare this result with Jack Hirshleifer's finding that the public arrival of information can destroy otherwise mutually beneficial insurance or risk-sharing arrangements—for instance, information revealed before contracts are signed that tells everyone in advance who will suffer some future loss, thereby eliminating the very uncertainty that made pooling risk attractive to all parties. Hirshleifer's result is sometimes glossed as showing that information can have negative value, in apparent tension with Good's theorem. Explain precisely why the two results do not actually contradict each other, identifying the specific assumption behind Good's proof that fails to hold in Hirshleifer's setting. Is "value of information" even the same quantity in the two analyses?
