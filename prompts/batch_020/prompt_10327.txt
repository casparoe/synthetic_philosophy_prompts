I'm a retired physicist and I've gotten properly into philosophy since I stopped working. It started, kind of randomly, because I knew Einstein's closest friend in his later years at Princeton was Gödel, and I got curious what the guy actually believed beyond the incompleteness stuff. Turns out that in a letter to his mother he laid out a whole argument for why there has to be an afterlife -- roughly, that the world is "rational" and purposeful, so it'd be absurd for someone to spend decades developing their mind and character only to have all of it wiped out right when its finally mature, like pouring a massive foundation and never building the house on top of it. I might be fuzzy on details but that's the gist.

It struck me as basically the same shape of move Kant makes with his postulate of immortality in the Critique of Practical Reason: we're obligated to strive for moral perfection but can't possibly finish the job in one lifetime, so practical reason has to posit an immortal soul so the whole project isn't pointless.

Can you compare these two argument's in some depth -- are they really doing the same logical work just dressed up in different vocabulary ("a rational world" vs "the moral law"), or is there an important structural difference I'm missing? And which one do you personally find more defensible, if either?
