About three weeks ago I had a small, unpleasant taste of internet fame: a single line from an essay I published in an online magazine was screenshotted, stripped of context, and passed around for about a day and a half, during which total strangers confidently pronounced on my motives, my politics, and my character, before the whole thing evaporated as quickly as it had appeared and everyone moved on to some other target. When I mentioned this to an old friend who works on nineteenth-century philosophy, she told me, half-joking, that I had basically just relived the Corsair affair — the months-long satirical campaign a Copenhagen newspaper waged against Kierkegaard in the mid-1840s after a minor literary dispute spiraled out of control — and that his diagnosis, soon afterward, of "the present age," "leveling," and "the public" in Two Ages was written by a man who had just been chewed up by exactly this kind of anonymous, ephemeral attention. I went back and reread the relevant pages, and I've been unable to settle a question that now feels more urgent to me than academic: is Kierkegaard's account of the public as a self-generated "phantom" — an abstraction, manufactured by the press, that judges every individual while itself answering to no one and standing for nothing — a genuine and generalizable piece of social philosophy, or is it, at bottom, an aristocratic and wounded man's grudge against a satirical newspaper, elevated into metaphysics after the fact?

I'd like an essay that actually resolves this for me rather than gesturing at both sides. Please lay out the strongest case that the critique of "leveling" and "the public" captures something structurally real about press- and crowd-mediated opinion — something that holds up independently of Kierkegaard's own biography, and that arguably anticipates later worries about mass society and, for that matter, social-media pile-ons like the one I just went through — and then lay out the strongest case for reading it as substantially biographically driven special pleading dressed up as philosophy, including whatever you make of the fact that its harshest formulations appear right when his own humiliation was freshest. Along the way, take a real position on the more general methodological question of whether learning that a philosopher was personally aggrieved when he formed a view should lower our credence in the view itself, or whether that inference is simply a version of the genetic fallacy. I don't want a "both sides have a point" ending: please commit to, and defend, an overall verdict on how much of Kierkegaard's critique of the press and the public survives as serious philosophy.
