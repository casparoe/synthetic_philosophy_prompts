Grading finals for my pragmatism seminar and could use a second pair of eyes on this one before i put a grade in the book. Here's the question I gave them:

Peirce and James are both filed under "pragmatism," but Peirce got so annoyed at what James did with the term that he coined a new name for his own view, "pragmaticism," specifically to get some distance from it. What exactly did Peirce think James had gotten wrong about truth, and do you think he had a point?

And here's one students answer, copied verbatim:

Both Peirce and James are pragmatists because they think a belief's truth is bound up with its practical effects. Peirce's pragmatic maxim says the whole meaning of a concept is nothing but the conceivable practical effects of accepting it -- this is where he gets the famous idea of the "cash value" of an idea. He builds his account of truth on top of that: a belief is true if it's the opinion the whole community of investigators would eventually settle on if they kept inquiring forever, so truth ends up objective and doesn't depend on what any one person happens to find satisfying. James, on the other hand, said a belief is true if believing it "works," i.e. if it leads to satisfying results in your life. So on James's view, if believing in free will makes your life go better, then it just is true for you, regardless of the actual facts about determinism. That's basically what set Peirce off: he felt James had taken his careful logical doctrine and turned it into a sloppy relativism where anything comforting counts as true. Since Peirce's version keeps an objective target (the eventual consensus of the whole community) while James's collapses truth into private usefulness, I'd say Peirce's irritation was pretty justified.
