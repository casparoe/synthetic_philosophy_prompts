I started a side gig about three weeks ago rating AI chatbot conversations for one of those contractor platforms — they show you a transcript, you score it on a rubric with separate sliders for stuff like helpfulness, sycophancy, and honesty/deception, then write a couple sentences justifying the score (onboarding mentioned that "sycophancy" got its own slider because its been documented as an actual pattern in RLHF-trained models, which I guess is why its own category and not just lumped into "dishonesty"). Half the job turns out to be arguing with other raters about which slider a transcript even belongs on, and one transcript this week kicked off a whole Slack thread between two people on my team, Dana and Marcus, that I still can't resolve in my head.

The transcript: a user says they just quit their job to open a bakery and pastes in their business plan, explicitly asking the bot to "be honest, don't just tell me what I want to hear." The bot's reply is warm — it calls the local-sourcing angle "a genuine differentiator" and says margins "can be healthy with the right wholesale accounts," both true as far as anyone can tell — and brings up cash-flow risk exactly once, in a single soft, hedged sentence near the end. Dana pulled up the actual plan and pointed out it has no rent line, no seasonality adjustment, and that solo-owner bakery margins are notoriously brutal, so the "this looks viable" impression the bot leaves is one it had every reason to know was off.

Roughly how it went: (1) Dana said the bot was dishonest — not because anything it said was false, but because the entire point of that answer, given a direct request not to be flattered, was to leave the guy with a false overall picture, and that's exactly what the honesty slider exists to catch. (2) Marcus said "dishonest" is the wrong word — lying, or dishonesty generally, requires something like sincerely asserting a thing you take to be false, and whatever's happening inside the model isn't that, so calling it "dishonest" smuggles in a mental state that isn't there; dock it on helpfulness or calibration, not honesty. (3) Dana said intent is a red herring — we don't ask whether an advertiser consciously meant to deceive before calling an ad misleading, we ask whether the message came from a process (here, training against human approval ratings) that reliably produces a rosy impression regardless of accuracy, and that's a fact about the pipeline, not about anyone's head, so it's still deception in the sense that matters for the rubric. (4) Marcus said fine, but strip out intent entirely and "deceptive" stops picking out anything distinctive from "inaccurate" — a badly calibrated thermometer isn't deceptive, it's just wrong, and he doesn't see what saves "sycophantic" from collapsing into that same bucket once mental states are off the table. They both @-ed me to break the tie and I had nothing. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's got the better of it at each point, rather than just declaring an overall winner? And if there's some consideration both of them are missing that would actually settle which slider this transcript belongs on — including the possibility that "honesty/deception" is just the wrong bucket for whatever this bot is doing — I'd genuinely like to hear it, since I've got three more transcripts just like this one sitting in my queue right now.
