Retired physicist here, pretty new to philosophy but loving it. Been reading about the "buck-passing" account of value -- the idea that something being good just consists in its having properties that give us reason to favor it, which reminded me of how we treat secondary qualities like colour as dispositions to affect observers. Then I ran into the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem: a demon threatens to torture you unless you admire a saucer of mud, which sure seems to give you a reason to admire it, but not because the mud is actually admirable. Can you write me a short dialogue between a buck-passer trying to save the analysis (I think the usual move is to distinguish "object-given" reasons from "state-given" ones) and a critic who isnt going to let them off that easy? I dont want a strawman on either side -- if the buck-passers move actually works, make the critic sweat for a real counter, and if the critic's circularity objection is a good one, let the buck-passer actually have to answer it rather than just conceding.
