I'm a retired physicist (three decades in collider physics before I hung up my lab coat) and these days I've fallen down a bit of a philosophy rabbit hole - lots of epistemology, mostly, which is a nice change of pace after a career of just trying to get my error bars right. A couple weeks ago I re-encountered Aumann's agreement theorem, which I vaguely remember from a stats seminar back in the 90s but never really chewed on philosophically: the result that if two Bayesian agents share the same prior, and their posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge (meaning not just that each one knows the others number, but knows that the other knows it, and knows that the other knows that they know, and so on forever), then those two posteriors actually have to be equal. So genuinely honest, rational people supposedly can't just "agree to disagree" the way we throw that phrase around casually.

This got me thinking back to my old collaboration, where it happened more than once that two perfectly smart, perfectly honest people would stare at the exact same dataset and walk away with pretty different gut feelings about whether some 3-sigma bump was a real signal or just background having a bad day - and that disagreement sometimes never fully closed even after weeks of arguing it out in meetings. I brought this up with an old labmate over email last week and neither of us could decide: does the theorem mean at least one of us back then must've been irrational or hiding something, or is there enough wiggle room in its assumptions (the common prior bit, or the "true" common knowledge bit) that it just doesn't apply to a messy real case like ours?

So here's what I'd like: could you write me a dialogue between two former colleagues - say, Priya and Marek - right after they've each separately reanalyzed the same dataset and ended up with noticeably different posteriors on whether the bump is real, and both of them know Aumann's theorem cold? I want genuine back-and-forth, neither one a pushover. One of them should press the point that their continued disagreement, now that they've told each other their numbers, is itself pretty damning evidence that somebody's being irrational or hasn't fully shared their reasoning. The other should mount a real defense of why the theorem doesn't actually force convergence here - whatever the strongest version of that defense is (common priors not really holding, true common knowledge never really being reached, or something else). I'd rather they get into the actual moving parts of the theorem than just trade the soundbite version of "rational people can't agree to disagree" back and forth.
