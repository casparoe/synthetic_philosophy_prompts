Here's the answer, copied exactly as they submitted it:

"Cohen's 'problem of easy knowledge' targets views on which a basic source like perception can give us knowledge or justified belief about the world without our first having any independent reason to think the source is reliable. The gas-gauge case brings out why this is troubling: Roxanne looks at her fuel gauge on several occasions, and each time, since she's also trusting the reading itself as accurate rather than independently checking the tank, she comes to believe both 'the gauge reads F' and 'the tank is full.' Repeating this over many occasions, she notices that every time the gauge said the tank was full, the tank (as she believed on the gauge's own say-so) was indeed full, and on this basis she concludes inductively that the gauge is reliable. Intuitively this is illegitimate, she'd get exactly the same run of evidence whether the gauge were working or stuck, and that's what makes the resulting 'knowledge' both easy and bad: really, the whole argument is just a case of circular reasoning, since the gauge's reliability, which is what Roxanne is trying to establish, has already been smuggled in as a premise every time she trusts an individual reading. Any argument that assumes what it sets out to prove is a textbook example of begging the question, which is exactly the flaw here.

Cohen's point is that this isn't just a quirk of toy examples, the same structure threatens to appear wherever a source is allowed to deliver basic knowledge, including perception itself, which is why the problem generalizes instead of just being a curiosity about fuel gauges. This is also why people compare it to Moore's proof of an external world: Moore argues 'here is a hand, here is another, therefore external objects exist,' moving from perceptual knowledge of his hands straight to the existence of an external world. Structurally, Moore's proof and the bootstrapping case both move from premises available via a basic source directly to a conclusion that vindicates that very source, without ever ruling out the skeptical alternative on which the source is systematically misleading, so anyone who's suspicious of Moore's proof for "proving too much too cheaply" should, by parity of reasoning, be equally suspicious of the gas-gauge bootstrapping, and vice versa.

Finally, this whole debate is a modern replay of the problem of the criterion, first posed by Descartes as a challenge to any philosopher who wants to trust their faculties without first certifying them. Descartes' methodism says we have to settle on a reliable method for telling truth from error before we're entitled to trust any particular belief, while particularists like Moore and Chisholm say we can start from particular cases we obviously know, this is a hand, the earth is old, etc., and work outward to a criterion from there, never needing to certify the source in advance. Dogmatists about perceptual justification and reliabilists more generally are particularists in this sense, since on their view we can have basic perceptual knowledge without antecedently knowing perception is reliable. The easy knowledge problem shows that this particularist starting point lets us derive the 'criterion,' that the source is reliable, too cheaply from the very particular cases it was supposed to be independent of, which is exactly the circularity methodists have always accused particularists of."
