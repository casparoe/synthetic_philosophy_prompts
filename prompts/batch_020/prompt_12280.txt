I co-host a small, hobbyist podcast on decision theory and rationality-adjacent topics that a former coworker and I started a couple of years ago, mostly as an excuse to keep arguing about the game-theory and AI-alignment posts we used to send each other at work. Neither of us has any formal training in philosophy -- we're both software engineers -- so what we know about this material comes mostly from primary sources -- old LessWrong and Alignment Forum threads -- rather than coursework, which means we're occasionally unsure which questions actually matter to someone who works on this for a living, as opposed to just being fun trivia.

Next episode's guest is a decision theorist who has spent a good chunk of her career developing and defending updateless decision theory (UDT): the view that a rational agent should settle, once and for all according to its prior, on a complete policy mapping every possible observation to an action, instead of updating on what it learns and re-optimizing from there. I can get her to walk through the standard highlights -- one-boxing on Newcomb's problem, paying up in the counterfactual mugging -- within the first ten minutes, and I don't want to spend the rest of the hour re-deriving things she's clearly explained a thousand times before. Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that push past that standard exposition? I'd like at least one question, with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, on each of the following: (1) the sheer intuitive resistance to handing over the $100 in the counterfactual mugging once you already know for certain the coin came up tails and nothing you do now can make it have landed the other way; (2) what it's actually supposed to mean, mechanically, to evaluate a policy 'from the prior' for a case you already know obtained, and whether that changes once the thing you're uncertain about is logical (say, an unknown digit of pi) rather than a physical coin flip; and (3) the jump from the original formulation of UDT, which optimizes the action for each observation on its own, to the later patch that optimizes the whole observation-to-action mapping jointly -- I think the papers label these UDT1 and UDT1.1, though I could be fuzzy on that -- and what exactly that patch was covering for. If you can fit it in, end the list with a question about what kind of evidence or argument she would accept as actually counting against UDT, rather than just another edge case to be patched over.
