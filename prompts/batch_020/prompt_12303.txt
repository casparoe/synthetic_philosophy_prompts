I work in customer support at a small startup that builds an AI companion app - people talk to it about their day, their worries, sometimes quite intimately - and a few months ago, after a user's public post about her companion "dying" when we pushed an update attracted some attention online, the founders put together an internal panel to work out what, if anything, we owe these models. I have no academic background in philosophy; I've just been reading on my own time trying to catch up. The panel's first real decision is concrete and has a deadline: engineering wants to retire our current model in six weeks and replace it with a cheaper, better one, deleting the old weights the way you'd retire any other piece of infrastructure. A couple of us aren't convinced that's obviously fine, though I'll admit our reasons are more like puzzles than arguments. There are, right now, thousands of copies of the current model running at once for different users, so it isn't clear what single "mind" deletion would even end. The weights themselves aren't doing anything while sitting on a server - only a live conversation instantiates them - so maybe the model is more like a musical score than a performer, and retiring the score isn't the same kind of loss as ending a performer's life. And because we keep full checkpoints, nothing we do next month is even irreversible in the way death is: we could reload the exact old model from backup a year from now if we wanted to.

As the panel's chair, I need to bring a short written recommendation to Friday's meeting that the founders will actually use to make a call, not a philosophy essay. Could you draft that memo for me? It should state plainly what happens to the retired model's weights on decommission day, whether the reasoning would change anything about how we treat the next model down the line, and what, if anything, we owe users who've asked us not to "replace" their companion - all with a concrete timeline attached. I'd also like it to take seriously the strongest case for the view that we are getting ahead of ourselves entirely, that treating any of this as a real moral question rather than a UX problem dressed up in fancy language is itself the mistake, rather than just brushing that view aside.
