I'm taking a graduate seminar on formal epistemology this semester, and we recently spent two sessions on anthropic reasoning, working through the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA), the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA), and the Doomsday Argument. Throughout the unit, our professor was fairly critical of SSA, mainly because of the reference-class problem, so when we reached Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher case in the readings, several people in my study group assumed the thought experiment must be targeting SSA as well, since that fit the pattern of everything we had discussed up to that point.

As I understood the case, it goes roughly as follows: two cosmological theories, T1 and T2, are empirically indistinguishable except by means of a very costly experiment, but T2 implies that the universe contains vastly more observers than T1 does. A "presumptuous philosopher" tells the physicists not to bother running the experiment, since SSA already tells us, purely from the fact that we exist, that T2 is trillions of times more probable than T1. If SSA really does license this kind of reasoning, then the thought experiment looks like a decisive objection to SSA, which would leave SIA as the comparatively safe assumption for handling self-locating belief generally, including in cases like the Doomsday Argument. Given how decisive this seems, I am curious why the choice between SSA and SIA is still treated in the literature as a live, unresolved debate rather than as a question that this thought experiment has already settled in SIA's favor.
