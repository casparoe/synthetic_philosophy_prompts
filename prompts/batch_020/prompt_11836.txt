so i went down a rabbit hole on lesswrong last night and found this old decision theory puzzle called the "absent-minded driver" (Piccione & Rubinstein, from the 90s i think). setup: a guy is driving home and has to take the second of two highway exits that look completely identical. he's absent-minded -- at any exit he can't tell if it's the first or second one, and has no memory of whether he's already passed one. so whatever probability he assigns to "keep driving vs exit," hes forced to use that exact same number at both exits, since he genuinely can't tell them apart.

work out the best strategy ahead of time (sitting at home before the drive) and the math says he should keep driving with probability 2/3 at each exit. but then people point out: once he's actually stopped at an exit, he can work out the odds of this being the first exit vs the second one (based on the very plan he just committed to -- comes out to like 3-to-2 in favor of it being the first), plug those odds back into the expected value calc, and the "optimal" probability of continuing comes out to 1/3 instead. same guy, zero new information between home and the exit, and yet two different "correct" answers depending on when you run the numbers.

i know this rhymes with the sleeping beauty thing, but every explanation ive found just states the paradox and moves on. i want the actual mechanism -- is the roadside recalculation simply a mistake (and if so where exactly does it go wrong), or is it defensible on its own terms, in which case what does that say about "rational" planning under this kind of memory loss? that's the part that's bugging me.
