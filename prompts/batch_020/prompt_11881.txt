A friend of mine, who's finishing a PhD in security studies with a focus on crisis bargaining, and I spent most of a long dinner after a workshop on emerging technology and international security arguing about "safe Pareto improvements." As she described it, the idea — which I gather comes out of recent game-theoretic work on delegated game-playing and multi-agent AI cooperation, though I won't swear I have the provenance exactly right — is that when two parties are negotiating under private information about each other's resolve or preferences, you can sometimes redesign how the negotiation itself is conducted so that the change is guaranteed to leave every possible "type" of every party at least as well off as some fallback outcome, without either side needing to know, or agree on, the probability distribution over the other's private information. She thinks this offers a genuinely useful escape from the kind of commitment and information problems that rationalist theories of war blame for costly, mutually regretted outcomes, and wants to extend the idea to hypothetical bargaining between AI systems trying to avoid a conflict neither side wants. I was more skeptical, and afterward wrote up the objection I'd been groping for at the table; I'd like your comments on it before I send it to her.

Here is roughly what I wrote: the appeal of a safe Pareto improvement is supposed to be that its guarantee doesn't rest on anyone getting the probabilities right — it holds for every type in the relevant type space, so neither side has to trust the other's account of how likely they are to be a hawk rather than a dove. But that guarantee is only ever safety relative to a type space someone has already specified in advance. The proof that the new arrangement is at least as good, for every type, as the fallback ranges over the types the modeler thought to write down; it is silent on a party whose actual situation falls outside that space, whether because the space was drawn too narrowly or because the relevant possibility hadn't occurred to anyone designing the mechanism. That looks like a harmless idealization in a toy game, but it seems least trustworthy exactly where the concept is being sold hardest: novel, high-stakes bargaining — a nuclear crisis, a negotiation between AI systems — where the parties don't already share a settled inventory of what "the possible types" even are. So the honest gloss on the guarantee isn't "safe regardless of private information," but "safe regardless of private information, conditional on having already bounded what could be private" — and that second clause is precisely what's hardest to be confident of in the cases that motivate interest in the concept to begin with.

I'd value your comments on a few things: whether I've represented the concept accurately or I'm arguing against a distorted version of it; whether this "type-space" worry is a known and already-answered objection I'm simply not aware of; and, setting accuracy aside, whether you think there's a genuinely interesting point here or whether I'm just dressing up something fairly obvious in jargon to win a dinner argument.
