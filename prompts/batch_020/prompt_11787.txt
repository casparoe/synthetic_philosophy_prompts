I've taught a graduate seminar on free will and moral responsibility more or less every other year for the past decade, which apparently hasn't stopped a small episode in my own department from leaving me far more rattled than any of the puzzle cases I assign my students.

Two months ago, our curriculum committee voted on a proposal to fold two long-standing electives into a single required methods course, a change I had genuine reservations about but, after weeks of deliberation, decided on balance to support. Mine was reportedly the decisive vote, and the change has proven divisive enough that a couple of colleagues have since half-jokingly held me personally responsible for it. Then, last week, over drinks, the committee chair told me why he had never been worried about the outcome: he had arranged beforehand with the associate dean to invoke a dormant emergency provision — meant for members taken ill mid-meeting — that lets the chair register a present member's vote on their behalf if that member becomes "unable to continue," and he had resolved to rule me unable to continue and record me as a yes the moment I lapsed into what he calls my "stalling routine," a set of verbal habits he has learned, across years of committee work with me, reliably precede a no from me. Because I never lapsed into it, and cast my yes of my own accord, he never had occasion to invoke the provision, and nothing about the deliberation that actually produced my vote was touched by any of this.

I find I can't settle what to make of my own case, and would welcome thinking it through with someone not already committed to one side of it. My first instinct is that none of this matters: my yes was produced entirely by my own weighing of the arguments, a contingency that was never activated cannot retroactively contaminate that process, and my colleagues are right to hold me responsible. But I keep getting stuck on the fact that the chair was not merely ready to override a completed vote — he was watching for an advance sign that I was leaning the other way, poised to act before I ever got there, which makes it hard for me to say that voting no was ever really something I was going to be permitted to do. I cannot decide whether that detail genuinely changes how responsible I am for the outcome, or whether I am chasing a distinction that cannot do any real work, and I would welcome your help sorting out which it is.
