For my AP U.S. Government and Politics class, we are expected to know Federalist No. 10 thoroughly as one of the required foundational documents, and for my end-of-year research paper I decided to put Madison's argument about faction in conversation with a stranger text I came across while looking for outside sources: Simone Weil's short 1943 essay "On the Abolition of All Political Parties," which argues that parties — all of them, regardless of platform — should simply be eliminated. What struck me is how differently the two frame the problem. Madison treats faction as a more or less permanent by-product of liberty: you cannot remove the causes without removing liberty itself, so the constitutional design is meant to check the effects instead. Weil, by contrast, argues that any party has three built-in features — it manufactures collective passion, it forces members to accept positions on questions they have never actually examined simply because those questions are bundled together with ones they do care about, and its real operating goal, apart from whatever it officially claims to stand for, is its own growth without any natural limit — and from this she concludes that the whole institution has to be eliminated rather than better regulated.

I would like help with two things I cannot quite work out on my own. First, could you walk through why Weil thinks this problem is structural rather than ideological, so that a party built around aims she would consider good and true is, on her account, just as corrosive to individual judgment as one built around bad aims? I can see that this claim has to be doing a great deal of work in getting her from "parties are often harmful" to "parties must be abolished," but I am not sure I can reconstruct the argument for it without it collapsing into mere assertion. Second, is there anything in her own position that blocks an obvious objection: if legislators still need to form some kind of voting bloc or coalition to accomplish anything once parties are gone, would those blocs not eventually reproduce exactly the dynamics — collective pressure, self-perpetuating growth, pressure to fall in line on issues members have not examined — that she used to condemn parties in the first place? Does she address this anywhere, even implicitly, or is it a genuine gap that later readers of the essay have pointed out?
