I've been going down a rabbit hole for a piece I'm writing on rational choice theory (theres a chance it ends up tying back to the Aumann/Schelling Nobel from 2005, though don't quote me on the year) and I keep running into the phrase "common knowledge of rationality" used in two contexts that feel importantly different, and I want a sanity check before I put anything in print. I was actually arguing about this with a game-theorist friend of mine over drinks and we couldn't fully untangle it ourselves. The first use is the thing that supposedly justifies backward induction in games like the centipede game: if I know you're rational, and you know that I know it, and so on all the way up, then we can reason back from the very last round and conclude the only "rational" move is to defect at the first opportunity, which has always struck me as a suspiciously strong conclusion to squeeze out of such an innocent-sounding premise. The second use is Aumann's own agreeing-to-disagree result, where two Bayesians who share a prior can't, if their posteriors on some event are common knowledge between them, go on rationally disagreeing about it.

So here's my actual question: are these two invocations of "common knowledge" doing the same kind of epistemic work, just applied in different settings, or is something structurally different going on between them? I have a vague memory that the backward-induction story runs into a weird self-undermining problem, where you end up having to fix beliefs about what a player would do at a node that, if common knowledge of rationality really held, should never be reached in the first place -- so you're reasoning about a counterfactual the theory itself says can't happen. I don't remember the agreement theorem having quite that flavor of trouble, but maybe I'm wrong. Could you walk through whether that impression holds up, name a couple of the people who pushed back on Aumann on the backward-induction side if you can recall them, and then actually commit to an answer (not a wishy-washy "both sides have a point") on which of the two uses of common knowledge is on shakier philosophical ground, and why? Apologies if I've mangled the setup somewhere -- my day-to-day is science writing, not formal epistemology, so feel free to tell me where I'm confusing myself.
