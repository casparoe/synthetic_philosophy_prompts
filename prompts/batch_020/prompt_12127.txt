After forty years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, I retired three years ago and have since been filling the gap with the philosophy of science I never had time for during my career. At the moment, a small reading group of similarly retired, similarly philosophically undertrained former colleagues and I are working through Popper's Conjectures and Refutations, and his notion of verisimilitude struck me immediately as naming exactly the intuition I had always had but never had a term for: even though I know perfectly well that every model I ever worked with will eventually be shown false in some respect, it still seems obviously right to say that one such model can be closer to the truth than another, and I liked that Popper wanted this to be more than a comforting figure of speech.

That satisfaction did not survive contact with the secondary literature for long. As I understand it, when Popper tried to make "closer to the truth" rigorous by comparing the classes of true and false logical consequences of a theory, David Miller and Pavel Tichý independently showed in 1974 that his definition has a rather catastrophic consequence: you apparently cannot add a new true consequence to a false theory without also picking up new false consequences along the way, so that, by Popper's own lights, no false theory can ever turn out closer to the truth than any other false theory. Since every theory any of us ever actually worked with was false in some respect, this seems to dissolve the very comparisons the concept was invented to license, and, by his own account, Popper accepted the objection almost as soon as it was put to him.

What I would like from you is a concrete example, preferably a real one drawn from the history of some natural science rather than an invented toy case, of two now-superseded theories or models of the same phenomenon that any working scientist would confidently rank by closeness to the truth. Using that example, could you show, in the actual terms of the debate, exactly why Popper's original consequence-based definition fails to deliver that ranking, and then tell me whether any of the later similarity- or "likeness"-based repairs to verisimilitude actually rescue the intuitive judgment in your example, or whether the trouble runs deeper than a fixable defect in Popper's first attempt?
