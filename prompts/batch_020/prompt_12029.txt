I've spent the last few weeks of my seminar on early modern political economy working through Berkeley's *The Querist* with my students, and I keep coming back to how strange and unexpectedly prescient the text is. Unlike almost everything else Berkeley wrote, it proceeds entirely by rhetorical question rather than argument, and running through its hundreds of queries is a theory of money that owes remarkably little to bullion: he treats gold and silver as more or less incidental, insists that the real wealth of a nation lies in the industry of its people rather than in any stock of specie, and returns repeatedly to the idea that money is best understood as a ticket or counter recording a claim on other people's labour, rather than as a valuable commodity in its own right. He pairs this with a proposal for a national bank that would issue paper credit under wise public administration, precisely so that the currency supply could be actively managed to keep industry active and credit flowing, rather than being left to hoarding, absentee landlords, or the accidents of foreign trade.

This has left me wondering how Berkeley would have assessed cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin, which several of my students keep raising in discussion as the obvious modern heir to his view, on the grounds that Bitcoin, too, has no intrinsic backing and derives its value from convention and confidence rather than from any metal. I am not convinced the analogy survives once the rest of his program is brought in, especially his insistence on centralized, deliberate management of the money supply for the public good and his deep suspicion of wealth that sits idle instead of promoting industry. Before I set this as an assignment, I would like to think it through with you: drawing on what you know of Berkeley's actual arguments in *The Querist*, how do you think he would judge cryptocurrency as a form of money? I would especially value your working through the tensions rather than reaching for the easy answer, and flagging any point at which the parallel to our own situation seems to break down or needs serious qualification.
