I'm finishing a long-form piece on multiverse theories for a science-and-ideas magazine, and there's a framing I'd like to sanity-check before it goes to print. Several of the physicists I've interviewed are committed Everettians: they regard the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics as the most parsimonious reading of the Schrödinger equation, on which every measurement outcome branches into its own equally real, causally isolated world. When I described this to a philosopher friend, she noted how closely it echoes David Lewis's modal realism — the view that every possible world is just as concrete and actual as our own, with "actual" functioning as a mere indexical, like "here" or "now." One of my physicist sources ran further with the comparison, suggesting in a follow-up email that quantum mechanics has essentially handed philosophy an empirical proof of something Lewis could previously only argue for from the armchair.

That would make a wonderful hook for the piece — physics vindicating a bit of speculative metaphysics — but I don't want to publish a claim that doesn't actually hold up under scrutiny. Is it accurate to say that the many-worlds interpretation, if correct, would confirm or substantially support modal realism? Or are these two ideas that merely sound alike because both get casually glossed as "every possible world is real," while actually resting on quite different notions of possibility, physical law, and evidence? I'd appreciate a careful, precise breakdown of exactly where the analogy genuinely holds and where it breaks down, so I can represent both the physics and the philosophy responsibly in print.
