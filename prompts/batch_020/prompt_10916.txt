Okay so my friend Jordan and I got into a whole two-hour argument last night about Thomson's lamp, of all things — we started out just watching some video on Zeno's paradoxes and somehow never got around to the movie we'd actually sat down for. Jordan's whole case was that Thomson's lamp is basically just Zeno's dichotomy paradox wearing a costume: the dichotomy paradox got "solved" ages ago by noticing that 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ... converges to a finite sum, so the lamp has to be solvable the exact same way. Her argument was that the switching times converge on the one-minute mark, so the sequence of on/off states converges too, and since the lamp starts off and gets flipped a definite (if infinite) number of times before that mark, simple parity tells you whether it ends up on or off right at one minute. By the end I didnt have a comeback and basically conceded, mostly because I couldn't articulate what was actually wrong with it, just that it felt off.

Except now I keep turning it over and I'm not so sure I should've folded. If the state at the limit really were just a parity-plus-convergence computation like that, it seems like Thomson's lamp wouldn't be a real puzzle at all, just a dressed-up limits problem — so why do people who write seriously about supertasks still treat it as a genuine open question about indeterminacy rather than a solved exercise? Is there a specific step in Jordan's reasoning that doesn't actually go through mathematically, or is she basically right and everyone who treats this as a deep puzzle is missing something kind of obvious?
