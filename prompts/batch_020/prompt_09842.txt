So I've been down a rabbit hole on the Lucas/Penrose argument, the one where they use Gödel's incompleteness theorem to claim a human mind can't just be a computer, since we can apparently "see" that a certain sentence is true even though any formal system standing in for our own reasoning could never prove it about itself. Got me thinking about Leibniz, of all people, since he's basically the OG computation guy - he built one of the first mechanical calculators and dreamed up a 'universal characteristic', a symbolic language where you could settle literally any dispute just by calculating ('calculemus!'). But he's also got that trippy mill passage in the Monadology, where he says imagine blowing up a thinking machine to the size of a windmill and walking around inside it - all you'd find is parts shoving other parts, never any perception, so mind can't really be a mechanism after all. So which way do you reckon he'd actually jump if you showed him the Gödel/Lucas/Penrose argument - would he take it as finally vindicating his mill hunch, or would he notice its actually a totally different kind of argument (about proving math truths, not about "why does anything feel like something") that doesn't really rescue his monads? And would it annoy him that the same theorem kind of torpedoes his own life's dream of a complete universal calculus? Try to get into his actual specific views on this rather than just generic "17th century rationalist" takes.
