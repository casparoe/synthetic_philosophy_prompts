Over the last quarter, the catastrophe-modeling team I work for -- we price reinsurance for tail risks such as flood and wildfire -- has been piloting a change to how we report uncertainty about parameters that are not well pinned down by historical data, such as the probability that a given river basin experiences a "1-in-500-year" flood under current climate trends. Rather than handing underwriters a single point estimate, we now output a range generated from a whole family of statistically defensible models, on the grounds that a single number would falsely advertise a precision the evidence does not actually support. I have since learned that this is an instance of what philosophers call "imprecise probability" or "imprecise credence": representing an epistemic state not by a single probability function but by a set of them (a "credal set"), with betting behavior governed by upper and lower bounds rather than one sharp number.

This has occasioned a genuine rift on my team. One of our senior actuaries argues that this is all well and good until someone actually has to price a policy: at that point the range must collapse into a decision, and any rule for making that collapse either smuggles a sharp probability back in through the choice of collapsing rule, or falls prey to a problem I came across, after some digging, in a paper by the philosopher Adam Elga. His argument, as I understand it, is that an agent who pairs imprecise credences with the seemingly natural rule "do not accept a bet unless every distribution in your set favors it" can be maneuvered, across two separately offered bets, into declining a package that is a sure gain regardless of which distribution in her own set turns out to be correct. I also learned about "dilation," the phenomenon whereby conditioning an imprecise credal set on new evidence can leave it wider rather than narrower, which seems to cut against the entire rationale for going imprecise in the first place, namely fidelity to the evidence. The modelers on the other side of the dispute maintain that the alternative is its own kind of fabrication: forcing a single sharp number onto a case of genuine ambiguity (of the sort illustrated by Ellsberg's urn) manufactures a precision that is simply not present in the evidence, and that Elga's argument shows at most that one particular decision rule is flawed, not that imprecise credences themselves are irrational.

I would like to work out who has the better of this before our next model review. Could you write an essay that lays out and weighs the central arguments for and against representing rational belief with imprecise probabilities rather than sharp, point-valued credences? Please engage specifically with the "arbitrariness" motivation for going imprecise under ambiguous evidence, with Elga's sequential-decision argument against imprecise credence, and with dilation -- including how a defender of imprecise probability might try to defuse the latter two objections, for instance by evaluating decisions holistically, as part of a plan, rather than one at a time as they arrive. Please do not simply present a balanced survey and stop there: reach an actual, defended verdict on whether imprecise credences are rationally defensible and, if so, under what constraints on the accompanying decision rule.
