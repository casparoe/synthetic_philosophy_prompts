I am several drafts into a novel built on a simple structural conceit: alternating chapters follow a woman in her sixties as she actually lived, and as she would have lived if, at twenty-two, she had accepted a two-year fellowship abroad instead of declining it. Somewhere in the writing, the declined-fellowship chapters stopped feeling like invention; I keep catching myself treating them as though they record something that truly happened to her, elsewhere, rather than a plausible story I constructed to serve the book. My editor thinks this is beside the point — a matter of tone, not truth — while a beta reader I trust insists the ending is hollow unless we settle whether the two women are one person who took different paths or two people who merely share a childhood and a face. Before I answer either of them, I want to know what my instinct is actually tracking: is there any sense in which an unchosen life is real, and if so, would the woman living it be her, or only ever someone very like her?
