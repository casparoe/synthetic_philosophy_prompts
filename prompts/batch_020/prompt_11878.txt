A few of us with no formal training in philosophy have been meeting for about a year now to work through Kierkegaard on our own, in translation, at a fairly slow pace. We recently finished the "Present Age" section of his Two Ages, and afterward two people in the group, Elena and Marcus, kept arguing about it by email for days. I'm reporting it here because I honestly can't decide who has the better of it.

Elena's opening point was that Kierkegaard is an odd source for a theory of impersonal, nobody's-fault "leveling," given how personally he seems to have brought on his own worst experience of it. In print, in a dispute with another critic, he more or less dared the gossip paper The Corsair to attack him, on the grounds that being passed over by it, while lesser writers got mocked, felt like the greater insult. When the paper obliged, spending months caricaturing his trousers and his walk all over Copenhagen, he turned around and wrote up "the public" as a monstrous abstraction that nobody could ever be held responsible for. To her, that looks backwards: a personal grudge dressed up afterward as a diagnosis of the whole age.

Marcus answered that this is a genetic fallacy: even granting that Kierkegaard provoked the attack, that doesn't show his diagnosis is wrong. If anything, months of anonymous caricature, with no single author ever answerable for any of it, is close to a textbook case of exactly the kind of abstraction he says can ruin someone while no concrete person bears the blame.

Elena replied that it isn't just that he provoked one attack. In his journals afterward, he described needing the humiliation, almost as a corrective handed to him so that he wouldn't settle into being a comfortable success. Someone who says his own persecution did him good, she argued, can't also turn around and use that same persecution as proof of how blindly crushing and impersonal the whole mechanism is; if it worked out well for him, on his own telling, then it wasn't simply the mindless leveling force he elsewhere makes it out to be.

Marcus's reply was that these are two different claims that don't actually conflict. What he says he needed was the suffering of being singled out and mocked, which he could later put to spiritual use; what he condemns is the public's capacity to do that to absolutely anyone, indifferently, with nobody ever accountable for it. Being able to make good use of an evil afterward doesn't mean it wasn't an evil, or that it wasn't produced by exactly the mechanism he describes.

Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each point, rather than just picking an overall winner? I'm particularly interested in whether either of them mischaracterizes what Kierkegaard means by "the public" or "leveling" at any stage, and in what, if anything, both of them might still be missing.
