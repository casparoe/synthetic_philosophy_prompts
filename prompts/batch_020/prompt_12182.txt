Over the past year I've been reading Hobbes's Leviathan on my own — I never studied philosophy formally, but a friend lent me a copy and I got hooked. One line in Chapter 4 has stuck with me ever since: Hobbes remarks, in the middle of a discussion of definitions, that geometry is "the only science that it hath pleased God hitherto to bestow on mankind," and his reasoning seems to be that geometry alone gives us certain knowledge because we ourselves draw the lines and construct the figures we reason about, so we can be sure of their properties in a way we could never be sure of natural things we merely observe. What struck me even more, once I started reading around the subject, is that Hobbes doesn't treat this as a peculiarity of geometry: he argues that civil philosophy — his whole theory of the commonwealth and sovereign power — can be just as demonstrable and certain, for exactly the same reason, since a commonwealth is likewise something we construct ourselves, by covenant, rather than something handed to us ready-made the way a tree or a planet is. I later learned, somewhat to my amusement, that this is the very same Hobbes who spent the rest of his life insisting he had squared the circle, and who was patiently and repeatedly shown to be wrong, in print, by John Wallis and the Oxford mathematicians, without ever once conceding the point.

I sit on the governing committee of a small residents' co-op, and a few years back several of us wrote our own rulebook from scratch, more or less assuming that because we ourselves had built it, we would understand how it worked in something like the way you understand a triangle you've just drawn — that we, of all people, would be able to anticipate how our own rules would play out. It hasn't turned out that way at all; the rules keep generating disputes and situations none of us saw coming. So I'd like you to lay out what you take to be the most important implications of Hobbes's argument — that self-construction is what grounds certain, demonstrative knowledge, first for geometry and then for the commonwealth — for how much predictive certainty ordinary people are really entitled to expect from institutions and rules just because they designed them. I'm especially curious whether Hobbes's own lifelong blind spot about his "proofs" in geometry, the one domain he treated as the very model of self-made certainty, should make us more skeptical of the whole analogy rather than less.
