I do community theater in my spare time, nothing serious, just local productions, and a couple of years ago our troupe nearly lost its city funding when a council member argued, apparently in earnest, that subsidizing a theater amounted to paying people to feel things about invented problems instead of doing anything about real ones. The argument stuck with me, and since I've never studied philosophy or literature formally, I went looking on my own to see whether anyone more serious than a council member had made a version of it. That is how I came across Jean-Jacques Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert: d'Alembert had written an article for the Encyclopédie proposing that Rousseau's home city of Geneva build a public theater, and Rousseau answered with an entire furious letter opposing it, a dispute that became tangled up with his final break from Diderot and the rest of the Encyclopédie circle, who counted the arts, theater included, among the civilizing forces of the age.

The more I read about what Rousseau actually argued, the less it looked like simple moralizing about "fun." He makes a specific claim that the pity we feel for a suffering character on stage can substitute for, rather than train, our willingness to help a suffering person right in front of us; a claim that the actor's trade requires a kind of professional self-counterfeiting that corrodes whatever authentic self he started with; and a claim that a culture divided into performers and spectators undermines the transparency that a small, self-governing city like Geneva depends on, which is why he wanted to replace theater with open-air civic festivals where everyone present is participant and audience at once. Around the same time, and mostly independently, Diderot was busy building his own theory of a new "serious drama" meant to do exactly the moral work Rousseau denied the stage could ever do.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between Rousseau and Diderot, set around the time the Letter came out, working through this disagreement face to face instead of in dueling pamphlets. Give Rousseau his real argument rather than a caricature of prudishness, and let Diderot answer as the theorist he actually was: someone with his own considered view of how sympathy, sentiment, and staged suffering can shape an audience, and with real questions to put back to Rousseau, including whether a republic of festival-going citizens who already know and trust one another is any kind of model for a city the size of Paris. I don't want either man handed a clean win; I want them landing real blows on each other's specific points, and ideally at least one moment where each concedes something genuine to the other before holding his ground.
