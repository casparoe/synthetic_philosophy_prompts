I'm taking a philosophy of language seminar this semester, and we just spent two lectures on Kripke's Naming and Necessity before the professor pivoted into experimental philosophy and showed us that Machery/Mallon/Nichols/Stich study where they ran a version of the Gödel case (the guy everyone credits with proving incompleteness vs. "Schmidt," the guy who supposedly actually did it and got his work stolen) on American undergrads and on undergrads in Hong Kong. From what I remember, the Americans mostly gave the Kripkean answer (the name still picks out the impostor, since that's who the naming practice causally traces back to), while a lot more of the Hong Kong students gave the old-school Fregean/descriptivist answer (the name picks out whoever actually satisfies the description). A few of us grabbed coffee after class and kind of talked ourselves into this conclusion: since the "correct" answer to Kripke's own thought experiment depends on which culture you ask, there just isn't a fact of the matter about reference, and Naming and Necessity is really just Kripke dressing up his own Western intuitions as necessary truths about language.

Is that basically the right lesson to draw though? Like, does a result like this actually show that reference/meaning is culturally relative and that theres no objective answer to the Gödel case, or are we overselling what one cross-cultural intuitions study can establish?
