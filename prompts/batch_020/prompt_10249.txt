I teach a graduate seminar on Buddhist philosophy, and this week we worked through chapter 10 of Nagarjuna's Mulamadhyamakakarika -- the "fire and fuel" chapter, where he runs his signature tetralemma over whether fire is identical to its fuel, different from it, both, or neither, then extends that exact analysis to the relationship between the self and the five aggregates. Every year I watch half the room nod along with the formal logic and then, the moment we hit discussion section, revert to treating emptiness (sunyata) as just a fancy word for "nothing really exists, so nothing matters" -- which is precisely the nihilist extreme Nagarjuna spends the whole text trying to avoid via the two truths doctrine. I suspect the pure logic-chopping approach (we're using Garfield's translation) is part of the problem -- students can follow every step of the tetralemma without ever feeling why the conclusion is supposed to be liberating rather than depressing.

So here's what I want to try in section this year: could you write me a short story, maybe 800-1200 words, set in an ordinary contemporary setting -- no monks, no meditation cushions, nothing Orientalist -- that dramatizes this exact piece of reasoning? I want a character's sense of self to come apart under something like the fire/fuel analysis (neither simply identical to, nor entirely seperate from, whatever "fuels" it -- memories, a body, a role, relationships, up to you), while still somehow gesturing at the conventional/ultimate two-truths resolution instead of just landing on flat nihilism. Please don't give me two characters reciting the argument at each other Socratic-dialogue style -- I want the logical structure embedded in the plot and images themselves, so a reader who's never heard of Nagarjuna still feels the vertigo, and a reader who has could point to exactly where each horn of the tetralemma gets tested and dropped.
