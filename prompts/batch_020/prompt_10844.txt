I've spent the past few weeks reporting a feature on the replication crisis in psychology, and one thread of it has been nagging at me enough that I'd like a more careful answer than I can construct on my own. The two most famous empirical props of the "situationist" critique of virtue ethics have both taken a serious hit in recent years. Thibault Le Texier's 2019 archival investigation, published in American Psychologist, found that Zimbardo's guards were extensively briefed on how to behave rather than left to improvise cruelty on their own, and that the Stanford Prison Experiment leaned heavily on an earlier, little-known prison simulation that students in one of Zimbardo's own classes had run a few months before. A separate re-analysis by Alex Haslam, Steve Reicher, and Jay Van Bavel, drawing on newly released archive material, went further, arguing that whatever cruelty did emerge was driven less by guards simply succumbing to a role than by their actively identifying with an identity that the experimenters, acting as leaders, had encouraged. And around the same time, Gina Perry's work digging through Stanley Milgram's own papers turned up evidence that a striking share of his subjects doubted the shocks were even real, and that the single "obedience rate" everyone quotes papers over enormous variation across his twenty-four experimental conditions.

This matters to me because when Gilbert Harman and John Doris built their influential argument, in the late 1990s and early 2000s, that social psychology had essentially refuted the Aristotelian picture of stable character traits, Milgram's obedience studies and the Stanford Prison Experiment were their two headline exhibits, even though the case also leaned on less dramatic studies, like Isen and Levin's dime-in-a-phone-booth experiment on helping behavior and Darley and Batson's study of seminary students who stepped past a man slumped in a doorway on their way to deliver a talk on the parable of the Good Samaritan. A philosopher friend I described all this to over dinner insisted that the critique never really needed Milgram or Zimbardo in the first place, which is what finally prompted me to ask someone who might actually know.

Given all that, could you lay out what you take to be the most important implications of this evidentiary shake-up for the philosophical situationist critique of virtue ethics? I'm especially interested in whether the case against robust character holds up on the strength of the more mundane studies alone, whether the "identity leadership" reading of the prison experiment ends up supporting a more Aristotelian picture of moral agency than either side originally assumed, and, more broadly, what this whole episode should teach us about how much philosophical weight to place on any single, vivid body of empirical psychology.
