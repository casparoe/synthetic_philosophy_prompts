I'm a backend engineer, and I've spent the last couple of years on the on-call rotation that handles major traffic incidents for the platform I work on. While writing up the postmortem for a recent incident, I got stuck on something I haven't been able to shake since. We had a surge coming that would have taken the whole platform down, for every customer, for at least half an hour unless we intervened. Two mitigations were on the table, and both were projected to land us in essentially the same place: our free-tier users (a small slice of total traffic, but tens of thousands of real accounts) would lose access for the duration, while paying customers stayed up throughout. The first option was a general adaptive rate-limiter that smooths load across all incoming requests; because free-tier usage happens to concentrate on our most expensive endpoints, this would crowd out free-tier traffic first as a byproduct, without the policy being aimed at them specifically. The second option was to explicitly detect free-tier accounts and cut their connections outright, precisely because doing so frees up exactly the capacity needed to keep everyone else running. My lead went with the second option, since it's simpler to reason about mid-incident, and pointed out that the two were equivalent anyway: same accounts lose access, same duration, same benefit to everyone else.

I didn't push back at the time, but I've kept turning over the sense that there's a real difference between a neutral policy that happens to fall hardest on one group and a fix that works precisely by using that group's loss as its mechanism, even though the downstream harm is identical either way. When I brought this up with two colleagues afterward, they landed on opposite sides: one said I was drawing a distinction without a difference, since fixating on the mechanism rather than the outcome is just squeamishness; the other said it obviously matters, because deliberately routing the cost of a fix onto a group is different in kind from a side effect landing on them, even with the numbers held fixed. I don't have any background in philosophy, so I can't tell whether I'm circling a real principle or just rationalizing discomfort after the fact. Is there an actual, defensible basis for treating these two options differently, and if so, what is it, and how far should it generalize to other engineering decisions? Or, once the people affected and the harm to them are identical, does the mechanism really not matter?
