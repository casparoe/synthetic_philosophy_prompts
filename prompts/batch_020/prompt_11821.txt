I am reporting a piece on the deep intellectual history behind debates over usury, and I want to pressure-test the argument that sits at the root of the Western tradition on this question. Across the Politics and the Nicomachean Ethics, Aristotle distinguishes household management aimed at securing what a family genuinely needs from the open-ended pursuit of wealth for its own sake, and treats money as a conventional device rather than a thing with a nature of its own — he even notes that the word for currency, nomisma, derives from nomos, or custom, to underline that its value is a human creation rather than nature's. His case against lending at interest turns on the fact that the Greek word for interest, tokos, also means "offspring": charging interest treats money as though it could breed more money, and since money is inert and exists only by convention, he takes this "birth" to be the most unnatural of all ways of getting wealth.

Contemporary readers split on how much weight this argument can bear. Some, echoing the way Marx credits Aristotle with glimpsing the difference between money as a mere symbol of exchange and money as self-expanding capital, think Aristotle is fumbling toward a real and still-useful distinction, one that later traditions — the scholastic ban on usury among them — inherited and sharpened. Others think the argument amounts to little more than wordplay: it is odd to fault interest for being "unnatural" after conceding that money's value is already a matter of custom, and lending at interest looks unproblematic as soon as the money is put to genuinely productive use, so that the lender's return tracks real value created rather than any literal breeding of coins.

Write an essay that weighs these two assessments against each other and defends your own verdict on whether Aristotle's specific argument against interest survives as serious reasoning, or should be set aside as a dead end that nonetheless pointed, by accident, toward a better distinction than the one he actually drew. Do not hedge into a non-committal conclusion.
