I'm writing a magazine piece on Harry Frankfurt's attack on the principle of alternative possibilities (PAP), and one claim that keeps surfacing in the secondary literature is that his Black-and-Jones case was effectively anticipated by a much older thought experiment in Locke. Here is the passage, from Book II, Chapter XXI ("Of Power") of the *Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, section 10:

"Again: suppose a man be carried, whilst fast asleep, into a room where is a person he longs to see and speak with; and be there locked fast in, beyond his power to get out: he awakes, and is glad to find himself in so desirable company, which he stays willingly in, i.e. prefers his stay to going away. I ask, is not this stay voluntary? I think nobody will doubt it: and yet, being locked fast in, it is evident he is not at liberty not to stay, he has not freedom to be gone. So that liberty is not an idea belonging to volition, or preferring; but to the person having the power of doing, or forbearing to do, according as the mind shall choose or direct. Our idea of liberty reaches as far as that power, and no farther. For wherever restraint comes to check that power, or compulsion takes away that indifferency of ability to act, or to forbear acting, there liberty, and our notion of it, presently ceases."

Could you first unpack this passage on its own terms: what exactly is the distinction Locke draws between "voluntary" and "at liberty," and how does it connect to the notions of restraint, compulsion, and necessity he develops elsewhere in the chapter? Then, for the piece itself, I'd like your honest assessment rather than the received lineage: does this passage really do the same philosophical work as Frankfurt's Black-and-Jones case against PAP, or is there a difference between what the man in the room and what Jones is each unable to do that should make us hesitate before calling one a straightforward ancestor of the other?
