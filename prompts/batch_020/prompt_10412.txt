Over the past year or so I've spent a lot of my free time reading around decision theory and AI safety, even though I have no background in philosophy or mathematics beyond what I picked up in school. It started, embarrassingly, with hearing about Roko's Basilisk as a kind of internet horror story that occasionally does the rounds, and it eventually led me into the more serious literature on why some people in those communities take "acausal" threats seriously in the first place, given that nothing is ever causally transmitted between the entity making the threat and the agent being threatened. From there I found my way to the idea of "surrogate goals": rather than trying to become the sort of agent that simply never responds to threats (which seems to demand a degree of self-knowledge and credible precommitment that no real agent has), you instead get your agent to adopt an additional, more or less invented goal that it is disposed to protect exactly as strongly as the thing it actually cares about. The hope is that a threatener ends up indifferent between targeting the real goal and the decoy, so that even a "successful" threat only ever costs you something you never valued in the first place.

What I can't settle for myself is whether this is doing any real, additional work, or whether it just relabels ordinary precommitment and makes it sound cleverer than it is. If a threatener is sophisticated enough to model an agent's actual policy or source code — which seems to be the whole premise behind acausal interaction to begin with — I don't see why it would be deterred by a goal that is, by construction, known on all sides to have been adopted purely for deflection. A threat only seems worth making if the threatener expects it to change the target's behavior, so why wouldn't a legibly manufactured goal simply get ignored in favor of the real one, or targeted alongside it? At the same time, I've come across responses suggesting this objection misses the point, since what matters is the agent's disposition to respond, not whether the underlying goal is "authentic" in some deeper sense, and a disposition can in principle be made just as firm and credible for a surrogate goal as for a real one.

Could you write me a dialogue between two people who both take this idea seriously but land on opposite sides of that question — one convinced that surrogate goals genuinely reduce the expected harm from acausal threats and blackmail, the other convinced that the strategy either collapses into ordinary precommitment or quietly assumes a threatener too naive to see through it? I'd like both speakers to be equally sharp and to actually engage with each other's specific arguments as the conversation goes along, rather than restating their own position past one another, and I'd especially like them to work through the "legibility" problem: whether an agent can be said to credibly protect a goal that both it and the threatener know was manufactured solely to be threatened in place of something else. It doesn't need to end in agreement — I'd just like to see the strongest version of each side.
