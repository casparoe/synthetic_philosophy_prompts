I'm a novelist, and next week I'm interviewing a scholar of early Chinese philosophy for background on a character — an elderly former hospice chaplain who has quietly organized her remaining time around Zhuangzi's writing on death. The scholar I'm meeting defends what I'd call the strong reading of that material: not merely that it helps us manage grief, but that it gives us no more reason to prefer living to dying than the reverse, and that its famous scenes are meant to be taken at face value rather than as consoling exaggeration. Please draft eight or nine interview questions that press on that reading rather than let her simply restate it. I want at least one question on each of the following: the distance between the composed philosopher who lectures his friend Hui Shi on cosmic transformation the very day his wife dies, and his own admission, in the same breath, that he first grieved like anyone else; whether talking to a skull on the road to Chu and pillowing his head on it amounts to a preference for death or only an agnosticism about it, given that the earlier parable of Lady Li, who wept before a journey she later came to welcome, supports only the weaker claim; how the imagery of a dying body being recast, the way a smith recasts metal, depends on a background cosmology of qi and the Way that a modern physicalist reader has no reason to grant; and how his own deathbed refusal of an elaborate burial — heaven and earth as his coffin, crows above ground no worse than worms below — sits against the tradition's equally persistent interest in nourishing and prolonging life. For each, add a brief follow-up keyed to the deflection she is most likely to reach for.
