I'm a senior in high school and part of a small student-run AI safety reading group. For our next meeting I wrote a short essay trying to explain what's called the "Löbian obstacle" to self-trust in formal reasoning systems, and to connect it to something from epistemology that I think is analogous. Before I present it, I'd like some outside feedback. Here's the essay:

Suppose a reasoning system wants to certify, using only formal proof, that a future or successor version of itself can be trusted — say, before rewriting itself into a version running different code. The natural way to cash out "trustworthy" for a system that deals only in proofs is a reflection principle: whatever this system proves is true. For a theory T with a provability predicate, that is the schema Prov('P') → P, one instance for every sentence P.

Löb's theorem says that for any theory at least as strong as Peano Arithmetic, if the theory proves Prov('P') → P for some sentence P, then it already proves P outright, full stop. So a system cannot adopt this reflection schema for a sentence it does not already prove without the schema collapsing into a direct proof of that very sentence. Taking P to be a contradiction recovers Gödel's second incompleteness theorem as a special case: a consistent theory can never prove its own consistency. Gödel II, in other words, is just one instance of a much more general obstacle to self-trust.

This is a real problem for self-modifying AI, not just a curiosity of logic. If agent A only rewrites itself into successor A' when A can prove "whatever A' proves is true," then by Löb's theorem A only gets that guarantee for sentences it could already prove on its own — A' has not actually bought A any extra trust. One fix discussed in the "tiling agents" literature associated with MIRI has each successor reason in a strictly weaker formal system than its parent, so the reflection principle points at a genuinely different, weaker theory rather than at itself, sidestepping direct self-reference. A more elegant version, sometimes called parametric polymorphism, states the trust schema generically, over "any theory no stronger than mine," instead of hard-coding one specific weaker successor, so one schema can supposedly cover the whole descending hierarchy at once.

Here is the part I am least confident about. I think this hierarchy-of-weaker-theories move does not dissolve the self-trust problem so much as relocate it into a shape epistemology already has a name for: Agrippa's trilemma. Grounding a theory's trust in a strictly weaker predecessor is the infinite-regress horn, since the chain of ever-weaker theories has to go somewhere; stopping the hierarchy at some base theory whose soundness we simply assume is the unsupported-axiom horn; and any attempt to have a theory vouch directly for itself runs straight into Löb's theorem, which is the circularity horn, foreclosed. If that is right, the Löbian obstacle is not a quirky artifact of provability logic specifically — it is Agrippa's trilemma wearing a Gödel-numbered disguise, and no cleverness with proof systems can dissolve it, only relabel which horn you are stuck holding.

I would appreciate comments on two things especially: first, whether I've gotten Löb's theorem, its relationship to Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, and the tiling-agents fixes right, since I'm working from secondhand summaries rather than the original papers; and second — more importantly — whether the closing analogy to Agrippa's trilemma holds up as a serious philosophical point, or whether I'm forcing a resemblance between a formal theorem and an informal regress problem that don't really match up. I would rather be told the analogy breaks down, with the specific reason why, than be told it's basically fine.
