I'm taking a research seminar on the Scientific Revolution this term, and we just spent two weeks on Kepler in a way that upended the tidy "father of celestial mechanics" story most general survey courses leave you with. Alongside the usual material on the Astronomia Nova, we read selections from the Tertius Interveniens (1610) — literally "Third Man in the Middle," Kepler's own term for the position he stakes out between astrologers he regarded as charlatans and critics who wanted the whole discipline abolished — plus the astrological book of the Harmonice Mundi (1619), where he grounds the "aspects" (the angular relationships between planets as seen from Earth) in a theory of harmonic resonance between geometry and the human soul, and supplements the traditional Ptolemaic aspects with several new ones derived from that same harmonic theory. Our secondary reading, a monograph by the historian Patrick Boner, argued that none of this was a compartmentalized side hustle: Kepler dismissed the zodiac signs, the houses, and most of the working astrologer's stock techniques as baseless, yet he kept a personal archive of several hundred horoscopes as data for an empirical reform of the discipline, and spent years as a working court astrologer producing nativities for patrons like Rudolf II — all while describing astrology, in his own memorable phrase, as astronomy's "foolish daughter," who nonetheless paid her "wise mother's" bills.

A classmate in the seminar who reads her phone's astrology app every morning joked, half seriously, that Kepler basically invented her horoscope four hundred years too early, and I haven't been able to shake the comparison since. Apps like Co-Star or The Pattern compute a user's full natal chart — planets, houses, and aspects included — from precise, real-time astronomical data, then generate personalized character readings and daily guidance for tens of millions of subscribers through automated language-generation software: a combination of genuine astronomical precision and mass-produced psychological content that Kepler's own idiosyncratic career seems strangely well suited to judge. Working strictly from his actual, specific commitments — the aspects-versus-zodiac distinction, the harmonic soul theory, and his insistence that astrology's real content be established empirically rather than assumed — what do you think Kepler's verdict on an app like this would be?
