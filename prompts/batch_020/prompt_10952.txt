I'm a philosophy PhD student working on formal decision theory (my dissertation concerns updateless and functional decision theories and their implications for bargaining), and for the past year I've also served, alongside four other volunteers, on the small grants panel of a private foundation that funds research on cooperation and conflict-avoidance among advanced AI systems, with a particular interest in steering away from worst-case ("s-risk") outcomes. We have a proposal on the table that has split the panel, and I've been asked to write up the panel's decision ahead of next month's board meeting, so I'd like your help drafting it.

The proposal, from a two-person independent research group, asks for a year of funding to develop and publicly publish a formal treatment of "surrogate goals" as a defense against acausal threats. The background idea, drawn from decision theories built to handle Newcomb-like prediction problems (updateless and functional decision theory), is that a sufficiently capable system can be threatened or extorted with no causal channel to the threatener whatsoever, simply because the threatener can predict or simulate how the system would respond to different policies. The proposed countermeasure is for the system, or its designers, to deliberately give it a secondary, low-stakes goal that it visibly treats as precious, so that a rational threatener's optimal target becomes that decoy rather than whatever the system truly values — capping the expected damage from any threat that is actually carried out. Three of the five panelists consider this exactly the kind of neglected, foundational work the foundation exists to fund, and think that subjecting the idea to open scrutiny now, while nothing of consequence hinges on it, is how we would discover its problems — whether a sufficiently sophisticated threatener would simply see through the decoy, say, or whether the strategy can be made credible without being dynamically inconsistent — before anyone builds something like it into a deployed system. The other two think a detailed, reproducible how-to for this kind of decoy defense is close to a textbook information hazard: it would hand future adversaries, human or artificial, a manual for defeating the defense, and, more speculatively but not (I think) dismissibly, risks seeding the very concept of extortion strategy into text that may end up training the systems we are trying to protect.

Could you draft the panel's decision memo? It should state plainly whether we fund the proposal as submitted, reject it, or fund it subject to specific conditions (for instance, about how and when any results may be published), and it should lay out reasoning the whole panel could recognize as fair — including a genuine, non-dismissive engagement with the strongest consideration on whichever side ends up losing, rather than just a restatement of the majority position.
