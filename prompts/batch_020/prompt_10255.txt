I've been thinking on my own about how philosophers try to define dispositions like fragility purely through a conditional: "x is fragile" means, roughly, "x would break if struck." As far as I can tell, two different kinds of counterexample cause trouble for this simple picture. In one, illustrated by a wire hooked up to a hidden switching mechanism, something flips the disposition on or off at the very instant the trigger occurs, so the object never really had the disposition the conditional was meant to track -- this is what's called a "finkish" disposition. In the other, the object keeps the disposition the whole time and the trigger genuinely occurs, but something extra steps in to block the manifestation -- a "mask" or "antidote," as when a fragile item is cushioned so a blow doesn't break it. I gather David Lewis tried to rescue the conditional analysis by tying the disposition to an object's underlying intrinsic causal basis, and that this fix is usually judged to succeed against finks but not against masks. Could you compare finkish and masked dispositions in detail: what each one actually shows is wrong with the plain conditional analysis, why a causal-basis fix would defeat one but not the other, and whether, in your own assessment, any purely conditional analysis could be built to withstand both at once?
