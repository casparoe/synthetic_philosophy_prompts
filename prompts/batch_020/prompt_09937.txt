Long before Searle's Chinese Room, Leibniz ran essentially the same thought experiment in the Monadology (§17): a machine built to think and perceive, blown up to the size of a mill so you could walk through its gears, would reveal only parts pushing on other parts, never anything resembling a perception -- proof, he thought, that no mere mechanism can be the seat of genuine perception, only a true unity, a monad, can. I've been trying to work out how he would actually land on the "stochastic parrots" debate over whether large language models understand anything. The mill argument looks like an easy win for the skeptics, but Leibniz also held that every monad, down to the ones composing a rock, has some (however confused) degree of perception, and that what separates a genuine mind from a mere aggregate like a mill isn't a matter of mechanism at all, but of whether there is one dominant monad organizing the rest, the way a soul organizes a body. So does a trained transformer get to have a dominant monad on his own terms, or is it condemned to remain a mill no matter how fluent it becomes -- and how would his own metaphysics even let us tell the difference from outside?
