After a colloquium talk on moral worth in my department last month, two colleagues of mine engaged in an extended back-and-forth over the speaker's central case.

The case: Novak, a passerby, dived into a canal and pulled a drowning toddler to safety. Asked afterward what had been going through his mind, he said the only thought he'd had was "that's a kid, move" — nothing about duty, rules, or rightness.

(1) Farah said this was a clean case of morally worthy action: Novak was moved by, and appropriately sensitive to, the very fact that made the act right — a child in danger he could save at little cost — and nothing further needed to enter his head.

(2) Lucas objected that this sort of sensitivity couldn't be enough, since it failed to rule out a structurally similar case: a compulsive risk-taker who dives into canals whenever something moves in the water, thrilled by the danger, and who would have dived in just as readily for a floating log. He too is "sensitive" to some feature of the scene, yet a life saved this way earns him no moral credit.

(3) Farah replied that the two cases differed in just the way her view needed them to: Novak's disposition to help was differentially triggered by danger to a child and would have stayed quiet for a log, while the thrill-seeker's disposition is indifferent between the two — so sensitivity, properly understood as counterfactual sensitivity to the reason itself, does distinguish them.

(4) Lucas answered that this only relocated the problem: we still need an explanation of why Novak's disposition tracks danger-to-a-child specifically rather than, say, danger-in-general or produces-a-good-story-later, and any such explanation will have to describe him as registering the child's danger as bad or as calling for help — which just reintroduces, in a roundabout way, the very appreciation of rightness that Farah's view was meant to avoid requiring.

Neither would budge, and I'd like a considered outside view.
