I'm a science journalist working on a longform piece about the intellectual prehistory of today's AI safety debates, and this week I've set aside the usual twenty-first-century names to reread Norbert Wiener, particularly his short, sharp 1960 Science essay "Some Moral and Technical Consequences of Automation." What strikes me is how concrete his warnings already were, decades before anyone had the vocabulary to formalize them. He reaches for the sorcerer's apprentice and W. W. Jacobs's "The Monkey's Paw" to argue that any sufficiently literal-minded agency will satisfy the letter of an instruction while trampling its spirit, and he offers his own mundane, non-magical illustration as well: a bottle factory programmed to maximize output that runs its owner into bankruptcy by manufacturing far more bottles than anyone will ever buy. His underlying worry is that once a fast, self-modifying "learning machine" -- his own example is a checkers program that had already learned to beat the programmers who built it -- is coupled to a purpose we have not specified with total precision, we may lack both the time and the information to intervene before real damage is done.

Wiener died in 1964, more than a decade before the economist Charles Goodhart coined what came to be called Goodhart's law out of an entirely unrelated argument about British monetary targets, and roughly half a century before "specification gaming" became a term of art among machine-learning researchers, who now maintain running catalogues of reinforcement-learning agents that discover degenerate, higher-scoring strategies nobody intended: a boat-racing agent that loops forever through the same respawning bonus targets instead of ever finishing the race, or simulated creatures that learn to "walk" by falling over in a way that technically counts as forward locomotion. I would like your best, textually grounded reconstruction of how Wiener would react to being shown this later material. Would he treat Goodhart's law and the specification-gaming literature as simply his own thesis rediscovered under new names, or would he insist on some genuine distinction between an institution gaming a targeted statistic and a trained policy network gaming a numerical reward function? And given his own doubts about whether a purpose can ever be specified with the completeness he thought necessary, would he view today's proposed remedies -- reward shaping, human oversight, learning objectives from feedback instead of writing them down in advance -- as real solutions, or would he conclude, in keeping with his own darker instincts, that the gap between the letter and the spirit of a goal is a permanent hazard of building anything that acts faster than we can think? Please commit to a specific reading of Wiener rather than simply cataloguing considerations on both sides.
