"The sorites paradox trades on the plausibility of a tolerance principle — e.g., that a difference of a single grain cannot turn a heap into a non-heap — together with the fact that repeated tiny differences can add up to a large one. Epistemicism and supervaluationism both try to block the paradoxical conclusion while explaining why the tolerance principle seemed compelling in the first place, but they do so in very different ways.

Epistemicists, most notably Timothy Williamson, hold that vague predicates like 'heap' or 'tall' really do have sharp cutoffs: there is some exact number of grains that separates heaps from non-heaps, we simply cannot know which number it is. On this view classical logic is left completely untouched — every sentence, including borderline ones, is true or false, so bivalence holds throughout. Williamson explains our ignorance by appeal to a margin-for-error requirement on knowledge: because a slightly different pattern of usage could easily have made 'heap' apply to a slightly different range of cases, no belief about any candidate cutoff is safe enough to count as knowledge. In effect, we are in the position of someone trying to read a sharp boundary off a ruler that is blurred by the limits of our measuring instruments: the fact is there, but our epistemic access to it is too coarse-grained to ever pin it down.

Supervaluationists — Kit Fine and Rosanna Keefe are the classic references — deny that there is any such fact to be ignorant of in the first place. Instead, 'heap' is associated with a range of admissible ways of making it precise (precisifications), constrained by penumbral connections (e.g., whatever the cutoff, adding one grain can't turn a heap into a non-heap). A sentence counts as true if it comes out true on every admissible precisification, false if it comes out false on all of them, and neither true nor false in between; borderline heaps are exactly the cases where precisifications disagree. Because bivalence fails for these borderline sentences, classical validities such as 'this is a heap or this is not a heap' can no longer be assumed to hold once we allow for borderline cases — this loosening of classical logic is the main price supervaluationists pay in exchange for keeping the paradox at bay.

Both views face the further challenge of higher-order vagueness: it seems just as hard to say exactly where the borderline cases begin as it is to say where 'heap' itself begins. Here epistemicism arguably has the tidier answer, since it can simply say that 'borderline heap' is itself governed by a further sharp-but-unknowable cutoff, and iterate the same margin-for-error story at every level. Supervaluationism, needing a further round of precisification to say which precisifications themselves count as admissible, seems to invite a regress that its critics have pressed hard."
