I am two years into a doctorate on Henry Sidgwick's ethics, and until this winter I treated his thirty-year involvement with the Society for Psychical Research as an embarrassing footnote best kept out of my thesis. That changed when my mother died in January. Rereading the concluding chapter of The Methods of Ethics not long afterward, where Sidgwick admits that the "dualism of practical reason" between self-interest and duty may leave ethics without any ultimate rational sanction unless something guarantees that virtue and happiness coincide, I finally understood why a thinker so allergic to wishful thinking spent decades sitting in on mediums' séances: he needed the universe to be arranged a certain way, and he refused simply to assume that it was.

Write a short dialogue, set at a Society for Psychical Research sitting sometime in the 1880s or 1890s, between Sidgwick and a skeptical fellow investigator on the Society's council — someone with just as little patience for charlatans as he has, but who thinks the whole enterprise rests on a confusion, since no finding about mediums, telepathy, or survival of death could do the rational work Sidgwick needs it to do in closing the gap between egoism and duty. Let Sidgwick offer the strongest version of his own case in reply. Neither figure should be easy to refute, and each should respond to what the other has actually just said rather than simply restate an opening position.
