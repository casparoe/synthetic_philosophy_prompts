I teach a graduate seminar that pairs canonical texts in the history of economic thought with the moral philosophy their authors absorbed as students, and this term's session on Keynes produced more disagreement among the students than I had anticipated. Everyone had read "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren" (1930) in the usual textbook way, as a forecast that within a century economic growth would solve what Keynes calls "the economic problem," leaving humanity to face the harder task of deciding how to use its new abundance of leisure well. Fewer of them had also read "My Early Beliefs" (1938), Keynes's memoir of his years among the Cambridge Apostles, and setting the two texts side by side raised a question I would like your help thinking through.

In the memoir, Keynes traces his generation's outlook to a rather selective reading of G. E. Moore's "Principia Ethica": he and his friends took from Moore's concluding chapter the doctrine that only certain refined states of mind -- love and personal affection, the contemplation of beauty, perhaps the disinterested pursuit of knowledge -- are good in themselves, while largely setting aside Moore's own, more cautious chapter on practical conduct, which had counselled deference to conventional rules on the grounds that individuals are poor judges of the remote consequences of their own acts. Keynes describes the result as closer to a religion than to a morality, one that licensed trusting one's own refined intuitions case by case rather than following general rules, and he even half-jokingly calls their youthful outlook a kind of immoralism. That same background seems to me to shape the 1930 essay's account of what people should do with their coming leisure, along with its rather elitist worry that most people, lacking such cultivated tastes, would need to go on practising the old habits of thrift and purposive money-making for a while yet, since only a refined few were presently fit to enjoy freedom from economic necessity and "to live wisely and agreeably and well." Yet the same memoir also finds Keynes looking back ruefully on that Moorean creed, suggesting that he and his friends had badly underrated how much orderly, civilised conduct actually depends on inherited custom and convention rather than on the unaided good judgment of enlightened individuals.

Could you explain how far the good life sketched in the 1930 essay really is Moore's ideal of intrinsically valuable states of mind transposed into a forecast about capital accumulation and working hours, and how far it quietly departs from that ideal -- particularly in its assumption that only some people are yet fit for the freedom it anticipates? And could you say something about whether Keynes's later, more chastened assessment of the Bloomsbury ethic in "My Early Beliefs" should make us doubt the 1930 essay's confidence that a liberated humanity will gradually learn to live well, or whether the two texts can instead be read as consistent?
