I am midway through a novel built around a son who declines to attend his estranged father's funeral, and over dinner I described the scene to two old friends, neither of them a philosopher, to gauge how they would react. Instead of discussing my plot, they spent the rest of the evening arguing about the question underneath it, and I have not been able to settle who had the better of the exchange.

(1) Ines maintained that Julian had no reason to attend: he had not spoken to his father in twenty years, felt nothing for him, and attending would have served nothing he cared about, so "no reason" was simply the accurate description.

(2) Callum objected that this gets things backwards: being someone's son gives you a reason to attend his funeral whether or not you feel like it, otherwise a father who has stopped loving his children would have no reason to feed them.

(3) Ines replied that a reason must connect, however loosely, to something the agent actually cares about, or it is not a reason for him, merely a fact that matters to other people; tell a man who feels nothing for his father that "he is still your father," and if nothing in him could ever be moved by that, you have told him no more than you would have told a lamppost.

(4) Callum answered that this proves too much, since people are routinely mistaken about their own reasons; we do not say that a man who has lost every wish to go on living therefore has no reason to live.

(5) Ines granted that this case was different, since there is a near-guaranteed route back to caring about one's own survival, given time or treatment, and denied that any comparable route exists for Julian, whose indifference to his father she took to be settled and considered rather than a malfunction of anything.

(6) Callum replied that this only relocates the disagreement rather than resolving it: Ines still owes an argument for why "would this attitude survive correction" is the whole story about what a reason is, since an obligation to one's father might apply to Julian whether or not any correction would ever touch him.

Could you work through their six moves in turn and say, for each, who has the better of it and why, rather than simply declaring an overall winner? I would also like to know whether Ines and Callum are each defending a genuinely tenable position, or whether one of them has gone wrong somewhere, and whether there is a consideration that neither has thought to raise. Please close with a one-line verdict in exactly this form: Verdict: Ines, Verdict: Callum, or Verdict: neither.
