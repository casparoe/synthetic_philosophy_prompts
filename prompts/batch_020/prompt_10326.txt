Before I was a science journalist, I spent three years as a graduate student under Professor Naomi Kessler, who is retiring next month after thirty-five years of working on one deceptively simple object: the centipede game. Robert Rosenthal introduced it in 1981, almost as a throwaway example inside a paper about something else entirely (predatory pricing and the "chain store paradox"); Ken Binmore gave it its now-familiar name several years later, because the branching diagram of a long version of the game resembles a many-legged insect. Naomi built her career on the gap the game exposes: backward induction says that two players who are rational, and know each other to be rational, should end the game on the very first move and settle for a small guaranteed sum rather than risk passing — yet in the lab (in the tradition of the experiments McKelvey and Palfrey ran in the early nineties), hardly anyone actually does that; people keep passing, round after round, well past the point where the theory says they should have cashed out. For thirty years she has argued, against colleagues who think Aumann settled the question in 1995, that this is not merely a fact about bounded human rationality but a real fault line in what "common knowledge of rationality" can coherently mean once a game has already run on longer than that assumption permits — the objection Pettit and Sugden are famous for pressing.

Her department has asked me to give the toast at her retirement dinner, and I know the room will split cleanly down the middle: half will be current and former students and fellow theorists who will catch every reference, half will be her husband, her children, and old friends who have heard her mention "the centipede" at every family gathering since the Reagan administration without once being told what it means. I would like you to write that toast for me — about five minutes, meant to be read aloud — built around the obvious parallel between the game and a life that has, by her own theory's lights, been spent playing "irrationally": never cashing out early, always passing the trust on to the next round, the next student. Please don't let that be the whole joke, though, because she would object to it on the merits: somewhere in there, work in the wrinkle that reaching a later round of a game is itself evidence against the very common-knowledge-of-rationality assumption the backward-induction argument needs — so that a thirty-five-year marriage and a career's worth of advising look less like a standing violation of her own theory than like a running argument with it. And don't reach for "irrational" as a compliment unless you've earned it; she'll notice.
