I spent my working life as a physicist and have only turned to philosophy in retirement. I've lately been reading Moritz Schlick's 1927 essay on the meaning of life, with some private amusement, since he himself trained as a physicist under Planck before turning to philosophy. His central argument is that a life devoted entirely to goal-directed "work" can never be meaningful, since each goal, once reached, is either momentary or becomes merely the means to some further goal, so that value is endlessly deferred. He locates real meaning instead in "play": activity valuable in itself, needing no external justification. He treats the scientist or artist absorbed in a task for its own sake as the ideal fusion of achievement and playfulness, and is correspondingly scathing about the modern habit of treating leisure as mere recuperation for more work.

Schlick died in 1936, well before today's vogue for "gamification" -- attaching points, streaks, badges, and leaderboards to work tasks, exercise routines, and language-learning apps that remain, at bottom, means to further ends. Given the specifics of his argument, would he most plausibly judge gamification a genuine recovery of play within an otherwise instrumentalized modern life, or a counterfeit that dresses the same old chain of purposes in playful clothing without ever escaping it? Please reconstruct the reasoning he would most likely have offered, rather than simply a verdict on whether gamification is "fun."
