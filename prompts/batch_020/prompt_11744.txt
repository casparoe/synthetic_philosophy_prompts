and here's the response, copied exactly, ~300 words:

"Whether neural networks really have representations comes down to whether the features interpretability work finds were discovered or just projected onto the network after the fact. The strongest case for realism starts from superposition: because a network typically needs to track far more concepts than it has neurons, it's forced to become polysemantic, packing many unrelated concepts into each neuron by encoding them as near-orthogonal directions in activation space instead of giving each concept its own neuron. This is exactly why sparse autoencoders work: by unfolding these superposed directions, they've recovered clean, human-interpretable features (a Golden Gate Bridge feature, a sycophancy feature, features for specific people) that were invisible at the level of individual neurons. If these features were purely observer-relative, a method with no access to our concepts shouldn't be able to recover them so reliably.

The probing literature points the same way: you can train a simple linear classifier on a layer's activations to decode a concept like sentiment with high accuracy, and because a linear probe is enough, that's usually read as evidence the network already represents the concept explicitly in its own geometry, rather than the concept merely being recoverable in principle by some arbitrarily powerful decoder. So both superposition/SAEs and probing point toward realism.

Philosophically, this is basically a vindication of Dennett's realism about patterns. In "Real Patterns" he argues intentional states are just as real as any pattern a good enough predictor can't ignore -- which is the same full-blooded realism about representation that Fodor wanted for the language of thought, just arrived at from a different direction. So a model isn't merely behaving as if it has a concept of the Golden Gate Bridge; on Dennett's view it actually has one, same way interpretability people now casually talk about models "knowing" things."
