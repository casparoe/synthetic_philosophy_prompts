Two friends of mine got into a running argument while helping a third friend split a modular sectional sofa into its two stand-alone loveseats and an ottoman, so the pieces could be used in different rooms of a new, smaller apartment. The question that derailed the afternoon was whether the original sofa had ever really been "one object" to begin with, as opposed to three pieces of furniture that merely happened to be sold and used together. (1) Mara said obviously it had: it was bought, delivered, and sat on as a single unit for years, and that's all "being one object" could reasonably require. (2) Leo disagreed: since the pieces work perfectly well apart, there was never a further thing, "the sofa," over and above them -- just two loveseats and an ottoman, temporarily pushed into a sofa-shaped arrangement. (3) Mara replied that this proves too much: a chair is equally "nothing over and above" a seat, legs, and a back arranged a certain way, and chairs obviously exist; if mere arrangement never suffices for existence, nothing does, not even Leo's own body. (4) Leo answered that a chair's seat isn't a seat of anything once removed from its legs, whereas each loveseat is fully itself when detached -- so it's separability, not arrangement as such, that settles the matter, and the sofa has it while the chair doesn't. (5) Mara said separability can't be the test either: pull a drawer out of a dresser and it still holds your socks just fine sitting on the floor, and nobody concludes from this that the assembled dresser was never really one thing. (6) Leo then suggested that they had simply been using "one object" in two different senses all along -- a strict sense on which only inseparable arrangements count, and a loose sense tracking how things are bought and used -- so there was nothing left to argue about. Mara called this a dodge: either the sofa had been one object or it hadn't, and the truth about that couldn't depend on which of them was talking.
