Lately I've been stuck on this thought loop about fate and effort that hits me mostly on my commute. Basically: if everything that happens was already going to happen no matter what, because of God's plan or just physics and cause-and-effect going back to the Big Bang, does it actually matter if I try hard at stuff, like my job, or the gym, or patching things up with my brother, or is the trying itself just another thing that was always going to happen anyway, with no real weight to it?

I brought this up over dinner with a friend who reads alot of old philosophy, and she said this exact worry is really old news, that the Greeks apparently had something called the "idle argument," with the classic example being why bother calling a doctor if whether you recover is already decided either way. She said Leibniz, the "best of all possible worlds" guy, took it seriously enough to answer it in his Theodicy, calling it the "lazy sophism," and that his comeback is basically that the outcome isn't fated on its own, separate from your effort, its fated together with your effort, so the trying is baked into how the fixed result happens rather than being some pointless extra layered on top. She also mentioned him separating out things that are necessary the way 2+2=4 is necessary from things that are only necessary given everything that came before, but I'll be honest that part went a bit over my head.

What I can't tell is whether that's actually solving the worry or just relabeling it so it stops bugging you. Could you break down what you think are the most important implications of Leibniz's answer to the lazy sophism thing, specifically for someone like me trying to figure out whether it's rational to keep putting in real effort on stuff if everything is determined one way or another anyway? Basically, does his answer give an actual reason to keep caring and trying, and if so what is it, or does it just dress the problem up in fancier language?
