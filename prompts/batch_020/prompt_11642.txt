I host a podcast on science and ideas, and next week's guest is a philosopher known for defending the view that the suffering wild animals endure from starvation, parasitism, disease, and predation is severe and widespread enough that we have at least a prima facie duty to intervene in nature to reduce it -- through measures such as immunocontraception for prey species or active management of predator numbers, not merely a duty to leave wild animals alone. When pressed on the more radical-sounding implications of this in his published work, he tends to retreat to some version of "that's a question for future research, not something anyone is proposing we actually do." Please draft eight to ten interview questions that make that retreat progressively harder to lean on. Among them, I'd like at least one question on the scope problem: why the duty stops at vertebrates when invertebrates -- whose sentience is far more uncertain -- outnumber them by many orders of magnitude and mostly die within days of hatching; one on the tractability problem, given how often well-intentioned ecological interventions have backfired (cane toads, myxomatosis); and one on whether the view's logic implies that it would sometimes be better for a wild population not to exist at all. For each of those three, add a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give, and close with a question about what finding would make him abandon the research program rather than merely defer it.
