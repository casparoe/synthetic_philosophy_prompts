I convene a small fortnightly reading group for our department's graduate students working in formal epistemology, and the most recent session, nominally on the Dutch Book argument for probabilism, ended in a disagreement between two of my strongest students that neither has let go of since. (1) Farah opened by defending the textbook case: an agent who assigns credence 0.6 to rain tomorrow and 0.6 to no rain as well can be sold a bet on each proposition at sixty cents on the dollar, paying a guaranteed $1.20 for a guaranteed $1.00 return whatever the weather does. Since the Converse Dutch Book Theorem shows that no such book can ever be built against credences that already satisfy the probability axioms, she took the pair of results together to show exactly that coherence is what separates the exploitable from the invulnerable. (2) Lucas objected that this shows less than advertised, since the sure loss only follows given two further and independently deniable assumptions: that the agent's credence just is the price at which she'll buy or sell the bet, which is a substantive operationalist theory of belief inherited from Ramsey and de Finetti rather than a neutral description, and that being willing to accept each bet on its own somehow commits her to accepting the whole pair at once, which doesn't obviously follow from either bet being fair in isolation.

(3) Farah answered that both worries can be routed around: run the argument over the agent's preferences among bets instead of stipulating up front that credence equals betting price, and coherence falls out as a theorem given only minimal consistency conditions on preference, in the spirit of Ramsey's own representation-theorem argument. (4) Lucas replied that this concedes the interesting part of his objection rather than answering it: once probabilities are being extracted from a preference ordering via a representation theorem, the work of justifying coherence is being done by whatever consistency axioms are imposed on preference, and the whole picture of bookies and sure losses becomes illustrative rather than load-bearing — at which point one might as well argue for coherence directly from considerations of accuracy, the way epistemic utility theorists like Jim Joyce do, without any mention of bets or money at all, which is a different kind of argument, not a rescue of this one. (5) Farah held her ground: the existence of some other, tidier argument for the same conclusion doesn't show that the original betting argument fails by its own lights, and refusing to guarantee yourself a loss looks like about as minimal a demand of instrumental rationality as one could make. (6) Lucas's closing shot was that this minimalism gets much shakier once you move from a single moment to updating over time — a diachronic Dutch book against a fixed updating policy seems to exploit the fact that the bookie can predict what the agent will believe later, not any defect in what she believes now, so an agent who conditionalizes impeccably could in principle be booked too, provided her policy is known in advance.

Neither of them thought they had lost by the time the room had to be cleared for the next class, and I confess I have gone back and forth myself while thinking it over since. Could you take the six numbered moves in order and say, for each, who has the better of the specific point being contested there — not just render an overall verdict — and flag anywhere that one of them is leaning on a premise the other should have pushed on but didn't? I would also value your sense of whether, once the dust settles, anything remains standing of the original claim that probabilistic coherence is a requirement of rationality, or whether Farah and Lucas have unwittingly argued their way into abandoning it.
