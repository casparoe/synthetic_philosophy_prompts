I'm assembling a collection of short fiction in the tradition of Ted Chiang and Greg Egan, where a story's plot grows directly out of a single piece of technical or philosophical machinery rather than serving as its backdrop, and for the next piece I want to dramatize a bargaining pathology I first heard about from a friend in AI safety research and haven't been able to stop turning over since: the "commitment race." As I understand the argument, it starts from the old Schelling insight that in a standoff, whoever can credibly and irreversibly bind themselves to an uncompromising position first tends to win, because they have destroyed their own capacity to back down and so force the concession onto the other side — the general who burns the bridge behind his own army, the driver who visibly rips the steering wheel out in a game of chicken. The trouble starts once both sides know this and are also capable of binding themselves almost instantly: each then has a reason to lock in a position before it has actually finished reasoning about which position is wise, purely so as not to be the slower party who gets exploited for having deliberated. Push this to the limit — especially for parties fast and legible enough to partially predict one another, so that bluffing stops working — and you get a race to foreclose your own future options as quickly as possible, which is exactly the kind of process liable to produce two locked-in, mutually incompatible threats that neither side can walk back, and that neither would ever have chosen if it had had the leisure to think first.

Could you write me a short story, somewhere around 1,500 words, that earns its ending from this specific mechanism rather than from generic AI-takeover or nuclear-brinkmanship atmospherics? I don't mind what form the two sides take — rival AI systems, corporations, nations, factions within one divided mind, even a divorcing couple whose lawyers can bind them to terms faster than either spouse can actually think a decision through — so long as the story's central turn is the moment some party chooses to commit before it is ready, and renders that choice legible to the reader as the locally rational response to knowing that whoever hesitates loses. What I want the aftermath to capture is the quieter, specific horror of this failure mode: not that a plan went wrong, but that everyone involved reasoned their way, quite lucidly and step by step, into a catastrophe that unhurried thought would have avoided — if only unhurried thought had been a luxury either side could afford.
