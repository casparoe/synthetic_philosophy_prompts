So I've fallen down a bit of an AI safety rabbit hole this week (I'm a backend/infra engineer, nothing to do with ML professionally), and something about the "shutdown problem" / corrigibility stuff that MIRI-adjacent people talk about is bugging me from a systems design angle. In basically every production system I've worked on, if you need a guaranteed way to kill a process regardless of what that process "wants" to do, you don't try to convince the process to want to die, you just put the kill mechanism somewhere it can never touch, informationally or physically. Think a watchdog timer, or an out-of-band management controller like IPMI sitting on a totally seperate network that the main OS doesn't even know exists. So why can't we just do the equivalent thing for an AI? Air-gap the shutdown button completely from the agent's sensors and actuators so there's literally zero causal pathway for it to affect whether the button gets pressed, and boom, corrigibility solved, no fancy utility function engineering required.

I brought this up with a friend who reads a lot of the LessWrong stuff, and he said this "doesn't actually solve corrigibility, it just relocates the problem," which honestly sounded like a bit of a cop out to me. If the agent genuinely has no physical or digital channel to influence the button, what's even left to worry about? Is there an actual technical reason a plain old security-engineering solution like this fails here, or are the alignment people just re-describing the watchdog-process idea with extra math and calling it a hard open problem?
