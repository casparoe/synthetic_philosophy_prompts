Student's answer: In the *Methods of Ethics*, Sidgwick distinguishes three methods for determining right conduct — egoism, intuitionism, and utilitarianism — and argues that a fully rational ethics requires these to agree with one another. Most apparent conflicts can be resolved, but Sidgwick admits one residual conflict, the dualism of practical reason, which arises between intuitionism and utilitarianism: commonsense moral intuition directs the agent toward certain duties independently of consequences, while the utilitarian calculus directs the agent to maximize aggregate happiness, and Sidgwick can find no argument that forces the intuitionist to accept the utilitarian's verdict whenever the two conflict. Since pure philosophical argument could not close this gap, Sidgwick hoped empirical inquiry might: if it could be shown that the universe is morally governed, e.g. that virtue is rewarded in a future life, then the demands of reason would converge after all. This is why he helped found the Society for Psychical Research in 1882, became its first president, and co-authored, with Gurney and Myers, its early flagship study *Phantasms of the Living*. In the end, though, Sidgwick remained privately disappointed, never finding evidence he considered strong enough to settle the question of survival.
