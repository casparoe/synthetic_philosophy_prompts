At a climate-economics workshop I was covering recently, two of the invited speakers -- an environmental economist who builds the kind of integrated models used to price carbon, and a philosopher who works on obligations to future generations -- ended up arguing well past the point when everyone else had left the reception. (1) He opened by insisting that any positive "pure" rate of time preference -- discounting a future person's well-being simply because it arrives later, apart from risk or economic growth -- is indefensible, since it treats a benefit's date as a reason to count it for less, exactly as discounting it for happening somewhere else would be. (2) She said that's too quick: most real-world discounting exists because the future is uncertain -- the people meant to benefit, or the civilization meant to deliver the benefit, might simply not be there -- and weighing a less certain good less heavily isn't arbitrary at all. (3) He agreed uncertainty is a legitimate reason to discount, but said it isn't the reason on trial: in the models he builds, catastrophe risk and the fact that richer future generations get less marginal benefit from a dollar are already their own separate terms, and the "pure" rate is defined as whatever discounting is left over after those are subtracted out -- so defending discounting-for-risk doesn't defend a positive pure rate. (4) She switched tack: even a pure rate fixed at exactly zero is untenable, because over a long enough horizon it implies the present must always yield to the future, which is vastly larger -- in effect demanding something close to total, indefinite sacrifice now. (5) He replied that the demandingness problem is real but gets solved by the terms she'd already granted him -- the diminishing value of a dollar to richer descendants, plus the fact that our own claims don't disappear just because a ledger says they're outweighed -- so reaching for pure time preference to fix it is solving the wrong equation.

Neither of them budged, and both had clearly thought about this longer than I had. Could you go through the exchange move by move -- not just an overall winner -- and say who has the better of it at each step, plus anything you think both of them are missing? Then tell me plainly: is a positive pure rate of time preference ever ethically defensible, or does every attempt to justify one turn out, on inspection, to be a different kind of discounting wearing its name?
