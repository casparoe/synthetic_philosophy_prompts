I spent about thirty-five years doing experimental optics before I retired, and these days I fill a lot of the extra hours reading my way through old philosophy of science texts, which is a totally new world for me. Right now I'm chewing on a bit of Poincaré's *Science and Hypothesis* (1902) that I keep running into whenever I read secondary stuff on "structural realism" — apparently this exact passage gets treated as one of the founding documents of the whole position. It's from the part of the book where he's discussing how Fresnel's elastic-solid theory of light got superseded by Maxwell's electromagnetic theory, which, as an old optics guy, hits kind of close to home — I was trained on Maxwell and never gave poor Fresnel's ether a second thought. He writes:

"...these equations express relations, and if the equations remain true, it is because the relations preserve their reality. They teach us now, as they did then, that there is such and such a relation between this thing and that; only, the something which we then called motion, we now call electric current. But these are merely the names of the images we substituted for the real objects which Nature will hide for ever from our eyes. The true relations between these real objects are the only reality we can attain, and the sole condition is that the same relations shall hold between these objects as between the images we are forced to put in their place."

(forgive the stilted phrasing, its an old translation). Here's what's tripping me up. Read one way, this sounds like Poincaré thinks there's still a "real object" back there behind the relations, doing the relating, that we're just permanently shut out from knowing directly — which is basically the "epistemic" version of structural realism I've seen attributed to Worrall. But I've also seen this exact same paragraph cited as the founding text for a much more radical, "ontic" version of the view, where there's no hidden thing wearing the relations like a coat at all — relations and structure are simply all there is, no further fact about "the real objects" underneath. Those don't sound like the same claim to me. So which is it: does Poincaré's actual wording here favor one reading over the other, or is he just being genuinely ambiguous (or sloppy) on this point, such that later writers are mostly reading their own preferred view back into him? I'd like you to actually dig into the specific phrases rather than just tell me smart people disagree about it.
