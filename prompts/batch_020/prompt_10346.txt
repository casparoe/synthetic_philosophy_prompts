I co-run a Friday reading group with a few of my decision theory and formal epistemology grad students, and this week's paper was on what's sometimes called the "commitment races problem" -- the worry that if agents can bind themselves in advance to strategies (threats, promises, policies they can't later revise), whoever commits earliest and most irrevocably tends to control the interaction, so everyone has an incentive to commit before the other guy does, even before they've worked out whether the thing they're commiting to is actually good for them. Two of my students, Marcus and Dana, got into it over drinks afterward and I ended up just listening instead of refereeing.

Marcus opened by saying the whole "problem" is just Schelling's old point about the value of commitment dressed up in scarier language: if binding yourself first gets you the better split, a rational agent should grab that advantage, full stop, and calling it a "race" we ought to worry about is alarmism about perfectly ordinary strategic behavior. Dana said that's fair when you already know the game and your own aims, but the interesting cases are ones under uncertainty -- you don't yet know your opponent, the situation, or even your own settled values -- so committing early trades a sure loss of flexibility for a speculative gain, and the "race" language is earned because each side's fear of being pre-empted pushes everyone to commit faster than any of them would think wise if they could just coordinate on slowing down. Marcus came back that this is still just a garden-variety arms-race problem, and those don't show any individual commitment is irrational: holding the others' strategies fixed, committing is still the individually best move, and Dana hadn't actually shown otherwise. Dana replied that she could: the instability isn't only about who lands the good equilibrium, it's that the race can undermine even a single agent acting alone, since being rewarded for committing early creates pressure to commit before you've done the reasoning needed to know whether the commitment is wise -- and "commit only after enough reflection" is no fix, since a sharp enough opponent can just exploit the gap while you're still reflecting. Marcus's last move was that this only shows you should commit early to the *right* higher-order policy -- something like binding yourself now to whatever your fully-reflected self would endorse -- which dissolves the supposed paradox rather than proving commitment itself is dangerous. Dana wasn't having it: picking the right meta-policy is itself a choice made under the same time pressure and the same risk of being out-committed by someone cruder, so Marcus has just relocated the race one level up, not escaped it.

Neither of them would concede the other had landed a real blow, and they tried to rope me into settling it, which I ducked at the time. Anyway, could you go through this turn by turn and say who's got the better of each specific exchange (feel free to say "both, partly" where that's the honest answer), and then, standing back from the individual moves, tell me whether you think there's a genuine unresolved problem here or whether Marcus's last move actually defuses it -- and what, if anything, you think both of them are missing?
