I don't have any background in philosophy, but a disagreement with a friend sent me down a rabbit hole a few months ago. We were arguing about whether calling a glass "fragile" tells you something real about the glass, or whether it's just a compact way of describing how the glass tends to behave, and that argument eventually led me to an encyclopedia article on dispositions. That's where I first ran into the idea that philosophers don't think you can simply define a disposition as "the object would do M if condition C occurred," because there are supposedly two different kinds of counterexample to that idea, called "finks" and "masks." I can follow the individual stories well enough — something about a wire hooked up to a device that cuts the current the instant someone touches it, so it never actually shocks anyone even though the wire is live, and something else about a poison that's still a poison even after you've swallowed an antidote that stops it from killing you — but on my own I can't pin down what actually makes these two different problems, rather than just two dressed-up versions of the same complaint that "the thing didn't do what it was supposedly going to do."

What I'd really like is an explanation of the underlying logical or causal structure that separates finks from masks, rather than another retelling of the two stories, since the stories themselves are exactly what's been tripping me up. I gather that David Lewis tried to repair the simple definition specifically to deal with finks — something about requiring the object to hang onto whatever inner property causes the behavior for at least a brief moment after the trigger occurs — and I'd like to understand, as concretely as possible, why that repair is generally thought to leave masking untouched. Is there something structural about masks that makes them immune to that particular fix, or is it more that Lewis's fix was never really aimed at problems like theirs in the first place? And if masking really is a separate, still-unresolved problem, I'd be curious whether that's taken as a reason to give up on defining dispositions through "would/if" conditionals altogether, or whether people think some further patch could still rescue the approach.
