A while back I went down a youtube rabbit hole on medieval philosophy and stumbled on this thing called the "flying man" (sometimes "floating man") argument from Avicenna, this old Persian philosopher-doctor guy. The setup is honestly kind of wild: picture a fully grown, fully rational man who pops into existence in a single instant, suspended in mid air or in a void, blindfolded, with his arms and legs spread apart so they can't even touch each other or anything else - basically zero sensory input of any kind, not even feedback from his own body. Avicenna's claim is that even in that state, with literally nothing to go on, the guy would still be completely certain that HE exists, his "self," even while having no clue whether he has arms, organs, a body at all. And from that he concludes the self (or soul) has to be a totally seperate thing from the body, since being aware of one apparently doesn't require being aware of the other at all.

I brought this up with my friends over drinks and we couldn't stop arguing about the one part none of the youtube videos really dealt with: doesn't this floating guy already need a fully working, language-having, concept-using mind just to even register that "oh, I exist" thought in the first place? And isn't that exact kind of mind, as far as we can tell from actual humans (kids raised with almost no human contact, people born deaf-blind, etc), something that only ever develops THROUGH having a body and senses bumping into the world for years? So it kind of feels like the thought experiment is smuggling in a bunch of embodied stuff through the back door while pretending to strip all of it away.

Rather then us just going around in circles about it again, could you turn this into an actual short story? Put some mind into something close to the flying man's position - doesn't have to be a human, could be anything that thinks - in a grounded setting rather than a sci-fi cliche if you can help it, and just let the story sit inside that moment of "I am, but I have no idea what I am" long enough for us to actually feel whether Avicenna's conclusion holds up or quietly falls apart. Please make it a real story with characters and stakes though, not an essay wearing a story costume - that's the part we can't get just from arguing about it ourselves.
