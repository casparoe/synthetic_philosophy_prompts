I'm taking a graduate seminar this term that sits at the intersection of formal epistemology and machine learning, and this week's unit was devoted to inverse reinforcement learning (IRL): the project of inferring an agent's reward function purely from its observed behavior. Our instructor walked us through two results that left the room a little uneasy. First, in the original Ng and Russell formulation, the problem is badly underdetermined, because a constant reward function -- including the reward that is zero everywhere -- makes every conceivable policy trivially optimal; so "this agent has no preferences at all" is always among the reward functions consistent with any behavior whatsoever, however purposeful that behavior looks from the outside. Second, and more unsettling, Armstrong and Mindermann later showed that even after you rule out such degenerate solutions, you still cannot uniquely split behavior into "what the agent wants" (its reward function) and "how competently or irrationally it pursues that want" (its planner): for essentially any reward function you might propose, there exists some model of bounded rationality that would have generated exactly the observed behavior, and no amount of Occam's-razor-style simplicity weighting on the decomposition rescues uniqueness.

A classmate joked afterward that this is just philosophical behaviorism's old underdetermination-of-the-mental problem, dressed up in Bellman equations -- which reminded several of us of the tired line about two behaviorists who, after intimacy, ask each other, "that was good for you; how was it for me?" I'd like something sharper and more specific to IRL itself. Could you write me a short, original joke -- just a few sentences, with a clear setup and punchline -- where the humor turns specifically on one of these two results (the reward/planner non-identifiability finding, or the zero-reward degeneracy, whichever you can make funnier), rather than being a generic joke about robots or AI that merely name-drops "inverse reinforcement learning"? I want to be confident that a classmate who has actually read the Armstrong-Mindermann paper would get exactly why it's funny, so please don't blur or oversimplify the underlying idea just to make the joke land.
