So I spent my working life as an experimental physicist doing a lot of Bayesian analysis on messy detector data, and now that I'm retired I've fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole -- currently decision theory. I recently ran into I.J. Good's old theorem that a rational Bayesian should always want a free look at more evidence before acting, since it can only raise your expected payoff, never lower it, so long as you actually update on it properly.

That's turned out to be more than academic for me: I sit on my hospital's ethics committee, and we're rewriting the policy on predictive testing for Huntington's and other untreatable, late-onset genetic conditions. Counselling here has always protected a strong "right not to know" -- people can request the test and still back out of learning the result at the very last step -- but two of the younger physicians want to flip the default to automatic disclosure, and their argument is essentially Good's theorem in clinical dress: it's your own genome, its free to tell you, so declining only makes sense if your being irrational. I'm not convinced, but I can't quite pin down where that reasoning actually breaks down for real patients, including relatives who get implicated by someone else's result without ever asking to be. Could you draft the memo I'll bring to Thursday's meeting -- a concrete recommendation on the disclosure default and how much opt-out we allow and when, the reasoning behind it, and a genuinely fair hearing for the disclosure-by-default view rather than a strawman?
