Each year I open my seminar on rational choice by running a version of Martin Shubik's dollar auction: instead of a single dollar, I auction off a ten-dollar bill, under the rule that whoever bids highest wins the bill, but both the highest and the second-highest bidder must pay whatever they last bid. As usual, most of the room drops out early, and it comes down to two holdouts who keep topping each other by a dollar well past the point where winning the bill would still leave either of them ahead. This year the two finalists took it to nineteen dollars before one of them finally quit, and afterward two of my PhD students who'd been watching got into a genuinely good argument about how to explain what they'd just seen. I'd like your help adjudicating it, since I honestly can't decide who has the better of it.

Marcus's position was that neither finalist behaved irrationally once the escalation was underway: if you're the second-highest bidder, refusing to raise locks in the total loss of your current bid, while raising by another dollar only risks a slightly larger loss in exchange for a real chance the other person quits first. So if a mistake was made, he said, it was made with the very first bid, not with any of the raises that followed -- each of those was simply the correct response to the one before it. Sofia objected that this can't be the whole story, because both bidders can run exactly that argument at every single round; a policy that looks locally sound at each step but that both players can see, in advance, is guaranteed to leave them worse off than stopping early isn't rescued just because no individual step violates "ignore sunk costs, weigh only the margin." Marcus replied that this doesn't show the marginal reasoning is defective mid-auction, only that the players should have bound themselves to a ceiling before ever entering -- a fact about the decision to play, not about how they reasoned once they were in. Sofia pushed back that this concession gives away her point: if avoiding disaster requires treating an earlier resolution as binding even when the local incentives point the other way, then "only weigh the margin" cannot be an adequate rule for choice over time, full stop. Marcus's closing move was that real bidders are obviously driven by pride and a refusal to look weak in front of the room, so perhaps this is really a story about ego rather than rationality -- to which Sofia answered that his own opening argument had already supplied a purely self-interested, ego-free reason to keep raising, so ego can't be doing the explanatory work he now wants it to do.

Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better argument at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also be curious whether you think there's some further consideration that both of them are missing.
