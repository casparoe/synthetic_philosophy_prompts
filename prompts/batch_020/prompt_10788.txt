A friend of mine, who is finishing a doctorate in modal logic, and I spent most of a long train journey arguing about Gödel's ontological argument — not the folk-philosophy caricature that gets mocked online, but the actual axiomatic proof that Dana Scott reconstructed from Gödel's notes, with its primitive notion of "positive" properties, its axiom on their closure under necessary implication, and its definitions of essence and necessary existence. She kept pressing what she called the "collapse" problem: Jordan Howard Sobel's result that Gödel's own axioms, taken together, entail that every truth is a necessary truth, so that the modal distinction the whole proof trades on — contingent versus necessary existence — turns out to be empty. On her view, this isn't an awkward side effect to be patched but a reductio of the entire axiom set, and she was dismissive of moves like Anthony Anderson's emendation, which revises the definitions of "God-like" and "necessary existence" precisely so as to block the collapse; she thinks any such fix either reintroduces collapse by another route, or else survives only by defanging the axioms so much that the proof no longer earns a theologically interesting conclusion.

I wasn't able to mount a good defense on the spot, and we never settled the question before she got off at her stop. I'd like you to do what I couldn't: make the strongest possible case that a principled emendation of Gödel's axioms — in Anderson's spirit, or one of your own devising — can block modal collapse while still yielding an argument that is recognizably Gödel's, using broadly the same apparatus (positive properties, essence, necessary existence, and the S5 principle that whatever is possibly necessary is necessary) to conclude that a God-like being necessarily exists. Please show concretely where the standard collapse derivation exploits the resources of the original axioms, identify exactly which axiom or definition your emendation alters and why that particular change blocks the derivation at that step, and confront directly the suspicion that any adequate fix is bound to be ad hoc or to purchase consistency at the cost of the argument's theological point.
