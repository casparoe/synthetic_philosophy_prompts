I've been reading LessWrong and its offshoots since roughly the Overcoming Bias days, and a few of us from an old rationalist-adjacent reading group have spent an embarrassing number of hours arguing about how seriously to take the parallel between Yudkowsky's Coherent Extrapolated Volition and the older philosophical literature on idealized-preference or "full-information" accounts of well-being (Sidgwick, Brandt, Railton, and that whole cluster). This term I'm TAing a small graduate seminar that pairs classic value-theory readings with AI alignment proposals, and I finally got to put a version of that argument on the take-home final. I now have a stack of these to grade and would like an independent second opinion on one answer before I commit to a mark, since the student writes fluently and confidently, which makes it easy to wave the whole thing through without actually checking each inferential step.

Here is the question as I set it:

"Yudkowsky's proposal of Coherent Extrapolated Volition (CEV) is sometimes described as an attempt to import 'idealized preference' or 'full-information' accounts of a person's good, of the kind developed by Sidgwick, Brandt, and Railton, into the design specification for an aligned superintelligence.
(a) Explain what it means, on Yudkowsky's proposal, to extrapolate a person's volition, and explain in what sense this is structurally similar to a full-information account of individual well-being.
(b) CEV does not stop at the individual level: extrapolated volitions across many people are then combined, with special weight given to whatever they converge on, and different treatment for cases of deep, persistent disagreement. Explain how this second, aggregative step relates to the individual-level extrapolation described in (a), and identify one philosophical commitment it introduces that a purely individual-level full-information theory never has to take on.
(c) State one influential objection to full-information/idealized-preference accounts of individual welfare, and discuss whether an analogous objection threatens CEV, or whether CEV's aggregative structure lets it escape the objection."

And here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"(a) On Yudkowsky's view, extrapolating a person's volition means asking not what that person currently wants, given their actual limited and often confused epistemic state, but what they would want under idealized conditions: if they knew more relevant facts, could think faster and more clearly, had become more the kind of person they would themselves endorse being, and had resolved internal tensions among their own current desires through further reflection. This is structurally very close to Railton's and Brandt's full-information accounts, on which what is good for a person is fixed not by their actual, under-informed desires but by the desires their fully informed, procedurally rational counterpart would have on their behalf. In both cases, the actual self's good is cashed out by appeal to a hypothetical, improved version of that very same self, rather than to some external, agent-neutral standard.

(b) The aggregative step is CEV's way of moving from many separately-extrapolated volitions to a single target for the AI to implement. Rather than privileging any one person's extrapolated volition, the procedure looks for whatever parts of everyone's extrapolated volition agree, gives those priority, and is more cautious about the respects in which different people's extrapolated volitions diverge. This effectively makes CEV a version of ideal observer theory: just as Firth's ideal observer is disinterested, dispassionate, and fully informed, and rightness is simply fixed by what such an observer would approve of, Yudkowsky's aggregation procedure asks what a fully informed, dispassionate version of humanity as a whole would endorse, and treats that as authoritative in exactly the way Firth's observer's verdicts are authoritative for rightness. The one thing Yudkowsky adds to the classical picture is a way of tracking how much the extrapolated volitions actually agree, so that the resulting AI can act more cautiously, or hold back altogether, exactly where that agreement breaks down.

(c) A standard objection to full-information accounts is that the idealized counterpart may end up so unlike the actual person, in experience, values, and even temperament, that it is unclear why the counterpart's desires should be authoritative for the actual person's good: full information, and on Yudkowsky's more radical version, greater intelligence and having 'grown up farther,' may change someone enough that they are no longer the right party to be deciding on the actual individual's behalf. This objection is considerably weaker against CEV, though, precisely because CEV was never in the business of fixing what is good for any single actual individual in the first place. Its target is the coherent volition of humanity in the aggregate, so the worry that one particular actual person's idealized counterpart might drift too far from them is defused at exactly the level that matters: what the resulting AI implements was never supposed to track any single person's good to begin with, only the coherent overlap across the idealized population."
