Me and my friend Dev got into a whole argument last night (over beers, as these things go) about Confucian role ethics. He's doing a masters in comparative philosophy and kept pushing this Ames/Rosemont-style reading where Confucian ethics isn't just "virtue ethics with Chinese characteristics" — it's a genuinely different picture of personhood, where you're not an individual self who happens to enter into relationships, you're actually constituted by your roles (son, father, elder sibling, friend, minister, whatever), and the ethical task is basically to inhabit those roles well.

I pushed the obvious objection: isn't this just nepotism dressed up in philosophy? There's that famous bit where someone tells Confucius about a guy so upright he turned in his own father for stealing a sheep, and Confucius says that's not real uprightness at all — real uprightness is fathers covering for sons and sons covering for fathers. Dev's comeback was that I was smuggling in a liberal "view from nowhere" notion of impartiality that doesn't even make sense once you take role ethics seriously on its own terms. Took me alot longer than I'd like to admit to even see what he meant, and I'm still not 100% sold.

Arguing about it in the abstract only gets you so far though, so I'd love to see it actually play out in a concrete story instead. Could you write me a short story (a page or two, doesn't need to be longer) set in some believable present-day setting — a family restaurant, a hospital, a small company, a school, you pick — where a character who genuinely reasons in these Confucian role-ethics terms hits a situation where the specific obligations of one of their roles (to a parent, a mentee, an employer, whoever) pulls hard against some impartial, rule-based duty they also hold? I don't want the "role ethics" side to just be an obvious stand-in for corruption or favoritism — I want their reasoning to feel recognizably Confucian (rooted in the actual relationship and what it calls for, not just "loyalty feels nice"), and genuinely compelling, so the dilemma has real bite instead of resolving itself by paragraph two.
