I am a novelist writing a hospital scene in which a chaplain persuades a grieving mother to authorize her son's organ donation by telling her she would be 'letting part of him go on helping people live' rather than 'authorizing surgeons to remove his organs' — two descriptions that are equally true yet move her quite differently. Before I write the scene, I want the philosophical dispute underneath it to be sound, so please write a dialogue between two philosophers who disagree about how to classify what the chaplain is doing. The first defends a 'bypassing' view: manipulation consists in swaying someone through a channel that routes around her rational agency — an emotionally resonant frame, say, rather than a neutral weighing of the facts — so that the chaplain's phrasing is manipulative regardless of her intentions, purely because of how it works on the mother. The second defends what might be called a 'disregard' view, on which the bypassing criterion is both too broad, since almost no description of anything is frame-free, and too narrow, since the chaplain could use the driest clinical language and still be manipulating the mother if her real aim were to help the hospital meet its donation targets rather than to help the mother reach a decision she could live with; what makes an act manipulative, on this view, is whether the speaker disregards the target's own perspective, independent of the words chosen. Have the two philosophers apply their accounts to this single case and press each other with the strongest objections available, so that neither view comes off as obviously correct, and end the dialogue with the disagreement still unresolved.
