I am several chapters into a novel that turns on a homicide trial: my protagonist's uncle, Daniel, is on trial for killing a man who broke into his house at night, and his defense is being built by a lawyer named Whitfield, a former seminarian who left his studies a few credits short of ordination. I want Whitfield's closing argument to draw on the doctrine of double effect in a way that feels genuinely earned rather than lifted from the flattened version that circulates alongside trolley-problem cases, so I went back to the passage everyone credits as the doctrine's actual origin: Aquinas's article on killing in self-defense, Summa Theologica II-II, Q.64, A.7, in the old Dominican Fathers translation. Having now spent an embarrassing number of evenings with the "I answer that" section, I have hit a real snag in the argument that I would like to understand properly before I let Whitfield build a speech on it. Here is the passage in full:

"I answer that, Nothing hinders one act from having two effects, only one of which is intended, while the other is beside the intention. Now moral acts take their species according to what is intended, and not according to what is beside the intention, since this is accidental as explained above. Accordingly the act of self-defense may have two effects, one is the saving of one's life, the other is the slaying of the aggressor. Therefore this act, since one's intention is to save one's own life, is not unlawful, seeing that it is natural to everything to keep itself in 'being,' as far as possible. And yet, though proceeding from a good intention, an act may be rendered unlawful, if it be out of proportion to the end. Wherefore if a man, in self-defense, uses more than necessary violence, it will be unlawful: whereas if he repel force with moderation his defense will be lawful, because according to the jurists, 'it is lawful to repel force by force, provided one does not exceed the limits of a blameless defense.' Nor is it necessary for salvation that a man omit the act of moderate self-defense in order to avoid killing the other man, since one is bound to take more care of one's own life than of another's. But as it is unlawful to take a man's life, except for the public authority acting for the common good, it is not lawful for a man to intend killing a man in self-defense, except for such as have public authority, who while intending to kill a man in self-defense, refer this to the public good, as in the case of a soldier fighting against the foe, and in the minister of the judge struggling with robbers, although even these sin if they be moved by private animosity."

What I cannot work out is how the final sentence is meant to sit with everything that comes before it. For most of the passage, Aquinas seems to be establishing that an ordinary private person's act of self-defense simply has two effects, one of them - the killing - being merely "beside the intention," and that this is precisely what makes the act lawful in the first place. But the closing sentence draws a sharp line between private individuals, who may never intend to kill even in self-defense, and those with public authority, such as soldiers, who may intend it, provided they refer that intention to the common good. If the private person's killing was never intended to begin with, it is hard to see what further permission the soldier is being granted that the private person does not already have by virtue of the "beside the intention" move; so it looks to me as though Aquinas might be quietly changing the subject in that last sentence, from a person defending his own body to something closer to the public execution of justice he discusses a few articles earlier, while still calling both of them "self-defense." Is that the right way to read the shift, or is there a single, consistent notion of intention running through the whole passage that would let Whitfield explain to a jury why the soldier's case and the ordinary homeowner's case are really the same kind of act, merely permitted to different degrees? I would be grateful for a close reading of exactly what work that last sentence is doing, and whether the tension I'm describing is one that commentators on the doctrine of double effect have already resolved.
