So I'm about a third of the way into a new novel with a secondary character — a former Evangelical who ends up editing a freethinking magazine in the 1870s — and I keep noticing I'm basically stealing George Eliot's biography for her without meaning to. Before I let that keep happening, I want to actually pin down what I think Eliot's own grown-up religious position was, rather than the coffee-mug version ("she lost her faith and became a humanist"), so I wrote up the little argument below. My writing group is sick of hearing about this so I'm bringing it to you instead — could you read it and tell me where it's shaky?

The move Eliot makes is basically Feuerbachs — she translated The Essence of Christianity in 1854, the only book she ever published under her own name, and the whole idea there is that theology is secretly anthropology: "God is love" really just means love is the highest thing we know, so you get to keep the sentiment and drop the metaphysical landlord. Fine as far as it goes. But I don't think she can actually run that move all the way through to the endings of her own novels, because the ethical payoff she wants — that Dorothea's unrecorded, unrewarded life still mattered, that people who "lived faithfully a hidden life, and rest in unvisited tombs" still made things better, that "the growing good of the world is partly dependent on unhistoric acts" — needs some guarantee that those small acts don't just wash out into noise rather than accumulate. That guarantee is exactly what Providence used to hand you for free. So the Finale isn't the modest, hard-won empirical claim it presents itself as (she obviously hasn't run the numbers on Dorothea's counterfactual impact); it's cosmic bookkeeping smuggled back in under cover of a metaphor about rivers and channels. Meliorism — the term for the view that the world can be improved, if never perfected, by human effort, which is often tied specifically to her — ends up quietly doing the reassuring work of "God, immortality, duty" once you strip out the first two and keep only the third (that's the old Myers anecdote about the Fellows' Garden at Trinity, if it rings a bell).

Three things I actually want from you: (1) is this a fair reading of the Feuerbach move and where it runs out, or am I flattening something she was more careful about; (2) is there a scene or novel of hers that just refutes my "hidden providence" claim outright — I keep half remembering Silas Marner, or Dinah's preaching in Adam Bede, or the Bulstrode plot, or even Mordecai in Daniel Deronda, as counterexamples I haven't actually dealt with; and (3) is this an interesting thing for a character to notice out loud in 1876, or is it the sort of point every bright undergraduate reinvents and actual Eliot scholars consider old news. I'd rather you tell me it's wrong now than have some reviewer catch it in three years.
