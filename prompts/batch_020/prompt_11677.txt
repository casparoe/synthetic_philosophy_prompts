Last weekend a friend and I got into a two-hour argument over dinner that neither of us really won, and I would like a more rigorous take on it before I have to make a decision. For the past few months I have been helping several neighbors set up a small time bank for our apartment building: instead of paying each other in cash for small favors — watering plants, fixing a shelf, watching someone's dog for an evening — members log hours in a shared ledger and can later draw on other members' time in whatever amount they have banked. We are set to launch it formally next week. My friend, who works in finance, thinks the whole project rests on a confusion: he says these "hour credits" are not really money at all, just a glorified favor-tracking spreadsheet, and that the system will collapse the moment people notice that nothing real backs the credits — no government standing behind them, no reserve of anything scarce, nothing but our mutual say-so. He kept contrasting this with "actual" currency, which he insists has value because it is backed by something more solid than a shared spreadsheet.

I pushed back that this objection proves too much, since the banknote he used to pay for dinner is not valuable because of the paper it is printed on, nor because some vault holds a scarce reserve against it; it is valuable only because people are confident that everyone else will keep accepting it in exchange for goods and labor. If that is really the source of a currency's value, I do not see why a transparent, community-enforced ledger of hours owed and worked should count as any less "real" than money is — after all, what actually circulates in both cases is a claim on other people's labor and goods, not the token itself. He thinks I am simply rationalizing a project I already like, and that any currency not tied to something scarce is bound to be especially fragile in a case like ours. Neither of us budged by the end of the night, so I would like to think the issue through properly: is my friend right that money must be backed by something scarce, or by a state's authority, in order to count as genuine wealth, or is that whole demand confused about what money and wealth actually are? And with an eye to our very concrete situation, does the answer suggest that our time bank is a sound idea, or that it is, as he would have it, built on sand?
