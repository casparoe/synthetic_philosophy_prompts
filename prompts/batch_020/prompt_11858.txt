I'm a novelist putting together a collection of short stories, each one built around a mathematical or scientific idea that quietly reorganizes how its protagonist understands their own life. A friend of mine who trades for a living has spent the better part of a year trying to explain Kelly betting to me, and it has finally lodged itself in my imagination enough that I want it to be the next entry. What interests me isn't the gambling application so much as the philosophical undertow: the notion that "rationality," as economists and decision theorists have traditionally defined it, quietly assumes you can average your fortunes across a crowd of parallel selves, when in fact any actual person only ever lives through one sequence of bets, one after another, in time.

The illustration that won't leave me alone is the standard one: a coin that pays a fifty percent gain on heads and a forty percent loss on tails, restaked in full every round, where the return averaged across a crowd of gamblers is comfortably positive and yet almost every individual who plays long enough is ruined — because it is the geometric mean, not the arithmetic one, that governs a single unfolding life. I would like a story that arrives at that distinction, or something structurally like it, without ever curdling into a lecture.

Could you write me a short story of perhaps 1,200 to 1,800 words in which a character's collision with this idea is a personal or moral crisis rather than merely an intellectual one? I can imagine several vessels for it — a proprietary trader who keeps re-entering a bet her models insist is favorable, a card counter whose mentor wagers a fixed fraction rather than everything the odds seem to justify, the rationing officer of a generation ship deciding how much seed grain a whole future can afford to hazard in one season — but I would rather you find the right one than have me over-specify it. I'd also like the story to gesture, by its close, toward the stranger claim some recent decision theorists have built on top of Kelly's own: that his rule may be only the best-known instance of a more general "geometric" rationality, one that governs how a person might rationally bargain among their own competing possible futures rather than a special trick for casinos. Please resist any tidy comeuppance or moral; I would like the ending to leave the tension between these two notions of rationality as genuinely unresolved as it still is among the people who study it professionally.
