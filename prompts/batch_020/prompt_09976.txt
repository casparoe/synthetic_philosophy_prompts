My little sister has bipolar I, and a couple years back, when she was doing well, she sat down with her psychiatrist and a lawyer and wrote a psychiatric advance directive — basically a Ulysses contract — naming me and her doctor as the people who could hospitalize her and start meds over her objection if she hit specific triggers (no sleep for three nights straight, giving away money, going off her outpatient meds). She even put in a line, word for word: "if I tell you this time is different, it isn't — that's what mania sounds like from the inside." Going through that with her is honestly the reason I ended up on the ethics committee at the hospital where I work.

Now we've got a case in front of the commitee that's uncomfortably close to home. A patient with the same diagnosis signed a near-identical directive, is hitting the listed triggers, and her named proxy wants it invoked. But she's not incoherent at all right now — she's sharp, funny, and adament that she's never thought more clearly in her life, and furious that a document she signed two years ago could override what she's telling us today. One committee member keeps invoking constitutional precommitment — supermajority rules binding a future legislature that hates the rule — to argue we should enforce the directive regardless of her current state. Another says the analogy doesn't hold: a future legislature is just a different set of people rotating through an office, but this is the literal same mind, so overriding her now isn't upholding a rule on a successor, it's just refusing to listen to her.

We're genuinely split and meet Thursday morning. Can you draft the memo I'll bring in — an actual recommendation, not "reasonable people disagree," with some concrete next step and timeframe, and one that actually takes on the constitutional-precommitment argument instead of dodging it?
