I sit on the five-person board of a small enterprise-software company (about 40 employees) that's been locked in a patent dispute with a much larger competitor for the past fourteen months. We sued them for infringing a scheduling-algorithm patent core to our product; they countersued seeking to invalidate the patent for prior art, and what began as a straightforward cease-and-desist has turned into full discovery and motion practice. Our legal budget started at $150,000 and we're now past $900,000, authorized through four separate board votes, each one justified at the time partly on the grounds that backing out then would have meant losing everything spent so far for nothing. Last week a magistrate judge issued a preliminary claim-construction ruling mostly in our favor, and our lawyers immediately came back with an estimate that another $350,000 gets us through trial with "meaningfully improved odds." The competitor, rather than settling, brought in a second outside firm and made clear they intend to contest this all the way through appeal.

I've spent enough time on LessWrong and adjacent blogs to recognize the shape of this. It has the structural signature of Shubik's dollar auction, where continuing to bid can look locally rational for whichever party is currently behind even though the collective outcome ends up worse than if neither side had bid at all, layered on top of the more familiar organizational-psychology story of escalation of commitment (the Staw "Knee-Deep in the Big Muddy" research on why decision-makers keep re-funding a failing course of action partly to justify the choices that got them there). Two of my fellow board members want to settle now and absorb the loss; two want to approve the next tranche, partly because the claim-construction ruling seems like genuine new information rather than just another rung on an escalating bid, and partly because they suspect folding now, after four rounds of escalation, would simply hand the competitor a costless win. That leaves me as the swing vote, and I need to bring a written recommendation to our emergency board meeting this Friday.

Could you draft that memo for me? I'd like it to do more than repeat the standard "ignore sunk costs" advice: it should state plainly where our situation actually does and doesn't match the dollar-auction/escalation-of-commitment pattern, given that we may genuinely have new information and not merely an escalating bid; it should close with a specific recommendation attached to a hard budget ceiling or some other pre-committed stopping rule, so we're not right back here after the next filing; and it should take the strongest version of my colleagues' argument for continuing seriously before explaining why it does or doesn't survive scrutiny.
