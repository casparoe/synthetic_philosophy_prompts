I've been teaching the problem of old evidence in my graduate seminar on formal epistemology this term, and a disagreement broke out among the students that I haven't been able to settle to my own satisfaction. Everyone in the room accepts Glymour's original setup: the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion was already sitting in the astronomical record, with credence effectively equal to 1, decades before Einstein arrived at General Relativity, so that naive Bayesian conditionalization cannot explain why physicists were nonetheless right to treat that old evidence as some of the most powerful confirmation the theory ever received. The room split, however, over Garber's proposed fix: dropping the assumption that credence functions are logically omniscient, so that a scientist's coming to realize, only after the fact, that the theory entails the evidence can itself be treated as a fresh piece of information, to be conditionalized on in the usual way. Half my students think this fully rescues Bayesianism from Glymour's objection; the other half, siding with the sort of skepticism one finds in Earman's *Bayes or Bust*, think it merely relocates the difficulty, since it now makes whether, and how much, old evidence confirms a theory hostage to essentially contingent, agent-specific facts about when a particular scientist happened to notice a deductive relationship, rather than to any evidential relation that holds independently of anyone's logical bookkeeping.

Could you provide an explanation, pitched at readers who already have ordinary probabilistic conditionalization down cold, of exactly what Garber's logical-omniscience move buys the Bayesian, and of why this "hostage to timing" complaint is supposed to be so damaging rather than a bullet that Bayesians can simply bite? I would also like you to bring in the rival family of counterfactual solutions, which try to define confirmation-by-old-evidence in terms of the credence a scientist would have assigned to the theory had the evidence not already been in hand, rather than in terms of that scientist's actual, historically situated credence, and to say whether, in your assessment, this rival strategy really escapes the timing objection or merely trades it in for an equally serious problem of its own concerning which counterfactual credence function is supposed to be the relevant one.
