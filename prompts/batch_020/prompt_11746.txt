Over the past month I've been dealing with a peer-review headache that turned out to be more philosophically interesting than I expected. I'm finishing my PhD in evolutionary biology, and one section of my dissertation manuscript traces how the malleus and incus of the mammalian middle ear evolved from the articular and quadrate bones that formed the jaw joint in early cynodonts, before being freed up for sound transmission once a new dentary–squamosal joint took over the job of articulating the jaw. Reviewer 2 flagged our repeated claim that these bones "function to transmit vibrations," arguing that we were sliding between two incompatible senses of "function" - one tied to what a structure was selected to do, the other tied to what it currently contributes to hearing - without ever specifying which one we meant. My advisor's first instinct was to dismiss this as "yet another philosopher-brained reviewer," but the more I've read into it, the more I suspect Reviewer 2 is pointing at a genuine fault line in the philosophy of biology that I don't know how to resolve on my own.

Before I draft a response to reviewers, I would like a careful comparison of the two accounts of function that seem to be at issue: Larry Wright's etiological (selected-effect) account and Robert Cummins' causal-role account. I want more than the standard one-line gloss of each ("what it was selected for" versus "what it currently does") - I'd like to understand the different explanatory jobs the two theories were originally built to do, since my impression is that Wright and Cummins were not simply offering competing answers to the same question. I'm especially interested in two stress tests: first, how gracefully each account handles ascriptions of malfunction or dysfunction, since that is the objection I keep seeing raised against Cummins-style views; and second, how each account would actually classify the malleus and incus, a case where the trait's original selective history and its present-day causal contribution to hearing point toward different stories about what its "function" really is. If you're familiar with later refinements of the etiological line, such as Ruth Millikan's proper functions or Peter Godfrey-Smith's "modern history" restriction, I would welcome you weaving those in too, since my (possibly mistaken) impression is that they were partly designed to handle repurposed structures like this one.

Finally, I would value your own verdict: does Reviewer 2's objection track a real conceptual distinction that evolutionary biologists should be more careful about when they use the word "function," or is this a case where demanding philosophical disambiguation adds rigor on paper without actually changing anything about how the science should be practiced or written up?
