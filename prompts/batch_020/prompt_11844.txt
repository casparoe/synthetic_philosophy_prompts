I've been reading LessWrong and the EA Forum for well over a decade now, and over the last year or two a striking proportion of that reading has been about whether current or near-future language models might have some degree of moral status. What strikes me about almost all of this writing, from cautious skeptics and worried advocates alike, is that it treats the question "is this system a moral patient?" as one with a determinate answer we simply haven't worked out yet: people argue about which functional, behavioral, or architectural signatures are the right ones to track, as though enough interpretability research, or a sufficiently mature theory of consciousness, would eventually settle the matter outright, the way better instruments settle an ordinary empirical dispute.

This started to bother me after I revisited the old debate, in the philosophy of vagueness, between epistemicism (Timothy Williamson's view that predicates like "bald" or "heap" have sharp, fixed cutoffs that we are constitutively barred from ever knowing, since no pattern of linguistic use could make us sensitive to a boundary that fine-grained) and supervaluationism (the view, developed by Kit Fine and defended at length by Rosanna Keefe, on which a vague sentence is true just in case it comes out true under every admissible way of making the relevant predicate precise, false just in case it comes out false on all of them, and neither true nor false in the genuine borderline cases). "Moral patient" looks like exactly the kind of predicate this debate is about, complete with sorites-susceptible borderline cases, and yet I have never once seen anyone writing about AI welfare pause to ask which of the two pictures they are implicitly assuming.

Could you lay out the most important implications of the choice between epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness for how we should reason about, and act under, our uncertainty about the moral status of borderline AI systems? I'm especially interested in whether the choice should change the credences we assign to a given system's moral patienthood, whether it changes what kind of evidence could even in principle move those credences, and whether one view makes precautionary measures, such as giving some weight to "model welfare" absent a confident verdict, easier to justify than the other, or whether the two theories actually converge on identical practical advice once you work through them carefully.
