Two of my friends got into a real argument at a barbecue a few weeks back and I haven't been able to let it go, partly because I'm a few years into a philosophy PhD working on responsibility and blame, and I TA intro ethics, where moral luck always gets taught through the same tired drunk-driver case. My friend Priyanka does application security for a mid-size company, her partner Doug is a corporate litigator, and the argument grew out of a case Priyanka brought home from work -- basically a fresh version of the same old problem, except neither of them ever used the phrase "moral luck."

The setup: two engineers at two unrelated companies ship the same login flaw, having skipped the same rate-limiting check for the same lazy reason (a deadline). At Company A nobody ever finds the hole. At Company B a bot happens to scan that port range two weeks later and 50,000 users' data leaks. Same code, same negligence, same foreknowledge -- the only difference is whose server a strangers script hit first. (1) Priyanka's claim: the two engineers are equally culpable, full stop, and treating the Company B one as having done something worse is a kind of moral illusion, punishing bad luck and calling it accountability. (2) Doug pushed back that the law obviously disagrees -- attempted murder and murder get very different sentences despite identical intent and action, and that's not an embarrassing bug, it's tracking something real about wrongdoing being partly about the world and not just the will behind it. (3) Priyanka said that just relocates the puzzle: the law being outcome-sensitive doesn't mean it's tracking desert rather than practical stuff like provability and deterrence optics. (4) Doug said fine, forget law -- imagine you're one of the 50,000 users, you'd be way angrier at "your" engineer than at the equally careless one three towns over who never hurt anybody, and that anger is tracking something real, not a mistake. Priyanka's comeback was that gut reactions like that are exactly what should make us suspicious here, since nobody would accept "I just feel angrier" as a justification in a case where they didn't already have skin in the game.

Could you go through moves (1) through (4) one at a time and say who has the better of it at each step -- not just who wins overall -- and call out anywhere a move is question-begging or quietly changes what's being compared? Then close with a short paragraph on what you think the actual answer to the underlying puzzle is, since my hunch is neither of them landed on it.
