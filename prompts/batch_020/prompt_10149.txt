After a reading from my new novel at a literary festival this spring, I ended up at the bar afterward with two friends, Nora and Felix, both of whom had been in the audience for the chapter where the narrator learns her brother has died; several people nearby were audibly crying by the end of it. Nora and Felix spent the next hour disputing what that crying actually was, and neither has let it drop since. (1) Nora said it couldn't have been grief in any real sense, since grief requires believing that someone existed and died, and nobody in that room believed any such thing about a character I had invented at my desk that winter; whatever the tears were, they rested on a confusion about their own object. (2) Felix said that was an odd conclusion to draw about something so involuntary and physical — nobody in that room decided to cry, people were reaching for tissues, and a few needed a minute before they could order another round — and that the response was a genuine psychological state the story had produced, not a mistake, though he'd grant it wasn't identical to grief for someone actually lost. (3) Nora replied that "not identical" was doing a great deal of unearned work: Felix's own evidence, the tissues and the minute it took people to recover, was evidence of something fully present, not partially so, so either he should drop the hedge and call it grief outright or say exactly what the state lacked that ordinary grief has; gesturing at a difference without naming one wasn't an argument. (4) Felix said he would drop it, but not in the direction Nora expected: grief, he said, has never actually required believing that someone existed and died, only entertaining the thought of it, in enough detail, which is available whether or not you think the loss is real — which is exactly why nobody in that room needed the brother to have lived.
