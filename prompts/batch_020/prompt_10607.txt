Longtime readers of Shtetl-Optimized will remember Scott Aaronson's 2014 "unconscious expander" post and the resulting exchange with Giulio Tononi; it made the rounds on Less Wrong at the time, and I've had a soft spot for it ever since. I now TA a seminar on philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and this term's take-home midterm included a short-answer question on Integrated Information Theory's exclusion postulate and that exchange. I have a stack of these to grade by tomorrow, and before I commit to a mark on one particular submission, I would like a careful, disinterested second opinion, since I would rather over-invest in precision now than have to defend an inconsistent rubric later.

The question, worth 20 points, read as follows: "Integrated Information Theory, in its 2014 ('IIT 3.0') formulation, individuates the physical substrate of a conscious experience by way of its exclusion postulate. (a) State the exclusion postulate and explain what work it does that the integration postulate, on its own, cannot do. (b) Summarize Scott Aaronson's 'unconscious expander' argument against IIT, and say precisely which claim of the theory — the necessity or the sufficiency of high Φ for consciousness — it targets. (c) How did Giulio Tononi respond to Aaronson, and is his response an adequate defense of the theory? Answer in around 300–400 words."

Here is the student's answer in full:

"IIT holds that a physical system possesses consciousness to the degree that it is integrated, and integration is quantified as Φ, found by identifying the system's minimum information partition (the cut that loses the least cause-effect power) and measuring how much is lost even at that best-case cut. The exclusion postulate is what tells us when a system is integrated enough to count as a subject of experience in the first place: it says that a mechanism whose cause-effect repertoire cannot be decomposed into the repertoires of causally independent parts is thereby 'excluded' from being reducible, and it is exactly this irreducibility — formalized as Φ greater than zero — that turns a candidate system into a genuine 'complex' rather than a mere heap of parts. Without exclusion, the integration postulate would tell us that experience is unified, but not why some systems, and not others, get to count as unified wholes at all.

Scott Aaronson's 'unconscious expander' argument targets exactly this feature of the theory. He constructs a large system whose connectivity implements a Vandermonde-matrix computation — the same linear-algebra trick that lets Reed-Solomon error-correcting codes recover data from a scratched CD — and shows that because every output is correlated with every subset of inputs, such a system's Φ can be driven arbitrarily high just by making the network bigger, far past any serious estimate of Φ for a human brain, even though the system is doing nothing anyone would call cognition. The target is sufficiency, not necessity: the point is not that consciousness requires a low Φ, but that a high Φ score plainly is not enough to guarantee consciousness, which is a real problem for a theory that identifies consciousness with integrated information rather than merely correlating the two.

Tononi's published reply was to concede the mathematical point and pull back the theory's scope: he granted that the Vandermonde system was a legitimate counterexample under the letter of IIT 3.0, and restricted the theory's claims to systems with brain-like, re-entrant architecture, sacrificing the more sweeping, panpsychism-adjacent commitments in order to save the theory's core predictions about cortex, thalamus, and cerebellum. That seems like the right call: a theory that only binds itself to biological cases is much less exciting, but also much less obviously refuted."
