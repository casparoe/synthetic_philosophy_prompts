One of my graduate students, who works outside philosophy, described a personal project to me recently that I have not been able to stop turning over. For a little over a year she has kept a running log of about a dozen personal traits she wants to improve — things like patience with her staff, financial discipline, punctuality, and honesty about small matters — and rather than attending to all of them at once, she concentrates on just one at a time, a week or two apiece, cycling through the whole list and back again, while simply recording, without actively working on, any lapses in the rest. She mentioned two features of this arrangement that trouble her at a dinner with some colleagues of mine, and the table split cleanly into two camps, which is part of why I would like a more careful answer than either side managed to give her.

The first difficulty is that whenever she throws her attention behind one trait, some other trait on the list reliably gets a little worse over that same stretch, as though she has a fixed reserve of attention that only ever gets reallocated, never enlarged. The second, which bothers her more, is this: on the rare week when her whole log comes back clean, she notices in herself a small, distinct glow of satisfaction — and she is self-aware enough to recognize that glow as itself a lapse in one of the very traits on her list, namely modesty, so that doing well everywhere else seems to manufacture its own quiet relapse. One colleague told her she is overthinking a habit-tracking exercise and should simply relax her standards and settle for "good enough"; another shot back that this concedes the whole project is pointless, since a standard you have already agreed to relax whenever it becomes inconvenient was never really a standard. She asked me, half in jest, which colleague was right, and whether her scheme is quietly self-undermining by its own design. I would like to give her something more principled than an intuition. Is there a coherent way to keep pursuing a demanding, multi-part self-improvement project like hers once both problems are taken seriously, or does sufficiently close attention to one's own moral progress eventually defeat itself?
