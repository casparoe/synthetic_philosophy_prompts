Over the past few weeks I've been finishing a project for a graduate seminar on automated reasoning that asked us to formalize a piece of classical philosophical argument inside a proof assistant, and I chose Kurt Gödel's ontological proof — essentially redoing, with the original paper open beside me, the formalization that Christoph Benzmüller and Bruno Woltzenlogel Paleo published in 2013 using Isabelle/HOL. Getting Dana Scott's tidied-up version of the axioms — positive properties, God-likeness, essence, necessary existence — encoded so that the argument actually goes through in quantified S5 modal logic was satisfying, if a little anticlimactic, since the prover accepts it within seconds once everything is stated correctly. What has actually stayed with me since is the notorious "modal collapse" corollary of those same five axioms: that every true proposition turns out to be a necessary truth, so that the system leaves no room for contingency anywhere in the world. I can watch the prover confirm this, and I can follow the symbolic derivation line by line, and I gather the worry actually predates the formalization by decades, but I still don't have a real gut-level sense of where, inferentially, the contingency is being squeezed out.

Could you talk me through this more intuitively than a bare proof trace does? Specifically, I would like to understand which axiom, or combination of axioms, is really doing the collapsing work (my own guess, from staring at the output, is that it involves some interaction between the necessary-existence axiom and the definition of an essence, though I can't yet articulate why that combination is lethal while the earlier axioms on their own are not), why the sheer strength of S5 is needed for the result to go through in a way a weaker modal logic would block, and how C. Anthony Anderson's emendation of the axioms is supposed to head off the collapse. I would also value your own assessment of whether Anderson's fix, or any comparable patch, actually rescues something still recognizable as Gödel's argument, or whether blocking the collapse always ends up costing the defender the very intuitions about "positive properties" that made the axioms look plausible in the first place.
