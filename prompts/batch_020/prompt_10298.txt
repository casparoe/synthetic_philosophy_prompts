I'm giving my aunt's eulogy this weekend and the situation's a bit more fraught than a normal eulogy, because she was, for something like sixty years, a very deliberate repeat offender: sit-ins as a young woman, chained to fences at protests through the 70s and 80s, blocking a pipeline access road as recently as a couple summers ago at almost eighty. Every single time she got arrested, she turned herself in and took whatever came, even the one time a lawyer told her she could probably skip it. She'd picked up a fair amount of political philosophy from a night class decades ago -- Rawls, I think, or maybe someone he was arguing against -- and afterward she was borderline pedantic about the vocabulary. She hated when reporters, or honestly when we in the family, called her 'a rebel' or said she 'didn't care about rules,' because her whole point was that what she did was basically the opposite of that: to count as real civil disobedience it had to be public and nonviolent and addressed to everyone's shared sense of justice, not just her own private conscience, which she considered a different and kind of lesser thing (she had an actual term for that -- 'conscientious refusal,' like a draft resister -- and was a little sniffy about it). And that, she always said, is also why turning herself in mattered so much: not gritting her teeth through a punishment, but proving she still respected the legal system even in the act of breaking one of its rules.

Here's the annoying part: a couple of my cousins want a straightforward 'badass rebel grandma' eulogy, and I get the appeal, but I don't think it's actually true to her, and I don't want to get her wrong on the one thing she was most particular about, at her own funeral. The crowd is going to be mostly extended family who just think 'grandma got arrested again' is a fun anecdote, plus a bunch of her old activist friends, plus two or three people from that same old discussion group who will absolutely notice if I flatten her actual position into generic 'she followed her heart' stuff. Could you write the eulogy, roughly five minutes long? It needs to get her actual distinction right -- not 'rebel,' not 'just followed her conscience' -- while still landing as a genuinely warm eulogy for a room full of people who've never opened a Rawls book in their life.
