Question (worth 20 points): "The reference class problem was originally raised in the context of frequentist interpretations of probability. Explain how it resurfaces in Bostrom's treatment of anthropic reasoning, using the Doomsday Argument as your central illustration. Then assess whether supplementing the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) with the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) provides a satisfactory resolution of the problem, or whether it merely relocates it. Illustrate your answer with the relevant thought experiments."

Student's answer: "The reference class problem originates with Reichenbach and was sharpened by Salmon: on a frequentist reading, the probability of a single event just is the frequency of that type of event within some reference class, but any single case belongs to indefinitely many classes that yield different frequencies, and the theory itself gives no principled way to pick out which class is the relevant one.

Bostrom's Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) imports exactly this difficulty into anthropic reasoning. SSA says we should reason as if we were a random sample from the set of observers in our reference class. Run on the class of 'all humans who will ever live,' this generates the Doomsday Argument: since you find yourself comparatively early in the sequence of humans born so far -- current estimates put total births to date at only just over a hundred billion, against the trillions of future humans that more optimistic scenarios predict -- that fact is more surprising, and hence weak evidence against, hypotheses on which humanity persists for a very long time and eventually contains enormous numbers of people, and correspondingly evidence for hypotheses on which our numbers stay comparatively modest, i.e., for sooner extinction. But the whole argument is hostage to what counts as the reference class. Take the class to be 'humans' and the argument goes through as stated; take it to be 'observers who have thought about the Doomsday Argument,' or 'all observer-moments across all intelligent species in the universe,' and both the numbers and sometimes the direction of the update change substantially, with nothing internal to SSA telling us which choice is correct. This is just Reichenbach's problem again, transposed from urns and mortality tables to birth ranks.

The standard patch is to add the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA), which says to weight hypotheses by the number of observers they predict, since you are more likely to exist at all under hypotheses on which more observers exist. Because SIA's boost toward populous hypotheses runs in the opposite direction from SSA's Doomsday-favoring shift toward sparse ones, on the standard way of combining them the two cancel exactly: once SIA is added, an early birth rank stops being evidence for early extinction, regardless of which reference class was used to run the SSA calculation in the first place. In that sense, SIA does not just counteract the Doomsday Argument's conclusion; it dissolves the reference class problem that generated it, since the final verdict no longer depends on the choice that was supposedly indeterminate.

The cost of this move shows up elsewhere, in Bostrom's own Presumptuous Philosopher case. Faced with two physical theories, one predicting a modest-sized universe and one predicting a far larger one with vastly more observers, a philosopher reasoning from SSA could announce, without waiting for the experiment that is supposed to decide between them, that the larger-universe theory is enormously more probable simply because it contains more observers. That is a bad way to do cosmology, since it lets a piece of anthropic bookkeeping override actual empirical evidence, and it is generated by the very same machinery that rescues us from the Doomsday Argument. So the overall picture is less a clean resolution than a trade-off: an argument that let an arbitrary reference class dictate our credence about humanity's future is replaced by one that lets sheer population size override the evidence in physics."
