"In 'Equality of What?' and his later work, Sen argues that neither Rawlsian primary goods nor Dworkinian equality of resources gives us the right space for interpersonal comparisons in a theory of justice, and proposes capability instead. Reconstruct Sen's argument against both rivals in your own words, then explain how capability is supposed to improve on them. And here's the answer one of my students turned in, copied exactly as recieved:

"Sen's target is the shared assumption behind utilitarian and Rawlsian theories that once you've picked the right currency of equality, the rest of the theory of justice takes care of itself. Utilitarians want to equalize welfare, understood as pleasure or desire-satisfaction; Rawls thinks that's too permissive because it would let aggregate happiness run roughshod over individuals, so he substitutes holdings of primary goods -- rights and liberties, powers and opportunities, income and wealth, and the social bases of self-respect -- the all-purpose means any rational person needs regardless of their particular plan of life. Sen's complaint is that Rawls doesn't go far enough: two people with identical bundles of primary goods can end up with very different capabilities, because people differ in how efficiently they convert goods into valuable activities and states -- a pregnant woman, a person with a disabling illness, and a fit young adult need quite different amounts of income and food to reach the same level of nourishment or mobility. This is essentially the same point Dworkin makes with his expensive-tastes example against welfare egalitarianism: fixing on one metric, whatever it is, papers over the fact that people convert a given bundle into what actually matters to them at very different rates. Sen's alternative, capability, is meant to fix this by looking directly at the substantive freedoms people have to achieve valuable 'functionings' -- beings and doings like being well-nourished or taking part in the life of the community. To make the approach usable for actual policy comparisons, Sen, following Nussbaum, sets out a definite list of central capabilities -- life, bodily health, affiliation, and so on -- which lets us compare people's real opportunities rather than just their bank balances. Whether this actually escapes the problem Sen raises against Rawls and Dworkin is a further question, though: since people also differ in how good they are at converting resources into capabilities in the first place, and not just capabilities into functionings, it isn't obvious the capability space is any less hostage to interpersonal variation than the resource space was."
