A friend and I spent most of dinner last night arguing about adaptationism, and neither of us managed to persuade the other. The disagreement centered on Gould and Lewontin's old broadside against what they called the "Panglossian paradigm": their claim that evolutionary biologists too readily invent a selective just-so story for every trait, when some features are better understood as spandrels — the incidental spaces, such as the mosaic-covered ones beneath the dome of San Marco in Venice, that open up wherever arches meet and must be filled in somehow. Such spaces exist only because of the surrounding architecture, not because anyone designed them as ends in themselves. My friend insists the critique has aged badly, since almost every supposed spandrel, Gould's own example of the human chin included, eventually turns out to have some adaptive story behind it after all; I think it still cuts against a great deal of adaptationist reasoning that mistakes a plausible tale for a tested hypothesis. Rather than rehash that argument, I would like a short story built around this exact tension: one in which a trait's uncertain status, adaptation or by-product, is not merely explained to the reader but actually matters to the plot and to how the characters come to understand one another.
