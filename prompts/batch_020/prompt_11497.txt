I don't have any formal training in philosophy - my interest comes from reading, on and off, about the history of mathematical logic, which is how I first ran into Gottlob Frege. He's usually credited with essentially inventing modern quantificational logic and, through his distinction between sense and reference and his attacks on what he called "psychologism" (treating logic as if it merely described how human minds happen to work), setting the whole analytic tradition in motion. What stopped me in my tracks was learning that Michael Dummett - the philosopher most responsible for making Frege central to twentieth-century philosophy of language - had, in the 1950s, gone through surviving transcriptions of Frege's private papers and found a diary, kept for about a month in 1924, in which Frege comes across as a bitter right-wing nationalist: hostile to parliamentary democracy and universal suffrage, to socialists and liberals, to Catholics and the French, and, most viciously and repeatedly, to Jews, whom he thought should at least be stripped of political rights. Dummett later recounted being deeply shocked to discover that the man he had "revered" as "an absolutely rational man" held views like these. The diary sat unpublished for some seventy years - the editors of Frege's collected papers apparently didn't consider it part of his properly "scientific" Nachlass - before it finally appeared in German in 1994 and in English translation in 1996.

Here's the part I haven't been able to settle for myself. Frege's mature philosophy holds that genuine thoughts (as opposed to private mental images) inhabit what he called a "third realm," distinct from both the physical world and any individual's inner life - objective, mind-independent, and equally accessible, in principle, to any properly functioning rational being. (As a strange aside, his own term for this in German was "drittes Reich," decades before that phrase acquired its later, infamous meaning; I assume this is pure coincidence rather than any substantive connection, but it's the sort of detail that makes you look twice.) Dummett himself seems to want it both ways on the main question: he insists the diary's reprehensible views "shake neither the truth nor the inventiveness" of Frege's philosophical work, while also saying that work becomes harder to approach with the same sympathy and admiration as before. That has always struck me as a position in tension with itself, and I can't tell whether there's a way to make it fully consistent. So I keep going back and forth between two pictures. On one, logical validity doesn't care about the character of whoever discovers it, so a bigot can still get the Begriffsschrift exactly right, and the diary is simply a sad fact about Frege the man that leaves the philosophy untouched. On the other, the diary might be continuous with the philosophy: the same insistence that there is exactly one correct, fully objective standpoint, and that failure to occupy it reflects a defect in the dissenter rather than a legitimate rival view, could plausibly be doing work in both his logic and his politics.

Could you write me an essay that actually takes a side on this, rather than presenting both camps and leaving things there? Please reconstruct the strongest version of the view that the diary is philosophically irrelevant and the strongest version of the view that it is philosophically relevant (feel free to draw on more general considerations about how a thinker's character bears, or fails to bear, on the assessment of their arguments, rather than sticking only to this one case), weigh them against each other, and then commit to and defend a specific conclusion. I'd also like you to say whether your verdict holds uniformly across all of Frege's legacy, or whether you'd distinguish the purely formal logical system from the more interpretive philosophical apparatus built up around it - the third realm, the theory of judgment, the rejection of psychologism, and so on - since it seems at least possible that the diary bears on one of these more than the other.
