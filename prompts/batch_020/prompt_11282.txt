During my career I worked in condensed-matter physics, and it is only since retiring three years ago that I have had the time to read philosophy with any care, mostly through a small reading group of retired scientists kept honest by one very patient professional philosopher. Lately we have become productively stuck on Gibbard and Harper's "Death in Damascus" puzzle: a traveller who must decide whether to remain in Damascus or flee to Aleppo, knowing that Death — an unfailingly accurate predictor — has already settled on one of the two cities, so that whichever act the traveller performs, deciding on it seems to undermine the very reasoning that recommended it, leaving neither option stable under reflection. What has kept us arguing well past our allotted hour is not the original puzzle so much as the long sequence of repairs decision theorists have proposed for it since 1978: Jeffrey's ratifiability requirement, Eells's "tickle defense," Skyrms's deliberational dynamics, Joyce's stability condition for causal decision theory, and, more recently, the treatment offered by "functional" or "logical" decision theory. A book of Arif Ahmed's that one member brought along was some help, but mostly it left us better informed about how deep the disagreement runs and less sure what a genuinely satisfying resolution would even look like.

As a former experimentalist, I am used to fixing my criterion for success before looking at the data, so let me ask you to do the same. Before ranking anything, state in a sentence or two the standard you are using to judge whether a given proposal actually resolves the instability at the heart of Death in Damascus, as opposed to merely relabelling it or quietly substituting a better-behaved decision problem. Then, using that stated standard, sort the following eight positions into four tiers, from most to least successful: (1) unadorned evidential decision theory, (2) unadorned causal decision theory with no ratifiability constraint, (3) Jeffrey's ratifiability requirement, (4) Eells's tickle defense, (5) Skyrms's deliberational dynamics, (6) Joyce's stability condition, (7) the treatment of predictor cases within functional or logical decision theory, and (8) the view that simply accepts that no stable, rationally recommended act exists in cases of this kind. Please give the result as a JSON object with keys "Tier_1" (most successful) through "Tier_4" (least successful), each mapped to a list of the relevant item numbers, plus a fifth key, "criterion," containing the standard you stated at the outset.
