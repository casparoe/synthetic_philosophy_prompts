In my seminar on Victorian moral philosophy, we recently spent two weeks working through the concluding chapters of Sidgwick's *Methods of Ethics*, and almost as an aside, the professor mentioned that Sidgwick was a founding figure of, and served multiple terms as president of, the Society for Psychical Research, personally investigating mediums, haunted houses, and early telepathy experiments alongside Frederic Myers and Edmund Gurney for roughly the last two decades of his life. Several of us found this juxtaposition genuinely strange: Sidgwick's whole reputation rests on an almost pedantic willingness to follow an argument wherever it leads and to admit openly when a proof falls short of what it promises, which makes it jarring to picture the same man patiently sitting through séances.

I don't think this can simply be dismissed as an unrelated personal hobby, however, since the chronology lines up suspiciously well with the problem Sidgwick himself raises at the end of the *Methods* under the heading of the "dualism of practical reason" -- the unresolved standoff between the rational authority of egoism and that of utilitarianism (or "universal benevolence," as he terms the relevant axiom), which he admits he cannot close through intuition or argument alone. Could you explain, in some detail, how Sidgwick himself understood the connection between these two projects? I am especially interested in why he thought empirical evidence bearing on a "future life" specifically, rather than a stronger philosophical argument or a return to the religious faith he had lost, was the sort of thing that could even in principle resolve the dualism, and whether contemporary Sidgwick scholars generally treat the psychical research as a coherent extension of his ethical theory or, instead, as a personally motivated search for existential reassurance that runs alongside the philosophy without really following from it.
