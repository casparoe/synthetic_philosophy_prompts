Here is the question as it was given to the seminar:

"Wittgenstein writes that certain propositions function for us like the hinges on which a door turns, and that our practices of doubting and investigating are only possible because such propositions are, as a matter of course, exempt from doubt (§341–343). (a) Explain what it means for a proposition to function as a hinge rather than as an ordinary empirical proposition, and explain why Wittgenstein takes Moore's use of 'I know that here is a hand' to mislocate the logical status of that proposition, even though Wittgenstein does not think we should doubt it. (b) Crispin Wright has proposed that our acceptance of such propositions can be given a positive epistemic standing via what he calls 'entitlement' — a warrant we hold without evidence, because some cognitive project or other depends on our holding it. Explain the proposal, and assess whether it is best read as making explicit something already present in On Certainty or as a substantive addition to it. (c) Using the river-bed image of §96–99, explain the sense in which the status of a hinge proposition is not fixed for good, and illustrate with an example of your own choosing."

And here is the student's answer, unedited:

"(a) A hinge proposition, for Wittgenstein, is not one more thing we believe on the basis of evidence, alongside our other empirical beliefs; it is a proposition that sits still while inquiry, doubt, and justification move around it, the way the hinges of a door stay put while the door itself swings. Propositions like 'the earth has existed for a long time' or 'here is a hand' are not conclusions we have reached and could in principle un-reach; they belong to the scaffolding that makes reaching conclusions about anything else possible. This is exactly what Moore's proof gets wrong. When Moore holds up his hand and says 'I know that here is a hand,' he is using 'know' in its ordinary sense, in which claiming to know something implies that one could, in principle, have been mistaken, and that one's claim answers to grounds. But a hinge proposition, on Wittgenstein's view, is not the kind of thing that is grounded or ungrounded, correct or mistaken, in that way — it is presupposed by the very practice of asking for and giving grounds. So Moore is not wrong that 'here is a hand' is beyond doubt; he is wrong about why it is beyond doubt, treating as the best-attested member of the class of empirical knowledge claims something that Wittgenstein thinks does not belong to that class at all.

(b) Wright's entitlement is meant to solve a problem Wittgenstein's own remarks leave open: if hinge propositions are not known and not evidentially supported, doesn't accepting them look simply arbitrary? Wright's answer is that we can be rationally entitled to accept a proposition even absent evidence for it, provided we have no evidence against it either, and accepting it is a precondition of pursuing some cognitive project we already have reason to pursue — his standard example is the reliability of our faculties, which we could not evidentially confirm without already relying on those very faculties. Applied to hinges, this gives a proposition like 'the external world exists' a positive rational standing that falls short of knowledge but is more than mere assumption. I think this is best read as continuous with, rather than added onto, Wittgenstein's own position: both Wright and Wittgenstein agree that we cannot give up these propositions without giving up on inquiry altogether, and both think holding onto them is nonetheless the reasonable thing to do even though we lack evidence for them. Entitlement just gives a name, and a tidy general form, to the status Wittgenstein was already gesturing at when he called hinges exempt from doubt.

(c) The river-bed image is meant to block the idea that the line between hinge and ordinary empirical proposition marks off two fixed sets of propositions once and for all. The hinges are the hardened bed of the river, the empirical propositions are the water flowing over it, and which propositions occupy which role can differ between communities and change over time, even though the bed moves far more slowly than the water and we are seldom in a position to notice it moving under us. Because the hardened propositions are precisely what make it possible to test the fluid ones, the process runs in one direction: a proposition that has been tested and re-tested enough can harden into a hinge as a world-picture settles around it, but a proposition that has already hardened into a hinge for a given community is not itself a candidate for being softened back into something open to test, since there would be nothing left to test it against. A plausible example is heliocentrism: for early modern astronomers, that the earth orbits the sun was a hypothesis weighed against rival models on the available evidence; for us, it is not something we treat as still open — apparent counter-evidence would far more readily be taken as showing a fault in our instruments than as showing that the earth does not, after all, orbit the sun."
