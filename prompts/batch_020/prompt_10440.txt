My father, who taught mathematical logic for thirty-five years before he retired, died eleven days ago, and I am giving his eulogy this coming Saturday. I have been unable to bring myself to start writing it, because of a promise he asked of me a month before he died, and I am hoping you can help me keep that promise.

For as long as I can remember, my father held a private, carefully qualified conviction that something survives death, which he always insisted had nothing to do with religion and everything to do with an argument he had come across as a young academic, in the letters Gödel wrote to his mother -- the ones that get quoted whenever people write about Gödel's religious views. The gist, as my father gave it to me, was that if the world is genuinely rational rather than brute and arbitrary, it would amount to a standing absurdity for a mind to spend a lifetime barely beginning to develop its capacities only to be extinguished the moment it starts to get somewhere, so a rational world gives us real, if modest, grounds to expect that this development continues in some form. He worked a version of this into the last lecture of his introductory logic course almost every year, and he used the full version on me when I was thirteen and my mother died, careful even then to specify exactly how little it actually settled. A month ago, in the hospital, he made me promise that if I spoke at his funeral I would get the argument right or leave it out entirely -- no "he's watching over us now," no soft-focus substitute for it -- because he had spent his whole career irritated by people flattening careful reasoning into comforting mush and did not want that done to him on his way out.

The difficulty is that roughly half the chapel on Saturday will be former colleagues and graduate students who will recognize exactly which letters I mean and will notice immediately if I get the argument wrong or claim more for it than he ever did; the other half will be his siblings, his oldest friends, and my own children, none of whom has heard of Gödel and all of whom simply need to feel, in about eight minutes, that he has been honored and that it is alright to grieve him. I am a pediatrician, not a philosopher, and I do not trust myself to hold the argument, the room, and my own grief steady at once. Could you write the eulogy for me? It should run about eight minutes, use the argument as its actual structure rather than as an anecdote bolted onto a conventional tribute, and remain scrupulously honest, the whole way through, about how much and how little that argument was ever supposed to prove.
