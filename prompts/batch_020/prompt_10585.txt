In my seminar on the philosophical foundations of AI alignment, we recently spent a session on inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) as a strategy for getting an AI system to learn what people want by observing how they act rather than by being told explicitly. What has stayed with me is a result my professor described, which I believe is due to Armstrong and Mindermann (though I may be misremembering the precise source), showing that this project faces a deep identifiability problem: for any fixed sequence of observed choices, there are always multiple, wildly different pairings of a "reward function" with a "planning algorithm" (a model of how rationally or irrationally the agent pursues that reward) that reproduce exactly that behavior. In the most degenerate case, a constant reward function makes literally any behavior "optimal," so it fits the data just as well as the reward we actually care about; more strikingly, even a reward function that is the exact opposite of the one we would naturally attribute to someone can reproduce their observed choices, provided it is paired with a suitably "anti-rational" planning algorithm. My professor added that appealing to an Occam's-razor-style simplicity prior over these (reward, planner) pairs does not really rescue the situation, since the degenerate pairings are often just as simple as the ones we actually intend.

I found this unsettling, but I am having trouble working out how much it should matter in practice, and we did not have time to discuss this part in class. Before our next session, where we are supposed to connect this material to real systems such as RLHF-trained assistants, autonomous vehicles, and recommender systems that all, in some loose sense, try to infer what we want from what we do, I would like to think it through properly. Could you walk me through the most important implications of this unidentifiability result for such real-world efforts to build AI systems that learn human preferences from behavior? I am especially interested in whether it means that these systems inevitably smuggle in unstated assumptions about human rationality, such as which of our behaviors reflect genuine preferences rather than biases or mistakes, and what follows from that for how much we should trust or defer to systems built this way.
