I'm a retired biochemist -- forty-odd years running enzyme assays, plus entirely too many nights in grad school babysitting fermentations for reasons that felt scientifically justified at the time -- and now that I've got free time I've finally started working through the philosophy classics I always meant to get to. I'm partway through Plato's Laws for a little book club I'm in (mostly other retired science types, apparently we're all doing this at once), and I didnt expect Books I and II to spend so much time on drinking parties. It's alot more specific than the "everything in moderation" one-liner I vaguely remember from a humanities requirement forty years ago. The Athenian Stranger actually argues that regulated wine-drinking, run by a sober "master of the feast," works as a touchstone that tests a man's self-control the same way the Spartans supposedly use manufactured fear to test courage. There's a whole graduated scheme too -- no wine at all for kids, moderate wine once you're a young man, and only once you're older are you actually meant to indulge properly at the Dionysian festivals, because the god's gift is apparently there specifically to loosen up crabby old men so they'll sing and dance and be educated without embarrassment. Magistrates, judges, and soldiers on duty, meanwhile, are supposed to stay completely sober, because in his framing a drunk person has basically lost the capacity to govern himself, let alone anyone else.

So here's the question I want to bring to book club, and I'd like your help thinking it through first: taking that whole specific setup seriously -- the touchstone-for-self-control logic, the graduated-by-age access, the sober supervisor, the explicit rejection of the Spartan/Cretan option of just banning symposia outright -- what would Plato actually say about how we handle intoxicants now? Two cases I keep chewing on: (1) the flat US drinking age of 21 with basically zero graduated exposure beforehand, versus the more supervised, earlier-exposure model you get in parts of Europe, and (2) the newer wave of clinically supervised psychedelic-assisted therapy for PTSD and depression (psilocybin, MDMA-type stuff), where a sober guide walks a patient through an intoxicated state for a therapeutic end -- which structurally sounds a lot like his sober symposiarch to me. Don't just hand me the greeting-card "moderation good, excess bad" answer -- actually work through his specific reasoning and tell me where it would point him on these two cases, and where his own logic might start to break down once you push on it.
