I've been rereading Scott Garrabrant's old "Geometric Rationality" sequence on LessWrong (the one that tries to generalize Kelly betting into this bigger framework that also covers Nash bargaining and exploration-vs-exploitation stuff), and one specific thing in it has been bugging me for weeks. The usual story for why you size bets with the Kelly fraction is a long-run, repeated-trials argument: bet that fraction over and over on the same kind of wager and the law of large numbers guarantees your wealth trajectory beats basically any other strategy almost surely, even though "just maximize expected value every round" has higher arithmetic expectation each and every time. But the geometric rationality framing wants to justify roughly that same fraction a totally different way as well, something like treating the bet as a Nash bargain among a bunch of "possible yous," one per hypothesis you're uncertain over, where each of them only cares about the payoff in the world where their hypothesis turns out true. That second version doesn't obviously need any repetition at all, its supposed to work even for a single decision made once.

So the thing I can't get my head around: is that second justification just the first one wearing a trenchcoat, or can they actually come apart? I want a concrete, numbers-on-paper example of a genuinely one-shot decision, no do-overs, no repeated trials to average over, where the "long run frequency" case for Kelly-style sizing straightforwardly doesn't apply, but the "bargain between your hypothesis-selves" case still pins down a specific fraction to bet, and that fraction isn't what plain one-shot expected-value maximization would recommend. If instead you think the two arguments have to agree once you actually work them out, that's an equally interesting answer, just show me why with a worked example rather than asserting it.

My friend who does quant trading keeps telling me this is all much ado about nothing, that Kelly is just Kelly and the different justifications are decorative, but I don't think he's actually sat down with the single-shot, no-repetition version of the argument. Figured you might actually enjoy chewing on this one.
