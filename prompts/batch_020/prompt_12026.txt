In my Free Will and Moral Responsibility seminar, we just finished Frankfurt's 1969 paper on alternate possibilities, and two classmates ended up arguing about it over coffee afterward. They were working with the standard case from the reading: Black wants Jones to perform a certain action and is monitoring him for any sign that he might decide against it; if such a sign appears, Black intervenes to make Jones do it anyway; but Jones decides to do it entirely on his own, so Black never actually has to act.

(1) Elena said the case simply refutes the Principle of Alternative Possibilities: Jones is obviously blameworthy, yet he could not have done otherwise, since Black was standing by the whole time.

(2) Marcus objected that Jones could have done otherwise in a thin sense: he could at least have begun to show the sign Black was watching for, before being cut off. That flicker of hesitation is itself an alternative possibility, so the principle survives once you grant that even a flimsy alternative counts.

(3) Elena replied that the flicker is irrelevant to responsibility, since nobody holds Jones responsible for a stray twitch he has no control over; calling it "an alternative possibility" changes the subject, because at most it shows that responsibility doesn't require a robust alternative, which was never the point at issue.

(4) Marcus said that's exactly his point: Frankfurt's case only threatens the robust reading of the principle. The plain version — no responsibility without any alternative, robust or not — is left standing, so if anyone is quietly narrowing the thesis to declare victory, it's Elena.

(5) Elena then switched tack: she said the real trouble for Marcus's flicker is a dilemma about how Black detects the sign in the first place. If Black can read it reliably in advance, the case already assumes determinism, which begs the question against a libertarian defender of the principle; but if the sign is merely a reliable-but-not-certain indicator, then right up until Jones decides, it remains genuinely open that he could decide otherwise without ever tipping his hand, so no alternative has actually been closed off.

(6) Marcus answered that this is a problem for that particular version of the case, not for Frankfurt-style cases as such, since a version without any prior sign for Black to monitor could presumably be constructed instead.

I couldn't tell who was getting the better of this. Could you adjudicate it move by move rather than just declaring an overall winner?
