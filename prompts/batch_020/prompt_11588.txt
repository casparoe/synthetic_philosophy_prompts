Two months ago my father was declared dead by neurological criteria after a brain hemorrhage, and the hours our family spent waiting through his apnea test -- followed, the next day, by a transplant coordinator explaining why a different patient down the hall was instead being evaluated for circulatory death -- left me unsettled by how much genuine disagreement sits underneath language I had always assumed was medically settled. I coordinate the monthly bioethics grand-rounds series at the hospital where I work, and I've decided to devote next month's session to this debate. Our invited speaker is a bioethicist who has spent her career defending the whole-brain standard as tracking a real biological fact -- the irreversible loss of the organism's own integrating, self-sustaining functions -- rather than a convenient legal fiction. Draft eight interview questions for her that push past a restatement of that position. I want at least one question on how the integrating-functions rationale survives Shewmon's documented cases of prolonged somatic survival, including continued growth and gestation, after a whole-brain-death diagnosis; one on whether her insistence on irreversibility for brain death is compatible with donation-after-circulatory-death protocols, which declare death on permanence rather than strict irreversibility after only a few minutes of asystole; one on the argument, associated with Truog and Miller, that transplant ethics should abandon the dead donor rule entirely rather than keep stretching the definition of death to accommodate it; and one on the recent, unresolved fight over requiring explicit family consent before apnea testing. For each question, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give.
