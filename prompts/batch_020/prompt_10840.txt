Since retiring from a career as an observational astronomer, I have been reading my way through the philosophical canon I never had time for during my working life, and I recently spent several weeks with G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica. I was struck by a thought experiment in the chapter titled "The Ideal," where Moore asks the reader to imagine two worlds existing in total isolation from any observer: one as beautiful as can be conceived, containing everything we might find lovely in nature or art, and the other as hideous and disordered as can be conceived, with the stipulation that no mind whatsoever will ever perceive either one. He then asks whether it would not still be better for the beautiful world to exist than the ugly one, and takes our apparent inclination to answer yes as evidence that beauty carries intrinsic value quite independently of anyone's experience of it. What I found less expected, reading on, is that a little further in the same chapter Moore argues that among the very highest goods we can conceive are not instances of solitary aesthetic contemplation of this kind, but "organic unities" in which personal affection — what we would now most naturally call friendship, or love — is combined with an appreciation of what is admirable or beautiful in the person toward whom that affection is directed, a combination he holds to be worth considerably more than the simple sum of loving someone and admiring something beautiful separately.

I would like to see these two claims, which sit together rather uneasily, worked out in a short story rather than an essay. Could you write me one, on the order of a page or two, that dramatizes the tension between, on one hand, Moore's insistence that beauty retains its value even if no mind ever encounters it, and on the other, his claim that the highest goods available to us are instead distinctly relational, mattering most when appreciated together with, or through, a personal bond with someone else? I would rather the philosophy stayed beneath the surface of the story than have it named outright — I do not need "Moore" or "Principia Ethica" to appear anywhere in it — but I would like a reader who knows the text to recognize both ideas doing real work in it, and I would like the story to leave some implicit sense, by its end, of how it understands the relationship between the two goods, rather than simply gesturing at both in turn. You are welcome to invent your own characters and setting; if a starting point is useful, I keep picturing something involving a place of great natural beauty that no person will ever set eyes on, though I am happy to leave the particulars to you.
