In my AP Psychology class, we recently covered the Müller-Lyer illusion as evidence that perception doesn't care what you know: even after you've measured the two lines and confirmed they're equal, the illusion doesn't go away, and one line still looks longer than the other. My teacher used this to argue that perceptual processes are basically sealed off from what we believe. That explanation satisfied me at first, but over the last couple of weeks I've been reading around the topic on my own, and it turns out philosophers are far less settled on this than my class made it sound. When I brought it up with a couple of friends from the class, we ended up disagreeing about whether the two views I'd found were even addressing the same question.

On one side, Jerry Fodor's idea of the modularity of mind holds that at least early, low-level visual perception is "informationally encapsulated" from the rest of cognition: beliefs, desires, and expectations can shape how you judge or react to what you see, but supposedly cannot reach into the perceptual experience itself, and the persistence of illusions like Müller-Lyer is offered as a textbook demonstration of this. On the other side, Susanna Siegel argues that perceptual experience itself, and not merely the judgments built on top of it, can be shaped by background beliefs and emotions. I remember one of her examples involves a woman who has irrationally come to believe that a man — Jack, I think his name was — is angry with her, and that this belief actually makes his perfectly neutral expression look angry to her, rather than just prompting her to interpret it that way afterward. (I might be misremembering the name, but the structure of the example is what stuck with me.)

Could you compare these two positions in some depth for me? I'd like you to explain why illusions like Müller-Lyer are supposed to support Fodor's encapsulation thesis, and then explain how Siegel's cases are meant to survive the obvious objection that they're really just cases of a fast, automatic judgment being mistaken for a change in perception itself. I'm also trying to understand what's epistemically at stake in her argument: I recall her wanting to say that if perception can be penetrated by irrational beliefs in this way, it threatens to make some perceptual beliefs unjustified even though they feel every bit as immediate and reliable as any other perception, and I believe she has a specific name for this idea, something like an "epistemic downgrade," though again I'm not fully sure I'm remembering that correctly. Finally, in your own assessment, does one of these positions come closer to being right, or is the more honest answer that Fodor and Siegel are simply describing different phenomena that don't really conflict? Please justify whichever conclusion you reach rather than just asserting it.
