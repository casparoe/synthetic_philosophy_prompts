I co-host a small interview podcast for my high school's philosophy club, where every month we sit down with an academic to discuss whichever thinker our reading group just finished. Next month's guest is a historian of philosophy of science who specializes in the political side of the Vienna Circle -- specifically the claim, made by Carnap and Otto Neurath, that their anti-metaphysical "scientific world-conception" was bound up with a definite political program of social and economic planning, rather than being a neutral logical exercise.

I got pulled into this after a footnote sent me to the Circle's 1929 manifesto, which says outright that the new outlook was meant to help reshape "economic and social life according to rational principles." It was published in the middle of "Red Vienna's" run of socialist municipal reforms, right as Neurath was separately arguing for a planned economy run on "calculation in kind" instead of prices and markets. What I can't get past is that these were the same people who insisted that "ought" statements aren't cognitively meaningful the way scientific statements are -- so by their own logic, nothing in physics or mathematics should have told them how society ought to be organized. Whenever my guest is asked about this in talks, she gives more or less the same answer: that there's no real inconsistency, because dissolving metaphysics and value-talk-as-fact is exactly what lets people hold and act on political commitments honestly, with science only ever supplying neutral means to ends that get fixed elsewhere.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that don't just let her restate that answer? I'd like at least one question pressing on whether "rational principles" and "planning" can really stay as value-neutral as her answer needs them to be, one asking her to distinguish Carnap's own, more cautious commitment from Neurath's much more activist one, one about whether ordinary Circle members experienced the politics as actually following from the epistemology or merely running alongside it, and one on why logical empiricism is mostly remembered today as dry, apolitical technical philosophy despite all this. For each question, add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, and end with one question asking what, if anything, would have to be true of the Circle's actual history for her to admit that her usual answer is a diplomatic gloss rather than a real resolution.
