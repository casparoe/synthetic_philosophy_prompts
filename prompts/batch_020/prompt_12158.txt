I'm reporting a longer piece on the quiet revival of constructive mathematics — the Brouwer/Bishop tradition that insists a proof of "there exists" has to actually hand you the object it's talking about — and I keep coming back to one result that, more than anything else I've read, explains why constructivists are so wary of the axiom of choice. I stumbled on it while trying to explain to a mathematician friend why Errett Bishop was so fussy about which choice principles he would allow into his constructive analysis. The result is usually credited to Diaconescu (1975) on the topos-theoretic side and, independently, to Goodman and Myhill (1978) on the set-theoretic side, and the claim is genuinely startling: in an intuitionistic set theory that still allows full, unrestricted Separation (forming {x : φ(x)} for any propositional formula φ, decided or not), the axiom of choice by itself hands back the full classical law of excluded middle. Before I put a version of this in front of a general-interest readership, I'd like someone who actually knows the logic to look over my reconstruction of the argument.

Here is my sketch of the argument, for an arbitrary, possibly undecided proposition p:
1. By Separation, form A = {x ∈ {0,1} : x = 0 ∨ p} and B = {x ∈ {0,1} : x = 1 ∨ p}. Both sets are inhabited (0 ∈ A and 1 ∈ B, trivially) no matter what p turns out to be.
2. Apply choice to the pair {A, B} to get a function f with f(A) ∈ A and f(B) ∈ B.
3. Equality on {0,1} is decidable, so either f(A) = f(B) or f(A) ≠ f(B).
4. If f(A) ≠ f(B): were p true, A and B would both just be {0,1}, forcing f(A) = f(B) — a contradiction. So ¬p.
5. If f(A) = f(B): unpacking membership gives f(A) = 0 ∨ p, and, since f(A) = f(B), also f(A) = 1 ∨ p. Since 0 = 1 is impossible, working through the combinations always forces p.
Either way, p ∨ ¬p — and since p was arbitrary, that is excluded middle in full generality.

The moral I want to draw is that unrestricted choice was never really the innocent, logic-neutral existence principle it is sometimes sold as: it smuggles classical bivalence in through the back door, which is presumably why Bishop-style constructivism only ever helps itself to weaker principles like countable or dependent choice. But I have also seen it claimed that Martin-Löf type theory treats a version of "choice" as an outright triviality, which makes me suspect I am either conflating two different things called "AC," or unfairly pinning on choice something that unrestricted Separation deserves at least equal blame for. Could you check whether every step above is actually intuitionistically valid — I am least confident about step 4's slide from "A and B are literally the same set" to "f(A) = f(B)"? And could you help me sort out whether choice or comprehension is the more honest thing to blame here, and whether my closing line that the axiom of choice is "secretly classical" is a fair statement for a general readership, or an overstatement?
