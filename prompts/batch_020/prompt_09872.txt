Two colleagues of mine -- Priya, who does con law, and Marcus, who does political theory -- got into this argument at a faculty happy hour last week and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it. It was about entrenchment clauses, the "eternity clauses" like Germany's Art 79(3) that puts human dignity and the basic democratic order permanently beyond the reach of amendment, even by unanimous vote. Priya's line was basically: this is just a Ulysses contract scaled up to the level of a whole polity -- a constitutional order tying itself to the mast against its own future capacity to be seduced into fascism, same as Ulysses tying himself against the Sirens. Marcus said the analogy doesn't hold up: Ulysses bound *himself*, same guy, earlier self constraining later self, and that's exactly what makes it autonomy-respecting rather than just paternalism. An eternity clause binds people born decades later who never voted on anything, so it isn't self-binding at all, it's the founding generation reaching into the grave and grabbing everyone after them by the throat.

Priya came back with: we already accept that earlier generations bind later ones constantly -- debts, property rights, treaties -- so "the binder and the bound aren't the same people" can't be the objection by itself, that's just how intergenerational obligation works generally, nobody calls inheriting your parents mortgage paternalism. Marcus said those cases are disanalogous because they're all revisable through some ordinary mechanism (default, renegotiation, withdrawal), whereas the whole point of an eternity clause is to foreclose revision even through the normal amendment process -- that's the real worry, not mere futurity. Priya said fine, but then his actual objection is about how hard the content is to unlock, not about self-binding versus other-binding as such, and the second you're grading entrenchment by content you need some independent theory of legitimacy anyway -- so the Ulysses framing was never doing the justificatory work for either of them, it cuts both ways.

They both had to run off to office hours before anyone won. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's got the better of it at each point, not just who wins overall -- and flag anything important neither of them raised?
