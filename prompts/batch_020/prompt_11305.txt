In my AP Microeconomics class, our teacher spent a day outside the normal curriculum on the Allais paradox as a supposed counterexample to expected utility theory, and I have not been able to stop thinking about it since. The setup was the classic one: in the first choice, you pick between (A) $1,000,000 for certain and (B) a 10% chance of $5,000,000, an 89% chance of $1,000,000, and a 1% chance of nothing, and most people choose A. In the second choice, you pick between (C) an 11% chance of $1,000,000 (and nothing otherwise) and (D) a 10% chance of $5,000,000 (and nothing otherwise), and most people choose D. Our teacher's claim was that this pair of choices cannot be reconciled with expected utility theory no matter what utility function you assign to money, which struck my friend Priya and me as too strong to accept without seeing the argument.

Here is why we doubted it: expected utility theory does not require indifference to risk, since nothing stops you from evaluating money through a concave utility function instead of raw dollars, and diminishing marginal utility already captures exactly the kind of preference for a sure thing that shows up in choice one. It seemed to us, then, that by making the utility function curved enough, you should be able to make both choices simultaneously consistent with maximizing expected utility, in which case the "paradox" just relabels ordinary risk aversion, and the independence axiom is not doing any work that risk aversion was not already doing on its own. When we raised this after class, our teacher said the failure is structural in a way that no amount of curvature can patch, but we ran out of time before she could show us why. I would rather work through the actual argument than take it on faith: is there any monotonic utility function, no matter how concave, that rationalizes both choices at once, and if there genuinely is not, could you show algebraically why the two choices produce a direct contradiction rather than merely reflecting different risk attitudes?
