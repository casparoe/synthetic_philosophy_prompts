I am a retired experimental physicist who has taken up philosophy in earnest in the three years since I closed my lab for the last time, currently by way of an online philosophy of religion course that has us grade one another's short written assignments in rotation. I confess a certain sympathy for the logical positivists' insistence that a sentence earns the right to be called true or false only once we can say what would count as evidence for or against it, which is presumably why our instructor set Ayer for this week's exercise. As an experimentalist I have always been wary of drawing firm conclusions from a single, unrepeatable data point, so I was curious to see how my assigned partner would handle the one case in Ayer's own career where he himself supplied just such a data point. The assigned question read: "A.J. Ayer argued that some claims about surviving bodily death are not so much false as meaningless, while others are perfectly meaningful even though we have no way of knowing whether they are true. Explain this distinction and Ayer's reasoning for it. Then discuss Ayer's own 1988 near-death experience: what did he, on his own later reflection, take himself to have changed his mind about as a result of it, and what did he explicitly deny having changed his mind about? Use the case to comment on the difference between revising one's confidence in a belief and revising the content of the belief itself." (20 points)

My partner's submitted answer was as follows: "For Ayer, a sentence is factually meaningful only if we can say what observation would confirm or disconfirm it. Applied to personal survival, this yields two cases. If 'survival' means that my body, or an exact physical replica of it bearing my memories, is reconstituted at some future time, that is a perfectly meaningful hypothesis, however unlikely, since we know what it would be like to verify it. But if 'survival' means that some wholly immaterial soul, detached from any body, persists and remains identical to me, no observation could possibly verify this, since our only grip on 'the same person' is through bodily or behavioural continuity. Ayer therefore concluded that claims about a disembodied afterlife of this second kind are false, since a purely spiritual survivor could never be shown to be numerically the same person who died.

Ayer's own brush with death complicates this tidy picture. In 1988 he choked and was clinically dead for several minutes, later writing about a vivid experience of light and of some power governing the universe. On reflection, he seems to have updated only his credence and not the content of his view, the way a scientist might grow less certain of a well-supported hypothesis without yet abandoning it: the experience was, on his own telling, vivid enough that he concluded some form of awareness can apparently outlast the brain that supports it for a short while, which is why he described his settled conviction that bodily death is simply the end of the person as having been overturned by what he underwent. He was always careful to add, however, that none of this had made him a theist."
