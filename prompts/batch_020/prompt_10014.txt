Three of us who used to work together at my first dev job still do a monthly video call where we take turns picking something short for everyone to read and argue about — usually it's sci-fi, but last month Alicia, who's way more into lit and philosophy than the rest of us, picked Borges's essay "A New Refutation of Time." I've got zero philosophy background so I was mostly along for the ride, but afterward Alicia and another guy in the group, Ben, got into it about what the essay actually shows, and I honestly could not tell who was winning. Alicia's position: the essay basically proves the "self" moving through time is a fiction. Borges leans on Hume's bundle theory (no persisting subject, just a pile of disconnected perceptions strung together by memory) plus Berkeley's denial of matter, and she pointed out that Borges apparently wrote the two halves of the essay years apart and just left them as seperate pieces instead of fusing them into one — which she read as him structurally demonstrating his own thesis, since the guy who wrote part one and the guy who wrote part two "weren't even continuous with each other." Ben pushed back that if that were literally true, Borges couldn't have produced a coherent essay at all, even in one sitting, let alone across years — finishing an argument requires exactly the continuity being denied. He also brought up the ending, where Borges admits that even after making the whole argument he still feels stuck in time, still can't help being Borges — Ben's read is that even the author doesn't buy his own conclusion, so the piece is more a gorgeous paradox in the spirit of Zeno than an actual metaphysical claim.

Alicia answered that this conflates two different things: Borges isn't denying the practical, memory-and-habit kind of continuity that let him finish the essay across years or that makes him still feel like "Borges" — that's just more bundle, nothing extra on top. What he's denying is a further fact, some persisting substance underneath the bundle that would make succession "really" real. So neither the two-part composition nor the anguished ending actually contradicts the thesis — they're exactly what you'd expect if the thesis were true. Ben's comeback was that if that's all it amounts to, the essay isn't telling us anything Hume didn't already say two centuries earlier, and nobody serious was defending some extra soul-substance anyway — so either Borges thinks he's shown something more unsettling about time itself than that (in which case her deflationary reading is too weak and his original objection stands), or he really is only saying the modest Humean thing (in which case the dramatic title and the anguished last lines are just theater, not philosophy). Neither of them would concede, and I was way too lost to referee.
