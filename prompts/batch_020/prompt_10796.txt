So I'm about two-thirds into a novel where one of the side characters, an old woman named Marguerite, basically functions as the family's moral oracle -- everybody brings her their dilemmas and just does whatever she says, no questions asked, because she's "always right." My writing group flagged this as a little too tidy, and a friend of mine who's finishing a philosophy MA mentioned that there's a whole area of philosophy about basically this exact setup, called "moral testimony." Naturally I fell down a rabbit hole for a few nights instead of actually writing.

From what I can piece together, the consensus is that moral testimony just doesn't work the way regular testimony does -- I can come to know Lisbon is the capital of Portugal purely because a friend told me so, but apparently I can't actually come to know that, say, eating factory farmed meat is wrong just because Marguerite told me so, even if she's completely right about it. Which got me wondering if this is actually a decent argument for moral anti realism -- like, if moral "facts" can't get transmitted person to person the way regular facts can, doesn't that suggest they were never really facts to begin with, just attitudes wearing a fact costume?

Before I go rewrite Marguerite's whole arc, I'd love a sanity check: am I even representing the moral testimony debate correctly here, and does the anti-realism thing actually follow, or am I overextending something I half-understood at 1am?
