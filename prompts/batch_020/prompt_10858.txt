I'm putting together a short essay for my school's philosophy club on Frederick Douglass's changing views on the U.S. Constitution, and I would really appreciate some feedback before I turn in the final version. I'm most curious whether I've represented the history fairly to both sides and whether the argument in my closing paragraph actually holds up or is too much of a stretch. Here's the draft:

"Most people who know Frederick Douglass at all know him as the man who asked, in his most famous speech, what the Fourth of July means to someone still in bondage. But that speech captures only one moment in a much longer argument Douglass was having with himself, and with the rest of the abolitionist movement, over what the Constitution actually says about slavery.

Through the 1840s, Douglass toured the North as a protégé of William Lloyd Garrison and accepted Garrison's position that the Constitution was irredeemably pro-slavery — a 'covenant with death, and an agreement with hell' that no genuine abolitionist should vote under or even remain in political union with. On this reading, clauses like the three-fifths compromise and the fugitive-slave clause were proof that slavery was built into the nation's foundational law, regardless of what the Preamble said about liberty and justice.

Around 1851, in a reversal that permanently split him from Garrison, Douglass began arguing the opposite: that the Constitution, read on its own terms, is actually an anti-slavery document. He credited Gerrit Smith and the lawyer Lysander Spooner with changing his mind, and he eventually laid out the full case in an 1860 lecture in Glasgow titled 'The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?' The argument was almost pedantically textual: the words 'slave' and 'slavery' appear nowhere in the Constitution. Where the document touches on slavery, it does so obliquely — 'persons' whom certain states might 'think proper to admit,' people 'held to Service or Labour' — language Douglass thought the framers chose deliberately, so the nation's fundamental law would never explicitly recognize property in human beings. Add to that the Preamble's declared purposes, to 'establish Justice' and 'secure the Blessings of Liberty,' and Douglass concluded that the only honest reading of the text was an anti-slavery one, whatever individual framers privately intended.

What I find most interesting isn't the conclusion but the method, because it is almost a mirror image of the method Chief Justice Taney used a few years later in Dred Scott to reach the opposite result — reading the Constitution through what he took to be the framers' actual beliefs about Black Americans to conclude the document could never have meant to include them among 'the people.' Douglass, by contrast, insisted that intentions never written into the text are legally beside the point, and that a legal document must be read by its words and declared purposes. That is a strikingly modern, almost textualist argument. But Douglass's willingness to let the Preamble's abstract principles override the plain effect of specific clauses also looks like a natural-law argument wearing textualist clothing — exactly the move most textualists reject. I don't think Douglass ever resolved that tension, and I suspect he didn't particularly want to, since leaving it unresolved was politically useful: it let him fold the abolitionist movement into electoral politics instead of following Garrison toward disunion."

Beyond fact-checking, I'd love your take on whether the closing paragraph's comparison to Taney and to textualism is doing real philosophical work or is a stretch, and more generally on whether the whole thing reads as an argument rather than just a history recap.
