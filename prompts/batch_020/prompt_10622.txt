I'm taking a seminar on Bentham's moral and legal philosophy, and we've just finished his unpublished manuscripts on sexual morality, material too incendiary to print in his lifetime and available now only through modern critical editions of his papers. What strikes me most is not his conclusion but his method: he ran the same felicific calculus over every category he grouped together as a sexual "irregularity," solitary acts included, and refused to let "unnatural" or "repugnant" count as a reason unless it cashed out as actual pain to someone. For my response paper, I want you to extend that same apparatus, his own rather than Mill's harm principle or anything from contemporary queer theory, to a case he never had to rule on: long-term romantic and sexual relationships between humans and AI companions convincing enough to simulate affection but not obviously sentient. Reconstruct the verdict his principles commit him to, and be explicit about where his commitments on solitary pleasure, on security of expectation, and on the sanctions of law versus of opinion pull against one another before you settle on a single answer.
