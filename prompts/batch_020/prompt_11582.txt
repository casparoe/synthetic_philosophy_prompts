I cover science and economics policy, and for the past year I've been tracking the U.S. government's quiet overhaul of how it discounts the future. In November 2023, OMB retired the old Circular A-4 approach of a flat 7% rate meant to capture the opportunity cost of capital, paired with a flat 3% rate meant to approximate the social rate of time preference, in favor of a single rate tied to long-run government bond yields that starts near 2% and gradually falls the further out the effects occur, recalculated every three years. Something similar happened to the social cost of carbon, which rose from an interim $51 a ton to roughly $190, largely because the assumed discount rate fell from 3% to 2%. Partly on the strength of that reporting, I've been asked to serve as the public member of a new state panel charged with recommending, for the first time, a standing discount-rate policy for capital projects whose costs and benefits run a century or more into the future.

The case forcing the panel's hand is a proposed storm-surge barrier for a low-lying river city. The minimal design is adequate for forty years and then leaks risk badly; the resilient design costs far more up front and pays off almost entirely in flood deaths and property loss avoided between 2080 and 2160. At 7% the resilient design isn't close to justified; at anything under about 2% it wins easily. The panel's chief economist wants to keep something near the old 7%, on the argument that money spent on the barrier is money not compounding elsewhere — an opportunity-cost claim, he insists, not a claim that future lives count for less. A moral philosopher retained by the panel wants the pure time-preference component set at or near zero, on the older argument, running from Sidgwick and Ramsey through Cowen and Parfit, that none of the usual defenses of discounting people's welfare merely for being later — not democratic mandate, not opportunity cost, not any special obligation to the nearer future — actually survives being made explicit, and that at 7% a flood death in 2150 is worth a rounding error today. Both sides accept that a separate, growth-related adjustment to the rate is legitimate; the argument is specifically about the pure time-preference term underneath it.

Could you draft the memo I'll need to circulate before the panel's vote next month? I want a specific methodology, not a single blended rate chosen by feel: it should state its pure time-preference component and its growth/opportunity-cost component separately and justify each, and it should set a review cycle rather than freeze the number forever. It also has to take seriously the strongest case against whatever it recommends — if you land near zero pure time preference, address the standard worry that undiscounted reasoning over long horizons can be used to justify almost any present sacrifice for a sufficiently long chain of small future gains; if you land on a substantial positive rate, address the worry that this is just a more comfortable way of writing off the people who'll still be dealing with the river in 2150. Make it something the panel could actually vote on, not a literature review.
