So I've been building a dumb little side project that scrapes odds off some Polymarket-style prediction markets to see if they move in predictable ways before news actually breaks, and while reading up on why markets are even supposed to be good at aggregating info in the first place, I fell down a wiki hole and hit this thing called the "no-trade theorem." Near as I can tell it says something like: if two people are both rational, both know the other is rational, and both started out with the same prior beliefs about the world, they literally can't agree to a bet based on private information alone -- because the mere fact that the other guy is *willing* to take your bet is itself evidence you should update on, and once you both factor that in enough times it kills off any reason to trade. There's apparently some older, related result by a guy named Aumann about how two rational people "can't agree to disagree" once their opinions are common knowledge, but honestly the wiki page lost me around there.

This seems totally bonkers to me as someone whose seen people trade against each other on election odds all night long. Can you actually unpack the mechanism for me -- like why does someone just being willing to trade with you count as "information" in the formal sense, and why does that unravel the gains from trade instead of just... not? And then, seperately, how do people who think about this stuff actually square it with real markets having insane volume, and with prediction markets still being treated as decent aggregators of belief? Is it mostly that real traders don't share priors, or that a lot of "trade" is really hedging/liquidity stuff the theorem doesn't even apply to, or is there some more interesting resolution I'm missing? I'd love the actual mechanics here rather than just "well obviously real people aren't perfectly rational," which feels like a cop-out.
