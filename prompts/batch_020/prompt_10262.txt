I've spent some time working through Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia on my own, and his Wilt Chamberlain example seems to me like it settles the whole redistribution debate in one stroke. He asks us to start from any distribution of holdings we like, even a strictly equal one, and call it D1. Then a million fans each choose to hand over twenty-five cents of their own money to watch Wilt Chamberlain play basketball over a season, and by the end he's $250,000 richer than anyone else, in a new distribution D2. Since every one of those transfers was voluntary and nobody had a prior claim on anyone else's share, D2 has to count as just too, no matter how unequal it looks next to D1. That seems to prove, quite generally, that once a distribution is fair, no amount of free trading between consenting people can ever turn it into something unfair, which means any tax scheme designed to claw back Wilt's earnings and restore the old pattern is, by definition, committing an injustice against him. Given that the logic seems airtight, I don't understand why professional philosophers still treat the case for redistributive taxation as a live, respectable position rather than something Nozick already closed the book on. What am I missing here?
