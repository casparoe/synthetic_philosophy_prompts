Epistemic status: half-baked shower thought I can't shake, sorry in advance. I've been on a bit of a decision theory kick lately, rereading old LessWrong posts on functional/updateless decision theory that use Parfit's Hitchhiker as the running example: you're dying in the desert, a driver will only give you a lift into town if she's confident you'll actually pay her the $1000 you're promising once you're safe, and the whole puzzle is that once you're safe, paying is pure cost with zero causal upside. FDT's answer is basically "pay her anyway, because the type of decision-maker who reliably pays is the type who gets picked up in the first place" -- no appeal to duty, just to being the right kind of policy. It occured to me that this is structurally the same move David Gauthier makes with "constrained maximization" in Morals by Agreement, which got me wondering whether this decision-theoretic story is secretly *the* answer to "why do promises bind" that philosophers like Scanlon (with his trust-based Principle F) were groping towards all along, just wearing decision-theory clothes instead of moral-philosophy clothes.

Here's the crux case that's bugging me though. Strip the hitchhiker scenario of everything that lets the decision-theoretic story do its work: say I promise my dying, estranged uncle -- who I'll never see, hear from, or be judged by again, who has no other family and no lawyer checking up on me -- that I'll scatter his ashes somewhere specific instead of the cheap default, and then he dies that night. Nobody's running a simulation of me, no future cooperation depends on my reputation, there's no iteration and no detection; being "the type of agent who follows through" buys me literally nothing here that a one-off defector wouldn't also get away with. If FDT-and-Gauthier-style reasoning is really what grounds promissory obligation, the obligation should evaporate right along with the incentive that generates it. Most people's intuitions (mine included, most days) say it doesn't evaporate.

So here's what I want: please argue for the position that promissory obligation really does just reduce to this decision-theoretic story, full stop -- there's no further moral residue once you subtract predictability-based self-interest, and whatever's left of my intuition in the ash-scattering case is a heuristic misfiring rather than evidence of some further, independent obligation. Don't just wave at Scanlon's trust-based objection (or the broader "that's not even what morality is for" objection) -- actually engage it and say why it fails. I want the strongest, most bullet-biting version of this view, not a strawman that folds the second someone brings up a sad uncle.
