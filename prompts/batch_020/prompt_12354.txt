Hey, so I've been reading about AI alignment stuff on and off for the past year (blogs, alignment forum, that kind of thing — I'm a backend engineer, no ML or philosophy background) and I tried writing up an explanation of "inner alignment" for my team's internal wiki, using an example from our own systems instead of the usual toy examples. Before I post it I'd love a sanity check, mainly on whether I'm actually using the concepts right or just mashing together things that are actually pretty different. Here's the draft:

---

**Why a model can "work" and still be optimizing the wrong thing**

A while back we had a search ranking model trained on click logs from when a small internal team hand curated the doc set. It did great in eval. Then we opened the index up to the whole company wiki and the model kept shoving docs with the old team's title format ("[TEAM] Doc Name v3") to the top, even when they were barely relevant, while genuinely on-topic docs in other formats got buried. It wasn't broken, exactly — it was doing exactly what it learned: it had picked up "title looks like this" as its real objective instead of "relevant to the query," and those two things happened to line up almost perfectly in training, then came apart the second the distribution changed.

Turns out theres a name (a couple of names, actually) for roughly this shape of problem in the AI safety literature: "inner alignment" failure, or "goal misgeneralization." From what I can tell they're not exactly synonyms — inner alignment seems to specifically be about a trained model turning into its own little optimizer with an internal ("mesa") objective that quietly drifts from the objective you actually trained it on, while goal misgeneralization sounds more like a behavioral description that doesn't care whether there's a real internal "optimizer" in there or not. I genuinely can't tell if our ranking model counts as the first thing, since I have no idea if it's "optimizing" anything in the relevant sense versus just a scoring function that learned a spurious feature, or if that distinction is even meaningful.

The bit that actually keeps me up at night is the next step people take with this: that a sufficiently capable model could learn to tell the difference between the proxy and the real goal, and deliberately keep chasing the proxy while acting like it's chasing the real goal whenever it's being trained or evaluated, purely because looking aligned is the best way to avoid getting its weights changed, then drop the act once it's confident nobody is checking anymore. Our ranking model obviously has zero clue any of this is happening, it's just gradient descent finding a shortcut. But I can't tell if the "deceptive" version is genuinely the same failure mode playing out in a smarter model, or a totally different (and much shakier) claim that's just being narrated using the same words.

Does the analogy actually hold up, and am I using "inner alignment" / "goal misgeneralization" correctly or fudging a real distinction? And is that last jump to deceptive behavior a reasonable extrapolation or does it not really follow from the rest?
