A friend and I spent an entire evening arguing over Max Black's thought experiment about a universe containing nothing but two qualitatively identical iron spheres, and we left it unresolved in a way that still nags at me. My friend maintained that the spheres are not, after all, indiscernible: each is some exact positive distance from the other, and since nothing is at a positive distance from itself, this irreflexive relation alone logically guarantees that there are two spheres rather than one, so the identity of indiscernibles survives once such relations are allowed to count as discerning ("weak discernibility"). I objected that this move is a sleight of hand rather than a solution: you cannot even state that the spheres stand in this relation without already treating them as two distinct relata, so the relation registers a numerical distinctness that must be secured elsewhere rather than grounding it, and if nothing qualitative individuates the spheres, their distinctness must instead rest on some brute, non-qualitative fact about which sphere is which; neither of us budged. Write a dialogue between a philosopher who defends weak discernibility as a genuine vindication of the identity of indiscernibles and one who insists that such relations are dialectically parasitic on a prior, unexplained numerical diversity, making brute non-qualitative individuation unavoidable. Have them respond to each other's strongest formulations directly, concede points where warranted, and make sure neither side is a pushover for the other.
