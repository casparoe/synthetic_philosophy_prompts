Okay, this one's a bit random but it's been stuck in my head for weeks. At a friend's going-away party last month I had a few too many and got into this whole argument with a guy I'd just met over some dumb historical trivia thing (don't even ask what, it's embarrassing). I was SO sure I was right in the moment, like genuinely lucid-feeling, and by everyone's account I "won." Next morning my friend Priya goes "yeah but you'd had like four beers, that doesn't count as you being right, that's just confident-you talking," which annoyed me way more than it should have, because in the moment it really didn't feel like drunk-reasoning, it felt like reasoning. I mentioned this to my housemate, who's finishing up an epistemology PhD, and apparently I'd wandered right into an actual debate about "higher-order evidence" -- whether its ever rational to keep believing something once you've got good evidence that your reasoning about it, right then, wasn't trustworthy. She had a name for the bad combo, "epistemic akrasia," believing something while also believing you have no business believing it, and something she called the "akratic principle" (or "enkratic," I forget which way round it goes) that's supposed to rule that combo out.

I co-run a tiny philosophy podcast (low three digits of listeners, mostly my mom and some guys from my cohort) and we've got a guest booked next month who takes the harder line on this: that akrasia of that kind can be totally rational, and that you're not obligated to let evidence of your own unreliability infect the belief itself. I don't want forty minutes of her just restating the position, I want to actually push on it. Can you draft me eight or nine interview questions that go past a restatement? I want at least one that uses a case like mine, or one of the classic drug-and-logic-puzzle cases from the literature, to ask how her view is different from just "it felt right to me, so it was rational"; one on the worry that the view hands a free pass to motivated reasoners and conspiracy-type thinkers who wave away real evidence of their own bias; one on the regress problem -- if higher-order evidence can undermine a first-order belief, what stops third-order evidence from undermining confidence in that higher-order judgment, and why does the regress get to stop wherever she stops it; and one on whether the whole picture survives once you swap flat belief for degrees of confidence, since it seems even weirder to be 90% confident in something while also being 90% confident your evidence doesn't support that much confidence. For each one, add a short follow-up keyed to whatever answer she's most likely to give, so it's not just a list of questions lobbed and abandoned.
