At our company's holiday party this year, HR ran a two-round prize wheel that anyone could play, and I've been turning it over ever since, because my own money was riding on it. In the first round you chose between (A) a guaranteed $1,000, or (B) a spin giving an 89% chance at $1,000, a 10% chance at $5,000, and a 1% chance of nothing. In a second, separate round, the choice was between (C) an 11% chance at $1,000 and an 89% chance of nothing, or (D) a 10% chance at $5,000 and a 90% chance of nothing. I picked A and then D, and so, it turned out, did most of the room, including two colleagues, Priya and Sam, both of whom had studied economics and immediately recognized what had happened: without meaning to, we'd reenacted the Allais paradox, the famous case in which those two choices, taken together, can't be squared with any expected-utility function.

That recognition set off a running argument between the two of them that still hasn't been resolved, and since it's my money on the line, I'd like an outside judgment on who's actually right.

1. Priya said the pattern is a plain inconsistency: replacing the 89% chance of $1,000 shared by A and B with the 89% chance of nothing shared by C and D shouldn't flip anyone's preference if they're maximizing expected utility, so the room's choices reveal real irrationality, full stop.
2. Sam said that moves too fast: Allais's whole point was that the independence axiom isn't a self-evident law of rationality -- getting rid of risk entirely is worth something on its own (the "certainty effect") -- and a theory that gives certainty this special weight can rank all four options in a perfectly orderly, non-arbitrary way. It just won't be an expected-utility theory, and there's no reason it has to be one.
3. Priya answered that "orderly under some other theory" isn't enough, because any theory that still recommends A over B and D over C can be exploited: chain the two decisions into a single multi-stage bet, and a clever counterpart can extract a guaranteed loss from you, which is exactly the signature of a genuinely incoherent set of preferences, whatever theory generates them.
4. Sam replied that this isn't free either: it only works if you're also committed to treating the chained, multi-stage version as equivalent to the two separate one-shot decisions, and to re-deciding from scratch at every branch. Someone who instead commits to a plan up front and follows through can't be exploited that way, so at most Priya has shown that one particular style of chooser is vulnerable, not that dropping independence is irrational as such.

Neither of them will concede the other's last point. Could you go through the exchange turn by turn and say, for each of the four numbered moves, whether Priya or Sam has the better argument (or whether both are partly right), with a sentence or two of justification for each verdict? Then close with a short paragraph on the larger question actually dividing them -- whether the wheel simply caught people, myself included, making a mistake, or exposes a genuine weak point in treating the independence axiom as a requirement of rationality -- and flag anything you think both of them are overlooking.
