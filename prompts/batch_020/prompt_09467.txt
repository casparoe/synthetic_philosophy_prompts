My dad died two weeks ago -- Vietnam vet, artillery, '68 to '69 -- and I've been asked to give the eulogy, which is terrifying for reasons that have nothing to do with grief and everything to do with something weirdly specific he believed. I cover science and philosophy for a living (not war reporting, more the "why do smart people disagree" beat), and a few years back I interviewed some just war theorists for a piece. My dad actually read the book I kept mentioning around the house, Walzer's Just and Unjust Wars, and got attached to the "moral equality of soldiers" idea in it -- that whether your allowed to kill and be killed in a war doesn't depend on which side has the just cause, so a soldier who follows the rules isn't a murderer even if his own country started the war for rotten reasons. He told me more than once that he did NOT want anyone at the funeral saying Vietnam was some righteous cause (he'd stopped believing that decades ago), but he also didn't want anyone implying that he, personally, or the NVA guy who put shrapnel in his leg, were murderers. To him those were the same mistake wearing two different faces.

Here's the wrinkle. My cousin is active duty, two tours, and has read her Jeff McMahan -- she thinks the whole moral-equality-of-combatants idea is exactly the comforting fiction his work spends a whole book taking apart, and that if the cause was unjust, the equality doesn't actually hold up morally, whatever use it still has as a legal convention. She's not going to stand up and litigate any of this at the funeral. But she'll be sitting there, and so will my dad's old army buddies, who have never heard of either book and just want to remember a guy who served.

Could you write the eulogy? Something like 5-6 minutes read aloud. I want the moral equality of combatants to actually be the spine of it, not a name-drop -- doing the work of explaining what my dad could and couldn't let himself believe about his own war -- but still something a room full of non-philosophers can just feel, and sturdy enough that it doesn't quietly fall apart if my cousin spends ten seconds during the reception thinking about McMahan's objection to it.
