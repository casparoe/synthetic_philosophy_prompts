In a graduate seminar on philosophy of mathematics, we have recently been discussing intuitionistic logic's rejection of the law of excluded middle, and I would like some help making sure I have understood correctly what this rejection actually commits one to, since the picture I have pieced together strikes me as rather strange. As I currently understand it, because intuitionists refuse to assert P ∨ ¬P as a universally valid law, they are thereby committed to holding that a presently open conjecture, such as Goldbach's conjecture, is neither true nor false as things stand, and that it would only acquire one of those two statuses once a proof or refutation is actually produced. On this reading, intuitionistic logic effectively introduces a third status alongside truth and falsity for any statement that outruns our current proof or refutation, so that its real departure from classical logic lies in an outright abandonment of bivalence, making it closer in spirit to three-valued or fuzzy logics than to a mere restriction of classical inference.

I would appreciate clarification on whether this is in fact the correct way to understand the intuitionist's position, since something about it strikes me as odd: it seems peculiar that a proof discovered next year could retroactively promote a statement from having no truth-value today to being true, as though truth itself were unstable over time. If this picture is mistaken, I would be grateful for an explanation of exactly where it goes wrong, and of how intuitionists, as constructivists who already reject the idea of mathematical facts holding independently of our ability to prove them, would actually characterize the status of an as-yet-undecided statement like Goldbach's conjecture.
