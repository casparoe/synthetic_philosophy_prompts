I'm a PhD student working mainly in philosophy of logic, currently cross-registered in a graduate seminar on Madhyamaka thought, and I've run into an interpretive puzzle about Nāgārjuna's use of the catuṣkoṭi (the "four-cornered" schema: p; not-p; both p and not-p; neither) that none of our assigned secondary readings tackle head-on. I'd like to lay out the relevant texts and get an outside opinion on the underlying logical structure, rather than just take our instructor's gloss on faith.

In the opening verse of the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā (1.1), Nāgārjuna uses the fourfold schema to reject every option at once: no existing thing, he says, arises from itself, from something other than itself, from both, or without a cause—so that, in the sense relevant to inherent existence, nothing ever really arises. The same move, closing off all four corners, recurs near the end of the text, in the examination of nirvāṇa, where he denies that the Tathāgata can be said to exist, not exist, both, or neither after his final passing. But in Chapter 18 (verse 8, in most editions), right after a verse about the Buddhas teaching "self," "non-self," or neither depending on the listener, Nāgārjuna seems to switch registers entirely. In paraphrase (I'd rather not put exact words in a particular translator's mouth), it runs roughly: "everything is real, and everything is unreal; everything is both real and unreal, and everything is neither real nor unreal—this is the Buddha's own teaching." Here all four corners are affirmed together rather than closed off, and the affirmation itself is credited to the Buddha.

What I can't work out is how to characterize the relationship between these two uses of the very same four-part structure without simply calling Nāgārjuna inconsistent and moving on. Is the shift best handled by treating each of the four horns in 18.8 as true only relative to different audiences or stages of instruction—in the spirit of the two-truths move a commentator like Candrakīrti might make—so that nothing is affirmed and denied from one and the same standpoint at once? Or is there real force to the reading associated with Garfield and Priest, on which Nāgārjuna's texts commit him to accepting certain claims together with their negations, so that a paraconsistent or dialetheist logic is needed to model his considered position rather than explain it away? I'd find it useful to have the actual disagreement between these two styles of reading spelled out—what textual or philosophical considerations favor one over the other—and to hear whether there's some third way of relating the negative and affirmative uses of the tetralemma that I'm overlooking.
