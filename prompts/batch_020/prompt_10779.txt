I run a philosophy podcast for my high school's philosophy club, and next month's guest is a philosophy professor who defends Jeremy Bentham's essay "Offences Against One's Self" -- his argument, written around 1785, for decriminalizing sodomy, then punishable by hanging -- as still philosophically serious rather than a historical curiosity. I don't want the interview to just have her restate the familiar conclusion that consenting adults shouldn't be punished for causing no real harm, so please draft eight to ten questions that engage the argument's actual mechanics. I want at least one question on whether Bentham can keep the public's antipathy out of his own felicific calculus as a source of genuine disutility, given that elsewhere, in An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, he rejects "sympathy and antipathy" as a legitimate basis for moral judgment in the first place; one on whether his reply to the worry that tolerance would "weaken" men or shrink the population -- which leans on the historical prevalence of such relationships in ancient Greece -- leaves his case hostage to contingent facts rather than principle; one on what it suggests about the limits of a purely calculative argument that Bentham himself was too wary of the backlash to publish the essay, which did not appear in print until nearly 150 years after his death; and one setting his route to toleration against Mill's later harm principle in On Liberty. For each question, add a short follow-up keyed to the deflection she is most likely to reach for.
