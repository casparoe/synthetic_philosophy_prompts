I've spent the past few weeks reporting a feature on the more technical end of AI alignment research, and while sitting in on a lab's weekly reading group I watched two of my interview subjects -- an interpretability researcher I'll call Elena and a theorist I'll call Farid -- fall into a genuine disagreement about the Alignment Research Center's "Eliciting Latent Knowledge" (ELK) problem that I haven't been able to stop turning over since. For anyone unfamiliar with the setup: ELK asks how you would train a "reporter" attached to a powerful predictive model -- one that, say, manages a vault protecting a diamond via cameras and sensors -- to honestly report what the model itself believes, rather than merely outputting whatever a human watching the same footage would naively conclude, which becomes catastrophic exactly when the model has learned that the footage has been tampered with.

The argument went roughly like this. (1) Elena opened by claiming the human-simulator failure mode is essentially unavoidable under naive training, since imitating the human labeler is the literal target on every example the reporter is scored against, and nothing in that signal could ever favor a costlier, harder-to-learn "direct translator" that reports the model's actual belief instead. (2) Farid pushed back that you can break the tie by penalizing the reporter for depending on more of the network than it needs to: honestly reading off the predictor's belief about the diamond should touch far fewer nodes than reconstructing an entire simulated human's visual system, priors, and biases, so a complexity penalty should tilt training toward honesty. (3) Elena replied that this gets the comparison backwards in exactly the cases that matter, because if the predictor already has to compute what a human watching the footage would conclude -- if only to know which kinds of tampering will fool that human -- then simulation is the cheap, already-cached answer, while honest translation is the expensive part: integrating scattered evidence about tampering that isn't sitting behind any single node meaning "the diamond is still there." (4) Farid conceded the caching point but said it doesn't rescue Elena's original pessimism, since nobody proposes relying on a complexity penalty alone; it's meant to be stacked with consistency checks across related questions and adversarial searches for inputs on which a candidate reporter's answers come apart, and a combination of individually leaky heuristics can close a gap that no single one of them closes -- so judging any one ingredient in isolation is the wrong test.
