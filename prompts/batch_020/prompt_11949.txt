Spent thirty-some years running a lab on tRNA synthetases before I retired, and now that I've got time on my hands I've fallen into reading philosophy of biology, which is how I got stuck on this question of whether the genetic "code" is an actual code or just a metaphor biology borrowed from Shannon back in the 50s and never gave back. Can't let it go. Write me a dialogue between two old grad-school friends, both retired now, who run into each other at a department reunion and end up picking back up an argument they apparently never finished, standing in front of a big framed poster of the standard codon table hanging in the hallway.

One of them, a code realist, thinks its a real code because the codon-to-amino-acid mapping is arbitrary (nothing chemically necessary ties UUU to phenylalanine rather than some other amino acid -- that's the whole point of Crick's frozen accident idea), the mapping only holds because of a physical adaptor sitting in between (tRNA, exactly the molecule Crick predicted years before anyone found it), and there's real normativity wired into the system -- synthetases have proofreading domains that catch and hydrolyze mischarged tRNAs, so there's a built-in fact about correct versus incorrect pairing, not something we're just projecting onto the chemistry.

The other, a skeptic, thinks none of that adds up to semantics. Arbitrariness plus error-correction still isn't meaning without a reader or interpreter somewhere; "information" language got bolted onto molecular biology because cybernetics was the fashionable vocabulary of the moment the code was cracked; and "error" in a synthetase just names a molecular event with bad downstream fitness consequences, not a normative mistake in any richer sense.

Have them really engage each other's specific points instead of talking past each other -- neither one's a strawman, they're both sharp and have clearly had this fight before. And don't let it resolve neatly. I want it to feel like a real argument between old friends that runs out of time before it runs out of steam.
