So this actually happened on a committee I'm on at work last week and I've been low-key obsessed with it since. I'm on a 3-person panel that decides promotions, and the rule is: someone gets promoted only if they clear BOTH the "technical bar" and the "leadership bar," which we determine by majority vote on each bar separately. We did that, tallied it up, and the result was "promote." Then our HR partner, just out of curiosity, asked us to also do one straight up-or-down vote on "promote this person, yes or no, overall" — and the majority came out AGAINST promoting, using the exact same three people who hadn't changed their minds about anything. Nobody flip-flopped, nobody lied, the numbers just came out opposite depending on how we sliced the vote. I brought this up with a friend who's a lawyer and she said "oh that's basically the doctrinal paradox," which sent me down a rabbit hole into judgment aggregation theory and something called the discursive dilemma.

From what I've pieced together, this isn't just a fluke of my particular commitee — List and Pettit apparently turned this exact issue into a general impossibility theorem for judgment aggregation, kind of an Arrow's theorem for panels/courts/committees, and there's a whole "premise-based vs. conclusion-based procedure" debate in the literature about which way groups are even supposed to vote when this happens. What I don't have is any gut-level feel for *why* issue-by-issue voting and direct-outcome voting have to be capable of splitting apart like this, as opposed to it just being a coincidence that happened to hit my three coworkers on one random Tuesday. Is there some minimal structural fact about how the "bars" logically connect to the verdict that guarantees a divergence like this is always possible for some set of honest opinions, no matter how you design the vote?

Could you walk me through the underlying logic in an intuitive way? I keep seeing terms like "systematicity," "anonymity," and "universal domain" thrown around in summaries of the List-Pettit result and I can't map them back onto my dumb promotion-committee example at all. I'd rather you build the intuition up from concrete cases (mine, or the classic multi-judge legal version) than just restate the axioms abstractly — and if you have a take on whether premise-based or conclusion-based voting is the "less wrong" fix, I'd be curious to hear it too.
