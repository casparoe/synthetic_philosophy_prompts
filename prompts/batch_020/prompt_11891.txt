A colleague of mine, Aaron, has had this running joke for as long as I've worked with him: he ends every single status update he gives, whether it's a two-minute standup or a full report to the department, with the exact same deadpan line: "And remember, nothing I just told you is true, including this sentence." It apparently started years ago as a joke about how status reports in our line of work are always unrealistically upbeat, but it's become such a fixed habit that he tacks it on even to the most mundane, easily-checkable updates, like "the server migration finished Tuesday, and remember, nothing I just told you is true, including this sentence."

This has turned into a real, ongoing disagreement on the team about how we're supposed to treat what he tells us. One camp says the closing line is just noise, and we should judge each factual claim in the update on its own merits, exactly as we would if he'd never added it. The other camp says that once you actually take the sentence seriously as part of the report, it drags everything else down with it: if nothing he told us is true, that has to include the migration update too, so in practice you can never take anything he says at face value until you've independently checked it, which makes the whole report pointless. When I sat down and tried to work out for myself, just privately, whether that closing sentence is true or false, I couldn't land on a stable answer either way, which is a strangely unsettling feeling for something that sounds like such a plain, throwaway remark. I don't have any formal background in this sort of thing; it's just been nagging at me since our last planning meeting.

What I'd like to understand is what's actually going on with a sentence like that: whether it can be true or false at all, and if not, whether that has any real bearing on the ordinary, checkable claims he makes in the same breath. And practically speaking, I'd like you to settle our office argument: is the "just ignore the closing line" camp right, the "it poisons the whole report" camp right, or is there a sharper way of framing the situation that shows both sides are missing something?
