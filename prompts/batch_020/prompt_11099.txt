"Lewis's puzzle starts from the fact that ordinary objects seem to change their intrinsic properties over time while remaining one and the same object. His go-to example is shape: when I'm sitting down my legs are bent, when I'm standing up they're straight, and bent-shaped and straight-shaped are incompatible properties. Since shape is supposed to be a paradigm intrinsic property -- a property you have just in virtue of how you are, not in virtue of your relations to anything else -- it looks like one and the same person has two incompatible intrinsic properties, which seems contradictory.

Lewis says there's three ways out. First, you could be a presentist: only the present moment is real, so there's no other time for the bent-shape to clash with, since strictly nothing else exists to compare it to. Second, you could say intrinsic properties are really disguised relations to times, so I don't have bent-shape simpliciter, I have bent-shape-at-t1 and straight-shape-at-t2, and these are perfectly compatible since they're different relations. Third, you could be a perdurantist: objects are extended through time the way they're extended through space, made up of temporal parts, and it's really the temporal parts that have the shapes, not the whole four-dimensional object.

Lewis rejects the first option because he's independently committed to eternalism (past and future are just as real as the present, just located elsewhere), and he rejects the second option because it makes intrinsic properties not really intrinsic after all -- if shape is just a relation to a time, then nothing is ever shaped 'on its own,' which defeats the purpose of calling it intrinsic. So he ends up endorsing perdurance.

On the perdurance view, strictly speaking it isn't the whole four-dimensional 'worm' that's bent or straight, it's the momentary part, and the part that exists right now is the only part that's really 'me' in the fullest sense -- my existing yesterday or tomorrow is really a matter of some other, numerically distinct part standing in the right counterpart relation to this one, exactly parallel to how Lewis handles de re modal claims with counterpart theory across possible worlds. That's how Lewis can say I'm not literally identical to my bent-legged self of five minutes ago, but I don't need to be, since ordinary persistence-talk is just made true by the counterpart relation holding between us.

One problem for this whole package, which Lewis himself raises as a further worry, involves a perfectly homogeneous spinning disk. If the disk is rotating and totally homogeneous, a static, non-rotating disk in the same shape would have exactly the same series of temporal parts with exactly the same intrinsic properties arranged the same way -- nothing in the pattern of the parts alone says which bit of disk-stuff is 'where' from one moment to the next. But intuitively the rotating disk and the stationary disk are different, so perdurantism seems to leave out a real difference between them.

A perdurantist can respond by denying that persistence is purely a matter of the qualitative arrangement of temporal parts -- they can add that parts get unified into a single continuant partly by causal or nomic relations between successive parts, not just their intrinsic qualities, which lets the rotating and static disks come out different even though their parts look alike at every instant."
