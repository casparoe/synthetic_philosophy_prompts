Here's what I've got:

Aristotle's puzzle (De Interpretatione, ch. 9) is basically: if it's already true today that "there will be a sea battle tomorrow," doesnt that mean the battle is already fixed/necessary, so nothing is really contingent anymore? People still argue about whether Aristotle's own answer was "future contingent statements just aren't true or false yet" or "they're true or false, that just doesn't make the event necessary" -- aparently even that much is contested.

The formal-logic version of this comes from Arthur Prior in the 50s/60s, who builds "branching time" models (a tree of possible histories from now) and identifies two rival ways to evaluate a future-tense claim at a given moment: the "Peircean" reading (after C.S. Peirce, hope I'm not butchering that name), where "it will be that p" only counts as true now if p happens on literally every branch -- so it collapses into "necessarily p," no genuinely open future gets a positive truth value -- versus the "Ockhamist" reading, where you evaluate relative to one specific branch/history, so truth is relative-to-a-history unless you also claim one branch is THE actual future (people call that posited branch the "thin red line," and whether that even makes sense is apparently its own whole debate).

Here's my "aha" moment: this is literally the setup behind CTL, where you've got path quantifiers A (all paths) and E (exists a path) over a tree of every possible execution from the current state. My first instinct was "CTL = Ockhamism, and whatever trace the program actually executes at runtime is the thin red line." But then I talked myself out of it, because nothing in CTL's actual semantics ever singles out one path as "the real one" -- you just quantify over the whole tree, which sounds more Peircean/supervaluationist to me (I think the gappy version is due to a guy named Thomason?). Meanwhile LTL, which checks a formula against one fixed trace, feels like the truer Ockhamist analogue, since it's explicitly relative-to-a-history.

So my tentative claim is: LTL ~ Ockhamist, CTL ~ Peircean, and "thin red line" talk only sneaks back in informally, after the fact, when we point at whichever trace actually ran. Is that basically right, or does it fall apart somewhere -- e.g., because CTL's branching usually just represents "we don't know what the scheduler will pick," not genuine metaphysical openness, which maybe isn't the same kind of "open future" Aristotle and Prior actually cared about? Would love your honest take on both the accuracy of the philosophy and on whether this whole mapping is actually illuminating or just a coincidence of tree-shaped diagrams.
