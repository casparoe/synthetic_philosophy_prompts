(a) State the problem clearly, and explain why it's supposed to be a special difficulty for actualist HOT theories in particular. And here's the student's answer:

"HOT theories say a mental state M is conscious exactly when the subject has a suitable higher-order thought (HOT) representing herself as being in M; the HOT itself standardly stays non-conscious, otherwise regress threatens. Rosenthal builds this monitoring capacity on the model of an inner scanner: the mind runs something like a perceptual mechanism that continuously scans first-order states and produces higher-order representations of them, much like our senses scan the outside world. The targetless representation problem points out that a scanning mechanism, like any perceptual system, can misfire: it can produce a HOT representing the subject as being in pain when no pain-state exists at all. Since on the HOT theory it's the HOT alone that makes a state conscious, we get the strange result that the subject is having a conscious experience of pain even though no pain-state is there to be conscious of — consciousness without anything for it to be consciousness of.

Carruthers thinks his dispositionalist version escapes this neatly: on his view a state is conscious just by being available to a higher-order-thought-forming system, i.e. disposed to produce a HOT, so there doesn't need to be any actual, occurrent higher-order thought at all. Since misrepresentation is a property of actual representational states, and dispositionalism trades in unactualized dispositions rather than actual representations, there's simply nothing there that could misrepresent — so the targetless problem doesn't even arise for Carruthers, only for Rosenthal's actualist picture.

Rosenthal's own response is to bite the bullet: these cases are just introspective errors, on a par with ordinary cases where we misjudge our own mental states, and what matters for consciousness is how things seem to the subject, not whether the seeming is accurate."
