So this is a weirdly specific one but here goes. I do tariff classification at a customs brokerage -- my job is basically arguing whether product X legally counts as product Y, in the grand tradition of Nix v. Hedden, the 1893 Supreme Court case that ruled a tomato is legally a "vegetable" for tariff purposes even though its botanically a fruit. We just got handed a new case in exactly that vein, and I've been half-joking to coworkers that we should start citing ancient Chinese philosophy at customs -- except it turns out there's an actual precedent for the joke. Gongsun Long's "White Horse Dialogue," from the old School of Names, seriously argues that a white horse is not a horse (白馬非馬). From some digging, people read this at least three ways: as a mix-up between "is identical to" and "is a member of," as a technical point about Chinese nouns behaving like mass terms (so "horse-stuff" plus "white-stuff" isn't the same stuff as "horse-stuff" alone), or as a deeper claim about names not tracking things the way we assume. I can't tell if Gongsun Long was onto something real that bears on classification fights like mine, or if it's just clever wordplay a first-year logic student could dismantle in five minutes. Write me an essay that lays out the strongest version of each reading, actually weighs whether any survives as genuine insight rather than equivocation dressed up as paradox, and lands on a definite verdict instead of "well, it depends" -- I want to know if I get to keep the joke, or if my coworkers are right to roll their eyes at me.
