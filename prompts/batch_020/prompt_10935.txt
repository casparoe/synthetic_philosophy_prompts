I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am currently reporting a longer piece on the resemblance between the Buddhist doctrine of no-self (anatta) and Derek Parfit's reductionist account of personal identity. Nearly every popular treatment of anatta I have come across leans on the same well-worn passage from the Milindapanha: King Milinda asks the monk Nagasena to point to the "chariot" among its wheels, pole, and axle, and concludes that "chariot," like "self," is merely a conventional label for a bundle of parts. That part of the argument I follow readily enough. But looking for the portion of the text that does real philosophical work, rather than merely supplying a tidy analogy, I went back to the original 1890 Rhys Davids translation (Sacred Books of the East, vol. 35) and found myself stuck on the chapter immediately following the chariot simile, where Milinda presses Nagasena specifically on personal continuity and rebirth. I would welcome some help working through it before I attempt to paraphrase it for readers.

Here is the exchange, lightly trimmed, that has me stuck: "The king said: 'He who is born, Nagasena, does he remain the same or become another?' 'Neither the same nor another.' ... 'Now, is it the same flame that burns in the first watch of the night, Sir, and in the second?' 'No.' ... 'Then is there one lamp in the first watch, and another in the second, and another in the third?' 'No. The light comes from the same lamp all the night through.' 'Just so, O king, is the continuity of a person or thing maintained. One comes into being, another passes away; and the rebirth is, as it were, simultaneous. Thus neither as the same nor as another does a man go on to the last phase of his self-consciousness.'" A little later in the same chapter, Milinda asks what it actually is that gets reborn. "Name-and-form is reborn," Nagasena answers, and when the king asks whether it is "this same name-and-form," he denies it: "by this name-and-form deeds are done, good or evil, and by these deeds (this Karma) another name-and-form is reborn." When Milinda objects that this ought to let the newly reborn being off the hook for the old one's crimes, Nagasena has him judge the case of a man caught stealing mangoes from a tree, who protests that the fruit he took cannot make him guilty, since "those that he put in the ground are different from the ones I took." The king rejects the defense: "he would be guilty in respect of the last mango which resulted from the first one."

What I would like help with is the underlying logical structure of Nagasena's position, rather than a restatement of it. Is "neither the same nor another" a genuine third option, distinct from both strict identity and straightforward non-identity, or is it, on inspection, just a weaker, causally continuous relation dressed up to sound more paradoxical than it actually is? Does the mango case suggest that, for Nagasena, moral responsibility across time - whether within a single life or across a rebirth - never required strict numerical identity in the first place, so that rebirth is simply a more dramatic instance of something already true of ordinary persistence, or is there something specific to the technical notion of "name-and-form" doing further work here that I am missing? And finally, I would value your sense of whether this passage genuinely anticipates Parfit's claim that identity is not "what matters" in survival, or whether that comparison, however popular among writers on Buddhism and analytic philosophy, papers over some real difference between the two arguments.
