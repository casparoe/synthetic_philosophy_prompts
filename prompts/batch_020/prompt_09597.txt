I'm grading take-home finals for my graduate seminar, "Gödel, Minds, and Machines," and I'd like a second, independent read on one submission before I finalize the mark. The question was:

*State the Lucas–Penrose argument against mechanism, as standardly derived from Gödel's first incompleteness theorem. Explain the classic objection to the argument that appeals to Gödel's second incompleteness theorem. Then explain how Penrose, in Shadows of the Mind, modifies Lucas's original argument in light of this objection, and assess whether the modification succeeds in escaping it.*

Here is the student's answer in full:

> Lucas's argument (1961) starts from Gödel's first incompleteness theorem: for any consistent formal system F rich enough to represent elementary arithmetic, there is a sentence G(F), constructed by the diagonal lemma, such that neither G(F) nor its negation is provable in F, and G(F) is true just in case F is consistent. Lucas's claim is that mechanism — the thesis that a human mind just is, or is fully captured by, some such F — cannot be right, because a mathematician reasoning about F from outside can see that G(F) is true, even though F cannot prove it. Since there is something F cannot do that we can do, no particular F can be an adequate model of the mind, and mechanism is false.
>
> The classic objection, due to Putnam, is that Lucas is entitled to assert that G(F) is true only if he is already entitled to assert that F is consistent. This is exactly what Gödel's second incompleteness theorem rules out: it shows that F is unable to even formulate its own Gödel sentence in the first place, since doing so would require the system to represent facts about its own unprovability, which already presupposes the very consistency that is in question. So the mathematician's "insight" into G(F) rests on an assumption — that F is consistent — that is exactly as unsupported as if the machine had asserted it about itself, and the argument ends up begging the question against the mechanist.
>
> Penrose tries to repair this in *Shadows of the Mind* by trading "consistent" for "sound." He argues that if we know the system F* underlying human mathematical competence is sound, we can derive that G(F*) is true; and, crucially, a system is in a better position to establish its own soundness than its own mere consistency, since soundness is the stronger and more informative property, and so is easier to confirm by direct reflection on one's own methods. On this basis Penrose thinks the regress that sank Lucas's version is blocked, and that the Gödelian argument goes through once "consistency" is upgraded to "soundness."
>
> I am not convinced this saves the argument, although for a different reason than Putnam's original one: even granting Penrose's shift, nothing has been said about why mathematicians should be able to agree on a single determinate F* in the first place, and "soundness" for an open-ended, evolving body of mathematical practice looks like a much less determinate notion than consistency is for a fixed formal theory. So while Penrose's version is more sophisticated than Lucas's original, it faces a structurally similar problem.
