Question: Present the hole argument as Earman and Norton use it against manifold substantivalism about spacetime, and explain why the mere fact that GR's field equations are generally covariant doesn't, by itself, get the argument going.

The student's answer:

General relativity's field equations are generally covariant: they keep the same form under any smooth coordinate transformation. Earman and Norton stress that this alone can't be what generates the metaphysical trouble, because Kretschmann had already shown in 1917 that literally any theory can be dressed up in generally covariant form, so general covariance in that sense is a purely formal property with no physical content of its own. What does the real work is an active diffeomorphism. Take a model (M, g) of the theory and pick an open region H, the "hole," containing no matter. Let d be a diffeomorphism of M that equals the identity everywhere outside H but is non-trivial inside it. Since (M, g) and (M, d*g) are related by a diffeomorphism and the field equations are diffeomorphism-invariant, both are solutions; and since d is the identity outside H, the two metrics agree everywhere outside the hole and disagree somewhere inside it.

For a substantivalist about the manifold -- someone who holds that the points of M are individuals in their own right, not reducible to the matter or metric relations instantiated at them -- g and d*g describe two distinct states of affairs, because the very same points end up carrying different metrical properties. So even a complete specification of the metric on and outside the boundary of H fails to fix which of the two futures is realized inside it: a failure of determinism that seems to have nothing to do with the physics and everything to do with a metaphysical commitment about points. Earman and Norton use this to press the substantivalist toward the view that diffeomorphic models like (M, g) and (M, d*g) just represent the same physical possibility -- what they call Leibniz equivalence, after Leibniz's shift argument against Newton's absolute space -- which is effectively the relationalist's view of the matter.

It's worth remembering that Einstein ran into a version of this himself, in 1915, while he and Grossmann were defending field equations that were not generally covariant; the hole argument (his "Lochbetrachtung") is the reason he thought general covariance had to fail for a couple of years. He talked himself out of it with the point-coincidence argument: everything we ever measure reduces to coincidences, like two worldlines crossing at a point, and d*g agrees with g on every such coincidence, so nothing physical actually distinguishes the two metrics after all. Butterfield and Brighouse later built a position, sometimes called sophisticated substantivalism, out of exactly this idea: keep the points of the manifold as real individuals, but deny that dragging the metric around with a diffeomorphism like d produces a new possibility. That lets the substantivalist have Leibniz equivalence too, without paying the relationalist's ontological price.
