But I actually sat down with a secondhand copy of A Theory of Justice (rough going, not gonna lie) because I wanted the actual argument, not the sparknotes version, and I got hung up on something none of the pop explanations ever mention.

From what I can tell, Rawls isn't just saying "you don't know who you'll be so just be fair about it." He's making this specific claim that the people behind the veil should reason using a "maximin" rule — basically, pick whichever setup makes the worst-off position as good as possible — rather than treating it like a normal bet where you'd assume you have an equal shot at being anyone and then just average out the expected outcome, like you would for a bet at a casino. And honestly the second way seems way more rational to me? If I truly don't know who I'll end up as, my instinct says I should play the odds, not build my whole choice around the absolute worst case scenario. But apparently there's actual reasoning behind why Rawls insists you can't/shouldn't reason like that in the original position, and that this move is basically where the difference principle comes from instead of just landing on regular utilitarianism.

Could you walk me through what Rawls's actual argument is for why the parties would go with this cautious "worst case" rule instead of just averaging the odds like a normal expected-value calculation? I don't want the hand-wavy "well he just assumed people are risk-averse" answer — I've seen people mention that this is actually a pretty contested move that economists and philosophers have argued about, and I want to actually get why, not just accept that Rawls waved a wand and got the answer he wanted.
