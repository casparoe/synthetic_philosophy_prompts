Question (20 points). DeRose's well-known Bank Cases describe a couple who need to deposit a paycheck. In the low-stakes version, it doesn't much matter whether the check gets deposited before the weekend, and the husband tells his wife, "I know the bank will be open tomorrow—I was there on a Saturday two weeks ago and it was open then." In the high-stakes version, the couple's evidence is exactly the same, but a large payment is due to clear on Monday and there will be serious trouble if the check isn't deposited in time; when his wife points out that banks sometimes change their hours, the husband now concedes, "I guess I don't really know that it's open." (a) [6 pts] Explain how an epistemic contextualist would use this pair of cases to motivate contextualism about the word "knows." (b) [6 pts] Explain how someone who accepts subject-sensitive invariantism (pragmatic encroachment on knowledge) would account for the very same pair of cases while denying that "knows" changes meaning across the two conversations. (c) [8 pts] Describe a further case, distinct from the two above, in which contextualism and subject-sensitive invariantism would issue different verdicts about whether a particular knowledge attribution is true. Say which verdict strikes you as correct, and use that to say which of the two theories you find more plausible.

Her answer:

(a) The contextualist says that "knows" is a context-sensitive word, a bit like "flat" or "tall": what counts as satisfying it depends on a standard fixed by the conversation it's used in, not on some fixed, absolute threshold. In the low-stakes conversation, ordinary everyday standards are in play, and the husband's evidence—having seen the bank open on a Saturday a few weeks back—is enough to satisfy them, so "I know the bank is open" comes out true. Once the stakes are raised, the possibility that the hours have changed becomes conversationally relevant, the standards go up, and the very same sentence now expresses a harder-to-meet proposition, one that the husband's unchanged evidence no longer satisfies. So the two cases don't show that the husband's epistemic position changes at all; they show that the same sentence can express two different propositions in the two conversations.

(b) Subject-sensitive invariantists—DeRose, Hawthorne, and Stanley are the names usually associated with this camp—deny that anything about the meaning of "knows" shifts between the two conversations. "Knows" picks out the same relation in both cases. What differs is whether the husband actually stands in that relation to the proposition that the bank is open, because what it takes to know something is partly determined by how much is riding on being right. When little is at stake, his so-so evidence is enough for knowledge; when a lot is at stake, that same evidence is no longer enough, so he genuinely fails to know, not just fails to satisfy some locally operative conversational standard.

(c) Take a case where someone in a relaxed, low-stakes conversation says, of a third party who (unbeknownst to the speaker) is actually facing enormous consequences riding on the very same question, "oh, he knows the bank is open." Contextualism predicts this comes out true, since the standards are fixed by the speaker's own low-stakes context, and nothing about the third party's situation is conversationally salient. Subject-sensitive invariantism predicts it comes out false, since what matters is the third party's own stakes, which are high, regardless of how casually the speaker is talking. I think subject-sensitive invariantism is the more plausible view overall, mainly because it does a better job with the original pair of cases: it feels much more natural to say that the stakes affect what the husband needs in order to actually know, than to say that the meaning of a word he's using quietly shifts underneath him while his evidence never changes.
