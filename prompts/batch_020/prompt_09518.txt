So I fell down a rabbit hole a few weeks back after watching some random video on Leibniz's "identity of indiscernibles" thing - the idea that if two things share literally every single property in common, they're actually not two things, they're the same thing. That's what led me to this thought experiment by a guy named Max Black, where he imagines a universe with nothing in it except two spheres, exactly the same size, same material, same temperature, everything, just sitting there some distance apart from each other. His whole point is that these are obviously two seperate spheres, but there's nothing about either one that makes it different from the other, so the identity of indiscernibles thing must be false. Ok, fine, I got that far and it made sense to me.

Then I kept reading and ran into this idea called "weak discernibility," where people try to rescue Leibniz's principle by pointing out that each sphere does stand in a relation the other one doesn't stand in to itself - like "is three miles away from that other sphere" - so maybe that counts as a real difference even if its not a property either sphere has on its own. And then, weirdly, it turns out physicists and philosophers use basically the same move for actual electrons that are stuck in the same quantum state, saying you can tell them apart because one "has opposite spin to the other one," even though neither electron has a spin value that belongs to it alone. I brought this up with a friend who's doing a physics degree and she just laughed and told me not to think about it too hard, which was not super helpful.

What I can't work out on my own is whether the sphere case and the electron case are really the same problem wearing two different costumes, or whether they're actually pretty different once you look closer. Is Black's thought experiment just a fun "what if" that doesn't tell us anything about the real world, while the quantum case is an actual real example of the same puzzle? And does "weak discernibility" save Leibniz's principle equally well in both cases, or is it more convincing for one than the other?
