A friend of mine, who is finishing her doctorate in mathematical logic, and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about how seriously to take the possibility that our basic mathematical axioms -- Peano Arithmetic, or ZFC -- might secretly be inconsistent. My position was fairly relaxed: these systems have been scrutinized by generations of mathematicians for a century or more without anyone ever turning up a contradiction, so surely that track record counts for something substantial. She considered this naïve, and pressed a point about Gödel's second incompleteness theorem that I hadn't fully appreciated before: that the theorem cuts both ways in an odd manner. It's not just that PA cannot prove its own consistency if PA happens to be consistent; it's that if PA were inconsistent, it would "prove" Con(PA) just as readily as it would prove absolutely anything else, since an inconsistent theory proves every sentence in its language, contradictions included. On her view, this means an internal proof of consistency could never really be good news, which is precisely why Gentzen had to step outside PA and invoke transfinite induction up to the ordinal ε₀ to obtain a genuine relative consistency proof -- and even that, she insisted, doesn't fully rescue Hilbert's original hope for an absolutely secure, finitary guarantee, since PA can prove transfinite induction for every ordinal strictly below ε₀ but not for ε₀ itself, and it's a live question whether induction up to ε₀ really counts as "finitary" in anything like Hilbert's original sense.

We went back and forth for a good while without resolving much, partly because neither of us could articulate cleanly why Gentzen's result should count as genuine progress rather than just trading one unprovable assumption for another, equally opaque one. I would like your help thinking this through properly. Could you explain exactly why Gödel's second theorem has this "inconsistent systems certify their own consistency for free" wrinkle, what Gentzen's proof actually accomplishes, and why ε₀ specifically -- rather than some smaller or larger ordinal -- is the relevant threshold? I would also value your sense of how proof theorists and philosophers of mathematics tend to regard this kind of relative consistency result: as genuine epistemic progress, or as merely relocating the original worry one level up. Finally, since I understand there is nothing like a full Gentzen-style ordinal analysis available for ZFC as a whole, I would like to know where that leaves us with respect to ZFC's consistency specifically, and whether my dinner-table complacency about "a century without contradictions" was actually defensible or just wishful thinking.
