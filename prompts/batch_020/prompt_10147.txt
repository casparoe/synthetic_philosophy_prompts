Over the last year or so I've been trying to get my head around the more technical side of AI safety by reading through old posts and papers in my spare time (I have no background in philosophy, math, or computer science — I fix HVAC systems for a living, so this is purely a hobby). One argument that keeps recurring, and that I still can't fully resolve for myself, is the claim that "coherence arguments" force any sufficiently capable, self-correcting agent toward complete and transitive preferences that can be represented as maximizing the expectation of some utility function. The reasoning is that an agent whose preferences are cyclic, or that simply has gaps where it neither prefers A to B, nor B to A, nor is indifferent between them, can supposedly be walked through a sequence of trades that leaves it strictly worse off than when it started, purely because of the shape of its preferences and not because of anything about the world — and no sufficiently rational agent would let that keep happening to it. People then use this to argue that advanced AI systems will predictably converge on stable, goal-directed, expected-utility-maximizing behavior — with all the instrumental convergence (resource acquisition, resistance to being shut down, and so on) that supposedly rides along with that — no matter what specific objective they were given or trained on.

The pushback I keep running into is twofold, and I can't tell how much weight either objection can actually bear. First, some people argue that an agent with gaps in its preferences can simply decline the exploitative trades without thereby being irrational, so it dodges the pump without ever tidying itself into a full expected-utility maximizer — though I've also seen it argued that this escape only works if the gaps are genuine, "there's-no-fact-of-the-matter" incompleteness, rather than a kind of vagueness that a cleverer pump can still exploit. Second, and separately, even an agent that is technically representable as maximizing some utility function might not thereby be "goal-directed" in any dangerous sense at all, since apparently almost any behavior whatsoever can be redescribed after the fact as maximizing some possibly bizarre or degenerate utility function — so the move from "coherent" to "dangerous optimizer chasing convergent instrumental goals" seems to need something more than the money-pump argument alone provides. Both threads seem serious to me, and I don't have the training to know whether they actually defeat the original argument or just complicate it around the edges.

Could you write a dialogue between two people on opposite sides of this: one who thinks coherence arguments really do show that advanced, sufficiently rational agents are bound to end up as stable, goal-directed expected-utility maximizers, and one who thinks this conclusion is considerably weaker than advertised. I'd like them to be equally sharp, actually engaging with each other's specific moves rather than simply repeating their own side of the argument, and I'd like the exchange to work through the two threads above in some detail — what exactly a money pump requires of an agent, whether declining a trade is always a free way out, and whether being representable as an expected-utility maximizer is doing any real work if the utility function itself is allowed to be arbitrary.
