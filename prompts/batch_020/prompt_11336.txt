So I went down a bit of a rabbit hole this week after re-reading the "agreeing to disagree" stuff around Aumann's theorem for the hundredth time, wich led me into the Milgrom-Stokey no-trade theorem, something I'd somehow never actually sat down and worked through properly despite years of lurking on LW and Overcoming Bias. The rough shape I've got is: if you start from a Pareto-optimal allocation and everyone shares a common prior, then a proposed trade based on private information should actually be self-undermining, because the mere fact that someone is willing to trade with you at a given price is itself evidence about what they know, and a fully rational counterparty updates on that and walks away -- basically the thing people sometimes call the "Groucho Marx theorem," you shouldn't want to trade with anyone who's willing to trade with you. Obviously actual markets have enormous trading volume, so something in that chain is failing hard once you leave the blackboard.

Here's what's bugging me though: it feels too easy to just say "well duh, people have different information, that's the whole point of markets" -- but isn't that literally the premise the theorem starts from (traders having different private signals), rather than a refutation of it? So that can't be the loose thread, or at least not the whole story. Can you actually walk me through the inference chain that makes the no-trade result go through in the first place -- why does an offer to trade reveal so much, and why does unwinding it seem to require this weird iterated common-knowledge reasoning, kind of like the blue-eyed islanders puzzle? And then tell me which specific assumption you think is doing the most work in making the result fall apart for real markets: common priors, common knowledge of rationality, common knowledge that a trade is even being offered, or something else I dont even have a name for. Bonus question, since it's actually the part I care about most: given whichever assumption you pin the blame on, what does that imply about how much epistemic deference I owe to a liquid market price, like a prediction market? Is heavy trading volume itself evidence I should trust the price less as an aggregator of information, or is that backwards -- and how does this square with the whole Yudkowsky-style pushback on naive "just defer to the consensus" epistemology?
