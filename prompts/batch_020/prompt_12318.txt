I'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology, and my dissertation concerns the metaphysics of biological individuality — what makes something like a holobiont, a colonial organism, or a lichen count as one organism rather than several, or several rather than one. This term I've been sitting in on a metaphysics seminar that spent several weeks on Peter van Inwagen's special composition question — the question of under what conditions some objects compose a further object — and I can't shake the feeling that his own preferred answer doesn't survive contact with the cases I actually work on. Van Inwagen's answer, from Material Beings, is roughly that composition occurs only when the activity of some objects constitutes a life: hence there are, strictly speaking, no tables, mountains, or clouds, but there are dogs, oaks, and people. My worry is that "life" turns out to be exactly as gerrymandered a boundary as the "contact" and "fastening" criteria he spends the book demolishing, once you bring in real borderline biological cases — a lichen, which is a stable partnership between a fungus and an alga or cyanobacterium that experimenters can pry back apart into its two components; a cellular slime mold like Dictyostelium, which lives as free-ranging solitary amoebae until food runs low and then aggregates into a single sluglike body; or a siphonophore such as the Portuguese man o' war, a "colony" of clonal but functionally specialized zooids that behaves, for most purposes, like one animal.

I'd like you to write a dialogue — genuine back-and-forth, not two people delivering prepared monologues — between a mereological universalist, who holds (in the spirit of David Lewis) that any objects whatsoever compose some further object, and an organicist who defends van Inwagen's restricted answer. Rather than rehearsing the debate in the abstract, have them fight it out over the three biological cases above. The universalist's central weapon should be van Inwagen's own argument from vagueness: if indeterminate composition is intolerable because it would leave the number of existing things indeterminate, then the real indeterminacy in when a life begins, ends, splits, or is shared between symbionts is just as intolerable as the indeterminacy in contact or fastening that he rejects on those very grounds. The organicist shouldn't just restate the official position; push them to reconstruct and defend van Inwagen's distinction between vagueness that is merely in our concept of a life and vagueness that would be ontic vagueness in composition itself, and to show that this distinction actually earns its keep against the slime-mold and lichen cases rather than just being asserted. Have each side engage the other's strongest move, and let the dialogue end either in genuine, acknowledged stalemate or with a real but narrow concession — I'd find it a lot less useful if either side just capitulated.
