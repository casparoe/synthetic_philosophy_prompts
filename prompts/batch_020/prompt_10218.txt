I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist, relying on Bayesian statistics every working day without ever examining its philosophical foundations, and retirement has finally given me time to do that homework properly. The argument that has stuck with me is the Dutch book argument: anyone whose degrees of belief fail to satisfy the probability axioms can be offered a set of bets, each one perfectly acceptable on its own terms, that together guarantee a loss no matter how events unfold -- an almost gothic idea, as though incoherence itself were a debt that eventually comes due. I would like you to write me a short story that takes this literally: some figure (a bookmaker, an actuary, an algorithm, or something stranger) who can only ever profit from someone whose beliefs are incoherent in this exact technical sense, and a protagonist who must work out precisely what is being exploited in them before they can escape the trap or turn it back on whoever is running it. Let the real logical structure of the argument drive the plot rather than merely supply atmosphere, and keep it to somewhere between 1,500 and 2,500 words.
