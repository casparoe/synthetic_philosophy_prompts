So I've been down a total rabbit hole for a piece I'm reporting on the history of "uncertainty" in economics, and I stumbled onto a parallel that decision theorists apparently already know about but that completely blew my mind: Keynes, in his 1921 Treatise on Probability, describes almost exactly the urn setup Ellsberg would later use in his famous 1961 paradox — forty years earlier, and as far as I can tell, totally independently. Keynes's version: you can bet on drawing a white ball from an urn with a known 50/50 split of white and black balls, or from an urn with the same two colors in a totally unknown proportion. The odds of white are 1/2 either way, he says, but the "weight of argument" behind the known urn is bigger, and he asks whether that alone should make us prefer the known bet even though the probabilities are, on paper, identical. Ellsberg later ran the equivalent as an actual experiment, found that yes, people reliably do prefer the known urn, and explained it completely differently — not through "weight" at all, but through people pessimistically weighting a whole range of possible priors for the unknown urn, which basically seeded Gilboa and Schmeidler's maxmin expected utility and the rest of the modern ambiguity-aversion literature. A decision-theorist friend confirmed the Keynes/Ellsberg overlap is a known bit of trivia among historians of economics, but couldn't tell me what Keynes himself would actually make of where that literature ended up going.

So here's the question I can't shake: if Keynes had lived to see the whole ambiguity-aversion research programme grow up around "his" urn, do you think he'd call it total vindication — proof his instinct was right all along — or would he say they kept the example and quietly dropped his actual claim, since none of the multiple-priors machinery leaves any room for "weight" as an independent, rationally relevant thing that could justify a choice even when the probabilities match exactly? And here's the twist that's really bugging me for the piece: Keynes spent years getting pushed on this by the young Cambridge mathematician Frank Ramsey, who thought rational belief has to be complete and comparable with no floating "weight" dimension allowed, and by most accounts Keynes more or less conceded the point to Ramsey in the warm obituary essay he wrote after Ramsey's death. So given that concession, who do you think the Ellsberg literature actually ends up vindicating in hindsight once you dig into the mechanics — Keynes, Ramsey, or neither? I'd love a real steelmanned answer here rather than a "bit of both" cop-out, since that's basically the arguement I'm trying to referee for this section.
