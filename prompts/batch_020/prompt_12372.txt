For my Minds and Machines seminar this term, we were assigned the full text of Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" rather than the usual secondary summary, and I have been asked to open discussion next week on the last of the nine objections he considers there, "The Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception." Every treatment of this section I have come across mentions it only to note, with some amusement, that Turing took E.S.P. seriously, and then moves on; but when I actually sat down with the text I found its argumentative structure genuinely hard to pin down, and I would rather understand what is actually going on than just repeat that line in front of my classmates. Here is the passage (section 9 of the paper) in full:

I assume that the reader is familiar with the idea of extra-sensory perception, and the meaning of the four items of it, viz. telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition and psycho-kinesis. These disturbing phenomena seem to deny all our usual scientific ideas. How we should like to discredit them! Unfortunately the statistical evidence, at least for telepathy, is overwhelming. It is very difficult to rearrange one's ideas so as to fit these new facts in. Once one has accepted them it does not seem a very big step to believe in ghosts and bogies. The idea that our bodies move simply according to the known laws of physics, together with some others not yet discovered but somewhat similar, would be one of the first to go.

This argument is to my mind quite a strong one. One can say in reply that many scientific theories seem to remain workable in practice, in spite of clashing with E.S.P.; that in fact one can get along very nicely if one forgets about it. This is rather cold comfort, and one fears that thinking is just the kind of phenomenon where E.S.P. may be especially relevant.

A more specific argument based on E.S.P. might run as follows: "Let us play the imitation game, using as witnesses a man who is good as a telepathic receiver, and a digital computer. The interrogator can ask such questions as 'What suit does the card in my right hand belong to?' The man by telepathy or clairvoyance gives the right answer 130 times out of 400 cards. The machine can only guess at random, and perhaps gets 104 right, so the interrogator makes the right identification." There is an interesting possibility which opens here. Suppose the digital computer contains a random number generator. Then it will be natural to use this to decide what answer to give. But then the random number generator will be subject to the psycho-kinetic powers of the interrogator. Perhaps this psycho-kinesis might cause the machine to guess right more often than would be expected on a probability calculation, so that the interrogator might still be unable to make the right identification. On the other hand, he might be able to guess right without any questioning, by clairvoyance. With E.S.P. anything may happen.

If telepathy is admitted it will be necessary to tighten our test up. The situation could be regarded as analogous to that which would occur if the interrogator were talking to himself and one of the competitors was listening with his ear to the wall. To put the competitors into a 'telepathy-proof room' would satisfy all requirements.

What I cannot work out is whether Turing's two moves here actually cohere. He opens by granting that E.S.P., if real, would threaten the idea that our bodies "move simply according to the known laws of physics" -- which sounds like a threat to the mechanist premise of the whole paper, since the imitation game is only worth playing if minds are the sort of thing a discrete-state machine could in principle reproduce. He even concedes that the usual scientific strategy of getting along fine by ignoring E.S.P. is "cold comfort" here, precisely because thinking might be exactly the domain in which it matters. Yet two paragraphs later he seems perfectly satisfied by the idea of a "telepathy-proof room," which only rules out one contestant using telepathy to cheat during the test itself; it does nothing to show that minds are, after all, produced by mechanical processes. Has Turing quietly swapped a metaphysical worry (is thought even the sort of thing a physical mechanism could produce, if the known laws of physics are as incomplete as E.S.P. would suggest?) for a narrower methodological one (how do we keep the test fair?), leaving the first question hanging unanswered? Or is there a more charitable reading on which the room really does dispose of the deeper worry -- for instance, by showing that the imitation game can be insulated from the truth of the metaphysical question rather than needing to settle it? I would also value a second opinion on the aside about the random number generator being "subject to the psycho-kinetic powers of the interrogator": it strikes me that this observation, taken seriously, cuts both ways and complicates Turing's position rather than shoring it up, though I cannot tell whether I am reading too much into a throwaway remark. Any help reconstructing the logic of this section, or pointing out a more sympathetic reading I might be missing, would be a great help before I have to present on it.
