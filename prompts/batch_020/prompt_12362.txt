I never studied philosophy in school, but for a while now I have been trying, on my own, to understand why I keep doing things in the moment that I know full well will work against me later on — snoozing an alarm I set myself, ordering takeout after telling myself all day I would cook, staying up scrolling when I already know exactly how the next morning will feel. In the course of reading about this, I learned that it has an old name, "akrasia," and that economists now describe something similar with "hyperbolic discounting," but that the actual philosophical puzzle goes back to Aristotle. So I tracked down H. Rackham's 1926 translation of Book VII of the Nicomachean Ethics and worked through the part where he tries to explain how a person can act against knowledge he genuinely possesses. One passage reads:

"Again, it is possible for men to 'have knowledge' in yet another way besides those just discussed; for even in the state of having knowledge without exercising it we can observe a distinction: a man may in a sense both have it and not have it; for instance, when he is asleep, or mad, or drunk. But persons under the influence of passion are in the same condition; for it is evident that anger, sexual desire, and certain other passions, actually alter the state of the body, and in some cases even cause madness. It is clear therefore that we must pronounce the unrestrained to 'have knowledge' only in the same way as men who are asleep or mad or drunk. Their using the language of knowledge is no proof that they possess it. Persons in the states mentioned repeat propositions of geometry and verses of Empedocles; students who have just begun a subject reel off its formulae, though they do not yet know their meaning, for knowledge has to become part of the tissue of the mind, and this takes time. Hence we must conceive that men who fail in self-restraint talk in the same way as actors speaking a part."

Taken on its own, that seemed reasonably clear: the knowledge is simply sitting there unused, like a memorized line. But later, in chapter 10, Aristotle returns to the very same comparison and immediately adds something that seems to pull the other way:

"Nor indeed does the unrestrained man even know the right in the sense of one who consciously exercises his knowledge, but only as a man asleep or drunk can be said to know something. Also, although he errs willingly (for he knows in a sense both what he is doing and what end he is aiming at), yet he is not wicked, for his moral choice is sound, so that he is only half-wicked."

A little earlier in that same book, contrasting this person with someone simply corrupt, he adds that "the unrestrained man is aware of his infirmity" and, unlike the truly vicious man, feels real regret afterward.

What I cannot reconcile is this: if the knowledge at the moment of acting is really as inert as a sleepwalker's mumbling or an actor reciting lines, in what sense is the person erring "willingly," or aware enough of what he is doing to be blamed for it and to regret it later? And if enough of the knowledge is still active to support that, the comparison to sleep, madness, and drunkenness looks like it is overstating how ignorant he really is. Is there one consistent picture of the akratic person's state of mind that Aristotle is actually entitled to across these passages, or is he running together two different answers without quite noticing the seam between them? I would like to work through what his view is committed to here, rather than settle for the usual one-line gloss that "he knows in one sense but not in another."
