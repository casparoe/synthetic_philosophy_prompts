For an independent research project in my AP Seminar class, I have been reading about the sixteenth-century Catholic dispute over grace, providence, and free will, and I have run into a question that none of my sources actually answer. The Jesuit theologian Luis de Molina argued in his 1588 Concordia that, logically prior to deciding which world to create, God possesses "middle knowledge" (scientia media): infallible knowledge of exactly what every possible free creature would freely choose in every possible circumstance. Because this knowledge is supposedly independent of God's own creative will, Molina held that God could providentially steer history toward his purposes simply by choosing which circumstances to actualize, without ever causally determining a single free choice. Molina had studied Thomas Aquinas closely enough to write a three-volume commentary on the first part of the Summa Theologiae. Even so, the Dominican Thomists of his day, especially Domingo Bañez, insisted that middle knowledge was flatly incompatible with Aquinas's own system, and the resulting controversy grew so heated that Rome had to convene a special commission which, after years of hearings, ultimately declined to rule on which side was correct.

What I cannot find anywhere is an actual argument, rather than a bare assertion, for what Aquinas himself would probably have concluded about middle knowledge, given that he died more than three centuries before Molina wrote and so never addressed the theory directly. I would like you to construct that argument, rather than simply summarizing the historical Bañez-versus-Molina dispute, which I already understand reasonably well. Specifically, Aquinas held that God's "knowledge of vision" is causal with respect to anything that actually exists or will exist, and he developed a detailed account of God as the universal first cause of every act of a created will, including free ones, without thereby eliminating the contingency of those acts at the level of secondary causes. Starting from those two commitments, please predict and defend a view: would Aquinas's own principles have made "middle knowledge" — a pre-volitional, non-causal, yet infallible knowledge of counterfactuals of freedom — a coherent extension of his theory of divine knowledge, or something his own premises rule out from the start? And if you conclude that his texts genuinely underdetermine the answer, please show me exactly where that indeterminacy arises and which of his commitments are actually in tension, rather than just declaring the question unanswerable.
