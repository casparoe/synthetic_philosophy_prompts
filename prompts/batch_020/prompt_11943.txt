I'm a backend engineer who spends a lot of time in runtime internals and garbage collection, not a philosopher, but a physicist friend of mine keeps bringing up something called "the hole argument" at dinner, and I've since fallen down a rabbit hole reading about it: Einstein's original worry about general covariance, the Earman–Norton revival of it as an argument against spacetime substantivalism, "Leibniz equivalence," and the "sophisticated substantivalism" literature that grew up in response. At some point it struck me that the whole setup maps uncannily well onto something entirely mundane from my day job: how a moving (compacting) garbage collector is allowed to relocate live objects to new addresses and rewrite every pointer that refers to them, on the understanding that this is completely invisible to the running program. I've sketched the analogy below as notes toward a short blog post, and before I subject anyone else to it, I'd like a rigorous outside check.

Here is the mapping as I currently have it. Spacetime points correspond to heap addresses; the metric and matter fields correspond to the graph of references between objects, i.e., which object's fields point at which other objects. A "hole diffeomorphism" — a relabeling of points that is the identity everywhere outside some region but differs smoothly inside it, dragging the fields along with it — corresponds to a garbage collector relocating some subset of live objects to new addresses while faithfully preserving the reference graph. Manifold substantivalism, on which a point's identity is primitive and independent of the fields defined on it, corresponds to treating raw reference identity (roughly, what pointer equality or an identity hash code exposes, as against mere structural equality) as semantically real rather than as an implementation detail. And Leibniz equivalence — refusing to count hole-diffeomorphic models as distinct possibilities — corresponds to the ordinary assumption that the pre- and post-collection heaps are just two descriptions of a single abstract program state, which is exactly why runtimes go to some trouble to stop raw addresses from leaking (hence the well-known headache of implementing identity hash codes that survive relocation).

Where I'm least confident is the indeterminism half of the argument, as opposed to the mere redundancy half that Leibniz equivalence is meant to dispatch. My best attempt: if two relocations that preserve the reference graph could still make the program behave differently later on (say, because iteration order over an identity-hashed collection quietly leaks address information into observable behavior), then the structural description of the heap fails to fix a unique continuation — which feels like the right shape, but I suspect it doesn't capture the specifically modal character of Earman and Norton's worry, which concerns rival possible continuations of one and the same past rather than just an underspecified implementation detail. Could you tell me: first, whether I've represented the actual hole argument correctly, since I only know it secondhand; second, whether the garbage-collection analogy genuinely tracks the haecceitism/anti-haecceitism distinction that separates naive from "sophisticated" substantivalists, or whether it's really just a pun on the word "relabeling"; and third, whether there's a cleaner way to force the determinism piece specifically, rather than the mere gauge-redundancy piece, into the analogy — or whether that is exactly where it should break down.
