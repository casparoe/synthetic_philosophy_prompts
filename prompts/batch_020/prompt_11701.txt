In the Metaphysics of Morals and the Lectures on Ethics, Kant argues that because animals lack self-consciousness and rational agency, we can have no direct duties toward them: apparent duties regarding an animal are really indirect duties to humanity, since neglecting them would coarsen dispositions — sympathy, gratitude — that we owe to our fellow human beings (MM 6:442–43). His illustration is that an old horse or dog which has served faithfully deserves care in retirement rather than disposal, not for its own sake, but because callousness here corrodes the disposition that morality toward other people requires; on similar grounds he tolerates using animals as instruments for a serious human purpose, such as vivisection, while condemning cruelty inflicted for mere sport (Lectures on Ethics, 27:459f.). Suppose Kant were confronted with modern industrial animal agriculture: the confinement and slaughter of billions of animals annually, at a scale and anonymity bearing no resemblance to the individualized relationship his own examples presuppose, and almost entirely hidden from the very people whose character his argument is meant to protect. Reasoning strictly from the structure of Kant's actual arguments, rather than from how you think he ought to have reasoned, would his framework condemn this practice, stretch to accommodate it as a "serious purpose" on the model of vivisection, or turn out to have nothing determinate to say about it? Address, too, whether subsequent research on animal cognition has, in your view, seriously strained the empirical premise — that no animal possesses anything resembling self-consciousness — on which Kant's entire position depends.
