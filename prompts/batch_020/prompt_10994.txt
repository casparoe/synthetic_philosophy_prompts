So I'm about halfway through a novel with a subplot set in a West German courtroom in the late 1940s: a judge character has to decide what to do with a woman who denounced her husband to the authorities for something he said against Hitler while he was home on leave, which got him a death sentence. It's loosely based on the actual "grudge informer" case that Hart and Fuller fight over in their whole exchange, though I'm fudging the specifics for fiction's sake, so don't worry about getting the history exactly right. My writing group keeps telling me the judge's internal monologue reads like a Wikipedia summary, and honestly they're right — I've absorbed the one-line version of the Hart/Fuller debate (positivists say law and morality are seperate, natural lawyers say their linked) so many times it's basically a reflex at this point, and reflexes don't make for good monologues.

What I actually want is the debate underneath that cliché. From what I can tell, Hart and Fuller basically agree the wife deserves to face some legal consequence — they just disagree, hard, about whether you can say that while still calling the old Nazi statute her husband was convicted under "valid law." Could you walk me through the real shape of that disagreement: Fuller's "inner morality of law" stuff (the procedural conditions — generality, no secret decrees, no retroactivity, and so on) versus Hart's response to it, and specifically why Hart thought it was more honest to admit the Nazi statute was law and then pass a new, openly retroactive law to punish the wife, rather than pretend the original statute never counted as law in the first place? I want each side's strongest jab at the other, not the textbook handshake where everyone politely agrees to disagree — enough real texture that my judge could plausibly land on one side after actually wrestling with it, instead of just gesturing at "law vs. morality" and calling it a day.
