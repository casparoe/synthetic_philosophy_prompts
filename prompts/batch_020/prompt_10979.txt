I'm finishing a feature on organ transplantation policy, and the background reading on the Uniform Determination of Death Act has pulled me further into bioethics than I expected. The sticking point is a long-running dispute between defenders of the whole-brain standard and the "brain death skeptics" who take their cue from the neurologist Alan Shewmon. Shewmon's case series describes patients who satisfied every clinical criterion for whole-brain death yet remained on ventilators for months, and in a few instances for years, all while healing wounds, fighting off infections, growing proportionately as children, and, in at least one class of case reports, sustaining a pregnancy well into fetal viability. He treats this as evidence that a body's capacity to function as a single, self-integrating organism does not actually depend on having a working brain — which was supposed to be the whole point of relocating death from the heart and lungs to the brain in the first place. If that point fails, it looks like we're pushed back toward a purely circulatory-respiratory standard after all.

What I haven't gotten a satisfying answer to, from the bioethicists I've interviewed so far, is the standard comeback the President's Council on Bioethics offered in its 2008 white paper on death determination: that the brain's job was never really "integration" in that crude sense, but rather enabling the organism to carry out its own fundamental vital work as a whole — the drive to breathe, to self-regulate, to engage the environment rather than merely persist — so that Shewmon's patients are being kept going by machines and nursing staff rather than doing that work themselves. Could you construct the strongest argument you can that this fallback rationale fails? I'd rather you not simply restate Shewmon's integration point; instead, show that "fundamental vital work," once made precise enough to do any philosophical work, either begs the question against the skeptic, is falsified by the same case reports it's meant to survive, or ends up implying that plenty of textbook whole-brain-dead patients are, by the Council's own criterion, still partly alive. If it strengthens the case, feel free to bring in the fact that the bedside tests actually used to diagnose whole-brain death don't check hypothalamic-pituitary function, which is often preserved.
