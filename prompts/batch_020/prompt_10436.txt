Over dinner last week, a friend and I ended up in a long and increasingly heated argument about Augustine's two treatises on lying, which we had both just read for a small reading group on his minor ethical works. The dispute concerned his famously absolute position in *De Mendacio* and *Contra Mendacium*: that any assertion made with the intention to deceive is sinful, full stop, regardless of the good it secures, because the wrongness lies in the inner rupture between heart and tongue rather than in the lie's consequences. My friend argued that this holds up better than it looks once you take Augustine's psychology of intention seriously, and that the graded, eightfold scale of lies he lays out — from malicious lies about doctrine down to a lie told only to protect someone's life or chastity — shows he was already alive to exactly the intuitions supposed to refute him, without ever conceding the key point. I argued that the position quietly collapses under its own hard cases: Augustine has to insist that Rahab, who sheltered the Israelite spies and lied outright to the men of Jericho about their whereabouts, still committed a real, if minor, sin in the lying itself, even though the New Testament holds her up as a paradigm of faith and good works with no hint that anything about her conduct needs excusing; and in *Contra Mendacium*, written against Consentius's scheme of infiltrating the Priscillianists under false pretenses, he refuses to allow lying even to expose and correct a dangerous heresy. Neither of us budged, and I would like a more rigorous adjudication than the two of us managed to reach.

Could you write me an essay on this? Please reconstruct Augustine's actual argument for treating every lie as intrinsically sinful — including how he locates the wrong in the intention to deceive rather than in outcomes, and what work the gravity-ranking of different kinds of lies is doing if it isn't meant to soften the underlying absolutism — and then use the hard cases (Rahab, the endangered innocent hidden from a would-be murderer, the Priscillianist infiltration question) to test whether the position is genuinely coherent or only superficially so. Don't let the essay end on a "there is much to be said on both sides" note: tell me plainly whether Augustine's absolutism about lying is, in the end, defensible on its own terms, or whether it needs to be abandoned or substantially revised — and if it's the latter, say what a better view would need to preserve from his account and what it would have to give up.
