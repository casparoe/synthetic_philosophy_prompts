I've been reading about Frank Ramsey, the Cambridge mathematician and philosopher who died in 1930 at only twenty-six, and I came across a short passage from a talk he gave in 1925 to a Cambridge discussion group called the Apostles, later printed as the closing chapter of one of his books under the title "Epilogue." I have wondered on and off for years whether the sheer size and age of the universe should make a person's own life and concerns feel small, and this is the closest thing I have found to a real answer to that worry, but I am having trouble pinning down exactly what the answer is. Here is the passage:

"Where I seem to differ from my friends is in attaching little importance to physical size. I don't feel in the least humble before the vastness of the heavens. The stars may be large, but they cannot think or love; and these are qualities which impress me far more than size does. I take no credit for weighing nearly seventeen stone. My picture of the world is drawn in perspective, and not like a model to scale. The foreground is occupied by human beings and the stars are all as small as threepenny bits. I don't really believe in astronomy, except as a complicated description of part of the course of human and possibly animal sensation. … I apply my perspective not merely to space but also to time. In time the world will cool and everything will die; but that is a long time off still, and its present value at compound interest is almost nothing. Nor is the present less valuable because the future is blank."

What I cannot work out is what kind of claim the "perspective, not a model to scale" image is actually making. Is Ramsey saying that he privately knows human affairs really are as small as the stars would make them look on any honest scale, and that he simply prefers to live inside a useful illusion instead — the way an artist's perspective drawing is not an accurate scale model but is still worth looking at? Or is he making the much stronger claim that there is no such honest scale for importance to begin with, so that a human-centred picture is not a distortion of anything and needs no excusing? And if it is the stronger claim, I do not see why he would then need the separate move about compound interest to deal with time: discounting a distant event only makes sense if near and far can be weighed against each other on one common scale, which sounds like exactly the "model to scale" he has just finished rejecting. Am I misreading the structure of his argument, or is he genuinely relying on two different, not obviously compatible ideas here?
