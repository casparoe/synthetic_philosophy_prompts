A friend of mine and I both serve as teaching assistants for the same philosophy of mind course, and last night the two of us spent the better part of two hours arguing over whether Stephen Yablo's proposal — that mental properties stand to their physical realizers as determinables stand to determinates — actually rescues non-reductive physicalism from Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument. Neither of us budged. I am now working through the stack of midterms for that same course, and the next one in the pile happens to answer almost exactly the question we were arguing about. Given how invested I still am in the dispute, I don't trust my own judgment of whether the answer is genuinely good or merely familiar-sounding, and I would like an independent assessment before writing anything in the margin.

Here is the question, worth 25 points:

"Reconstruct Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument against non-reductive physicalism, making explicit the roles played by the causal closure of the physical and by the exclusion principle. Then explain how Stephen Yablo's proposal that mental properties are determinables of their physical realizers is meant to block the argument, and assess whether it succeeds."

Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"Kim's exclusion argument targets non-reductive physicalism, the view that mental properties are irreducible to, though realized by, physical properties, so that there are no mental-physical type identities even though every mental token presumably is identical to some physical token. The argument rests on four commitments: the causal closure of the physical (every physical event that has a cause at a time has a sufficient physical cause at that time), an exclusion principle ruling out two distinct sufficient causes for one effect except in genuine cases of overdetermination, the assumption that mental causation is real, and the distinctness of mental from physical properties. Suppose a mental event M causes a physical event P*. Since M is realized on this occasion by some physical property P, and P is already, by closure, sufficient for P*, we have two candidate causes competing for the same effect. Because this is not a plausible case of overdetermination, one candidate must be excluded, and given the closure of the physical it is M that goes: mental properties turn out to be epiphenomenal unless they can somehow be reduced to physical properties.

Yablo tries to block this conclusion by modeling the mental-physical relation on the relation between a determinable and its determinate, the way scarlet is a determinate of the determinable red. Just as an object's being scarlet does not compete with its being red for credit in causing a color-sensitive detector to fire, a physical property does not compete with the mental property it realizes, because the mental property is the determinable of which the physical property is the determinate. This is really just a restatement, in different words, of the familiar point from multiple realizability that one mental property can be realized by many different physical properties across occasions and creatures; once the two properties are seen as related this way rather than as separate competitors, it becomes clear that they are ultimately the same property picked out at different levels of grain, which is exactly why one does not exclude the other. On this picture, Yablo's strategy succeeds: since a determinate never competes with its determinable, physical realization can never exclude mental causation, and non-reductive physicalism is safe."
