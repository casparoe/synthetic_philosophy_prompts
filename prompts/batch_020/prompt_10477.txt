The question was: "Brouwer held that the Law of Excluded Middle (LEM) should not be used as an unrestricted logical law in mathematics. (a) [6 pts] Explain the constructive conception of mathematical existence and truth that motivates this restriction, and state the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov (BHK) clauses for ∧, ∨, →, and ¬. (b) [5 pts] Some students infer that intuitionists must therefore also reject reductio ad absurdum and the principle of explosion (ex falso quodlibet). Is this correct? Explain, with reference to how ¬A is defined in intuitionistic logic. (c) [4 pts] Illustrate the failure of at least one classically valid inference, other than LEM itself, in intuitionistic logic. (d) [5 pts] Dummett offered an influential defense of intuitionistic logic grounded in a general theory of meaning rather than in Brouwer's own philosophical outlook. Briefly characterize how Dummett's route to intuitionism differs from Brouwer's."

The student answered: "(a) For Brouwer, mathematics is a mental construction carried out by an idealized mathematician, not a body of truths existing independently of us. A statement is true only when we possess a construction -- a proof -- demonstrating it, and an existential claim ∃xP(x) is only assertable if we can actually produce a specific x together with a construction verifying P(x); merely refuting ∀x¬P(x) does not suffice, since that refutation need not itself hand us a witness. Because assertability is tied to possession of a construction, and because for many propositions A we currently have neither a proof of A nor a proof of ¬A, we are not entitled to assert A∨¬A as a general law -- only case by case, once one of the two constructions is actually in hand. This is codified in the BHK interpretation: a proof of A∧B is a pair of a proof of A and a proof of B; a proof of A∨B is either a proof of A or a proof of B together with a specification of which; a proof of A→B is a construction turning any proof of A into a proof of B; and a proof of ¬A is a construction turning any hypothetical proof of A into a proof of absurdity (⊥) -- i.e., ¬A is defined as A→⊥.

(b) No -- this is a common overreach. Since ¬A is just shorthand for A→⊥, proving ¬A intuitionistically proceeds by assuming A and deriving a contradiction, which is exactly reductio ad absurdum; intuitionistic logic uses this constantly and has no quarrel with it. Explosion is also intact: from ⊥ (and so from any A together with ¬A) one may derive an arbitrary B, since intuitionistic logic keeps the rule ⊥→B. This is part of why intuitionistic logic is standardly classified as a paraconsistent logic: it is precisely this careful handling of what can be extracted from a contradiction that marks it off from classical logic. What intuitionists actually reject is a different, narrower move: concluding the positive statement A itself from a mere proof of ¬¬A. That inference, double-negation elimination, would license asserting A on the strength of refuting A's refutation, without ever constructing A -- and that is the move BHK is designed to block.

(c) A standard failure is one direction of De Morgan's law. ¬A∨¬B→¬(A∧B) is intuitionistically valid, but the converse, ¬(A∧B)→¬A∨¬B, is not: refuting the conjunction only tells us that A and B cannot both be proved, which needn't hand us a construction of ¬A or, alternatively, one of ¬B.

(d) Brouwer's own case for restricting LEM was inseparable from his broader mentalist and solipsistic philosophy of mathematics: his view that mathematics is founded on a primordial intuition of time and is essentially a languageless activity of the individual mind, with language and logic serving only as an imperfect secondary record of constructions already performed. Dummett, writing decades later, gave what is essentially the same argument in updated dress: he held that grasping the meaning of a mathematical statement just is grasping what would count as a proof of it, so Brouwer's constructive reading of the logical connectives falls directly out of a general verificationist theory of meaning -- and Dummett used this to argue that realist, bivalent semantics cannot be sustained even outside mathematics."
