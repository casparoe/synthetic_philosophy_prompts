So I'm about four months into a novel about a professional poker player, and I've been doing interviews to make sure I get the world right (my agent keeps saying readers can smell it when a writer fakes this stuff). Last week I talked to a guy who played high-stakes cash games for something like fifteen years, and he said something that's been rattling around in my head ever since. He told me that even on a hand where the math is heavily in his favor, he'll never put more than a smallish slice of his bankroll on it, no matter how good the odds look for that one hand. When I pushed back and said isn't that just leaving money on the table, since betting bigger wins you more on average, he said no — maximizing what you win "on average" isn't the same thing as maximizing what you actually walk away with after a lifetime of playing, and mixing the two up is a mistake that's busted plenty of talented players who really should've known better. He wasn't jittery or supersticious about it either, he said it the way you'd state a fact about gravity, not a personal comfort level.

This is going to be a pivotal scene where he lays out his whole philosophy to a hotshot rookie he's mentoring, so I want to get it right and not just fake the swagger. My co-writer thinks the guy is basically rationalizing ordinary risk-aversion with some math he doesn't fully have, and that I should write him as a little overconfident. But the sheer certainty in how he said it is nagging at me — it felt less like "I'm scared of variance" and more like he genuinely thought the other way of playing was a logical error, full stop. Is there an actual principle that could justify that, something that would make sense of playing this way even for someone with zero fear of losing? I'd love to understand the shape of the argument, if there is one, before I put words in his mouth.
