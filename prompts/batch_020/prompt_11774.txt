and here's the student's answer, retyped as-is from the bluebook:

"Dialetheism holds that some contradictions are literally true - the Liar sentence 'this sentence is false' is the standard case, since classical reasoning about it forces us to conclude it is both true and false. Priest's whole point is that this shouldn't commit us to saying every sentence whatsoever is true (trivialism), and paraconsistent logic is what keeps the two apart.

(a) Classically, once you have A and ¬A you can derive any B at all (ex contradictione quodlibet). Priest blocks this by moving to LP, a three-valued logic with values True, False and Both, where Both counts as a designated (assertable) value right alongside True. Validity in LP is preservation of designated value across every valuation, and since a glut only ever takes the value Both rather than forcing the rest of the language into Both too, an arbitrary B is no longer derivable from A and ¬A - explosion fails.

(b) Because dialetheism says some contradictions are true, LP also has to give up the Law of Non-Contradiction: if A∧¬A can be true, ¬(A∧¬A) can't be a logical truth of the system anymore, and Priest treats this as the central bullet dialetheists have to bite.

(c) Modus ponens, though, is untouched by any of this. The conditional A→B is just defined as ¬A∨B, and disjunction takes the max of its disjuncts' values, so whenever A and A→B both come out designated, B has to come out designated too - LP validates modus ponens exactly like classical logic does."
