Question: Explain the debate over whether perceptual experience can be cognitively penetrated by belief or desire. Your answer should (a) state Fodor's informational encapsulation thesis and explain how he uses the Müller-Lyer illusion to support it, (b) explain one significant methodological challenge to citing empirical demonstrations as evidence of genuine cognitive penetration, and (c) explain what is epistemically at stake if perception is cognitively penetrated, with reference to at least one specific philosopher's view.

Classmate's answer: Fodor's Modularity of Mind (1983) argues that perceptual input systems are informationally encapsulated: they process stimuli using only proprietary, domain-specific information, without access to what the subject believes or desires. Fodor's classic illustration is the Müller-Lyer illusion: even once a subject learns that the two lines are equal in length, the illusion persists, so the perceptual system does not update its output in light of the newly formed belief. Fodor treats this stubbornness as evidence that perception is cognitively impenetrable, insulated from revision by central, inferential processes, and that only encapsulated modules of this kind could explain the speed and mandatory character of perception.

Critics have not been content to let this rest on introspective examples, however. In the decades since, a large empirical literature has tried to demonstrate penetration directly, for instance by manipulating subjects' emotional states, desires, or background beliefs and measuring whether the resulting percept changes accordingly (e.g., desirable objects being seen as literally closer or larger). Firestone and Scholl's widely discussed 2016 review, however, showed that cognitive penetration is not a genuine phenomenon: case after case, the supposed top-down effect could instead be explained as a shift in subjects' attention, a demand characteristic of the experimental design, or a bias in how subjects reported their experience rather than a change in the experience itself. Their paper is generally taken to have settled the empirical question in the encapsulationist's favor.

The stakes are not merely psychological but epistemological. Susanna Siegel argues that when a perceptual experience is cognitively penetrated, it can no longer play its normal role of justifying belief, since the very belief that shaped the experience cannot then be independently confirmed by it -- the process becomes viciously circular, and any cognitively penetrated experience is thereby epistemically downgraded. Her example of Jill, who has come to unjustifiably believe that Jack is angry with her and then "sees" his neutral expression as angry, is meant to show that cognitive penetration compromises perception's evidential value, which is why the modularity debate bears directly on classical foundationalist appeals to the "given."
