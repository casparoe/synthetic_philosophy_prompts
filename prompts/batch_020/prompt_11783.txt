I'm taking a seminar on classical Chinese philosophy this term, and we recently spent two sessions on the School of Names (名家), focusing on Gongsun Long's "White Horse Dialogue" (白馬論) and its deliberately provocative thesis that "a white horse is not a horse" (白馬非馬). My professor walked us through the dialogue's own arguments for the thesis -- for instance, that "horse" names a shape while "white" names a color, so that to name a color is not to name a shape; and that someone asked to fetch "a horse" may bring a horse of any color, while someone asked to fetch "a white horse" may not bring a black or yellow horse, so the two requests cannot be satisfied by the same things -- and then showed us just how divided the secondary literature is on whether any of this amounts to serious semantics or metaphysics, rather than a fallacy that trades on an unrecognized ambiguity between "is" as identity and "is" as class-membership.

For my response paper, I've distilled what I take to be the six main strategies interpreters have used to make the dialogue come out as a piece of serious reasoning (or, in the last case, to argue that it can't be made to): (a) a mereological/mass-noun strategy, following Chad Hansen's proposal that classical Chinese common nouns function like mass terms, on which "horse" and "white horse" denote different aggregates of stuff, the latter a proper part of the former; (b) a kind-identity strategy, on which "fei" (非) denies identity between the kinds "horse" and "white horse" while leaving untouched the fact that every particular white horse is a particular horse; (c) a compound-name strategy, resting on Gongsun Long's own point that "white horse" names a combination of two names, and that a combined name cannot, simply as such, be identical to either name taken uncombined; (d) a selection-criterion strategy, built on the "fetch a horse" argument, which individuates the referents of "horse" and "white horse" by what would satisfy a request for each; (e) a realist-universals strategy, which reads the dialogue alongside its companion piece, the "Discourse on Referring to Things" (指物論), as committed to an ontology in which every predicate picks out its own independently existing universal; and (f) a deflationary strategy, on which the whole dialogue is a clever but ultimately fallacious piece of sophistry that never escapes the ambiguity noted above.
