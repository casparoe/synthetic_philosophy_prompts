So in my school's philosophy club we've been doing a whole unit on personal identity, and this week we finally got to Parfit's teleporter stuff. I think I mostly get the basic version -- a machine on Earth scans your body down to the atom, destroys it, and beams the info to Mars where an exact copy of you gets built out of new matter, and then everyone argues about whether that's really you surviving or whether you just died and some other dude who thinks he's you is walking around up there. Fine, I can follow that. But then our club leader (he's a senior, kinda a philosophy nerd, no offense to him) brought up this "branch-line" version where the machine scans you but doesnt destroy the original body -- so now there's a you on Earth who's still alive but has some kind of fatal damage from the scan and is gonna die in like a week, AND a totally healthy copy of you on Mars who's gonna live out the rest of "your" life like nothing happened. And apparently Parfit thinks THIS wierd version, not the normal one, is actually the one that proves his big point -- that personal identity isn't what matters in survival, and what actually matters is this "Relation R" psychological continuity thing instead.

Here's what I can't wrap my head around: in the branch-line case, obviously the dying Earth-guy can't literally BE both the future Earth-guy and the Mars-guy, since those end up being two totally separate people who never even meet again -- so identity breaks down there for pretty boring logical reasons (one thing can't be identical to two different things). But how is that supposed to teach us anything about a completely normal case, like me just going to sleep tonight and waking up tomorrow, where nobody's duplicated and identity doesn't break down at all? It feels like Parfit's using a freak-accident scenario to sneak in a conclusion about totally ordinary life, and I can't follow WHY the mere existence of some random duplicate on Mars should change what matters for the guy dying on Earth, when nothing about the actual relationship between him and his own future self changed one bit. Could you walk me through the actual logical steps of that argument, not just restate the thought experiment? Extra credit if you can also explain why Parfit thought this is supposed to make people feel less freaked out about ordinary death too, because that's honestly the part that got under my skin the most.
