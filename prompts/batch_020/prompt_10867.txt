I recently read a popular-science article about "Boltzmann brains" that left me genuinely unsettled, and I want to make sure I am understanding its implications correctly. The article explained that because our universe's expansion appears to be accelerating due to a positive cosmological constant, the standard ΛCDM cosmological model predicts that it will eventually settle into an eternal, nearly empty de Sitter phase with a tiny but nonzero temperature. Given infinite time, random quantum and thermal fluctuations in that near-vacuum will occasionally assemble an isolated, momentarily conscious brain complete with false memories of an entire orderly life and cosmic history. Because producing a single such brain through a chance fluctuation is astronomically more probable than producing one through billions of years of galaxy formation and biological evolution, these "Boltzmann brains" would, over the lifetime of the universe, vastly outnumber ordinary observers like us. The article concluded that, since this is apparently a genuine feature of our best current cosmology, a "typical" observer reasoning about their own existence should conclude that they are overwhelmingly more likely to be one of these spontaneously assembled brains than a real person with an actual past.

A friend I discussed this with afterward pressed the point further, arguing that because this really is what the relevant cosmology implies, honesty about the evidence requires accepting it as a description of our actual epistemic situation rather than dismissing it as a curiosity confined to theoretical physicists. Given that, I would like some guidance on how seriously to take this: if a "typical" conscious observer truly is overwhelmingly more likely to be a spontaneously assembled brain with illusory memories than an observer with a genuine cosmic and biological history, doesn't this mean I should substantially lower my confidence in my own memories and in the past history of the universe I seem to remember? And if that conclusion can legitimately be resisted, what exactly justifies setting it aside in practice?
