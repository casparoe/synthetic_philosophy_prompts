So a few weeks back I went down a youtube rabbit hole of theist-vs-atheist debates (don't ask), and one of the theists kept using this word "Molinism" to get himself out of the whole predestination-vs-free-will mess. I looked it up and the gist seems to be that God's knowledge comes in three "logical moments" -- first natural knowledge (all necessary truths, every possible way things could go), then middle knowledge (what every possible free creature would freely do in literally any circumstance you could drop them into, aka "counterfactuals of creaturely freedom"), and only after that does God pick, out of all the feasible combos, which world to actually create -- which becomes his free knowledge. The whole sell is that this preserves real libertarian free will, since God isn't forcing anyone's hand, he's just choosing which already-true set of "if put in situation C, person A would freely do X" facts to make actual.

As a dev this smells a lot like a config file that's fully populated before the program is even compiled -- if there's a complete, fixed answer for what "I" would do in every possible circumstance, sitting there true before I exist to do anything, and "creating the world" is just loading in one branch of that giant, already-settled lookup table, in what sense was any of it ever actually up to me? Molinists apparently answer this with a "logical priority, not temporal or causal priority" move: the counterfactuals aren't making me do anything, they're somehow grounded in what I would freely do, even though their true "before" (logically, not in time) I exist to do anything. I saw a couple articles call this whole issue the "grounding objection" but they mostly just talked past each other and I didn't come away with a real verdict either way. Can you actually build the strongest case you can that this move doesn't work -- that once you grant there are settled, non-arbitrary truths about what every possible version of me would do in every possible circumstance, prior to any decision to create, you've got something functionally indistinguishable from determinism no matter what label you put on the ordering of "moments," and that the logical-vs-temporal distinction is doing rhetorical work rather than real philosophical work here. Don't just lay out both sides for me, i've already read the wikipedia summary -- I want the actual argument made.
