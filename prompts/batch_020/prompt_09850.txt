A friend and I spent last night arguing over which side of the modern "colorblind Constitution" debate Frederick Douglass would land on, and we never quite settled it. She cited his 1865 address "What the Black Man Wants" -- where he tells the nation to stop asking what to do with Black Americans and simply let them alone to compete as equal citizens -- as an early version of the colorblind constitutionalism behind cases like Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard. I countered with his postwar advocacy for the Freedmen's Bureau and, further back, his break with Garrison, in which he rejected the framers' pro-slavery intentions as controlling the Constitution's meaning and argued instead for a purposive reading anchored in the Preamble -- a method that looks built to license exactly the remedial, history-sensitive reasoning colorblind doctrine now forecloses. Setting aside his general reputation as an egalitarian, and reasoning only from these specific and sometimes-competing commitments in his own writing and practice, tell me which side of today's fight over race-conscious remedies his own interpretive method would actually commit him to, and what it would cost him, on the side he doesn't pick, to stay consistent with it.
