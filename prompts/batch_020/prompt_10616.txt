I spent about thirty-five years running an analytical chemistry lab before I retired, and lately I've fallen down the rabbit hole of philosophy of science, partly to figure out what exactly I meant all those years whenever I said a result "confirmed" a hypothesis. A few weeks back a little reading group I joined got stuck on Hempel's raven paradox — the business where "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens," so spotting a red herring or a white shoe technically confirms the raven claim too. Hempel's own fix sat fine with me at first: yes it confirms, but only by a ridiculously tiny amount, since non-black things vastly outnumber ravens.

Then somebody in the group brought up I.J. Good's old rebuttal to that — I think he even punned on it in the title, something about the white shoe itself being "a red herring" — where he cooks up a scenario, using made-up numbers of ravens vs. other birds, in which actually finding a black raven should make you slightly less confident that all ravens are black. That's not just quibbling about how much confirmation the shoe gives you, it seems to break Nicod's criterion itself, the one part of the whole mess everybody had been treating as beyond dispute. Its been bugging me ever since, and the reading group has basically split into two camps that mostly just talk past each other over wine.

Could you write me a dialogue between the two camps? One character should hold that Hempel is basically right: confirmation, properly understood as relative to no prior background knowledge, still works the way Nicod said, and Good's example only seems to break it because Good is smuggling in a bunch of extra assumptions about bird populations that were never part of the original problem. The other should hold that this is exactly the point — there's no such thing as confirmation "relative to no background knowledge" in any science that actually matters, so Good's counterexample shows the whole apparatus was quietly relying on an idealization that does no real philosophical work. I want them evenly matched, actually responding to each other's strongest moves rather than just restating their own view louder, and if you can, have them get concrete about what a Good-style scenario would even look like, since that's the part I'm still hazy on myself. Bonus points if by the end they've made some progress on whether this is a real disagreement about how evidence works or just a verbal spat over how to define "confirmation."
