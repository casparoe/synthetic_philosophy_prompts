"Norms of assertion are supposed to say what condition has to be met before it's proper to assert something. The main candidates are the belief norm (assert p only if you believe p), the truth norm (assert p only if p is true), a justified or reasonable belief norm (assert p only if you're justified in believing p), and the knowledge norm (assert p only if you know p), which is most associated with Timothy Williamson, building on earlier work by Peter Unger.

One classic argument for the knowledge norm comes from extending Moore's paradox. Moore pointed out that a sentence like 'it's raining, but I don't believe it's raining' sounds absurd even though it could easily be true, which tells us something about what's involved in sincere assertion. The knowledge-norm theorists point out that 'it's raining, but I don't know it's raining' sounds every bit as bad, and arguably worse, than the belief version. Grice's maxim of Quality already said not to assert what you believe false and not to assert what you lack adequate evidence for, and the knowledge norm is really just this same Gricean idea restated more precisely, since having 'adequate evidence' for Grice just means knowing in Williamson's sense. A pure truth norm or belief norm can't explain why the 'don't know' version is just as bad as the 'don't believe' version, but the knowledge norm can, since knowledge requires both truth and belief, so it inherits whatever was right about both of the other two views.

The lottery case is supposed to push the same conclusion from another angle. Say you own one ticket in a fair lottery of a million tickets and have no inside information, just the odds. Even though you're justified to an extremely high degree (999,999 out of a million) in believing your ticket lost, it still sounds wrong to flatly assert 'your ticket lost' rather than something hedged like 'it probably lost.' If a justified-belief norm were correct, this assertion should be totally fine, since your justification here is about as strong as justification gets. The fact that it still sounds improper suggests that justified belief isn't sufficient for warranted assertion, and that you need to know your ticket lost, which mere statistics don't give you, since you could still, however unluckily, have won. So the lottery case is normally used to argue against a justified-belief norm and in favor of the knowledge norm, since the knowledge norm, unlike the justified-belief norm, correctly predicts that this assertion is out of bounds.

Jennifer Lackey's cases of selfless assertion are supposed to create trouble for the knowledge norm from the opposite direction. In her most discussed example, a science teacher personally disbelieves evolutionary theory because of her religious upbringing, but she has thoroughly studied the evidence, recognizes that it's overwhelmingly supported, and teaches it to her class as true and well-established because she thinks it would be professionally irresponsible to let her private doubts get in the way. Since she doesn't believe the theory, she can't know it either, on the standard assumption that knowledge requires belief. But her assertion in the classroom still seems perfectly proper. Since the knowledge norm entails the belief norm, this is a real problem: if an assertion can be proper without belief, it can be proper without knowledge too, so knowledge can't be necessary for proper assertion. Lackey uses the case to argue for a truth norm instead, on which all that matters for proper assertion is that the proposition asserted is true, since the teacher's claim about evolution is true regardless of what she personally believes.

I think the knowledge norm's best response is to distinguish two things that can come apart: whether an assertion is permissible by the norm, and whether the speaker is blameworthy for making it. The knowledge-norm theorist can say that, strictly speaking, the teacher's assertion violates the norm because she lacks knowledge, but she isn't blameworthy for it, in something like the way a person can be blameless for a false assertion if they were reasonably and non-culpably deceived. This lets the defender keep knowledge as the norm while still explaining why we don't want to criticize the teacher."
