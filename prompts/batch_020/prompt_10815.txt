For a feature I'm writing on the vocabulary of AI safety research, I've been struck by how freely papers on deceptive alignment and interpretability now say that a model "believes" something false about its situation, is "deceiving" its evaluators, or "wants" to preserve its current objective through retraining. When I asked several researchers where this kind of talk gets its philosophical license, more than one pointed me to Daniel Dennett's intentional stance, so I went back and read the original essays myself before writing any of this up.

My takeaway was that Dennett's whole point was to deflate the question of whether beliefs and desires are "really" there: on his view, a system counts as having beliefs just in case it's useful for a predictor to describe it that way, and there's no further fact about what's happening inside that could settle the matter one way or another. That is presumably why he was willing to say that a thermostat "wants" to keep the room at a set temperature — the attribution isn't tracking some hidden inner state, it's just a convenient shorthand that happens to work.

If I have that right, though, I'm struggling to see what today's researchers think they're disagreeing about when they argue over whether a model's false belief is "genuine" rather than mere behavioral mimicry of belief. Dennett seems to have already shown, decades before anyone was training neural networks, that this contrast doesn't correspond to anything real: belief-talk was never meant to track more than usefulness, whether the system in question is a thermostat or a large language model. For the piece, I want to explain to readers why AI researchers keep treating this as a live, substantive dispute rather than a disagreement about words. Could you help me get that explanation right?
