I'm reporting a longer feature on the unexpected technical sophistication of the Warring States "School of Names" (名家) — the circle of Chinese disputers best known in the West through Gongsun Long's paradox that "a white horse is not a horse" (白馬非馬). Partly for research, I've spent the last several weeks sitting in on a friend's graduate seminar on classical Chinese philosophy of language, working through the White Horse Dialogue alongside the secondary literature (Graham, Hansen, Fung Yu-lan). My friend, who teaches the seminar, is buried in end-of-term grading and asked me — since I've effectively been living in this material for the article anyway — to do an independent pass on one student's short-answer response before she finalizes marks. I'm not a trained philosopher, though, so I'd rather have a rigorous outside read than trust my own sense of whether the reasoning actually holds together.

Question (20 points). (a) [8 pts] Reconstruct Gongsun Long's argument in the "White Horse Dialogue" for the claim that "a white horse is not a horse," identifying at least two distinct moves the text uses to support it. (b) [6 pts] Explain the standard diagnosis, in the secondary literature, of what is equivocal or fallacious about the argument. (c) [6 pts] Situate the dialogue within the broader project of the School of Names by contrasting Gongsun Long with the other major figure associated with that school, Hui Shi, and explain briefly how Chad Hansen's "mass-noun hypothesis" bears on Fung Yu-lan's comparison of Gongsun Long to Plato.

Student's answer:

(a) Gongsun Long defends "a white horse is not a horse" with at least two distinct moves. First, he distinguishes what a name fixes by shape from what it fixes by color: "horse" names a shape, "white" names a color, and "white horse" is a compound name that fixes both at once. Because "horse" alone leaves color open while "white horse" does not, the two names are not the same name, and so, he infers, not the same thing. Second, he points to a difference in what satisfies each name: if you ask for a horse, a yellow or black horse will do, but if you ask for a white horse, a yellow or black horse will not do. Since the two names are satisfied by different things, they cannot be identical — which again yields "white horse is not horse."

(b) The standard modern diagnosis is that the argument equivocates on "is not." Read as denying identity, the conclusion is true but trivial: the compound concept "white horse" is indeed not identical to the bare concept "horse." Read as denying class membership, the conclusion is false: every white horse is a horse in the ordinary sense of belonging to the class of horses. The sophistical force of the dialogue comes from establishing the conclusion under the first reading while letting it be heard under the second, more startling one.

(c) Gongsun Long is conventionally grouped with Hui Shi under the "School of Names," and the two are best understood as pursuing the same underlying insight from different angles. Hui Shi's paradoxes — "the sun at noon is the sun declining," "love all things equally, for heaven and earth are one body" — use the relativity of names to dissolve the boundaries we ordinarily draw between things, and the white horse argument is best read as making the identical point in miniature: names like "horse" and "white horse" carve up a single undivided reality along lines that are ultimately arbitrary and collapsible into one another. On the historiography, Chad Hansen famously read Gongsun Long as a kind of Chinese Plato, holding that universals such as "whiteness" and "horseness" subsist independently of the particular things that instantiate them. Fung Yu-lan's "mass-noun hypothesis" complicates this picture, arguing that classical Chinese nouns like "horse" behave semantically like English mass nouns such as "water," picking out stuff rather than members of a class, so that there is no need to posit separately subsisting universals at all.
