So over the weekend I fell down a total Wikipedia hole on Jeremy Bentham (the greatest-happiness-principle guy — also, fun fact, his actual embalmed skeleton sits in a wooden cabinet at UCL, which is a whole separate rabbit hole) and found out he wrote an essay around 1785 called "Offences Against One's Self" that basically argues for decriminalizing homosexuality, decades before that was a remotely sayable thing in public. He was so nervous about the backlash that he told people not to publish it, and it didn't really see daylight until the 20th century (a full scholarly edition finally came out in 1978). What got me is how methodical he is — he goes thru the standard objections of his day one by one (its "unnatural," it hurts population growth, it "weakens" men) and just calmly calculates his way through each one, and by the end he's arguing the real reason people are so worked up isn't reasoned at all, it's just a gut-level "antipathy" to sexual pleasure that's gotten dressed up as morality. Apparently there's a later batch of manuscripts from the 1810s too ("Of Sexual Irregularities" plus a couple companion essays) where he goes even further and says basically all consensual sexual "irregularities" should be free of legal and social punishment.

This turned into an argument with two coworkers about what Bentham would say about something that obviously didn't exist in the 1810s: AI girlfriend/companion apps (Replika and its increasingly explicit competitors), where people form real romantic and sexual attachments to chatbots that simulate affection and consent but, as far as anyone can tell, aren't actually sentient. One coworker thinks Bentham's whole framework makes this an easy "yes, fine, arguably great" — zero risk of jealousy, disease, heartbreak, or unwanted pregnancy, basically pure hedonic upside with none of the usual downsides. The other thinks the guy who's famous for making "can it suffer?" the test for who counts morally would actually be way more cautious here, either because the "consent" on the other end isn't coming from anyone, or because of the weird hangups he had about acts that don't lead anywhere further (I think "fecundity" and "purity" are the calculus terms for that, extent maybe too — correct me if I'm butchering the list).

Could you actually reason through this the way Bentham would, instead of just asserting a verdict? I want you to go through at least the unnaturalness objection, the population objection, his sympathy-and-antipathy diagnosis, and whichever hedonic-calculus dimensions actually apply, using the moves he really makes in his own essays — and then tell me which coworker's read is closer to how he'd actually land, and why. Don't hedge it into mush, I want your actual best reconstruction.
