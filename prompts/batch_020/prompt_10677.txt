Been down a rabbit hole on AI-alignment stuff for a piece I'm writing, and I can't shake a thought about Eliezer Yudkowsky's old "coherent extrapolated volition" idea -- the 2004 proposal, from back when he was still at the Singularity Institute, where instead of hard-coding an AI's values you have it extrapolate what humanity would want if we knew more, thought faster, and were more the people we wish we were. Everybody treats the "coherent" part like a footnote, but it seems to me the whole scheme is quietly betting that idealizing people's preferences makes them converge instead of diverging into sharper, more entrenched versions of whatever they already believe -- which is basically the same bet philosophers make with "ideal observer" and full-information theories of well-being, and its a bet that gets contested plenty over there. Write me an essay that actually grapples with whether that convergence assumption holds up: give me the strongest case for it, the strongest case against it, and then commit to an actual verdict instead of both-sidesing it into mush (a hedge like "plausible for basic needs, not for contested values" totally counts as committing -- I just don't want mealy-mouthed nothing).
