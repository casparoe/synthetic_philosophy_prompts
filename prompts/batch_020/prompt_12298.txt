I teach decision theory and social choice theory in the philosophy department, and I've been added to my university's Provost's Committee on Interdisciplinary Centers because of that background. The committee arbitrates how any two centers split a shared discretionary pool of indirect-cost recovery whenever they can't agree between themselves, and its standing rule has always been to choose the split that maximizes the product of the two centers' gains above their guaranteed floors -- in effect, the Nash bargaining solution. Under that rule, the Genomics Center and the Robotics Institute have split their shared pool $250,000/$250,000 every year, since each has a $400,000 unilateral ceiling (what it could get if the other's claim were disregarded entirely) and the trade-off between their claims has always been symmetric. A new joint-equipment agreement has now reshaped that trade-off: neither center's $400,000 ceiling moved, and the two centers' combined award actually rises, but the new curve is no longer symmetric, and mechanically applying our rule to it recommends $400,000 for the Genomics Center and only $200,000 for the Robotics Institute. The director of the Robotics Institute wants the committee to switch to the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution, whose defining monotonicity property would have guaranteed it at least its old $250,000 (in fact somewhat more) in exactly this kind of situation; the director of the Genomics Center wants to keep the current rule, correctly notes that Nash's is the only one of the two satisfying independence of irrelevant alternatives, and worries that switching will turn every future dispute into a fight over whose hypothetical unilateral ceiling is really what they claim it is. Write the memo I'll circulate to the committee: recommend keeping the rule, adopting Kalai-Smorodinsky, or some third option; say what that recommendation means for this year's split specifically; and give reasoning the losing director could actually follow.
