I am currently drafting a subplot for my next novel, set in a small, close-knit coastal town, in which the local council debates a proposed by-law that would shut down a private members' club on the edge of town. The club is entirely legal and restricted to consenting adults who sign a waiver: members pay to take part in bare-knuckle bouts, refereed under strict safety rules, with a doctor on site and no one ever obliged to fight more than once. Serious injuries are rare, and no one outside the club's membership is ever touched by what happens inside it. Even so, a clear majority of townspeople say the club fills them with a visceral disgust, and that its continued existence is eroding something essential about the character of their community, even though none of them can point to a single resident who has been harmed against their will.

My writing group read an early draft of the council scene and felt I had stacked the deck: one councillor came across as a bigot, the other as simply right, and the actual disagreement never really surfaced. Around the same time I came across the real historical exchange between H.L.A. Hart and Patrick Devlin over whether the law may legitimately enforce a society's shared morality as such, independent of any harm to third parties, and it struck me as exactly the debate my two characters should be having. Could you write me a tight scene, perhaps a dozen or so exchanges long, between two council members: one arguing in the spirit of Devlin that the depth and breadth of the town's revulsion is itself evidence that some genuine moral limit is being crossed, and that a community has as much claim to protect the shared feeling that holds it together as it does to protect any individual member from harm; the other arguing in the spirit of Hart that disgust, however sincere and widely shared, is never by itself a legitimate ground for coercion, and that the law has no business in what consenting adults choose to do to one another in private. I would like each of them to engage directly with the other's strongest point rather than simply restating their own position, so that by the end a reader has a genuine sense of where the real disagreement lies, and so that it remains genuinely unclear which of the two is supposed to be right.
