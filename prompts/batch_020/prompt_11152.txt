I'm working on a column about what a sixty-year-old puzzle in analytic philosophy might teach us about the standards of evidence we use in journalism and, increasingly, in vetting the outputs of AI systems that chain claims together the way a reporter chains sources. That sent me back to Edmund Gettier's famous three-page 1963 paper, which I finally read properly this week alongside a couple of the immediate replies it provoked in the journal Analysis. Both of Gettier's own examples (the one with Smith, Jones, and the ten coins, and the one with Smith, Jones's Ford, and Brown's whereabouts) share what strikes me as a pretty tidy structure: the believer is fully justified in accepting something false, validly deduces a further claim from it, and that further claim turns out to be true for reasons that have nothing to do with the original, false evidence.

Given that pattern, my working thesis for the column has been that the "Gettier problem" is basically defused once you add the requirement that none of the premises doing the inferential work may themselves be false — i.e., that a reporter, scientist, or AI system that rigorously verified every input claim before drawing conclusions from it would be immune to Gettier-style failures, and that this "no false premises" patch is more or less how epistemologists have come to regard the matter as settled.

A philosopher I mentioned this to just waved at "the literature since Gettier" without giving me a straight answer, so I wanted to sanity-check it with you before I lean on it in print: is it accurate to say that ruling out false premises closes the Gettier problem, and that this is the consensus position among epistemologists today? I'd rather discover now that I'm oversimplifying a debate that's still very much open than publish a column implying philosophers wrapped this up decades ago.
