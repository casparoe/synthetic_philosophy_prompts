And here, verbatim, is my classmate's answer:

"(a) Hacking distinguishes 'indifferent kinds' from 'interactive kinds.' An indifferent kind is a category whose members are unaffected by being classified: calling a sample 'copper' or an organism a 'beetle' doesn't change what the copper or the beetle does, because copper and beetles have no idea they've been classified. Interactive kinds, by contrast, are categories where the classified beings are aware of (or can become aware of) the classification and can respond to it — chiefly, though not exclusively, kinds of people. When a new way of classifying people is introduced, the people so classified may change their behavior, self-understanding, or way of presenting themselves in light of the category; this in turn can prompt the experts who maintain the category to revise its criteria, which changes who counts as falling under it, which can again change how classified people behave, and so on. This iterative feedback between classification and classified is what Hacking calls the looping effect, and it's precisely because natural-kind terms in the sciences pick out indifferent kinds that this loop doesn't arise for something like 'quark' or 'gold.'

(b) Hacking's own central illustration of this mechanism is multiple personality disorder, discussed at length in Rewriting the Soul. The idea of a personality split into dissociated parts goes back to the pioneering work of Janet and Freud on hysteria and dissociation in nineteenth-century France, and it was around this time, as the diagnosis became fashionable in French and American clinical circles, that the number of recognized multiple-personality cases first climbed rapidly. As doctors came to expect that hidden trauma lay behind the dissociation, patients in therapy increasingly produced memories of early abuse and additional 'alters' that matched clinicians' expectations, which further entrenched the trauma-based diagnostic criteria and encouraged still more diagnoses. The category and the people it was applied to shaped each other continuously, which is exactly Hacking's point: the disorder was not a stable, pre-existing target that psychiatry simply got better at detecting over time.

(c) Hacking is careful to distinguish his position from the bare claim that mental illness is 'just' a social construct. He grants that classified individuals really suffer, and that the interactive loop he describes is a genuine causal-historical process, not merely a matter of stipulating a label by fiat in the way naive constructionism is sometimes caricatured as doing. But what follows from the looping effect is that a category like multiple personality disorder cannot be understood as carving up an independent condition that exists prior to and apart from the classificatory and clinical practices engaging with it, the way a real, mind-independent disease entity would. So although Hacking resists saying the suffering is fake, the looping effect does show that a diagnosis like multiple personality disorder is not tracking a genuine disorder in the way that, say, a diagnosis of tuberculosis is."
