Hey, I want to push on something that's been bugging me about Bentham's old "Paederasty" essay (the manuscript Crompton dug up and published in the Journal of Homosexuality back in '78, also catalogued under the title "Offences Against One's Self"). Bentham flat out admits that antipathy toward the act generates a real, recurring pain in the people who feel it, plus a malicious pleasure when they see the "offender" suffer for it -- and he says as much, that this is genuine mischief and one legitimate reason for punishing it. He only blocks the pro-punishment conclusion with two moves: the antipathy is "grounded only in prejudice" rather than in the act's real mischievousness, and it already brings its own informal social punishment along regardless of what the law does.

I want you to argue that the first move isn't actually avaiable to Bentham as a strict hedonist. His official felicific calculus (intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, extent) has no slot for how warranted a pain is -- so discounting antipathetic pain as mere prejudice quietly imports some further standard for which preferences get to count, and that standard looks suspiciously like the very principle of sympathy and antipathy he trashes elsewhere as no principle at all. Push the argument as far as it'll go, and say whether the second move (the bit about informal punishment already being in effect) escapes the same charge or not.
