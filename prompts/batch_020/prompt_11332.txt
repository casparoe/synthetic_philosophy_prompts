I'm a senior currently writing my IB Extended Essay on Henry Sidgwick, whom I first got interested in after reading that Derek Parfit treated The Methods of Ethics as one of the most important works of moral philosophy ever written. While researching Sidgwick's life for background, I discovered that he was also a founding member and the first president of the Society for Psychical Research, and that for almost two decades he devoted enormous time and energy to investigating telepathy claims, mediums, and haunted houses — work that included the investigation that led the Society to publicly expose the Theosophist Madame Blavatsky as a fraud. When I brought this up with my EE supervisor, she dismissed it as "a curious biographical footnote" and told me to keep my essay focused on the "real" philosophy of the Methods. But the more I read about the dualism of practical reason that Sidgwick describes near the end of that book — the worry that egoism and utilitarian morality might both be rationally self-evident axioms of practical reason, with no further principle available to adjudicate between them unless something guarantees that duty and self-interest actually coincide — the more it seems to me that his psychical research could be read as a direct, rational response to that exact worry, rather than as an unrelated hobby he happened to also pursue.

Before I commit my whole essay to this reading, I'd like to see the strongest possible version of the argument laid out. Could you argue, as persuasively as you can, that Sidgwick's psychical research program was rationally motivated by his own diagnosis of the dualism of practical reason, rather than being a separate, merely biographical episode in his life? I'd especially appreciate it if the argument explained why a philosopher in Sidgwick's position would need actual empirical evidence of something like an afterlife or a moral order of the universe, rather than simply adopting a Kantian-style postulate of practical reason on faith, and if it directly addressed the obvious objection that a purely normative problem about the rational authority of self-interest versus morality could never, even in principle, be settled by empirical findings about ghosts or telepathy.
