As a journalist who's spent years covering the history and philosophy of science for a general readership, I've just been invited to give the commencement address to the graduating class of a Jesuit university's physics and astronomy program, largely on the strength of a piece I once wrote on Galileo's 1615 letter to the Grand Duchess Christina. I want the address built on what that letter actually argues, not the usual "brave scientist versus dogmatic church" story: that scripture and nature both come from God and so cannot truly conflict, that scripture's language is deliberately accommodated to ordinary understanding because its purpose is salvation rather than astronomy, and that once a claim about nature is genuinely demonstrated, it is our reading of scripture that must give way, not the demonstration -- a more demanding position than today's comfortable idea that science and religion simply occupy separate domains. A few Jesuit faculty in the audience will know Cardinal Bellarmine's counter-argument by heart; most of the graduates' families won't. Write the address so that Galileo's actual argument is doing the shaping work -- determining what the speech can and cannot honestly conclude -- for both halves of the room.
