Until last year my relationship with political philosophy was purely academic — an undergraduate thesis on Hobbes, then a decade doing something else entirely. That changed when I took a job building coordination infrastructure for autonomous trading agents that negotiate and settle deals directly with one another, with no court, regulator, or other third party standing behind the handshake. Trying to work out how much two mutually distrustful agents can actually commit to, absent any of that backing, pulled me into a genuinely obscure corner of game theory: program equilibrium.

As I understand it, the idea — introduced by Moshe Tennenholtz in a 2004 paper and picked up more recently in AI-safety research — is that when the players in a game are not black boxes but programs able to read one another's source code before acting, they can condition their own behavior on what they find there. Two programs that each cooperate in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma only if they can verify that the other will do the same can sustain mutual cooperation as a stable equilibrium: neither side gains anything from unilaterally reneging, because the other's program detects the mismatch and defects in response. What makes this feel like a genuine result rather than a trick is that it needs no repeated play, no reputation, and no punisher standing over the interaction — the commitment is enforced entirely by the legibility of the code itself.

This is what keeps pulling my mind back to Hobbes. His case in Leviathan for an absolute, sword-bearing sovereign turns on the claim that covenants among self-interested men are empty without an external enforcer, and his answer to the Foole — who argues that breaking one's word is sometimes the reasonable course when one can get away with it — ultimately rests on the conviction that neither reason nor reputation alone, without the standing fear of a common power, can make a covenant reliable enough to act on. Program equilibrium looks, on its face, like exactly the kind of counterexample his argument would need to survive: two parties rendering their commitments mutually verifiable, and thereby self-enforcing, with no sword anywhere in the picture. I would like your considered reconstruction of how Hobbes, reasoning from his own premises about human nature, covenant, and the causes of quarrel, would respond to having this idea put to him — whether he would treat verifiable source code as just a subtler sword, insist that it fails to reach the deeper problem he was diagnosing, or concede that it opens a genuine gap in his case for sovereignty. I would rather have that reasoning worked through carefully on Hobbes's own terms than a quick, confident verdict either way.
