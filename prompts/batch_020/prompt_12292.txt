And here's the student's answer, copied exactly as written:

(a) Even though Lumpl and Goliath are made of all the same matter at every moment they both exist, most philosophers deny that they're the very same object. The standard argument runs via Leibniz's Law: if a is identical to b, then a and b share literally all of their properties, including modal and historical ones, not just the ones they have "in the actual world right now." But Lumpl has properties Goliath doesn't: Lumpl existed for weeks before the sculptor got around to shaping it, whereas Goliath only came into existence once the shaping was finished. Likewise, if the clay were squashed back into a ball, Lumpl would survive (it would just be a lump with a different shape), but Goliath would be destroyed -- a statue can't survive being squashed. So Lumpl has properties (having existed unshaped; being able to survive squashing) that Goliath lacks, and by Leibniz's Law that's enough to show they are numerically distinct, even though at every moment of Goliath's existence they are built of exactly the same atoms arranged in exactly the same way. This is essentially the reasoning behind Allan Gibbard's famous Lumpl/Goliath case: Gibbard argues that the two must be distinct precisely because they differ in their persistence conditions, which is meant to vindicate the intuition that "statue" and "lump of clay" pick out different, sortal-dependent kinds of thing.

(b) One influential response, associated with Wiggins and worked out in detail by Lynne Rudder Baker as "the constitution view," accepts that Lumpl and Goliath are numerically distinct but denies that this makes their relationship merely accidental. Instead, Lumpl constitutes Goliath: constitution is supposed to be a real, intimate unity relation, weaker than identity but much stronger than two things simply happening to overlap in space. Concretely, the view says Goliath and Lumpl are two overlapping aggregates of temporal parts: nearly all of Lumpl's temporal stages are also stages of Goliath, and the two only part ways at the edges, where Lumpl has "extra" stages -- before the shaping, or after a hypothetical squashing -- that Goliath's history doesn't include. Because the two objects share the overwhelming majority of their stages and only diverge at the boundaries, we get the closeness of constitution without full identity. A natural problem for this kind of view is what Karen Bennett calls "the grounding problem": if Lumpl and Goliath are made of all the same matter arranged in exactly the same way at every moment they coincide, it's mysterious what could possibly ground the fact that one of them can survive squashing and the other can't. Baker's answer is to treat this modal difference as a brute fact about the different sorts, statue and lump, that constitution has to respect; critics worry that this just relabels the mystery rather than resolving it.
