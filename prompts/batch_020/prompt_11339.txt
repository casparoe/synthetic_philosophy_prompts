I write a science-and-ideas newsletter, and I'm putting together a piece on analytic philosophers who took unexpected religious turns late in life. Hilary Putnam is the one I keep getting stuck on, so I wrote out the core of my argument and could use a second pair of eyes before I go further (he's been gone almost a decade now, which is basically my whole peg for running this at all). Here's the paragraph that's giving me trouble:

"For most of his career Putnam was the guy who said there's no 'God's-eye view' -- no ready-made, perspective-free fact about how the world really is that our best theory either nails or doesn't. That was basically his whole case against metaphysical realism: even an omniscient being would still need some conceptual scheme to carve up the world, so 'the way things are, full stop, no viewpoint attached' is a fantasy. Then somewhere in his sixties and seventies he became a geniunely observant, practicing Jew, wrote a whole book putting Rosenzweig, Buber, Levinas and Wittgenstein together as resources for living, and started talking like someone who thinks he has real, if modest, knowledge of God. I don't think this is the reversal it looks like -- I think he's running the identical God's-eye-view argument on theology that he ran on physics for thirty years. He won't accept old-school realist theism, which claims to describe how God 'really' is with no viewpoint attached, the exact picture he trashes everywhere else. But he also wont take the easy non-realist exit, the move where God-talk just expresses a commitment or a way of living with nothing truth-apt about it. He wants a third box: real truth for religious claims, minus the God's-eye metaphysics holding it up."

My problem is I can't tell if that third box actually exists or if I'm just describing Wittgensteinian fideism with better branding -- the exact thing Kai Nielsen accused Wittgenstein (and by extension people like Putnam) of, and that Putnam spent real effort denying. Is there an actual difference between "truth-apt religious claims with no God's-eye-view backing them" and fideism, or am I fooling myself? And separately, does the sixties/seventies framing even make sense as the shape of his religious life, or is that me flattening something messier into a tidy essay arc? Be blunt about it, I'd rather find the hole now than after this goes to my editor.
