In English class last week, we finished a novel in which one of the two main characters dies in the final chapter, and several people in the room were visibly upset, even though, as our teacher pointed out, everyone there knows perfectly well that neither of them ever existed. She treated this as a fairly unremarkable fact about how storytelling affects the brain, and at the time that explanation seemed sufficient.

A few days later, something similar happened to me in a very different setting, and now I'm less sure the matter is so simple. I've been playing a long, choice-based role-playing game that a couple of friends are also going through on their own, and about thirty hours in, I made a decision without realizing how much weight it carried. It ended up getting a companion character I had grown genuinely attached to killed off for good, and I was surprised by how upset I actually felt afterward. When I mentioned this to one of them, he argued that my reaction didn't really make sense: the character is nothing more than dialogue trees and character models, and, unlike in a novel or a film, I could simply reload an earlier save and choose differently if the outcome truly bothered me. In his view, that makes my being upset even less justified than crying over a book, since I am the one choosing to let the loss stand instead of undoing it.

I haven't been able to explain to him why this doesn't fully sit right with me, even though I can't really dispute that the option to undo it is right there. Is there a principled way to determine whether an emotional reaction like mine is actually justified, and does the fact that I had some control over the outcome, but chose not to use it, change that analysis at all — or is my friend simply mistaken about what that control is supposed to show?
