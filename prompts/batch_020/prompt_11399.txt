I'm the student representative on my high school's science-fair review committee, which vets every project before it goes to our regional fair. Following the rules used by major national fairs, our policy requires a named supervising scientist, written pre-approval, and strict pain-and-distress limits for projects on live vertebrate animals, but nothing of the kind for invertebrates. A classmate has proposed a project, based on a real study, that would give crayfish mild electric shocks and then see whether they avoid brightly lit areas afterward — a response similar enough to human anxiety that it's reportedly reversible with the same class of anti-anxiety drug used in people. Because crayfish are invertebrates, the project needs no special review, even though an identical design on a fish would face heavy restrictions. Please draft the short memo I'll bring to Tuesday's meeting: recommend whether we approve the project as compliant, hold it to the vertebrate standard anyway, or find a middle path, and seriously engage the strongest argument for just applying the rule as written — that letting a committee reinterpret "vertebrate" case-by-case based on contested science makes approval arbitrary and unpredictable for students who followed the rules in good faith.
