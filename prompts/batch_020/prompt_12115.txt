Here's the question as it was given to the student: "Explain the backward induction argument that's standardly used to derive the equilibrium outcome of the finite Centipede game. Then explain why this outcome is considered paradoxical, both in light of the formal result connecting backward induction to common knowledge of rationality, and in light of the experimental evidence on how people actually play the game. Finally, describe one philosophical response to the paradox and evaluate whether you think it succeeds."

And here's the student's response, typed up exactly as they wrote it: "In the Centipede Game, two players alternate choosing between 'take' (ending the game and pocketing a slightly larger share of a growing pot) and 'pass' (continuing the game, which grows the pot further but hands the next decision to the other player). Backward induction solves the game by starting at the very last decision node: at that point the player facing the choice can only gain by taking, since there is no future round left to justify passing. Given this, the player at the second-to-last node should anticipate that the last player will take, so passing is pointless for them too, and they should take as well. This reasoning propagates all the way back to the first move, so the unique Nash equilibrium of the game is for the very first player to take immediately, even though this leaves a lot of money on the table compared to what the players could get by cooperating for a while. This conclusion is paradoxical for two seperate reasons. First, Robert Axelrod showed formally that if it is common knowledge that both players are rational, then the backward induction outcome must occur — rationality alone, once it's transparent to both sides, forces immediate defection. This is a strong result because it doesn't just say immediate defection is an equilibrium, it says any departure from it is inconsistent with the common knowledge assumption. Second, and in tension with this, actual human subjects overwhelmingly do not play this way: in experiments (e.g. McKelvey and Palfrey's), most games run for several rounds before ending, and very few players take at the very first opportunity. So a prediction that seems to follow almost inescapably from minimal rationality assumptions is barely ever observed, which suggests the backward induction argument, whatever its formal validity, doesn't describe what rationality actually demands once you take seriously what would have to be common knowledge in practice. One influential response, due to Binmore, distinguishes 'eductive' justifications for an equilibrium (players reasoning their way to it a priori, from the rules and common knowledge of rationality alone) from 'evolutive' justifications (an equilibrium as what a population converges to over repeated play and learning). Binmore's point is that backward induction is much better supported evolutively than eductively — it's a fine description of what happens once players have had time to learn a game, but nothing forces a rational agent to derive it purely a priori, since the reasoning requires assuming things about what a player would believe after witnessing a deviation that was supposed to be impossible under common knowledge of rationality in the first place. I think this response basically works: it explains why the experimental results don't refute rationality as such, since most experimental centipede games are one-off or short series with no time for evolutive convergence, so we shouldn't have expected backward induction play to show up in the data at all."
