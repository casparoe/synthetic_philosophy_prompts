I don't have any formal background in theology or philosophy, but over the past year I've spent a lot of my spare time reading around Isaac Newton's religious writings, ever since I found out that he wrote far more about biblical prophecy and the early history of the church than he ever did about physics, and kept almost all of it hidden because it would have exposed him as a heretic. That curiosity eventually took me to the Newton Project's online archive, where I read through his 1690 letter to John Locke, later published as "An Historical Account of Two Notable Corruptions of Scripture." Most of the letter is a long, patient argument, built verse by verse and manuscript by manuscript, that two texts traditionally used to prove the Trinity (1 John 5:7 and 1 Timothy 3:16) were never in the original Greek at all. I've been working slowly through the shorter section, on 1 Timothy 3:16, and there is one move in it that has stopped me, because it doesn't read like the historical argument surrounding it at all: it reads more like an argument about what a sentence can or can't sensibly mean than like a piece of history. I mentioned it to a friend who studied philosophy at university, and she said it reminded her of something logicians or philosophers of language argue about, but she couldn't tell me on the spot what to make of it.

Here is the passage, keeping Newton's own spelling (I've cut out the many pages of manuscript citations that sit between the two halves and marked the gap with an ellipsis): "What the Latines have done to this text the Greeks have done to that of S. Paul 1 Tim. 3.16. For by changing Ο in ΟΣ & both into ΘΣ (the abbreviation of Θεὸς) they now read Great is the mystery of godlinesse God manifested in the flesh: whereas all the Churches for the first four or five hundred years, & the authors of all the ancient Versions, Ierome as well as the rest, read, Great is the mystery of godliness which was manifested in the flesh. [...] And besides to read Θεὸς makes the sense obscure & difficult. For how can it properly be said that God was justified in the spirit? But to read ὃ, & interpret it of Christ as the ancient Christians did without restraining it to his divinity, makes the sense very easy. For the promised & long expected Messias, the hope of Israel is to us the great mystery of godlinesse, & this mystery was at length manifested to the Iews from the time of his baptism, & justified to be the person whom they expected."

Could you help me get clear on the "makes the sense obscure and difficult" step? First, I'd like a plain, line-by-line account of why Newton thinks it is actually incoherent, and not merely theologically unwelcome, to say that God was "justified in the spirit," while the very same words make good sense once "Christ" or "the Messiah" is understood as the subject instead. Second, I'd like your honest assessment of how much this little argument can really carry on its own: is it an independent check on the mountain of manuscript evidence he spends the rest of the letter assembling, or does it only look convincing because he has already set aside, on other grounds, the obvious Trinitarian reply that it is God the Son, in his assumed human nature, who is said to be "justified"? I can't tell whether Newton has found a genuine logical problem with the Trinitarian reading or is quietly smuggling his conclusion into his premises.
