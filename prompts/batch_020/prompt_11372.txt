During my career in condensed-matter physics, I grew used to the idea that an apparently smooth, qualitative change—water boiling into steam, a magnet losing its magnetization—turns out, once you examine the underlying statistical mechanics closely enough, to hinge on a perfectly sharp critical point. Now that I've started reading philosophy in retirement, this makes me instinctively sympathetic to Timothy Williamson's epistemicism about vagueness, which holds that predicates like "heap" or "bald" have exact boundaries we are simply constitutionally unable to know, rather than the supervaluationist view that borderline cases are genuine gaps in truth-value.

I've read that the real test between the two views is how they handle higher-order vagueness: the fact that it is equally unclear where the "clear cases" shade into the "borderline cases," and where those in turn shade into the "clear negative cases." Epistemicism supposedly absorbs this as just more ignorance, while supervaluationism needs extra machinery (precisifications of precisifications) to avoid either a regress or smuggling back in the very sharp boundaries it was meant to avoid.

Please compare epistemicism and supervaluationism with special attention to this higher-order vagueness problem, tell me whether the claim above is accurate, and say whether my physics analogy genuinely supports epistemicism or instead trades on a confusion between vagueness in language and indeterminacy in nature. Reach and defend a verdict on which theory gives the more coherent overall account of vagueness.
