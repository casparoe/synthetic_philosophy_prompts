Since retiring from a career in theoretical cosmology, I have been auditing a graduate seminar on ethics. The instructor, aware that I still think naturally in terms of infinite series and convergence, asked me to give one of the problem sets a first read before she grades it herself, and I would welcome a second, more expert opinion on my own assessment before I return it to her.

The question was: "In his discussion of infinite ethics, Bostrom argues that if the universe contains an infinite quantity of value, total utilitarianism faces a problem he calls 'infinitarian paralysis.' (a) Explain this problem and why it threatens the action-guiding role of the theory. (b) The 'overtaking criterion,' borrowed from welfare economics, is sometimes proposed as a way of comparing infinite utility streams without summing them to a single number. Explain how the criterion works, and assess whether it succeeds in resolving the paralysis problem."

The student answered:

"(a) Total utilitarianism tells us to choose whichever available action produces the greatest sum of utility across everyone affected. Bostrom points out that this breaks down if the universe already contains an infinite amount of positive value — for instance, if it is spatially infinite and populated by infinitely many happy beings. The underlying difficulty is that an infinite sum of utilities does not behave like a finite one: because infinitely many positive and negative contributions can be reordered, the very same values can be made to add up to almost any total you like, or to no determinate total at all, depending on the order in which you add them. Since the theory can no longer say which of two infinite worlds has the greater sum, it also cannot say that saving a child or preventing a war makes things go better overall, because the 'total' was never well-defined to begin with. Any finite act is swamped, and utilitarianism is left unable to prefer one option to another — hence 'paralysis.'

(b) The overtaking criterion says that one infinite utility stream is better than another if, from some point on, its running total is always at least as large as the other's, and eventually strictly larger. This lets us compare two streams that share the same long-run 'tail' but differ at only finitely many points, without ever adding either one up to a grand total. Applied to Bostrom's case, if I save a drowning child at time t, the resulting stream has a strictly higher running total than the alternative from t onward, forever, so the overtaking criterion ranks it as better. Because this is exactly the comparison ordinary total utilitarianism could not make, Bostrom adopts the overtaking criterion as his solution to the paralysis problem, and the puzzle is thereby resolved: we no longer need a well-defined grand total in order to say that helping the child was the right thing to do."

Could you grade this out of 20 and assign a corresponding letter grade, with comments keyed to specific claims in the answer rather than a general impression — including anything that reads smoothly but that a grader working quickly might wave through? A short rubric in JSON, covering the diagnosis of the paralysis problem, the mechanics of the overtaking criterion, and the final assessment of it, would be a useful supplement.
