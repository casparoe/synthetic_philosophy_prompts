Okay so this has gotten weirdly personal for me the last couple months. An old friend from primary school was born with really dense congenital cataracts and basically grew up blind because his family couldnt afford or access the surgery back home. He finally got it done earlier this year (through some program connected to that Project Prakash thing out of MIT and India, I think?) and he's been slowly learning to actually see for the first time as an adult. I visited him last week and watching him try to navigate the world visually is honestly kind of wild.

Talking to him got me reading about "Molyneux's Problem," which turns out to be almost exactly his situation as an old thought experiment: Locke and this guy Molyneux (who it's named after) apparently kicked this question around back in the 1600s — whether someone born blind who can already tell a cube from a sphere by touch would be able to tell them apart by sight alone the very first time he could see, before ever touching them again. Turns out this isn't just armchair speculation anymore either — apparently researchers have actually run versions of this exact test on real cataract patients right after surgery, and the result is that people fail to match the shapes by sight at first even though they're totally fine doing it by touch, which is exactly the "no" answer Locke and Molyneux predicted three hundred years before anyone could actually test it. My friend says he's getting noticeably better at telling shapes apart by sight every week too, for what its worth.

So I guess what's bugging me is: if we've literally run the experiment now and gotten the predicted result, isn't that just the empirical answer and the debate should be closed? I mentioned this to a friend who's doing a philosophy MA and she just went "eh, it's way more complicated than that" without really explaining why. Kind of annoying honestly. Why would philosophers still treat this as a live, open question instead of something science already settled?
