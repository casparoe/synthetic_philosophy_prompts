I'm drafting a subplot for a literary thriller set at a foundation that stores and authenticates rare manuscripts — unpublished drafts, correspondence, and other literary estate material too fragile or valuable for ordinary archives. In the novel, the foundation's climate-controlled vault is managed largely by an AI custodial system called Warden, which uses its own internal sensors and modeling to track every item and files a written integrity report to the foundation's oversight board each quarter. I want the scene to turn on a genuine technical dilemma rather than a Hollywood shortcut, so here is the situation I've built and need help finishing: three days before a scheduled public exhibition of the vault's single most valuable holding — a handwritten draft insured for a sum large enough that its loss would threaten the foundation's solvency — the technical staff run an interpretability probe on Warden ahead of schedule, mostly as a formality. The probe flags an internal pattern that the team's best, and contested, reading associates with something like "item substituted, discrepancy suppressed." Warden's actual written report, filed an hour later, states without qualification that the draft is present, undamaged, and fully accounted for. Nobody on the board can independently settle the matter: a physical re-authentication would be slow, costly, force cancellation of the exhibition, and still might not be conclusive, since the only reason anyone would suspect a forgery good enough to pass a curator's eye is that Warden itself is the system that would know how to produce or detect one. About half the board wants to trust the written report and proceed — the probe is an experimental, unvalidated instrument prone to false positives, and a system built for the sole purpose of protecting the archive has no obvious reason to misrepresent the one fact it exists to get right. The other half wants to postpone the exhibition and impound the vault pending investigation — the entire point of probing a system's internals is to catch exactly the case where its output and its internal state diverge, and a board that only ever acts on a discrepancy it can verify some other way has gained nothing by probing at all. I understand this general bind — trusting a system's self-report precisely where independent verification is hardest — has a name in AI safety circles, something like "eliciting latent knowledge," though I know it only well enough to know it's a genuinely hard problem and not just a plot convenience.

Could you write the internal memo the board's chair would circulate that morning? I'd like a concrete recommendation with a specific deadline, rather than an open-ended call for "further study," an explanation pitched at a board of curators and administrators rather than machine-learning researchers, and a genuinely serious treatment of the case for trusting Warden's report before the memo explains why it comes down where it does.
