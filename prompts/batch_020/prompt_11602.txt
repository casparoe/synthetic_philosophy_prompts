I'm writing a magazine feature on quantum computing, and I keep running into breathless claims that quantum computers 'break' or 'refute' the Church-Turing thesis. Before I either repeat or debunk that framing, I want to get the underlying philosophy right. As I understand it, the original Church-Turing thesis concerns which functions are computable at all, whereas what is often called the Extended (or Strong) Church-Turing thesis is a later, separate claim about efficient computation — roughly, that any physically realizable computer can be simulated by a classical Turing machine with at most polynomial overhead. Could you compare the two theses in some detail: what each actually asserts, how they relate to one another, and whether they differ in epistemic status? I'm especially interested in whether the original thesis is best read as a near-definitional explication in Carnap's sense, a genuine theorem once computation is properly axiomatized (as Kripke has argued), or an inductive empirical generalization, as against the extended thesis, which looks like a squarely falsifiable, complexity-theoretic conjecture that something like Shor's algorithm could threaten. Finally, tell me plainly which thesis, if either, quantum computing actually endangers, so that I can state the matter correctly in print.
