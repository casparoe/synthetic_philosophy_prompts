Random rabbit hole from last night: I was arguing with a coworker about whether our LLM-based support bot actually "understands" tickets or if its just autocomplete on steroids, and that somehow turned into me googling Descartes, since I vaguely remembered he thought animals were basically robots (there's apparently even a term for it, "beast machine" or something). Turns out the actual argument is way more specific than the pop-science version - in the Discourse on Method he lays out two concrete tests for telling a real thinking thing from an automaton dressed up to look like one: (1) a machine could never handle language well enough to give an appropriate reply to literally anything said to it, the way even the dullest human can, and (2) even if a machine nails a few specific tasks, it'll always faceplant on others, because reason (per Descartes) is supposed to be a general-purpose tool that works in any situation, while a machine's parts are each built for one specific job and nothing else.

So now I can't stop thinking about how weirdly relevant this is to LLMs and was hoping you could help me reason it through properly. Using his actual two tests, not the dumbed down "animals = machines" summary - what do you think Descartes would conclude if he sat down with something like GPT-4 or Claude? Does a model that can respond fluently to almost any prompt basically pass his language test in a way he never saw coming, or would he say its still failing it in some deeper sense he'd actually care about? And for the second test - is "the model falls apart outside its training distribution" basically the modern version of what he meant by machines having a fixed "disposition of organs" instead of universal reason, or is that comparison too cute to actually hold up? Genuinely not sure which way he'd land on this, or if he'd even think it's a coherent question to ask about something with no body or organs at all.
