Over dinner last week, a friend and I fell into an argument that began over whether last winter's transit strike caused the brief improvement in the city's air quality, and ended as a dispute about the nature of causation itself. I defended David Lewis's view that causation is, at bottom, counterfactual dependence between distinct events; she, trained in epidemiology, defended the interventionist theory associated with James Woodward and Judea Pearl, on which X causes Y just in case some possible intervention on X would change Y while everything else is held fixed. Neither of us could produce a single case that forced a verdict between the two accounts: whatever example one of us raised, the other found a way to reinterpret it to fit her own view. I am curious whether you can succeed where we could not. Construct one detailed, original scenario — not the standard neuron-diagram, preempted-assassin, or rock-throwing cases — in which the two theories are committed to different verdicts about whether one event caused another, and explain precisely why each theory rules as it does.
