I have no formal training in philosophy, only years of picking things apart in my own time, and I recently worked myself into an argument that I cannot find my way out of. It started from an ordinary situation: a friend of mine refused to even look at a report that seemed to contradict something he was certain of, and when I tried to articulate exactly what was wrong with that attitude, I ended up building an argument that seemed to justify it instead. Some searching afterward suggests I had rediscovered a known puzzle, sometimes called the dogmatism paradox, but I have not seen it paired with the example or the diagnosis below, so I would value your comments on both.

The argument, as I would state it:

1. Suppose I know that P.
2. If I know that P, then I know that P is true, and so I already know that any evidence that later turns up against P must be misleading in some way, whatever form it takes.
3. If I already know, in advance, that some future evidence will be misleading, I am entitled to discount it, or even decline to examine it, once it appears.
4. Therefore, for anything I currently count as knowing, I am entitled in advance to dismiss any future evidence against it.

That conclusion looks like a license for total closed-mindedness, yet each step seemed sound to me when I first wrote it down, and I still cannot locate the flaw with any confidence.

Here is the case I used to pressure-test it. I am confident I did not run a particular red light, since I remember coming to a full stop. Suppose a ticket then arrives with a camera photograph attached. On the argument above, I already knew, before opening the envelope, that whatever the photograph showed had to be misleading somehow, whether through a mistimed light, a misread plate, or something else. So it seems I would be entitled to throw the envelope away unopened.

My best current guess is that step 3 is where things go wrong: knowing, in the abstract, that any evidence against P would have to be misleading if it existed does not obviously transfer into an entitlement to dismiss some specific, particular piece of evidence once it is actually in front of me with its own internal features to assess. But I can also imagine someone locating the trouble earlier, in step 2, by denying that the everyday "knowledge" doing the work there is ever the flat-out, evidence-proof sort the argument needs; on that view, what I am really reporting is strong, revisable confidence rather than knowledge proper, and the argument never gets off the ground.

I would appreciate your comments on three things: first, whether my four-step reconstruction is actually valid as stated; second, whether the red-light case is a faithful instance of the argument or quietly changes the subject somewhere; and third, whether either of my two candidate diagnoses genuinely blocks the argument, or whether both fail for the same underlying reason once examined closely.
