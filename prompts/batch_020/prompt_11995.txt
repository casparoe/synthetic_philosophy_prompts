In my seminar on formal epistemology this term, we have spent the last couple of weeks on David Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals. As I understand the argument, Lewis starts from Adams' Thesis, the claim that a rational agent's credence in "if A, then C" should equal their conditional credence P(C | A), and shows that if this equation is required to hold generally, both for an agent's actual credence function and for every credence function that could result from conditionalizing on new evidence, the underlying probability function is forced into triviality: A and C end up probabilistically independent no matter what they are, which is absurd once the space of propositions is even modestly rich. The upshot, as our professor put it, is that no conditional connective can validate Adams' Thesis across the board while still denoting an ordinary proposition.

A few friends and I run an informal betting pool on campus and local political events, and we recently started offering conditional bets – for instance, a bet that pays out only if a particular ballot measure passes, conditional on turnout exceeding some threshold, with everyone's stake refunded if turnout falls short. We set the odds for these bets by simply asking participants what they take the conditional probability of the measure passing to be, given high turnout. When I described this practice to a friend from the seminar, she argued that the whole thing is quietly incoherent, since we are effectively treating "the measure passes, given high turnout" as a proposition whose probability just is our conditional credence – exactly the identification that Lewis's proof shows cannot hold up in general. That struck me as basically right, and it seems to generalize to plenty of other everyday cases, such as conditional prediction-market contracts or a doctor quoting a patient's odds of recurrence conditional on a given treatment choice. Before I take that conclusion for granted, though, I would like to understand whether it is actually correct: does Lewis's result really show that our betting pool, and these other everyday uses of conditional probability, are undermined, or does the argument not apply to cases like this, and if not, why not?
