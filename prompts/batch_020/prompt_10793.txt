Working through Eliezer Yudkowsky's old "Human's Guide to Words" sequence, especially "Disputing Definitions" and its treatment of the tree-falls-in-a-forest question, left me with a fairly strong prior that most metaphysical disputes dissolve once you taboo the contested term and ask what each side's position actually cashes out to in anticipated experience. It was gratifying, then, to discover that academic metaontology seems to have converged on the same insight from another direction: Eli Hirsch's quantifier variance thesis holds that rival camps in the debate over mereological composition are simply using "exists" with different, equally legitimate meanings, while David Chalmers's method of elimination offers a formal test for exactly this sort of merely verbal dispute. Given all that, I'm puzzled by philosophers like Ted Sider, who argue in Writing the Book of the World that one candidate meaning of "exists" really does carve reality at its joints better than the rest. Isn't Sider just declining to apply the taboo test to his own vocabulary? Or is there some real disagreement left over in a case like his, even after every disputed term has been tabooed?
