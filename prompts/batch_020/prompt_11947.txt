I'm a novelist on the three-person jury for the Crisis Circle Prize, a debut-fiction award founded in homage to the literary contests W. E. B. Du Bois ran through The Crisis in the 1920s. Its deed of gift instructs jurors to weigh entries partly by their value as "propaganda for the race" — the founder's own gloss on a claim Du Bois makes in his 1926 essay "Criteria of Negro Art": that all art is propaganda and ever must be. Our two finalists have split the jury: a spare, formally ambitious novel about a woman's ambivalent homecoming that makes no political claim and gives its Black characters flaws a hostile reviewer could seize on; a courtroom novel dramatizing a wrongful conviction in unambiguous moral terms, already drawing interest from advocacy groups, though two of us find its prose thin and its characters built to argue rather than to live. One juror says the deed settles the question for the courtroom novel; another calls that a shallow reading of Du Bois. Please draft the memo I'll circulate to the jury: a clear recommendation for which novel should win (or a different resolution, if you think the choice as posed is false), grounded in what Du Bois actually argued rather than the slogan, plus a fair statement of the losing side's best case.
