For a piece I'm reporting on how philosophy departments are updating their curricula to address AI, I recently sat in on a graduate seminar on moral responsibility and autonomous systems. The instructor shared one of her exam questions along with a student's response and asked, half-seriously, whether I would grade it the same way she had. I'd like an independent assessment before comparing notes with her.

Exam question: In 2004, Andreas Matthias argued that what he called "learning automata" create a responsibility gap: situations in which harm results from a machine's autonomous behavior, yet no human involved satisfies the traditional conditions for being held morally responsible, while the machine itself cannot be a bearer of responsibility either.
(a) Reconstruct Matthias's argument for why increasingly autonomous, learning-based systems threaten to open this gap.
(b) Some have argued that assigning strict legal liability to manufacturers — making them pay for harms regardless of fault — is sufficient to close the gap. Assess this claim, drawing on the distinction between liability and culpability.
(c) Does building in mechanisms for "meaningful human control" close the gap, or merely relocate it?

Student's answer: Matthias's worry is that moral responsibility has traditionally rested on two conditions: the agent had to know what she was doing, or could reasonably have foreseen the consequences, and she had to be in control of the action. Ordinary machines don't threaten this picture, because their behavior is just an extension of the designer's instructions — if a calculator gives the wrong answer, the programmer is on the hook. Learning systems are different, because they are black boxes whose internal decision-making is opaque even to the engineers who built them, so no one can really be said to have known or controlled what the system would do in a given situation. This is what opens the gap: the system caused the harm, the humans around it don't satisfy the knowledge and control conditions, and the system itself isn't the kind of thing that can feel guilt or be meaningfully punished, so it cannot inherit the responsibility either.

On the liability question, I think assigning strict liability to manufacturers does close the gap in the sense that actually matters practically. Even if no one is at fault in the traditional sense, strict liability guarantees that a determinate party — the manufacturer — has to answer for the harm, whether by compensating victims or by being publicly named as responsible for it. Since the worry behind the responsibility gap is that harms might occur for which nobody answers, a rule that always assigns an answerable party removes the gap, even if that party didn't do anything blameworthy.

Meaningful human control looks like a more direct fix, since it tries to keep a human "in the loop" who can genuinely be said to authorize the system's actions. If regulations require this kind of oversight, the gap shouldn't arise in the first place, because there will always be a human whose judgment the system is tracking. Of the three responses considered here, I'd give this one the best chance of actually preventing gaps rather than just managing them after the fact.
