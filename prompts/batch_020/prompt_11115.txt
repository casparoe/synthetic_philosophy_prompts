"Multiple realizability is often said to have been the downfall of mind–brain type-identity theory and the making of functionalism. (a) Reconstruct the multiple-realizability argument against type-identity theory as precisely as you can, being careful to distinguish it from other well-known objections to identity theory. (b) Functionalism has itself been accused of facing a structurally similar dilemma once we ask how finely to individuate the relevant causal/functional roles. (c) Briefly say whether you think functionalism is ultimately better off than type-identity theory here, or whether it just relocates the problem."

And here is the student's answer, in full:

"Type-identity theory holds that every mental state type is identical to some physical state type — pain, for instance, just is C-fiber firing. The multiple-realizability argument, due to Putnam and developed further by Fodor, points out that the same mental state can be realized by different physical states in different systems: octopuses and hypothetical silicon-based Martians could both be in pain despite having entirely different underlying physiology, so pain cannot be identical to any single physical type. Kripke's discussion of rigid designators reinforces this point: since 'pain' and 'C-fiber firing' would, if truly identical, be necessarily identical, but we can easily conceive of pain occurring without C-fiber firing — in an octopus, for instance — the identity claim has to be false. Together, these considerations show that type-identity theory mistakes a local, contingent correlation for a metaphysical identity. Functionalism improves on this by individuating mental states functionally, in terms of their causal role among inputs, outputs, and other mental states, so that anything that plays the pain-role counts as being in pain, whatever it is made of.

Functionalism, however, faces an analogous worry once we ask how coarsely or finely to specify the relevant causal role. If the functional description is very fine-grained and tailored closely to human neurophysiology, a Martian whose internal states play a recognizably similar but not identical causal role would wrongly be excluded from feeling pain. If, instead, the description is very coarse-grained, systems that merely mimic the right input–output profile without anything plausibly mind-like going on underneath — Block's famous Chinese Nation, in which a billion people each act as a 'neuron' relaying messages by radio — would count as literally being in pain, which is deeply counterintuitive. Block calls the coarse-grained problem, where a system like the Chinese Nation wrongly counts as sentient, the problem of chauvinism, and the fine-grained problem, where Martians are wrongly excluded, the problem of liberalism.

On balance, I would say functionalism doesn't so much solve the problem type-identity theory faced as restate it one level up: both theories need some principled way of saying which differences among realizers matter and which don't, and neither Putnam's nor Block's discussion gives us a clean criterion for drawing that line."
