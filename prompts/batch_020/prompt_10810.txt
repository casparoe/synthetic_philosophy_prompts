Okay so I've fallen down a rabbit hole lately reading about Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity (started from a youtube video explaining AIXI, then went further into some actual textbook stuff), and there's a claim that keeps getting repeated that really bugs me: that Solomonoff induction "formalizes" or even "solves" Occam's razor, because it gives simplicity an actual rigorous meaning (shortest program that outputs the data) instead of it just being a vibe. But then I hit the invariance theorem, which says any two universal Turing machines only agree on complexity "up to an additive constant" -- and from what I can tell that constant is basically just the size of a compiler/interpreter translating between the two machines, which can be enormous, way bigger than any string you'd actually care about in practice. People wave this away by saying it "washes out asymptotically," but that feels like a total non-answer to me since real inductive reasoning (science, ML, whatever) always happens on finite data, not in some infinite limit.

I tried explaining this to a coworker who's way more into theory then I am, and he said I was conflating a practical/engineering limitation with a philosophical one -- that Solomonoff induction was never meant to be a practical algorithm anyway, just a proof that ideal induction is well-defined at all. That half-satisfied me but I still feel like somethings fishy: if the "objectivity" of the simplicity ranking depends entirely on which arbitrary constant got baked into your choice of machine, in what sense has Occam's razor actually been formalized here, rather than just relabeled?

So here's what I want: can you make the strongest possible case FOR my suspicion -- i.e. argue that the machine-dependence exposed by the invariance theorem means Solomonoff induction/Kolmogorov complexity do NOT give us a real vindication of Occam's razor, or at least not one that should comfort anyone who actually has to reason with finite data (which is everyone, always). I don't want a wishy washy "well, there are two sides to this" answer -- actually go to bat for that conclusion. But please engage seriously with the best counterarguments too (the asymptotic/idealization defense my coworker gave, and whatever else you think is the strongest pushback), rather than strawmanning them and declaring victory.
