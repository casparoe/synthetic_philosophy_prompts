So I had this whole argument with my friend Marco last night that started from something small - whether two people can ever rationally disagree about whether a stock is a buy or a sell - and it turned into a huge back-and-forth about the no-trade theorem and Aumann's agreement theorem. Marco's position (he does quant trading, I just took one game theory course years ago and apparantly that was enough to get me roped into this) is basically: if two traders are both genuinely rational and it's common knowledge between them that they're both rational, then one wanting to buy at exactly the price the other wants to sell is close to impossible unless there's a private info edge or some liquidity/hedging reason totally separate from beliefs about value - so most of the "healthy disagreement" you see between traders is either not really about beliefs at all, or somebody's making a mistake somewhere. I pushed back that this is too strong, because rational people can just start from different priors, and there's nothing irrational about that on its own, so genuine disagreement (no trickery, no hidden info) should be possible even between two perfectly rational people. We argued about this for like an hour and never actually got to what I think is the real crux: is "we just have different priors" a legit way out, or is it cheating, given that Aumann's theorem (or at least the intuition behind it) seems to put pressure on rational people's priors to converge too, not just their conclusions, once they fully know how the other person reasons?

Could you write a dialogue between two characters holding our two positions that gets further than we did? One character should defend the "common knowledge of rationality basically rules out honest disagreement" line, leaning on the no-trade theorem and Aumann's agreement result as the backbone, and should actually have to deal with the obvious counterexamples (liquidity trades, differing risk aversion, asymmetric info) instead of just waving them away as "not real disagreement" without argument. The other should push the heterogeneous-priors defense as far as it can reasonably go, but also has to grapple with whether demanding that priors converge too, once reasoning is shared, is a fair thing to ask of rational agents or whether that's smuggling in something question-begging. I want both sides to be genuinely sharp, not an obvious strawman on either end, and I want them to actually respond to each other's specific points as the conversation goes rather than just restating their opening position with different words each time.
