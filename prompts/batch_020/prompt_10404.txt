I run a small, self-produced podcast about ideas -- no university affiliation, just me, a decent microphone, and whatever question happens to be occupying me that month. About a year ago, while I was weighing whether to have a second child, a friend sent me an essay that illustrated the decision with a strange example: imagine you are offered the chance to become a vampire, painlessly and permanently, and every vampire you ask says the change was wonderful -- except that you have no way of knowing what it is actually like until you have already been changed by it, and the people vouching for it were themselves changed by the very thing you are trying to evaluate. I have been turning that puzzle over ever since, entirely on my own and with no formal training in philosophy, and next week I am interviewing a philosopher who has built her career defending the framework that grew out of that example: the idea that some choices are "epistemically transformative" (you cannot know their real value without living through them) and others are "personally transformative" (they change the very values you would use to judge them), and that together these put real pressure on the assumption that our biggest life decisions can be handled by ordinary expected-utility reasoning.

I do not want the episode to consist of her simply restating the vampire case one more time, so I would like your help drafting nine or ten interview questions that push on the actual joints of the position rather than its surface. Please include at least one question that presses on why testimony from people who have already undergone the change should carry any evidential weight at all, given that the change itself may be exactly what produced their glowing reports -- essentially an adaptive-preferences worry dressed in her own vocabulary; one question on whether her appeal to "revelation" -- valuing the bare fact that an experience will teach you something, even without knowing what -- genuinely rescues rational choice or merely retires it under a friendlier name; one question that sets her account against Agnes Callard's picture of aspiration as a gradual, effortful process rather than a single leap, asking what is left of "deciding rationally" once the moment of choice is stretched out that way; and one question that leaves the vampires behind and asks what concrete guidance, if any, her framework actually gives someone facing a decision like becoming a parent or undergoing a major medical or psychological transition. For each question, please also add a short follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to reach for, so the conversation does not stall the moment she gives the expected answer.
