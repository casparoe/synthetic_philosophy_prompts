Over dinner last month, a friend who teaches probability theory insisted that there is a perfectly well-specified coin-flipping game whose expected value is not merely infinite, in the manner of the old St. Petersburg game, but strictly undefined — dependent on an arbitrary choice among equally legitimate ways of summing the very same numbers, with no principled way to settle which sum is correct. I haven't been able to let the idea go, partly because it feels like exactly the engine I need for a subplot in the novel I am currently drafting, whose narrator, a former quantitative analyst, has grown quietly obsessed with a notebook of what she calls "impossible wagers."

The example my friend described is the one Alan Hájek and Harris Nover introduced as the "Pasadena game": a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads; if that happens on toss n, the player receives (−1)^(n+1) × 2^n / n dollars. Weighting each payoff by its probability of 1/2^n gives the series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + …, which sums to ln 2 when added in its natural order. But that series is only conditionally, not absolutely, convergent, so by the Riemann rearrangement theorem the very same countable partition of outcomes can be reordered to sum to any real number whatever, or made to diverge to positive infinity, negative infinity, or no limit at all.

Before I put an opinion in my character's mouth, I want to work out what I actually think is defensible here, rather than gesturing at "paradox" and moving on. So: write an essay that lays out and weighs the main positions on what this case shows about rational choice under risk. Consider at least: the view that the game simply falls outside the domain of expected-utility reasoning, so that no attitude toward it — indifference, refusal to play at any price, willingness to pay any finite sum to do so — is any more rational than another; the view, associated with Mark Colyvan's reply to Hájek and Nover, that a normatively adequate decision theory can tolerate gambles that lack an expectation without this counting as a defect in the theory; and attempts to rescue a single determinate value by replacing the naive infinite sum with some more disciplined summation procedure, such as Cesàro-style summation or a "weak" expectation defined through long-run averages rather than a one-shot sum. Don't simply set these positions side by side — tell me which one you think is actually correct, and defend it against the strongest form of the alternatives. A qualified verdict is acceptable (something like "the paradox is genuine only under assumption X, and dissolves otherwise"), provided you state precisely what the qualification is and why it matters, rather than retreating into even-handedness for its own sake.
