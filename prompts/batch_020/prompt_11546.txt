I spent my career as a condensed-matter physicist, where one grows used to the idea that many of the entities we reason with most fluently — phonons, quasiparticles, order parameters — are coarse-grained bookkeeping devices whose reality is at best a pragmatic matter: real enough to explain and predict, but not obviously carved at a joint that would survive a change of basis or a renormalization-group flow. Since retiring I have been filling in the gaps in my philosophical education, and two threads of reading recently collided for me in a way I have not quite been able to shake. The first thread is Quine's "Word and Object" and its argument for the indeterminacy of translation, which I had previously known only at second hand.

The second thread, which I owe to a former postdoc of mine now working in AI safety, is the interpretability literature coming out of places like Anthropic on "superposition" and "dictionary learning" in neural networks: the finding that a trained network can represent more concepts than it has neurons by storing them as overlapping, non-orthogonal directions in its activation space, so that the seemingly natural basis of individual neurons is not obviously privileged over countless other bases one might rotate into, and different runs of a sparse autoencoder can turn up different, equally defensible "dictionaries" of features (the "Golden Gate Bridge" feature that Anthropic once isolated and artificially amplified in one of their models is the most vivid example I have come across). The parallel to Quine's gavagai case struck me forcefully: just as rival translation manuals can each fit every disposition of a speaker to assent or dissent, with nothing left over to decide between them, rival feature-bases can each fit every input-output disposition of a network, with nothing beyond convenience seeming to decide which basis "really" contains the concepts.

What I would like your help with is reconstructing, as carefully as you can from Quine's actual commitments rather than from a slogan-level gloss on indeterminacy, what he himself would probably have made of this. Would he have welcomed the analogy as a natural extension of radical translation and ontological relativity to a new subject matter, or would his naturalism and physicalism have pushed him toward a disanalogy, on the grounds that his indeterminacy arguments were always about the meaning and reference to be assigned to a speaker's terms, not about the individuation of the underlying causal-computational states themselves, which a thoroughgoing physicalist might insist are perfectly determinate even if redescribable in many ways? And on the question dividing interpretability researchers today — whether it is a mistake, or merely a convenient manner of speaking, to say that a network "really contains" some specific feature — do you think the tie-breakers Quine reached for elsewhere when adjudicating among empirically equivalent systems, such as simplicity and fit with the rest of natural science, would have let him privilege one basis after all, or would he have been content to call the question badly posed? I am less interested in a general summary of either Quine or the interpretability debate, both of which I already have some independent handle on, than in a close, textually grounded argument for exactly where you think the analogy holds and where it breaks down.
