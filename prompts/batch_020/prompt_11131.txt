I'm preparing a seminar on Pascal's political thought and want to check my own reading of the pivotal fragment before I teach it. Here is the passage (Pensées, fragment no. 298 in the Brunschvicg ordering, from the section "Justice and the Reason of Effects"):

"Justice, force.—It is right that what is just should be obeyed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be obeyed. Justice without force is powerless; force without justice is tyrannical. [...] We must then combine justice and force, and for this end make what is just strong, or what is strong just.

Justice is subject to dispute; force is easily recognised and is not disputed. So we cannot give force to justice, because force has contradicted justice, and has declared that it is she herself who is just. And thus, being unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just."

What I can't settle is the status of the closing move. On one reading, Pascal is drawing a straightforwardly Hobbesian or proto-positivist conclusion: since natural justice cannot be secured, whatever force settles on simply counts as just. On a rival reading, this is a particular application of his doctrine of the "reason of effects," on which the wise recognize that deference to instituted force is a necessary surrogate for a justice that fallen reason cannot reach, without force thereby becoming just in anything more than a nominal sense. Could you help me work out which reading the passage's own argument actually bears out, paying close attention to the shift from the impersonal "it is necessary" of the opening to the agentive "we have made" of the closing line, and to how each reading sits with Pascal's later distinction between the order of the body, to which political force belongs, and the order of charity?
