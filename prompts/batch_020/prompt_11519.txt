I'm reporting a feature on the philosophical afterlife of quantum indistinguishability — the fact that, on the standard formalism, two electrons in the same state are not merely practically but in principle interchangeable — and several of the philosophers of physics I've interviewed for it kept steering me toward a much older, purely metaphysical thought experiment that supposedly anticipates the puzzle: Max Black's 1952 case of two spheres, alone in an otherwise empty universe, exactly alike in every respect (size, composition, temperature, and so on) and separated by some fixed distance, with nothing to individuate them beyond the bare fact that there are two rather than one. Black used the case to argue against Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles — roughly, that no two distinct things can share absolutely every property — and it has since spawned a small literature of replies, each trying either to rescue the principle or to explain why losing it isn't the disaster it looks like. I've now read my way through most of that literature, but I don't trust my own judgment about which reply actually earns its keep, and my editor wants the piece — aimed at a general-interest science audience — to argue for a view rather than just cataloguing the options neutrally.

So I'd like your help ranking six of the standard replies to Black, judged purely on how successfully each one resolves the tension his case creates, not on how famous or historically central it has been:

1. Hacking's redescription: recast the whole scenario, via a non-standard spatial topology, as really being about a single sphere reached by two different routes, so that there were never two indiscernible objects to begin with.
2. Primitive thisness (Adams): concede that the spheres share every qualitative and relational property, and conclude that individuals possess a further, purely numerical "thisness" that outruns any such profile.
3. Weak discernibility (Saunders and others): point out that each sphere bears an irreflexive relation to the other — being some fixed distance away — that it does not bear to itself, so a suitably relaxed version of the principle survives.
4. Twenty spheres (Della Rocca): generalize the case to many indiscernible spheres to argue that our willingness to tolerate brute, unexplained numerical difference in the two-sphere version is an artifact of its small scale, not a genuine insight.
5. Biting the bullet on "two": the orthodox Leibnizian move of denying that the scenario, correctly described, contains two objects at all — if truly nothing discerns them, "there are two spheres" was the wrong description from the start.
6. Quantum vindication (French and others): treat Black's fable as anticipating a real physical phenomenon, arguing that actual indistinguishable particles show that individuality is not a basic feature of nature after all.
