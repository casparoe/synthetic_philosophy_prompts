So I'm working on a longer piece about AI alignment and I keep running into the same disagreement in my interviews. A bunch of researchers describe inverse reinforcement learning as basically the fix for the "how does a machine know what humans actually want" problem: instead of hand-coding a reward function (which everyone agrees goes badly), you just have the system watch what people do and back out whatever reward function best explains the behavior, since the behavior is presumably optimal (or close to it) relative to what the person actually wants. That's more or less how I understood the pitch in Stuart Russell's book too, where the AI's uncertainty about the "true" objective is supposed to get resolved through watching us over time. One of the more optimistic people I talked to basically waved away the hard version of the alignment problem and said its really just a data-and-modeling issue at this point — better demonstrations, better architectures, and the inferred reward converges on someone's actual preferences.

Then I mentioned that framing to a friend who does ML research for a living and she got kind of annoyed and said that's "not even a little bit true," but had to run before she could explain why. So now Im stuck on it — if IRL already gives you a mathematically principled way to read someone's real preferences off their actions, why do the more pessimistic alignment people keep treating "value learning" like some deep unsolved problem instead of an engineering one? Is my friend just being reflexively doomer about this, or is there something about how IRL actually works that means no amount of additional data closes that gap?
