Me and my friend Jamie got into a whole thing last night after dinner about Galileo, of all things. She thinks his actual position was basically "the Bible is just wrong about nature and we should stop pretending otherwise once science says different," while I was arguing that his real view, at least in the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, is way more careful than that — that scripture and nature both come from God so they cant really contradict each other once each is understood properly, and that a lot of the Bible is written in accommodated language for ordinary people (God having hands, God getting angry, etc) rather than literal fact. We pulled up the actual letter to settle it, and the early parts back me up pretty well — hes very clear that in physical questions you should start from demonstration and plain sense experience instead of twisting a verse to fit.

Then near the very end he does something that reignited the whole fight. He spends several paragraphs on the Joshua story, where Joshua tells the sun to stand still so the Israelites get extra daylight to finish off a battle, and instead of treating it as obviously non-literal like everything else he's discussed, he tries to show that the Copernican system actually gives a MORE literal reading of it than the geocentric one does. The prose itself is slow going so I'm mostly paraphrasing, but the gist is: under the old Ptolemaic setup the sun's own motion runs opposite to the direction the outer "primum mobile" sphere drags everything each day, so if the sun literally just stopped, the day would get shorter, not longer — the words don't even work literally under geocentrism. Whereas if the sun sits at the center and, per Galileo's own sunspot observations, its rotation is basically what drives every planet's motion, the way a heart's beating keeps the rest of the body moving, then stopping the sun's spin halts the whole system at once and the day gets miraculously long exactly as advertised. He even uses this to solve a side-puzzle about the verse having the sun stand still "in the midst of the heavens," which bugged commentators because the miracle seems to happen near evening rather than noon — Galileo just says the sun is always literally in the "midst," i.e. the center, of a Copernican universe, no matter the hour. He wraps up by basically taking a victory lap, saying the day gets lengthened in a way "which agrees exquisitely with the literal sense of the sacred text."

So now Jamie's saying, "see, this proves your guy didn't actually care about the accommodation stuff and was totally happy to use science to defend a literal reading whenever it suited his case," and I said that's too quick but couldn't really explain why. Is there an actual coherent way to fit this Joshua section together with the accommodation argument from earlier in the letter, or did Galileo basically talk himself into a contradiction here? I'd love a real close reading of what's going on rather than the standard "Galileo kept science and religion in separate boxes" one-liner.
