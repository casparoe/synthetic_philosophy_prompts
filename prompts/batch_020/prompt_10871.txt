I recently agreed to chair the bylaws subcommittee of a small scholarly association I belong to (we have roughly two hundred dues-paying members and elect a new president every two years), and a controversy from this year's election has left me wanting to understand something more fundamental than I expected when I took on the role. For years the presidency was decided by simple plurality among three or four candidates, but two elections ago a widely liked moderate lost to a more polarizing candidate after two similar moderates split the vote between them. That prompted a change to a ranked-choice ballot, on which members list every candidate in order of preference. At our first meeting after this year's election, though, one of our more candid members admitted, with a certain pride, that she and several friends had deliberately ranked their sincere second choice dead last, beneath a candidate nobody took seriously, purely to help their actual favorite win. It worked, and it plainly wasn't in the spirit of what the new ballot was supposed to achieve.

This has set off a debate on the subcommittee about what to do next. One faction wants to move to a point-scoring system, in which each rank on a ballot earns a candidate a certain number of points, confident that this will finally remove any reason for members to misstate their true preferences. Another member, only half joking, says that whatever system we adopt, someone will eventually find an angle to exploit, and that we would be better off spending our energy on something else entirely. I don't have a technical background in this area, but before I recommend anything to my colleagues I would like to genuinely understand the issue: with three or more candidates, can a voting rule ever be designed so that every member's best strategy is always to rank the candidates honestly, no matter how everyone else votes? And if not, is there some principled reason this keeps happening to us, rather than just a string of unlucky rule choices on our part?
