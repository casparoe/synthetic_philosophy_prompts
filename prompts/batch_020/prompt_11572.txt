I spent thirty-five years as a research chemist before retiring, and for the last couple of years I've been attending a small philosophy discussion group that recently worked through several papers on the cognitive penetration of perception. At our last meeting, two members, Elena and Dmitri, fell into a disagreement the group never quite settled, and I would like a more careful adjudication than we managed on the night.

(1) Elena opened by citing two findings: Hansen et al.'s (2006) result that a banana image rendered a physically neutral gray still needs a touch of blue added before observers will call it truly gray, and Witt and Proffitt's (2005) finding that softball players who are hitting well over the course of a game report the ball as looking bigger. She took both as evidence that what we know, or how we're performing, can change the content of visual experience itself, contrary to the Fodor/Pylyshyn picture of perception as informationally encapsulated from belief.

(2) Dmitri answered that Firestone and Scholl's (2016) review of the "top-down effects" literature makes a decisive point against cases exactly like these: the effect only ever shows up in a comparison, adjustment, or self-report task, never in a measure that isolates the percept from the judgment about it. He added that the ball-size result is a mere correlation between reported size and that day's batting performance, so it cannot even fix the direction of causation, let alone show that perception itself changed.

(3) Elena granted the point about the ball-size study specifically, but said Dmitri's general move proves too much: since every way of finding out what an experience is like routes through some comparison, report, or other response, his standard would make the encapsulation thesis true by stipulation, because nothing could ever count as evidence against it.

(4) Dmitri replied that asking for convergence across independent, dissociable measures isn't asking for the impossible, since genuinely low-level effects, such as brightness-contrast illusions, do show up in forced-choice or adaptation paradigms and not merely in unspeeded ratings; the burden, he said, is on Elena's examples to clear that bar, and so far they haven't. He also pressed a further point against the banana case specifically: consciously knowing that the gray banana has been artificially desaturated doesn't make it look gray to you, and he suggested that this persistence in the face of contrary belief, the same signature found in the Müller-Lyer illusion, looks more like an encapsulated, hard-wired expectation than like the belief-driven penetration the debate is supposed to be about.
