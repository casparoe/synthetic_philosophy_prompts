My father was recently approved for a closed-loop deep brain stimulation implant to treat obsessive-compulsive disorder that hasn't responded to years of medication and exposure therapy. As his neurologist explained it to us, the device continuously monitors for an electrical signature in his brain associated with an oncoming compulsion and delivers a brief pulse of stimulation to head it off just before he would act on the urge; on the many days when he manages to resist a compulsion on his own, the device detects nothing unusual and simply does nothing. Reading through the consent materials, I couldn't shake the feeling that I had met this setup before, and it eventually clicked: it is structurally very close to something I was taught, and never really understood, in an undergraduate philosophy class years ago -- Harry Frankfurt's counterexample to the "principle of alternative possibilities" (PAP), in which a counterfactual intervener ("Black") stands ready to force a given outcome but only ever intervenes if the agent ("Jones") shows some sign of choosing otherwise; Jones is thereby supposedly shown to be responsible even though he could never actually have done otherwise.

Curious, I went down a bit of a rabbit hole and found some of the literature responding to Frankfurt, though I can't say I really followed all of it. I gather there is a "flicker of freedom" reply that is generally considered a dead end, and then a more serious objection -- often called the "Dilemma Defense," associated mainly with Robert Kane and David Widerker -- which argues that if the "prior sign" Black relies on is itself causally sufficient for Jones's later choice, the example has quietly smuggled in causal determinism and so begs the question against libertarians about responsibility, whereas if the sign is not sufficient, Jones could in principle display the sign and still choose otherwise, so some alternative possibility sneaks back in either way. Alfred Mele and David Robb apparently designed a "buffer" case meant to be immune to this dilemma by dispensing with the prior sign altogether.

I would like to actually understand this exchange rather than just be able to name-drop it. Could you explain why the Dilemma Defense is supposed to be a genuine problem for ordinary prior-sign Frankfurt cases, how buffer cases like Mele and Robb's are meant to escape it, and whether you think that maneuver actually succeeds? And, for what it is worth, I would be curious whether my father's implant strikes you as closer to an old-fashioned prior-sign case or a genuine buffer case, and whether that distinction should matter at all to how much credit he deserves on the days the stimulator never has to switch on.
