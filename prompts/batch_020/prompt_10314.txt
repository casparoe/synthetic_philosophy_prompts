I'm taking a philosophy of mind seminar this term that's built substantially around Dennett's work on intentional systems, and a discussion in our last session has left me genuinely unsure what to think. We spent the first few weeks on essays like "True Believers," where Dennett argues that even a thermostat can usefully be described as "believing" the room is too cold, and insists that there is no principled, non-arbitrary line -- no "magic moment" -- separating such minimal cases from full-blown believers like us. On his view, whether an attribution of belief is warranted is just a question of how much predictive and explanatory work the intentional stance does compared to the alternatives, not a question of what's "really" happening inside.

A classmate took this and ran with it during discussion: she pointed out that for large language models, the gap between predicting their outputs mechanistically (tracing activity through billions of parameters) and predicting them intentionally (attributing beliefs, goals, and something like reasoning) is enormous -- far larger than the corresponding gap for a thermostat, whose "belief" bottoms out in nothing more than a bimetallic strip. Since it's exactly this kind of gap that makes an intentional attribution non-arbitrary on Dennett's view, she concluded that the intentional stance isn't just a convenient way of talking about these models; it's telling us that there is something it is like to be one, in Nagel's sense.

I couldn't immediately see the flaw, and it's stuck with me since. If Dennett's own framework already gives us grounds to say that LLMs have genuine subjective experience, why do so many philosophers of mind still write as though machine consciousness were a wide-open question, rather than something the intentional stance has, in effect, already settled?
