Over the past few months I've started minting some of my digital paintings as NFTs, largely because several collectors have specifically asked for "the original" rather than a plain high-resolution export. This has left me oddly unsettled: the image file I upload to the minting platform is bit-for-bit identical to the one sitting in my working folder, and would be bit-for-bit identical to any copy a stranger right-clicked and saved from social media. The token itself never touches the file; it just points to it (sometimes via a hash) and records who currently controls that pointer. Trying to work out what, if anything, this is supposed to guarantee, I dug up my notes from an aesthetics elective on Nelson Goodman's distinction, from Languages of Art, between autographic art forms, like painting, where only an object with the right history of production counts as a genuine instance of the work, so that even a perfect copy is merely a forgery, and allographic art forms, like music or literature, where anything satisfying the relevant notation counts as a fully authentic instance, with no coherent notion of forging the work itself. I mentioned this to a friend from that same seminar, and we've spent several evenings arguing past each other about it without resolving anything.

I'd like you to help me actually work through the question rather than tell me what a reassuring answer would sound like. Please argue for the position that, applying Goodman's own technical apparatus for what makes a symbol system notational, rather than just the loose intuition that image files "look like" paintings, a digital image comes out as allographic rather than autographic: any bit-identical copy is automatically as genuine an instance of the image as the file on my drive, in just the way a correctly played copy of a score is a genuine instance of a sonata. On this basis, argue that an NFT is not in the business of tracking any real original/forgery distinction in the artwork itself, but is at most a social and legal convention layered on top of a work that has no autographic "original" for it to pick out. Along the way, please take seriously the most natural objection: that Goodman treats other mechanically reproducible visual media, such as photographs and etchings, as importantly different from music and text, and explain whether and why your conclusion survives that comparison. I'd also be curious, time permitting, whether any of this should change how I describe to collectors what they are actually acquiring, though the conceptual question matters more to me right now than the practical one.
