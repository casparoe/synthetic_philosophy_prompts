I'm taking a grad seminar on philosophy of AI this term, and this week's reading was Turing's original 1950 paper, 'Computing Machinery and Intelligence' — the one everyone cites for the imitation game but apparently almost nobody actually reads past the first few pages. I got down to section 6, where he runs through a list of objections to the whole project, and near the end there's one called 'The Argument from Extrasensory Perception' that I had to read twice because I assumed it was a joke. It's not: Turing says the statistical evidence for telepathy is basically overwhelming, then works out an actual scenario where the interrogator asks the human witness to guess the suit of a hidden card, the human scores way above chance because they're telepathic, the machine can only guess randomly, and so the game gets given away — and his fix for this is to put everyone in what he calls a 'telepathy-proof room.'

My professor spent about ninety seconds on this before moving on to Lady Lovelace's objection, but its been bugging me way more than that one has. I mentioned it to my flatmate, who's really into parapsychology stuff, and she went "see, even Turing believed it," which is not remotely the point I want to make, but it made me realize I don't actually understand the philosophical mechanics well enough to say what the point is. Turing is basically the guy who tells us to stop asking deep questions about what's "really" happening inside a mind and just look at behavior instead — so why does he treat a phenomenon like ESP, which is supposedly information moving between minds through no physical or behavioral channel whatsoever, as a serious threat to the test rather than a total non-issue? Does the telepathy-proof room actually rescue the imitation game, or does taking ESP seriously at all already concede something that breaks the operationalist logic the whole test depends on? I'd like to walk into seminar on Thursday with an actual argument here, not just "lol Turing believed in psychic powers."
