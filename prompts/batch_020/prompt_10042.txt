My grandpa had a stroke back in March and it's left his speech kind of scrambled - not randomly scrambled though, theres clearly SOME logic in there, we just can't always tell what it is. He'll ask my mom to "turn up the radio" while pointing at the thermostat, or the other night, right after we'd cranked the heat up for him, he said "somebody get me the cold blanket," and none of us could tell if "cold" was just the wrong word for "warm," or if he's actually picked up some genuine belief that that specific blanket cools you down, or if he even meant that blanket at all. His speech therapist's whole approach is to assume he's still in there thinking mostly sensible, mostly accurate thoughts, and to use that assumption to work backward to what he actually means and wants, rather than treating his sentences as noise we just have to live with. He was a mechanical engineer for 40 years and took real pride in precision, so its extra strange to now be constantly finishing his sentences for him.

My uncle thinks this generous approach is basically a trick we're playing on ourselves - once you've decided in advance that he means something sensible, you can always cook up SOME interpretation that fits, whether or not that's what's really happening in his head, and we have no independent way to check any of it since his garbled speech is basically our only window into his beliefs at this point. It's led to actual arguments at family dinners over whether to "translate" for him, gently push him to try again, or just let the confusing stuff go unresolved. I get both sides - it's not like he's a stranger, the guy has 84 years of pretty well-documented, consistent ways of thinking behind him, which feels like it should count for something - but I can't tell if that actually settles anything. Is there a real principled way to know when this kind of charitable reading is justified versus us just projecting sense onto noise, or is my uncle right that we're stuck either way? Mostly just want to think about this clearly instead of getting stuck in the middle every Sunday dinner.
