I began serving as a teaching assistant for our department's graduate seminar, "Statistical Inference and the Crisis of Replication," this term under slightly strange circumstances: over the summer, a social-priming effect that formed the empirical backbone of one chapter of my own dissertation failed to replicate in a large, preregistered, multi-lab project, and I spent what was supposed to be a summer break rewriting that chapter instead. So now, working through a stack of take-home finals for the professor, I find that when I reach the question on frequentist versus Bayesian inference and the replication crisis, I care a great deal more than I otherwise would about getting the grading itself right, rather than just checking that the expected phrases show up.

The question, worth 20 of the exam's 100 points, came with no detailed answer key beyond the professor's note that partial credit should track genuine understanding rather than keyword-matching. It read as follows:

"One diagnosis of the replication crisis holds that the crisis is, at bottom, a problem of frequentist statistical practice, and that adopting Bayesian methods -- reporting posterior probabilities or Bayes factors instead of p-values, credible intervals instead of confidence intervals -- would substantially resolve it. Explain the core technical disagreement between frequentist and Bayesian inference that this diagnosis trades on, and critically evaluate whether the proposed Bayesian fix actually addresses the sources of the crisis."

Here is one student's answer, reproduced in full:

"The frequentist and Bayesian approaches disagree about what 'probability' refers to, and this drives a disagreement about what a statistical test can license us to believe. On the frequentist view, probability is a long-run frequency, and a p-value is P(data this extreme or more | H0) -- it says nothing directly about P(H0 | data), which is what researchers actually want to know. Because the field treats p < .05 as a bright line, and because the true prior probability that any given tested hypothesis is correct is often low, a large share of 'significant' results will be false positives even if every individual researcher behaves honestly. This is essentially Ioannidis's argument that most published research findings are false, and it points to a structural driver of the replication crisis that need not involve any individual misconduct.

Bayesians instead treat probability as a degree of belief, updating a prior over hypotheses by the likelihood of the data to get a posterior. This reframes the question in the terms researchers actually care about. A 95% Bayesian credible interval and a 95% frequentist confidence interval both amount to the claim that the true parameter lies in the interval with 95% probability; they are two different routes to essentially the same piece of information, so it isn't surprising that they tend to line up numerically in ordinary cases.

The diagnosis in the question is right that p-values are constantly misread as if they were posterior probabilities, and that this feeds the crisis. But the proposed fix overreaches. Bayesian updating obeys the likelihood principle, so a Bayesian analysis is not distorted by why or when the experimenter decided to stop collecting data, whereas a p-value is invalidated by optional stopping. Since undisclosed researcher degrees of freedom like optional stopping are exactly what generate p-hacked results, and Bayesian inference is simply not vulnerable to that kind of manipulation, switching the field's default reporting from p-values to Bayes factors would remove the main engine driving the crisis. Publication bias toward positive findings would still need to be handled separately, through preregistration, but the statistical mechanism that produces spurious results within any given study would essentially be defused."
