So in my world religions elective we spent a couple days on the Fatima apparitions in Portugal, and our teacher brought up the "Miracle of the Sun" thing that supposedly happened on October 13 1917 — like 30,000 to 70,000 people showed up in this field because the kids who'd been seeing the Virgin Mary said a miracle would happen that day to prove it, and a bunch of witnesses (including reporters from a big secular newspaper that was actually anti-Church politically, not just random peasants) all said the sun spun around, changed colors, and seemed to zig-zag toward the ground before it stopped. Totally seperate from that, in my actual philosophy class we just finished reading Hume's essay "Of Miracles," and now I can't stop thinking about how he would've picked this one apart.

Here's what's bugging me about it: Hume's argument leans a lot on stuff like the number and character of the witnesses, whether they had a motive to lie, and whether the story could've spread from ignorant sources — and Fatima kind of complicates all of that since it wasn't just two or three superstitious villagers, it was a massive crowd that supposedly included skeptical journalists with no reason to help the Church's case. So does that actually make it a harder case for Hume to wave away, or does his argument have some other move (like the bit about miracles from different religions cancelling each other out, or his rule about weighing "proofs" against each other) that lets him dismiss Fatima just as easily as any village legend? Can you actually walk through his reasoning step by step on this one instead of just saying "well Hume didn't believe in miracles so he'd say no"? That's the answer I keep getting from people and it feels like it's missing the point of what actually makes his argument interesting.
