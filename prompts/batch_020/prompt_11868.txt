I have no academic background in philosophy, but over the past year I have read everything on supertasks I could find on my own, and Thomson's lamp is the one puzzle in that literature I still haven't managed to settle to my own satisfaction. Thomson's argument is that a lamp switched infinitely often within a finite interval could end up neither determinately on nor determinately off, and he takes this as grounds for concluding that the task itself is incoherent. The standard reply, as I understand it, is that this moves too fast: the sequence of switching times never actually includes the limiting instant itself, so the description simply leaves that instant's state unspecified, and an unspecified state is a gap to be stipulated, not a contradiction to be feared. What I can't decide is whether this reply really vindicates supertasks in general or only rescues this one case -- a doubt sharpened for me by the Grim Reaper paradox, where nothing is left unspecified (the victim is straightforwardly dead) and yet no particular reaper in the infinite sequence can be identified as the one who caused it, since each reaper's strike would already have been preempted by an earlier one. Could you write me an essay that sets out Thomson's argument and the "gap, not contradiction" reply to it, works through what the Grim Reaper case does to that reply's chances of generalizing, and then actually commits to a verdict on whether supertasks of this kind are coherent, rather than leaving the question open?
