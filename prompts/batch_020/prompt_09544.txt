I've been elbow-deep in revisions on my third novel for the better part of a year now -- it's got four unreliable narrators and a timeline that folds back on itself twice -- and my editor keeps telling me it's "impressive" but that she doesn't feel anything when she reads it. That comment sent me back to Tolstoy's What Is Art? for the first time since undergrad, and I'd forgotten how genuinely brutal he is in it. He's not just saying some art is better than other art -- his whole "infection" theory says a work only counts as good art if it transmits a real, sincerely felt emotion clearly enough to "infect" a wide audience and unite them in something like universal brotherly feeling. By that standard he trashes Shakespeare (a critique he later sharpened into a whole separate essay tearing into King Lear specifically), mocks a Wagner opera he sat through as basically theatrical fraud, and dismisses Beethoven's Ninth as noise people only pretend to be moved by -- and, most wild to me, he lumps his own War and Peace and Anna Karenina in with the "bad," counterfeit art made by and for the leisure class, ranking peasant folk tales and Uncle Tom's Cabin above either one as truer art.

Part of me thinks Tolstoy is onto something real about the difference between art that actually moves people and art that just impresses critics or requires the right education to "get." But another part thinks this is just moralism wearing an aesthetic costume, and that alot of what makes difficult or ambiguous art valuable is exactly the stuff his theory throws out. I keep going back and forth and can't settle it for myself, so I want an outside take before I decide whether to take my editor's note seriously.

Could you write me an essay that actually takes a side on this? Lay out the strongest version of Tolstoys case for the infection theory plus the universal-feeling criterion layered on top of it, then the best objections to it (bring in how contemporaries or later critics like Orwell pushed back, if that's relevant, not just abstract counterarguments), and then reach an actual verdict on whether his standard is a defensible way to judge art or a fundamentally wrongheaded one. I'd rather have something with real teeth -- like "his diagnosis of counterfeit art holds up even if his criterion for good art doesn't" -- than a wishy-washy "both sides have merit" ending.
