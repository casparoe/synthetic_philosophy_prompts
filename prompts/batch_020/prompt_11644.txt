I have spent a long time reading rationalist blogs and forums, and one thread that has stuck with me for years is Eliezer Yudkowsky's old "Where Recursive Justification Hits Bottom" post on Less Wrong, which is really just a vivid way into the classical epistemic regress problem: does justification for a belief eventually bottom out somewhere, loop back on itself, or run on forever, and can any of those options avoid being unsatisfying? That interest is part of why I ended up serving as a teaching assistant for an epistemology seminar this term, and this week's take-home question was exactly that trilemma and the three standard replies to it. I am currently marking a stack of these submissions, and before writing my own comments on one particular answer I would like an independent, disinterested pass at it, since I suspect my fondness for the material makes me either too generous toward answers that deploy the right vocabulary or too harsh on ones that skip steps I personally consider important.

The question as set, worth twenty points on the take-home, read: "State Agrippa's trilemma and explain how foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism are each best understood as a response to one horn of it. Then take any one of the three positions, present the strongest standard objection to it, and say whether that position's usual reply to the objection succeeds."

Here is the student's answer in full:

"The regress problem arises because if every justified belief needs some other belief to justify it, justification either goes on forever (infinite regress), loops back on itself (circularity), or stops at some belief that isn't itself justified by anything further (arbitrariness). This trilemma was first laid out by the Pyrrhonist skeptic Agrippa, and is sometimes called the Münchhausen trilemma after Hans Albert's later discussion of it.

Foundationalists take the third horn: they hold that there are 'basic' beliefs that are justified without needing any further beliefs to back them up, because they are self-evident, incorrigible, or directly given in experience. Classical foundationalists such as Descartes required basic beliefs to be infallible; modest foundationalists only require that basic beliefs be non-inferentially justified to some degree, not indubitable.

Coherentists deny that justification has this linear structure at all, so the regress never really gets started: a belief is justified by standing in relations of mutual support and consistency to the rest of one's belief system, not by resting on anything more basic. BonJour is the paradigm case of a coherentist, and he consistently defended the view from The Structure of Empirical Knowledge onward, arguing that coherentism avoids collapsing into an ungrounded, 'floating' system by adding an observation requirement that keeps the system responsive to experience.

Infinitists take the remaining horn and hold that justification is a non-repeating, infinite chain of reasons. Peter Klein's version requires that a believer actually have consciously run through infinitely many reasons for a belief in order to count as justified, which is exactly what makes the position seem psychologically impossible to critics: no finite mind could complete such a chain.

Of the three, I think coherentism faces the strongest objection: the isolation objection, which says a belief system could be perfectly coherent while being cut off from the world entirely, as in an elaborate, self-consistent fantasy, so coherence alone cannot guarantee that justification tracks truth. Coherentists reply, as BonJour does, by requiring the system to include spontaneous, experientially triggered beliefs that keep it answerable to the world. I think this reply succeeds, since once the system must be responsive to experience in this way, the isolated-fantasy scenario the objection relies on is no longer an instance of coherentist justification as the position actually describes it."
