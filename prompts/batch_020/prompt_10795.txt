I write novels, and research for one set partly in a seminary has had me reading Augustine's two treatises on lying. That reading has turned out to be unexpectedly useful: I'm one of five jurors for a small foundation's annual prize for narrative nonfiction, and our shortlisted memoir has run into trouble. In a podcast interview, the author admitted that a recurring minor character is a composite of three real people, and that several long stretches of dialogue were reconstructed from memory rather than notes or recordings -- none of this flagged anywhere in the book itself, which was written and marketed as straightforward autobiography. Our prize rules require entrants to offer "a true account of the author's own experience" and define the phrase no further. The jury is split: some call this ordinary memoir craft, and argue that disqualifying it on these grounds would knock out half the genre's acknowledged classics; others say the undisclosed composite alone settles the matter. Please draft the short memo I'll circulate before Friday's vote. It should recommend keeping the book on the shortlist, dropping it, or keeping it conditional on a disclosure, reasoning from Augustine's actual criteria for what turns a falsehood into a lie -- the requirement that the hearer be meant to take it as true, which is why he refuses to count jests as lies -- and from his separate refusal, against Consentius, to let a good end excuse a lie, rather than resting on a generic appeal to honesty. It should also give the losing side's strongest version of its case.
