As a software engineer, I regularly encounter probability figures whose meaning I've never quite pinned down: a vision model reports "73% confidence" that one specific image contains a cat, and a weather app gives a "30% chance of rain" for one specific day. Both are one-off events — that image either does or doesn't contain a cat, and it either does or doesn't rain — so I got curious what such a claim is even supposed to refer to. Some reading turned up three rival philosophical accounts of probability, and each runs into a specific, well-known problem exactly when applied to cases like these. Frequentism defines probability as a long-run frequency within a reference class, but a single event can be classified into many different reference classes that yield different frequencies, and there's no principled way to pick the right one. Subjectivism (Bayesianism) only requires that credences be internally coherent, typically justified via a Dutch book argument, which seems to leave any consistent number equally "valid" — making probability sound like pure opinion, with no further fact about who is right. And propensity theory, which was devised partly to rescue single-case probabilities, runs into "Humphreys' paradox": conditional probabilities always have a well-defined algebraic inverse via Bayes' theorem, but it's unclear what an "inverse propensity," running backward from effect to cause, could even mean.
