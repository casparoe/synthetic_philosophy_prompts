My grandfather, who spent almost thirty years as a firefighter, died last week, and I've been asked to give his eulogy this Saturday. Everyone in town remembers him as a hero because of one particular fire: his captain had already ordered everyone out of the building and his own air tank was getting low, but he went back in anyway and found two children in a bedroom nobody had reached yet, at a cost to his lungs and knees that he carried for the rest of his life. The department's commendation for it uses almost exactly the standard phrase for this sort of thing, "above and beyond the call of duty," which he kept folded in a drawer rather than framed on the wall, because he hated being called a hero and always answered with some version of "I was only doing my job" or "any one of the guys on that crew would have done the same thing."

As it happens, my high school's ethics elective just spent two weeks on supererogation -- actions that are praiseworthy to perform but not required, such that failing to perform them isn't wrong -- and now that I actually understand the concept, I don't think what he always said about himself was true, and I don't want to stand up and repeat it as if it were. At the same time, I don't want my eulogy to contradict how he saw himself, and several firefighters from his old crew -- the ones who followed the evacuation order that day and did not go back in -- will be sitting in the second row; I will not let anything I say imply, even by accident, that doing what the order asked of them made them less courageous or made them do something wrong. Could you write the eulogy for me? It should run about four or five minutes when read aloud. It has to work equally well for relatives who have never thought about any of this and for former firefighters who will hear "above and beyond" and know precisely what it's technically supposed to mean. And most importantly, it actually has to resolve the tension I've just described, rather than quietly stepping around it or turning into a philosophy lecture in the middle of a funeral.
