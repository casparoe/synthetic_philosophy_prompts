Over dinner last night, a friend and I got into a long argument about infinite ethics that neither of us really won, and I'm still a little bothered by it. The flashpoint was a toy case we improvised about whether total utilitarianism can even assign a determinate value to an infinite population's welfare in the first place. Take a population of countably infinitely many people whose lives alternate forever between wonderful (+1) and terrible (-1). Group the series as (+1 - 1) + (+1 - 1) + ... and the "total welfare" comes out to zero; group it instead as +1 + (-1 + 1) + (-1 + 1) + ... and it comes out to one; average the partial sums instead, Cesàro-style, and you arrive at one-half. My friend took this as proof that total utilitarianism is simply broken for infinite populations and should be scrapped in favor of some overtaking or catching-up criterion that never demands a "total" at all. I argued instead that the order-dependence is closer to the way not every set of natural numbers has a well-defined density: an annoying limitation of infinite aggregation to live with, rather than a refutation of the whole aggregative project. This is, in effect, a toy version of the puzzles that show up throughout the wider infinite-ethics literature (Bostrom's "Infinite Ethics" paper is the usual entry point), and after an hour of back-and-forth, neither of us had budged.

To get some closure, I'd like a genuinely good joke about this specific problem: the fact that the "total value" of an infinite world can hinge entirely on the arbitrary order or grouping used to add up individual welfares, even though no such order or grouping has any real ethical significance. Please don't hand me a generic "infinity is confusing" or "philosophers overthink everything" joke; the punchline should only really land for someone who understands why this order- and grouping-dependence, specifically, is the crux of the problem.
