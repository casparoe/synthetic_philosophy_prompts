I've spent a fair amount of time in the LessWrong/Overcoming Bias archives, and I keep running into the same rhetorical move in threads about the "value of information": someone cites I.J. Good's 1967 result (from "On the Principle of Total Evidence") that a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer facing a fixed decision problem can never be made worse off, in expectation, by costlessly observing a relevant signal before choosing an action — since she remains free to ignore it and act exactly as she would have otherwise — and then treats this as though it settles a much larger question: that a genuinely rational, truth-seeking agent should always want more evidence, should always be willing to hear out one more argument, and that any policy of declining to engage further (Scott Alexander's "epistemic learned helplessness" is usually the example people reach for) is at bottom just a polite name for motivated ignorance. A friend of mine, who thinks about formal epistemology for a living, insists this inference doesn't actually go through once you look closely at what Good's proof assumes, but he hasn't managed to pin down exactly where it fails to my satisfaction.

I'd like you to make that argument in his place. Please argue against the generalization from Good's theorem — a claim about an idealized agent with a fixed utility function, a well-posed decision problem, and a costlessly and non-strategically supplied signal, who is guaranteed to update by Bayesian conditionalization and then act optimally on the result — to the informal norm that a boundedly rational human reasoner, especially one embedded in an adversarial or social argumentative context, should always prefer more evidence or argument-exposure to less. I'd like you to identify explicitly which of the theorem's assumptions (dynamic consistency of the post-signal self is one obvious candidate, but there may be others) a real reasoner is liable to violate, and then work through at least one concrete case — Alexander's essay is a natural starting point, but you're welcome to construct your own or draw on a different one — in which declining to look, read, or listen further leaves an agent genuinely better off by her own lights and in expectation. Please don't rest on the platitude that "real life is messier than the idealized model"; I want the essay to show exactly which premise gives out, and why its failure is enough to overturn the informal conclusion rather than merely complicate it.
