For nearly four decades I worked as a physicist, mostly on problems in statistical mechanics and thermodynamics, and in all that time I never thought twice about invoking the "equal a priori probabilities" postulate when setting up an ensemble: absent further information, every accessible microstate of a given energy is treated as equally likely, and this always struck me as less an assumption than a plain requirement of intellectual honesty. Since retiring I have been reading my way through the philosophy of probability for pleasure, and Bertrand's chord paradox has left that old confidence rather shaken. I understand its bare mechanics well enough: depending on whether one randomizes over the two endpoints of the chord, its perpendicular distance from the center, or the location of its midpoint within the disk, one arrives at three different, individually impeccable answers (1/3, 1/2, and 1/4, respectively) to the probability that a "randomly chosen" chord exceeds the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and nothing in the bare instruction "choose a chord at random" seems to settle which of these one was supposed to compute.

What I have not managed to work through on my own is Jaynes's proposed way out in "The Well-Posed Problem," where he argues that the statement of the problem, properly read, does fix a symmetry the answer must respect: since the circle's size, location, and orientation are never specified, any acceptable solution must be invariant under scaling, translation, and rotation of the figure, and he claims this requirement singles out the distribution that gives 1/2. I would like you to walk me through why this invariance argument is supposed to do the work Jaynes wants of it -- not simply restate the three classical constructions, which I already have well in hand -- and then tell me plainly whether, in your judgment, it succeeds as a genuine vindication of the principle of indifference, or whether it merely pushes the original arbitrariness back a step, into an equally unforced choice of which transformation group the problem is required to respect. And if it is the latter, I would be curious whether that same worry reaches back into the equal-a-priori-probabilities postulate I relied on for so many years in statistical mechanics, which is itself usually justified by an appeal to exactly this sort of symmetry, via Liouville's theorem, under Hamiltonian dynamics.
