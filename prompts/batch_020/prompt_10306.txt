I'm reporting a long piece on the philosophy and neuroscience of free will, and although the article itself mostly concerns Libet-style experiments and how routinely they get overinterpreted in the press, I've spent the past couple of weeks stuck in a much narrower corner of the literature that will probably never survive into the final draft: the argument between defenders of the Principle of Alternative Possibilities and the Frankfurt-style compatibilists who think they've refuted it. Every explainer I can find walks through the original Black-and-Jones case, mentions that PAP's defenders replied with something called a "flicker of freedom," and then jumps straight to the supposedly decisive countermove — Mele and Robb's "buffer" cases, engineered so that there's no prior sign for the intervener to monitor at all — as though that settled things. It clearly hasn't: able philosophers still disagree about whether the buffer really removes every trace of a flicker or just relocates one more cleverly, and none of the summaries actually let me see that disagreement happen — they just report that it exists.

So rather than another summary, I'd like to watch two equally capable people argue it out. Please write a dialogue between two philosophers of comparable skill: one who runs what's sometimes called the "dilemma defense" against Frankfurt-style cases (after David Widerker) — any prior sign the intervener relies on must be either strictly deterministic, in which case the case simply begs the question by assuming that a determined choice can still be free, or merely probabilistic, in which case a robust alternative possibility survives after all — and who insists this dilemma still bites even against a buffer case like theirs; and one who constructs and defends such a buffer case as the move that finally escapes it. Neither should get a strawman's exit: the first philosopher has to say exactly where, in the buffer's causal architecture, a residual sign or flicker might still be hiding, and the second has to answer that specific charge rather than simply reasserting that the buffer works. If it's honest to do so, have them close by locating the actual crux of their disagreement — is it a substantive dispute about the metaphysics of the buffer case, or have they simply run out of new ways to restate the same impasse?
