I do interpretability-adjacent work at a smallish AI safety org (maybe 15 people, we're not exactly a household name) and somehow ended up on the internal committee that has to sign off before anything goes out under the org's name. I've been lurking on LW and the Alignment Forum for like a decade at this point, so I know this exact argument has been had a hundred times in the abstract -- but now I actually have to resolve one real instance of it by Tuesday, and "go read more Chris Olah posts" is not gonna cut it.

Here's the case. One of our teams trained a sparse autoencoder on a model's residual stream, found a direction that fires reliably on stuff like lying-to-the-user, and showed that clamping it down measurably reduces deceptive completions. Genuinely cool result. But the draft write-up is titled "We Found the Model's Deception Feature," and the framing throughout just asserts that the network represents deception, full stop, the way you'd say a thermostat represents temperature. Two of the five of us on the committee think that's a real overclaim -- a direction that correlates with and causally influences a behavior isn't automatically a representation of it in any sense that earns the definite article, and the SAE-feature literature already has a track record of confident feature stories that didn't survive people poking at them harder. The other three think we're being precious about metaphysics, and that some working notion of "the model represents X" is just load-bearing: you can't design the next experiment, or explain the result to a funder, without positing something like that.

I need to bring an actual memo to Tuesday's meeting, not a list of considerations -- something that tells the team concretely whether to publish as-is, reframe the write-up and how, or hold it for revision and for roughly how long, with the reasoning spelled out well enough that whichever side looses the vote can see their strongest point got taken seriously instead of steamrolled. Can you draft that memo for me?
