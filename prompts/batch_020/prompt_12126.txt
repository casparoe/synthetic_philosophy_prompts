Student's answer: Van Inwagen's Special Composition Question (SCQ) asks: for any objects the xs, under what conditions do the xs compose some further object y (that is, are all and only parts of y)? This differs from the General Composition Question, which asks what composition itself is — what it means for the xs to compose y in the first place — rather than when that relation holds. Van Inwagen thinks the SCQ, not the general question, is the one philosophers can make real progress on, and Material Beings is organized around answering it.

There are three broad camps. Universalists (David Lewis, later Ted Sider) hold that composition is completely unrestricted: any objects whatsoever, however scattered or dissimilar, compose some further object, so there is an object composed of my left ear and the Eiffel Tower. Nihilists hold that composition never occurs, so that, strictly, there are only simples and no composite objects at all. Van Inwagen's own answer is a version of nihilism: he holds that the xs compose something only when their activity constitutes a life, so the only composite objects are living organisms, and strictly speaking there are no chairs, statues, or stars — only simples arranged chairwise, statuewise, and so on.

The obvious objection is that this is wildly revisionary: it is simply obvious that chairs and statues exist, so any argument to the contrary, however plausible its individual premises look, is more likely to contain a subtle flaw than to be sound — we should be more confident that there are chairs than we are in any of the premises of van Inwagen's argument. Van Inwagen answers this by paraphrase: he argues that ordinary sentences like "there is a chair in the corner" can be systematically translated into sentences about simples arranged chairwise, and since this paraphrase preserves everything a speaker was actually trying to communicate, no ordinary belief is really being given up. Because communication goes through unaffected, the revisionary sting of the view is removed and the common-sense objection is answered.
