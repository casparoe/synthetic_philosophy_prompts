David Lewis's *Convention* is also where the technical notion of "common knowledge" — now invoked constantly in game theory and epistemic logic — actually comes from. On his account, a regularity counts as a convention only if conformity is explained by each agent's expectation that others will conform, that expectation is sustained by common knowledge, and some genuinely different regularity could have done the coordinating job just as well, which is precisely what makes the solution arbitrary rather than dictated by rationality alone. This is what I can't square with the later evolutionary literature: Brian Skyrms and collaborators showed that simple reinforcement learners playing repeated Lewis signaling games reliably converge on stable, arbitrary signaling systems with no representation of the other player's mental states at all, and the same pattern now turns up at scale in deep multi-agent reinforcement learning, where populations of agents settle on arbitrary communication protocols through nothing but reward-driven learning. The result looks just like one of Lewis's own textbook cases — a population settling on a side of the road to drive on — minus everything he thought was doing the explanatory work.

Write a dialogue between a committed Lewisian and a Skyrmsian evolutionary game theorist arguing about whether these evolved or trained conventions are conventions in Lewis's actual sense, or only a convergent phenomenon that resembles one from the outside. Push them specifically on the arbitrariness condition: can a regularity be arbitrary, in Lewis's sense that some other regularity would have worked just as well, if nothing in the system ever represents that alternative to itself? Neither side should win easily — each should press a genuine weakness in the other's position and receive a substantive reply rather than a concession.
