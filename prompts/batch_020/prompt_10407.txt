I'm a novelist and I've been noodling on a Substack post to go alongside my next book (it's about a Newfoundland outport in the years after the '92 cod moratorium gutted the fishery), and I want a sanity check before I actually post the thing. The rough idea: while researching I got stuck on that old fight in philosophy of history between the "covering-law" people (Hempel, basically, explaining the town's collapse as an instance of general laws about resource depletion and migration under economic pressure) and the narrativist crowd (Dray, Danto, Hayden White) who'd say that kind of explanation misses what actually mattered to the people living it -- a string of specific, non-generalizable decisions nobody could've generalized their way into. The post's actual claim is a bit more self-flagellating though: I want to argue that being a novelist doesn't get me off the hook here just because I write narrative instead of laws. Danto's whole point about "narrative sentences" -- that describing an early event through a later one (like calling 1618 "the start of the Thirty Years War") is only available in retrospect -- basically means any novel with a shape at all is quietly doing the same totalizing move as the covering-law model, just dressed up as plot instead of physics envy. So the "openness" I thought I was preserving by writing scene to scene, decision to decision, was maybe always a bit of an illusion, and I'm not sure that's a comforting thing to put in an author's note.

I only took one half-remembered philosophy of history seminar in college, like fifteen years ago, so I could easily be strawmanning one or both sides, or re-treading a point that's totally standard in that literature and I just don't know it, which would be pretty embarassing to post as if it were original. Could you give me comments, specifically on: (1) whether I'm representing Hempels and Danto's actual positions fairly, (2) whether the central move -- a novelist's retrospective narration has basically the same structure as Danto's narrative-sentence problem -- is genuinely interesting once you spell it out, or kind of trivial, and (3) whether there's an obvious move from the narrativist side, like Dray's stuff on rational explanation, or Mink's idea that narrative just "grasps together" events in a way no covering law could, that would let me keep some version of the "openness" claim instead of just torching it. Not looking for a rewrite, just tell me where the reasoning's thin.
