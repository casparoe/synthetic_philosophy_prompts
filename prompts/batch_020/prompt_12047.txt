I've spent the last few weeks reporting on extreme-weather attribution science -- the field where climatologists run a climate model twice, once with our actual greenhouse gas emissions and once in a hypothetical world without them, then say things like "this heatwave was made three times more likely by climate change." Which, if you squint, is just a fancy statistical way of answering a counterfactual: if we hadn't pumped out all that carbon, would this particular heatwave still have happened? Chasing that thought down a rabbit hole, a philosopher I talked to for background on a totally different story mentioned something that's been rattling around in my head since: Humes basically walked right up to this exact problem 250 years earlier and, depending on who you ask, either solved it or completely missed that there was a problem at all.

In the Enquiry, Hume gives two "definitions" of cause back-to-back like they're interchangeable. The first is a regularity claim: a cause is an object followed by another, where things like the first are always followed by things like the second (whenever A, then B). Then, introduced with a casual "or in other words," comes a second one: if the first object hadn't existed, the second never would have either. But that's not obviously the same claim at all -- one is about a general pattern, the other is a full counterfactual about one specific case, and it turns out that's more or less exactly the seam modern philosophers of language cut along when they distinguish indicative from counterfactual (subjunctive) conditionals. The stock illustration is "if Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did" (fine to believe, since we know Kennedy was killed) versus "if Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would've" (not fine to believe, absent evidence of a backup assassin) -- two sentences that can take opposite truth values despite looking like minor tense variants, which is why there's a whole technical literature (Adams, Stalnaker, Lewis) on why you can't just read one off the other.

So, for a piece I'm drafting, here's the question I cant get a satisfying answer to from my one philosopher source, who's lovely but slow to email back: if you sat Hume down and actually walked him through the modern machinery -- possible-worlds semantics for counterfactuals, Adams' probability-based treatment of indicatives, the results showing the two really do come apart and neither reduces cleanly to the other -- what would he say? Would he insist, given everything he believed about necessity being nothing but a habit of mind produced by observed constant conjunction, that the "real" analysis has to be the regularity one, and that Lewis's talk of similarity between possible worlds is smuggling back in exactly the kind of necessary connection he spent his career trying to debunk? Or would he concede his two definitions were quietly doing different jobs, and if forced to keep only one as fundamental to what causation is, which would actually fit better with the rest of his empiricism? I don't want the "wow, Hume was ahead of his time" version of this one -- I want to know which way the argument actually goes once you push on it.
