I'm due at a conference at the end of the week and would like grades finalized before I leave, but I've learned not to trust my own first impression on the middling scripts: after enough years of teaching the same material, it's easy to read generosity into an answer simply because it echoes one's own lecture phrasing back at oneself. So I'd like an independent, careful assessment of one submission, judged strictly on the philosophical merits rather than on fluency of expression.

The question, exactly as set, read as follows:

"Explain why Lewis held that counterpart theory was preferable to transworld identity as an account of de re modal predication, addressing both (a) the problem of accidental intrinsics, and (b) Kripke's 'Humphrey' objection to counterpart-theoretic semantics. Close with your own assessment of whether Lewis's overall package is, on balance, the more attractive view."

And here, verbatim, is the answer submitted in Script 14:

"Two rival theories try to make sense of de re modal claims such as 'Humphrey could have won the 1968 election.' The transworld identity theorist holds that Humphrey himself — the very same individual — exists in other possible worlds and does there what he failed to do here, namely win. The counterpart theorist, following Lewis, denies that any individual exists in more than one world: each of us is 'worldbound,' and claims about what we could have done are made true by our counterparts, that is, individuals in other worlds who resemble us closely enough in the relevant respects. On this view, 'Humphrey could have won' is true just in case some counterpart of Humphrey wins in some other world; Humphrey himself never leaves the actual world.

Lewis's central argument for preferring counterpart theory is the problem of accidental intrinsics. Consider an intrinsic property like shape. I am actually straight-backed, but I could have been stooped; on the transworld identity theory, the very same individual, me, is straight in this world and stooped in another. But shape is supposed to be had by a thing simpliciter, not relative to a world or anything else, and a single thing cannot simply be both straight and stooped. Since the transworld identity theorist has no way to defuse this contradiction, the view cannot be maintained. Counterpart theory faces no such trouble, since it is never the numerically same individual who is both straight and stooped: I am straight, full stop, and a distinct individual, my counterpart, is stooped, full stop, in another world.

Kripke resisted this move in Naming and Necessity. His worry was that the counterpart relation, being a matter of similarity, is irreducibly indeterminate: whether some other-worldly individual counts as my counterpart depends on which respects of similarity we choose to weight, and different weightings can yield different, equally defensible answers, so that I might turn out to have several counterparts, or none, in a given world. Since ordinary de re claims about Humphrey do not seem to be hostage to that kind of indeterminacy, Kripke took this to show that counterpart theory cannot be the correct semantics for de re modal discourse.

On balance, I find Lewis's package more attractive. The accidental intrinsics argument tells decisively against transworld identity, and while Kripke's indeterminacy worry is a genuine cost, Lewis can reasonably reply that some indeterminacy in cross-world comparison is just what we should expect once we give up the idea that individuals are literally spread across worlds."
