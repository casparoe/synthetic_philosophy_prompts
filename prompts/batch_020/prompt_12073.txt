I don't have any background in philosophy - I'm a nurse, actually - but for the last couple of years I've spent a lot of my spare time reading about effective altruism and utilitarian ethics on various blogs and forums, mostly because Peter Singer's argument about the child drowning in a shallow pond got under my skin and I haven't been able to shake it since. The logic seemed airtight to me: if I can prevent something very bad at little cost to myself, I ought to do it, and once you accept that, it's hard to see why the "little cost" should stop at ruined shoes rather than extending to most of what I earn beyond bare necessities. When I raised this with friends, the usual response was that this can't be right because morality "just isn't that demanding" - but the more I read, the more I realized that this reaction, which philosophers apparently call the demandingness objection, isn't obviously a knock-down reply on its own. I came across Shelly Kagan's argument, from The Limits of Morality, that ordinary morality has never actually earned a principled place to draw the line between what we owe others and what's merely optional, so the fact that a requirement feels overwhelming doesn't yet show that it's false. On the other side, I found Samuel Scheffler's attempt, in The Rejection of Consequentialism, to build an "agent-centered prerogative" - a permission to give extra weight to your own life and projects - into an otherwise impartial, promote-the-good morality, specifically so that it doesn't end up crushing the person who has to live by it.

What I can't work out on my own is whether Scheffler's prerogative actually solves the problem or just relocates it one step back. If giving your own projects extra weight is rationally justified by how important they are from your own point of view, it's not obvious to me why that same reasoning wouldn't keep pushing outward until you're basically back to "do whatever you can live with" - which looks like exactly the kind of arbitrary, unprincipled stopping point that got ordinary morality into trouble with Kagan in the first place. So I'd like you to write me a dialogue between two people holding these two positions - one taking the Kagan-style line that there is no honest way to cap the demands of morality short of full impartiality, the other defending a Scheffler-style prerogative - arguing face to face about a specific case: a young accountant named Priya who already gives ten percent of her income to effective charities and wants to know whether she is rationally required to give far more. Please make both sides equally sharp, and have them really engage with each other's strongest moves rather than just restating their opening positions - for instance, whatever specific limit the prerogative-defender proposes, I want the other character to press on why that particular limit, rather than some other one (including none at all, or almost everything), is the principled one. They don't need to reach agreement by the end, but whatever disagreement remains should be a real, substantive one rather than a misunderstanding that better definitions would dissolve.
