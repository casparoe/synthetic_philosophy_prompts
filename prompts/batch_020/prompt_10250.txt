My grandfather served as a young conscript in his country's army during a war that, by the time I was old enough to understand any of it, historians had come to regard fairly unanimously as a war of aggression rather than one of self-defense or genuine necessity. He almost never spoke about his service, but a few years before he died he said something that has stayed with me ever since. He told me he had never done anything wrong during the war: he never harmed a civilian, never mistreated a prisoner, and only ever fired on other young men in uniform who, in his words, "were out there trying to do to me exactly what I was trying to do to them." As far as he was concerned, whatever the generals and politicians back home were answerable for was simply not his affair, and it didn't change the fact that, soldier to soldier, he and the men shooting at him were in exactly the same moral position.

I've been turning this over in my head ever since he passed. Part of me wants to agree with him: he was barely out of school, he hadn't chosen to start the war, and it seems strange to hold an ordinary foot soldier personally responsible for a policy decided by people he never met. But another part of me can't quite accept that this settles the matter. If the cause really was unjust, then surely the killing done in service of that cause was wrong too, in some sense, however carefully he stuck to the rules of engagement on the ground. And if that's right, it's hard to see how he could really have been on equal moral footing with a soldier on the other side, defending his own country from invasion -- though saying that out loud feels almost disrespectful to a man who never chose any of this in the first place.

I mentioned all this to a couple of friends recently and we went back and forth for a while without really getting anywhere, so I'd like to know how you'd think it through. Is there a coherent way of deciding whether my grandfather and an "equivalent" soldier on the defending side were really moral equals simply because both stuck to the same battlefield rules, or does the justice of the cause they were fighting for have to make some difference to how we judge them as individuals?
