I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind working on cross-modal perception, and I'd like an essay that treats Molyneux's problem as an empirical question rather than the purely armchair dispute it is usually presented as.

Briefly, by way of background: Molyneux asked whether a person born blind, upon first gaining sight, could immediately tell a cube from a sphere by vision alone, having previously known both shapes only by touch. Locke predicted no; Leibniz, with qualifications, predicted yes. Sight-restoration surgeries on congenitally blind patients — notably the population studied through Pawan Sinha's Project Prakash program — have since supplied real data bearing on the question. The general pattern reported in that literature runs as follows: immediately after surgery, patients perform at or near chance on cross-modal touch-to-vision shape matching; accuracy then climbs rapidly, reaching well above chance within days of ordinary visual experience; and even within this early window, performance is markedly better when the relevant object is moving than when it is static.

Write an essay that uses this evidence to adjudicate the Locke-Leibniz dispute. Weigh whether the initial chance-level performance simply vindicates Locke; whether the speed of the subsequent improvement instead supports a qualified rationalist story on which amodal representations of properties like object unity or boundedness are innate and need only be recalibrated onto vision rather than learned from scratch; and whether the motion/static asymmetry shows that the classical question quietly bundles together several distinguishable capacities that should never have been treated as one. Defend a specific verdict about what the evidence actually shows — "both were partly right" is acceptable only if you specify exactly which component of the original question each side gets right, and why the question as classically posed was therefore ill-formed.
