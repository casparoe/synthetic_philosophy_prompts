So this happened at dinner with people from my program (I'm doing a PhD in personality/social psych) and it's been bugging me. Our friend Nate is famously, almost annoyingly honest -- corrects the barista when she undercharges him -- and it came out that at a conference last month he fudged his reimbursement form, claiming a cab ride he never took, because the reimbursement office is brutally slow and he was genuinely broke that month.

My friend Simone said this is a textbook situationist result, like the seminary students in the Good Samaritan study who stepped past someone slumped in a doorway because they'd been told to hurry, not because they were bad people -- small situational pressure swamps whatever "honesty trait" we credit Nate with, so his usual scrupulousness barely predicts what he does under different pressure.

Her partner Owen pushed back: virtue ethics never claimed the honest person acts the same regardless of stakes -- that's the whole point of phronesis, honesty cashing out differently when you're getting screwed by a slow reimbursement system than when you're just lazy. One rough night under real financial stress doesn't touch a trait that's shown up reliably a hundred other times.

Simone said that's suspiciously convienent -- if every deviation gets redescribed as "the practically wise response," nothing could count as evidence against Nate's honesty. She also cited the famous Mischel finding that cross-situational consistency in behavior tops out around a .3 correlation, not what you'd expect if character were doing much of the work.

Owen said .3 isn't nothing once you aggregate it across a whole life of choices, and that half the situationist canon is shakier than advertised anyway -- like how the guards in the Stanford Prison Experiment turned out to have been coached rather than spontaneously turning cruel. One bad night with a slow reimbursement office doesn't get close to overturning a track record.

Neither of them backed down and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning. Can you go through it move by move, say who's got the better of it at each stage, and flag whatever you think they're both missing -- then tell me straight: does the Nate story actually show anything about whether he's honest?
