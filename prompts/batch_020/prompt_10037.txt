I'm drafting the fourth chapter of a novel whose central figure is a retired analytic philosopher -- unmistakably modeled on Quine, though I've stopped short of using his name -- who is subpoenaed to give a deposition in a class-action suit against a tech company whose flagship product is a real-time "universal translator" built on a large language model. He is cross-examined by his estranged daughter, herself a computational linguist retained by the plaintiffs, and I want their exchange to hinge on genuine philosophical pressure points rather than the sort of hand-wavy "but can a machine really understand?" dialogue that shows up in lesser novels. Before I write a word of the scene, I want to work out what Quine's actual position commits him to.

Working strictly from the argument he lays out in Word and Object and extends in Ontological Relativity, would Quine say that a system trained end-to-end on vast parallel corpora, producing translations no bilingual native speaker could distinguish from fluent human output, thereby closes the gap his gavagai thought experiment was designed to keep open -- or would he insist that the indeterminacy survives underneath, exactly as it does, on his account, for the field linguist who has fully "gone native"? I'd also like you to bring in the distinction, pressed hardest by Chomsky, between indeterminacy of translation and the more familiar underdetermination of theory by evidence, since that is exactly the distinction I expect the daughter to invoke against her father. And finally, tell me whether the holism built into a neural network's distributed representations -- the fact that no single weight or unit corresponds to a single word's meaning -- is something Quine could plausibly claim as vindication of his skepticism about determinate synonymy, or whether that's a seductive but sloppy analogy that a philosopher as careful as he was would have batted down in the first minute of cross-examination.
