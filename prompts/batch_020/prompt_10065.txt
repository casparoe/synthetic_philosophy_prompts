A few of us from the local Astral Codex Ten meetup run a standing Sunday reading group that has been working through the classic literature on Bayesian epistemology, and a disagreement broke out last week that nobody at the table could fully settle. We had just finished discussing the Dutch book argument for probabilism -- the claim that your credences must satisfy the probability axioms on pain of being exploitable by a bookie who offers you a set of bets that you would accept individually, one by one, but that jointly guarantee you a loss no matter how things turn out -- when two members, Daniela and Marcus, ended up going back and forth on it for most of the remaining hour.

(1) Marcus opened by objecting that the argument only shows something about a contrived betting arrangement, not about belief itself: nothing forces you to accept every bet that looks fair by your own lights, so a bookie who needs your cooperation on each leg of the book has not uncovered any defect in your credences, only a fact about what happens to people willing to play along. (2) Daniela replied that this evades the framework the argument is embedded in: on the operational reading the group had been using all along, your credence in a proposition just is the betting quotient at which you are indifferent between the two sides of the bet, so refusing the bet is not cleverness but a refusal to have a determinate credence at all -- and she added that the argument's real force is symmetric, since credences that do satisfy the axioms are provably immune to being booked no matter how the bets are arranged, which is a fact about consistency rather than about anyone's appetite for gambling. (3) Marcus answered that this renders the whole argument trivial: if credence is defined as betting quotient, then of course incoherent betting quotients can be combined into a sure loss, since that is simply arithmetic on the definition, and it shows nothing about whether our actual beliefs -- the states that make us assert, doubt, or act surprised -- need to be sharp numerical probabilities at all. (4) Daniela's last word was that the argument does not need that operational premise to have teeth, because the diachronic version concerns policies rather than stipulated identities: if you decide in advance to update your credences by any rule other than conditionalization, a bookie can exploit that policy using only bets you yourself will regard as fair at the time you make them, which is a claim about the rationality of a plan rather than a disguised tautology.
