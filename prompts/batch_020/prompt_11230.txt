So I write about science and ideas for a living, and I just got talked into something slightly outside my usual beat: giving the commencement speech at the Universidad del Claustro de Sor Juana in Mexico City, which is literally built inside the old convent of San Jerónimo — the same cells and courtyard where Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz actually lived, wrote, and died. I did one exchange semester there years ago and apparently thats enough of a connection for the dean to remember my name. The room is going to be a weird mix: a couple of the literature faculty have spent entire careers on her (one has published three books on the Respuesta a Sor Filotea alone), sitting a few rows from graduates' abuelas who mostly know her as the nun on the 200-peso bill. Both halves need to feel like the speech was written for them, not just one.

My instinct is to actually build it around her real argument in the Respuesta rather than the postcard version of "she believed women should be educated." The specific claim she makes is almost architectural: you can't reach theology, which she calls the queen of the sciences, without climbing up through logic and rhetoric and physics and arithmetic and music first, because they're rungs on one ladder, not separate subjects — and she backs it up with a whole catalogue of learned women from scripture and history (Deborah, the Queen of Sheba, St. Paula) as precedent, so the argument reads less like "let us have opinions too" and more like a genuine claim about how understanding is structured. That's the part I want in there. What I keep getting stuck on is the ending: a few years after writing that, hounded by her confessor and the archbishop, she gave up her library (by some accounts one of the largest private collections in the Americas at the time), sold off her scientific instruments, and signed a renewal of her vows — supposedly in her own blood — as "yo, la peor de todas." She died not long after, nursing other nuns through an epidemic. A friend who teaches colonial Latin American lit told me point-blank: dont stand up in that exact courtyard and give them the "follow your dreams, nothing can stop you" version, because it isn't what happened to her.

Can you actually write the speech? Something like 700-900 words, in my voice rather than an academic's, that uses her actual argument for why learning matters, doesn't dodge how her own story ends, and still leaves a graduating class with something real to do with their ambition once they walk out of that courtyard.
