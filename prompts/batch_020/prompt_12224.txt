Over the past month I've been asked to lead a pay-equity review at the engineering firm where I work as an internal analyst, and it has sent me down a bit of a statistical rabbit hole. In the company-wide numbers, women were promoted at a distinctly lower rate than men over the last three years; but once I broke the results down by division (hardware, software, and field engineering), the gap shrank to nothing, or even reversed, in two of the three divisions. A colleague on our diversity committee, who is considerably more statistically literate than I am, told me this is a textbook instance of Simpson's paradox, and that the lesson to draw is that the disaggregated, within-division figures are always the "real" picture, while the company-wide figure is just a statistical artifact that should be discarded once you've identified the lurking variable behind it.

I have to put together a report for leadership next quarter, and before I build the whole thing around that principle I want to make sure it actually holds up. Is it true, as a general rule, that whenever Simpson's paradox appears you should defer to the within-group figures rather than the aggregate one? Or does it matter, in a case like this, whether the divisions themselves were arrived at through some process that might already be shaped by the very bias I'm trying to measure -- for instance, if women were quietly steered toward divisions with fewer promotion slots to begin with, rather than divisions being neutral categories people just happen to fall into? I would appreciate a fairly careful answer rather than a restatement of the paradox itself, since everything I have read so far explains the reversal very clearly and then goes quiet exactly at the point where I need to decide which number belongs in the executive summary.
