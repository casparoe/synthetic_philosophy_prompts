I'm a backend engineer, not a philosopher, but I fell into this rabbit hole through Jaynes' "Probability Theory: The Logic of Science," which kept getting recommended in a machine-learning reading group I'm part of. In the section he calls "the well-posed problem," Jaynes revisits Bertrand's old chord paradox: you're asked for the probability that a "randomly drawn" chord of a circle is longer than the side of the circle's inscribed equilateral triangle, and it turns out there are at least three ways of formalizing "randomly drawn" that all sound equally innocent -- picking two independent uniform points on the circumference, picking a uniform random point along a fixed radius and drawing the chord through that point perpendicular to the radius, and picking a uniform random point inside the disk to serve as the chord's midpoint -- yet they give three different answers: 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4, respectively. I didn't believe this until I coded up all three procedures and confirmed the numbers by simulation myself. What bothers me is that this looks like a direct hit against the principle of indifference: if "no reason to favor one outcome over another" can be spelled out in several genuinely different, equally symmetric-looking ways that don't converge on the same answer, then indifference doesn't seem to fix a probability at all -- it just relocates the question of which parametrization counts as the fair one.

Jaynes thought he could rescue the principle rather than bury it. His move is to insist that "at random" is only well-defined relative to some definite physical generating process, and that for a natural such process (he imagines straws tossed onto the circle from far away, so that we're ignorant of the exact position and scale of the circle relative to the toss) the resulting distribution over chords has to be invariant under translating, rotating, and rescaling the circle. Under that requirement, he argues, only the "random radius" method's distribution survives, which is how he recovers 1/2 as the answer. I've since run into the objection that this doesn't really rescue indifference so much as relocate it one level up: the invariance requirement isn't forced by the bare problem statement, it's smuggled in through the choice of physical setup and the choice of which transformations count as the relevant symmetries, so the original arbitrariness just reappears in the choice of invariance group instead of the choice of sample space.

Could you write me an essay that actually settles this, rather than just cataloguing the disagreement? Lay out Jaynes' invariance argument and the strongest version of the objection to it (feel free to bring in other angles on Bertrand's paradox if they genuinely bear on the question), and then commit to a real verdict: does the transformation-group strategy rehabilitate some defensible version of the principle of indifference against paradoxes like this one, or does Bertrand's paradox show that the principle can't be salvaged as a general method for turning ignorance into probabilities, clever invariance arguments notwithstanding? I want a position defended on the merits, not an even-handed summary that declines to pick a side.
