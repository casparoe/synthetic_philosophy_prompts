In my graduate seminar on decision theory, we recently spent a couple of sessions on Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility (REU) theory, which lets a rational agent weight a gamble's outcomes by a "risk function" defined over cumulative probabilities rather than by the raw probabilities themselves, so that caution or boldness about risk need not be reducible to how sharply one's marginal utility for money diminishes. Buchak's showcase application is the Allais paradox: on REU it can be perfectly rational to prefer a certain $1,000,000 to a gamble offering a 10% chance of $5,000,000, an 89% chance of $1,000,000, and a 1% chance of nothing, while also preferring a 10% shot at $5,000,000 (versus a 90% chance of nothing) to an 11% shot at $1,000,000 (versus an 89% chance of nothing) — a pair of preferences no expected-utility function can jointly accommodate, however it is shaped.

To press on the theory, I gave the class a further wrinkle, adapted from Arif Ahmed's review of the book. A marble will be drawn from an urn containing 100 marbles: 1 red, 10 blue, 89 green. "Steady" pays $1,000,000 whatever is drawn; "Bold" pays nothing on red, $5,000,000 on blue, and $1,000,000 on green. Matching the Allais pattern above, a suitably risk-avoidant REU agent prefers Steady to Bold before the drawing. But if she first learns only whether the marble is green or not (if green, the two acts agree, so nothing turns on it), then on learning it's non-green, the residual bet is 1/11 nothing against 10/11 five million versus a sure $1,000,000 — and she now rationally prefers Bold. Foreseeing the switch, a "sophisticated" version of her would rather pay a small fee up front to avoid ever learning the color group than take that information for free, since acting on it would predictably pull her away from Steady. An expected-utility maximizer with the same probabilities never faces this temptation, never pays anything, and ends up choosing Steady regardless — so the REU agent pays for a result the EU agent gets free, and both can see this coming from the start.

Two students, Daniel and Meera, kept arguing about this after class. Daniel said it settles the matter against REU theory: a theory that certifies, in advance and with certainty, paying for nothing beyond what you'd have gotten anyway is licensing guaranteed self-harm, whatever independent appeal risk-weighting has. Meera answered that this begs the question of what is "available" to the agent: learning the color group for free and still choosing Steady was never really open to her, since her rational self at that later point would in fact choose Bold — so she isn't giving up anything she could actually have had. Daniel replied that this proves too much: by the same logic no sequence of choices could ever be convicted of being self-defeating, since you could always say the better alternative wasn't "available" because your future rational self wouldn't have chosen it. Meera responded that there's nothing ad hoc here, since Buchak makes exactly the same move for cases with no risk at all — someone who abstains from a first drink because she knows it will rationally, if regrettably, make her want a second she'd now rather avoid — and that planning around one's own predictable future choices is an unremarkable feature of practical reasoning, not a patch invented to rescue REU. Daniel pushed back that the drink case is disanalogous: the drinker's tastes are altered by something happening to her, the alcohol, whereas nothing happens to the REU agent except learning a true, free fact, which the same fixed risk-weighting rule then converts into a reversal — and that, he said, is exactly the pathology a theory of rational risk attitudes shouldn't have: making bare, costless information a threat to one's own future welfare. Neither budged, and both later asked me who was right.
