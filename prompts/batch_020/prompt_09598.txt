In a seminar on philosophy of mind, we've spent the last few weeks working through Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument, and I want to see how well the resources of an actual historical theory of mind hold up against it, rather than merely resembling a response to it. Kim's argument runs, in outline: physical effects always have sufficient physical causes (the causal closure of the physical domain); a mental event that merely supervenes on, or is realized by, some physical event is nonetheless numerically distinct from that physical event, since otherwise the physicalism in question would simply be reductive rather than non-reductive; and, barring systematic and otherwise unmotivated overdetermination, one and the same effect cannot have two distinct sufficient causes operating on it at once. The apparent result is that the mental event's causal contribution either collapses into that of its physical realizer, which is reduction rather than genuine non-reductive physicalism, or turns out not to be a real contribution at all, which is epiphenomenalism. Kim presses a version of this same worry specifically against Donald Davidson's anomalous monism: since Davidson denies that there are strict psychophysical laws and ties causal relevance to strict laws, a mental event on his view seems to be causally efficacious only in virtue of whichever physical properties are covered by such laws, and never in virtue of its mental properties as such.

Suppose Spinoza were somehow shown this argument in its full modern dress, including the vocabulary of supervenience, realization, and overdetermination that it presupposes. His own system already contains a piece of machinery that looks strikingly relevant to exactly this kind of worry: mind and body are one and the same singular thing, conceived now under the attribute of extension and now under the attribute of thought, with causation confined within each attribute and never crossing between them, even though the order and connection of the two series correspond exactly (Ethics, Part II). Would this doctrine give Spinoza the resources to dissolve Kim's argument outright, on the grounds that a mental cause and a physical cause could never really be rival candidates for one and the same effect in the first place? Or would a sufficiently sharp contemporary critic be entitled to press essentially the objection that Kim presses against Davidson – namely, that whatever actually does the causal work occurs entirely within the order of extension, leaving the order of ideas exactly as idle as ordinary epiphenomenalism would have it, merely redescribed as "the same thing under another attribute" instead of honestly conceded?

Please work through the actual moving parts of Kim's argument – causal closure, the individuation of the relevant events, and the rejection of systematic overdetermination – rather than resting on a generic "mind and body are somehow linked" gloss on Spinoza's system, and say explicitly whether you take his parallelism to be a genuine third option beyond reduction and epiphenomenalism, or an elaborate metaphysical version of one of them wearing the other's clothes.
