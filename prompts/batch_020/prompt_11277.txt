My decision theory reading group has been working through puzzles about imperfect recall, and last week's session on the absent-minded driver problem produced an argument between two members that the rest of us couldn't settle. For context: a driver must take the second of two indistinguishable highway exits to get home; taking the first exit is bad (payoff 0), taking the second gets him home (payoff 4), and missing both leaves him at a motel for the night (payoff 1). Since he can't tell the two exits apart, any plan he makes has to assign the very same probability of continuing to both of them, and the probability that maximizes his expected payoff, worked out in advance, is two-thirds.

(1) Dana said the answer is just two-thirds, full stop: that's what a rational planner commits to before setting off, so that's what he should do at every exit he reaches.

(2) Marcus objected that this is too quick: once you're actually standing at an exit, not knowing whether it's the first or the second, the only belief that respects your actual information is that it's fifty-fifty -- and on that belief, exiting immediately looks strictly better than sticking to the two-thirds plan, so the "optimal" plan isn't even what a rational agent would choose to do once he's living through the moment it's supposed to cover.

(3) Dana replied that fifty-fifty is exactly the mistake: your odds of being at the first exit versus the second aren't free-floating, they depend on the very policy being followed, since you only ever reach the second exit having already continued once at the first. Plug in the odds implied by the two-thirds policy itself instead of assuming fifty-fifty, and the conflict disappears -- continuing two-thirds of the time turns out to be exactly what you'd also choose in the moment.

(4) Marcus answered that this is circular: Dana is fixing the driver's beliefs about where he is using the very plan she's defending, so it's no surprise the two agree; that shows the plan is self-consistent, not that it's correct. He added that the same maneuver can apparently bless more than one policy as "locally fine" at once, which suggests it isn't singling out two-thirds as uniquely rational so much as just failing to catch it in a contradiction.
