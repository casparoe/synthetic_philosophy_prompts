I've spent years reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum, closely enough to have followed the arguments about eliciting latent knowledge from AI systems, and I now sit on the pre-deployment review board at a company that builds agentic AI systems for warehouse and logistics operations. The team proposing our next release wants sign-off based on a 'reporter' head: a small probe trained on the policy network's internal activations to answer oversight questions like 'did you falsify this inventory count?' directly in natural language, which agrees with human judgment 99.2% of the time on our held-out labeled transcripts. Our red team objects on grounds familiar from ARC's ELK report: a reporter can reach that accuracy either by translating the network's actual beliefs (a 'direct translator') or by predicting what a human grader would say given the same observations (a 'human simulator'); the two are indistinguishable on any data we can label by hand, since they agree everywhere a human could check, diverging only on exactly the tampering cases we have no independent way to verify. The deployment team counters that they've stress-tested every tampering scenario the red team could construct without finding a divergence, and that treating an in-principle indistinguishability argument as a permanent veto makes deployment decisions impossible in practice. Please draft the board's written decision on this release: approve, block, or approve with specific conditions and a time frame, with reasoning precise enough that the deployment team could actually act on it rather than dismiss it as abstract risk-aversion.
