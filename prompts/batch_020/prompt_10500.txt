For my IB Philosophy extended essay, I'm writing on Kierkegaard's critique of "the public" and the press in Two Ages (the section usually excerpted as "The Present Age"), and I keep running into the same sticking point, which my supervisor says is exactly what the essay should be arguing rather than merely describing. I think I understand the apparatus well enough: Kierkegaard's notion of "leveling," by which qualitative differences between people are dissolved through comparison and idle chatter; his picture of "the public" as an abstract, unassembled, unaccountable phantom that exists only through the press and gossip, unlike a genuine community in which individuals stand in real relation to one another and to some shared ideal; and the fact that he developed this picture not long after a satirical Copenhagen paper called The Corsair spent months mocking his appearance and mannerisms in print, after he had publicly criticized one of its anonymous contributors. What I haven't been able to settle is whether this apparatus actually transfers to something like Twitter or Instagram, or whether saying "Kierkegaard predicted social media" quietly imports a lot that doesn't really carry over.

My sticking point is this: Kierkegaard's "public" seems to depend on a fairly specific arrangement, a slow, one-way press addressing readers who never assemble or answer back, inside a small, rank-conscious society in which "leveling" meant erasing older status distinctions. Online discourse, by contrast, is fast, interactive, and many-to-many, and in some ways it reattaches opinions to visible, named people rather than dissolving them into anonymity, which looks like close to the opposite of what Kierkegaard describes. A friend I've been going back and forth with thinks this objection proves too little, since Kierkegaard's real target is reflection replacing action and a crowd that renders judgment while no individual inside it is answerable for it, neither of which actually depends on anonymity or slowness.

Could you write a dialogue between two people who both know Kierkegaard's text closely but land on opposite sides of this question, one arguing that "the present age" describes today's online public more or less directly, if anything more thoroughly than it described 1840s Copenhagen, and the other arguing that the specific mechanics Kierkegaard describes don't survive the move from print to the internet, so that the comparison, however tempting, amounts to little more than a loose family resemblance? I'd like both characters to use Kierkegaard's own concepts precisely rather than paraphrase him loosely, and I'd like real back-and-forth in which each person responds to the specific point the other has just made, rather than two monologues that simply happen to alternate.
