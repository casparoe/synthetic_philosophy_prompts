Six weeks ago I began exposure therapy for a longstanding phobia, and the protocol my therapist uses rests on an assumption I had never thought to question until recently. During each exposure exercise, I'm asked to report my anxiety, moment to moment, on a 0–100 scale, and the exercise is only considered complete once that number has clearly fallen from its peak — the idea being that ending too soon, before I have genuinely registered the drop, would fail to retrain my fear response.

The trouble is that when I try to do this honestly, I can never locate the moment the drop actually happens. Descending from a 95 to a 60 over twenty minutes doesn't feel like crossing any particular line; each instant feels essentially identical to the one just before it, and yet by the end something has unmistakably changed. My therapist isn't bothered by this — she says only the overall trend matters — but it has left me with a different, more unsettling worry that I can't quite shake. Setting the gradual-change puzzle aside, I'm no longer confident I can trust my in-the-moment ratings at all. On more than one occasion I've felt entirely sure, at the time, that I was accurately reporting a 70, only to discover afterward, comparing notes with my partner who was watching me, that I had actually been far calmer or far more distressed than that. What unsettles me isn't just that I was wrong about my own mind; it's that, in the moment, I had no way of telling that anything was off, since my confidence felt exactly the same as it does on days when I later confirm I was right. That makes me wonder whether it's even possible, in principle, to know in real time whether you currently know your own mental state, as opposed to merely feeling as if you do.

I mentioned this over coffee to a friend finishing a philosophy PhD, expecting her to reassure me that of course our own minds are transparent to us in the moment. Instead, she said this brushes up against a genuinely contested question in epistemology that she didn't have time to unpack before we had to leave. I would like an actual answer: is there a serious argument that creatures like us can be in a mental state, and even truly know that we are in it, without being in a position to know that we know it — so that the kind of instant, guaranteed self-knowledge my therapy protocol assumes may simply not exist? And if that argument holds up, does it actually threaten what the exercises are supposed to accomplish, or can the practice still work even if this underlying assumption turns out to be false?
