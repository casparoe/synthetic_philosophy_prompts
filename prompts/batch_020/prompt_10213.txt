I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but a friend finishing her PhD in philosophy of language recently got me hooked on something that's been nagging at me for weeks: David Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals. My first instinct, before she corrected me, was the obvious one for anyone used to Bayesian updating -- that the probability you should assign to "if A, C" is just the conditional probability P(C|A). She explained that this is essentially Adams' Thesis, and that Stalnaker's semantics amounts to a stronger claim: that "if A, C" names an actual proposition, A > C, whose probability equals P(C|A) for every reasonable credence function, not merely one. Lewis's 1976 paper, and a follow-up piece about a decade later that pushes the result further, showed this can't hold in general: assume there is a fixed proposition A > C such that P(A > C) = P(C|A) for every probability function in a class closed under conditionalizing on new evidence, and you can derive that A and C are probabilistically independent under every single function in that class -- which is absurd, since such a class is supposed to be rich enough to represent credence states in which A and C are genuinely, and variably, correlated.

I've sat with the standard derivation long enough to follow it mechanically: condition on C and on not-C, apply the Equation again to each of those two conditionalized probability functions, then reassemble the results with the law of total probability, and the collapse just falls out. What I don't have is the kind of intuition that would let me explain to a colleague, in a sentence or two, why this had to happen, rather than it being a clever symbol-pushing trick. At bottom, what is it about treating a conditional as an ordinary proposition that is fundamentally incompatible with its probability tracking a relation -- conditional probability -- that shifts as the underlying credence function shifts? And separately, I gather there's a whole family of proposed escapes: denying that conditionals express propositions at all, the way Adams and Edgington do, or restricting how rich the class of admissible probability functions is allowed to be. I understand people like Hájek and Hall have argued that each of these routes gives up something substantive rather than being a free lunch. Could you give me the intuition behind why the theorem has to hold, and then, in similarly intuitive terms, explain what each of the major escape routes actually costs?
