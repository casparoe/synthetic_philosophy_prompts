At work, a few of us run an informal lunchtime reading group that's nominally about Bayesian methods for our machine-learning pipelines, but it keeps sliding into decision theory and formal epistemology, and this week someone brought up the Sleeping Beauty problem. I understand the basic setup: Beauty is put to sleep on Sunday, a fair coin is tossed, she is woken on Monday regardless of the outcome, and if the coin landed tails she is woken again on Tuesday after having all memory of the Monday awakening erased, so that whenever she wakes up she cannot tell whether it is her first or second awakening, or even which day it is. The dispute is over what credence she should assign to Heads upon waking, and apparently a substantial camp of philosophers, known as "thirders," say it should be 1/3 rather than 1/2.

This seems wrong to me on pretty elementary Bayesian grounds, and I would like someone to tell me where my reasoning breaks down, because right now it looks like a lot of very smart people are just ignoring the basics of conditionalization. Before the experiment ever starts, Beauty knows with certainty that she is going to wake up at least once no matter how the coin lands. So when she actually does wake up on some unspecified day, she has not learned anything that discriminates between Heads and Tails: "I am now awake" was already certain to happen under both hypotheses, so the likelihood of that observation is 1 either way. Standard Bayesian updating says you only revise a credence when you condition on evidence whose likelihood differs across the hypotheses in question, so her credence in Heads should simply stay at the prior of 1/2, and the thirders' 1/3 has to be smuggling in some kind of error. Given how basic this point seems to me, why does the thirder position still have so many serious defenders in the philosophical literature instead of everyone just converging on 1/2, the way they presumably would for any other elementary violation of conditionalization?
