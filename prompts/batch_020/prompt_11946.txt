This term I am taking a graduate seminar, cross-listed between the philosophy and computer science departments, on rational agency and multi-agent bargaining. We recently spent two sessions on the idea of "delegated game-playing": situations in which two principals — they could be firms, governments, or even rival AI systems built by different labs — do not interact directly but instead hand the actual play of some strategic situation over to agents or representatives, at least one of whom holds private information that the other side can neither observe nor verify. Before any delegation takes place, the principals can try to agree on some joint modification to the interaction, judged against whatever the "status quo," or default outcome, would otherwise be. My professor was careful to distinguish an ordinary Pareto improvement over that status quo, which typically only works as intended if someone's private information, once disclosed or acted upon, turns out to be exactly as claimed, from what she called a genuinely safe Pareto improvement: a modification that is guaranteed, whatever the private information on both sides actually turns out to be, to leave neither principal worse off than the status quo, without either side ever needing to disclose, verify, or simply trust what the other claims about itself.

On reviewing my notes, I find I can restate both definitions accurately enough, but I still lack a case that makes the distinction feel real rather than merely formal: my intuition keeps suggesting that no principal would knowingly agree to a renegotiation that might leave it worse off, so whatever the two sides actually agree to should already count as "safe" by default. Could you construct one concrete scenario involving two parties with some relevant private information (they can be two firms, two states, or two AI negotiators, whichever makes the logic clearest) that includes both (a) a proposed renegotiation of the status quo that looks at first like a Pareto improvement but is not actually safe, since it could leave one party worse off than the status quo under some realization of the hidden information, and (b) a different renegotiation of that very same situation that does qualify as a genuine safe Pareto improvement? I would especially like you to identify the precise feature of the scenario that accounts for the gap between (a) and (b), since that is exactly what I have not managed to pin down on my own.
