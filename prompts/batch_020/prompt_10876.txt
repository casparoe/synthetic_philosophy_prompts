hey so for my philosophy elective we have to pick an old thinker and connect their ideas to something in modern life, and i picked kierkegaard bc we read some excerpts from "the present age" and i thought the stuff about the public and the press was kind of insane how on-the-nose it was for social media. here's my draft, only like 4 paragraphs so far. can you give me comments? specifically i wanna know: (1) am i actually getting kierkegaard's ideas right or am i just projecting modern stuff onto him that isn't really there, (2) is the "kierkegaard predicted social media" angle actually a fresh take or is it the kind of thing everyone already says, and (3) whats the weakest part of the argument / an obvious counterpoint i'm missing. if you could go through it kind of point by point instead of just a general reaction that'd be super helpful.

draft:

Kierkegaard wrote "The Present Age" in 1846, before newspapers even had photos, but it honestly reads like he had a Twitter account. His big target is what he calls "the public," which he says is basically a made-up thing — a "phantom" that doesn't exist anywhere in particular but that the press invents and then treats like it's a real person with real opinions. Nobody in "the public" has to sign their name to anything or actually do anything, they just get to have takes. That's basically a comments section.

The other big idea is "leveling," which is this process where a society thats obsessed with comparing everyone to everyone else ends up flattening anyone who tries to stand out. Kierkegaard says envy is basically the engine of it — the crowd doesn't like individuals, it wants to drag people down to the average so no one is different. That's basically describing cancel culture and online pile-ons: a bunch of randos decide someone said the wrong thing and it turns into a whole dogpile, except no single person is responsible for it, its just "the public" doing it.

Kierkegaard also talks about "chatter" (i think the danish word is something like snak?), which is talk that doesn't actually commit to anything, unlike real conversation between people who stand behind what they say. That's basically a group chat or a twitter thread — everybody chattering with zero stakes.

So yeah, i think kierkegaard basically called online mob mentality almost 180 years before it happened, which is impressive for a guy whose other main hobby was being sad about a girl he broke up with.
