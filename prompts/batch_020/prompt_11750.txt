I'm several drafts into a historical novel set during the Congregatio de Auxiliis, the tribunal that Clement VIII convened in Rome to adjudicate the dispute between the Jesuits, following Luis de Molina, and the Dominicans, following Domingo Báñez, over grace, providence, and free will — a dispute Paul V eventually shut down in 1607 without ever ruling on its merits. My narrator is a fictional secretary to one of the Jesuit consultors, present at the hearings and increasingly unsure that his own side has the better of the argument, which means that before I can let him be unsure of it on the page, I need to have worked out for myself where the real pressure points are. I've been reading Molina's Concordia in Freddoso's translation alongside Flint's Divine Providence, trying to hold the sixteenth-century dispute and its afterlife in contemporary analytic philosophy of religion in the same imaginative frame.

Alongside the writing I run a small research podcast where I interview scholars, partly for my own preparation and partly as bonus material for readers, and next month's guest is a historian of early modern philosophy who has written on Molina's scientia media and on its relationship to the present-day Molinism of philosophers like Flint and Freddoso. I don't want the episode to turn into a walk-through of the three moments of divine knowledge that any reader could get from an encyclopedia entry; I want it to leave listeners with a real sense of where the view is under the most pressure.

Could you draft nine or ten questions for this conversation? I'd like at least one on the grounding objection — the worry that a counterfactual of creaturely freedom is supposed to be contingent and prevolitional, true quite apart from anything God wills or any world's obtaining, so that nothing is left to explain why it is true rather than false — paired with a follow-up ready for the reply I expect, which is simply to deny that every truth needs an external truthmaker in the first place. I'd also like at least one question on the older, more theological worry that animated Báñez and his fellow Dominicans: that middle knowledge makes God's providence hostage to facts about creatures he does not himself determine, coming uncomfortably close to the semi-Pelagian error the Dominicans explicitly accused Molina's school of reviving, in which grace's efficacy would ultimately turn on the will's own foreseen cooperation rather than on grace as such — with a follow-up ready for the reply that "weakly actualizing" a world is no less an exercise of providence than strongly actualizing one. And I'd like one question that gets her to explain what the distinction between a would-counterfactual and a mere might-counterfactual is actually doing in the claim that some possible worlds — including ones in which every free creature always freely chooses rightly — are simply not feasible for God to create. Please close with a question addressed to her rather than to the position: what she thinks a genuine refutation of Molinism would have to look like, since it's the one question none of the Molinists in my reading group have ever managed to answer to their own satisfaction.
