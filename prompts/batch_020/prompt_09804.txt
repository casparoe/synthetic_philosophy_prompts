Over the past few months I've become an active participant in the governance of a small DAO that allocates a shared grant treasury, and something about our new decision process has been bothering me. Frustrated with deadlocked token votes, the group replaced ordinary voting with a scheme in which members first agree on a single number to maximize — projected treasury value a year out — and then let two conditional prediction markets settle any contested proposal: traders bet on what that number will be if the proposal passes, and separately on what it will be if it fails, with whichever side prices higher automatically winning. The idea was to vote on what we want and bet on what will actually happen, rather than leave it to politics.

The first real test case is a proposal to fund one contributor's plan to aggressively court a rival protocol's user base. She holds a large token stake and has bought heavily into the "passes" side, which the market now favors — but she is also the only person with the access and reputation to actually run the campaign, so it's genuinely unclear whether her bet reflects foresight about an independent fact or simply the fact that she is positioned to make the outcome true and profit either way. When I raised this, one of the other stewards brushed it off, saying a market cannot and need not distinguish those two cases: money at risk is money at risk, and if her confidence were somehow illegitimate, someone else could profit by betting against her. That answer feels too quick, but I can't pin down exactly where it goes wrong. Is there a real distinction being papered over here, or is he right that the mechanism has no reason to care where a trader's confidence comes from?
