I've spent the past few weeks reporting a longread on "the causal exclusion problem" in philosophy of mind: the argument, developed above all by Jaegwon Kim in books like *Mind in a Physical World*, that if every physical effect already has a sufficient physical cause, a distinct mental cause of that very same effect starts to look redundant — leaving anyone who isn't a strict reductionist stuck choosing between systematic overdetermination, reducing minds to brains after all, or conceding that mental states don't really do any causal work. Looking for some historical ballast for the piece, one of the philosophers I interviewed pointed me toward T. H. Huxley's 1874 address to the British Association, "On the Hypothesis that Animals Are Automata, and Its History" (later collected in his *Method and Results*), which argues from reflex physiology, decerebrated frogs, and a sleepwalking soldier who could write whole letters with no apparent awareness, to the conclusion that consciousness never actually causes anything. I already knew the famous "steam-whistle" sound bite, but going back to the full argument, I'm finding it harder going than I expected, and I'd like help before I put any of it in front of readers.

Here is the stretch I keep circling back to. Huxley first writes: "It may be assumed, then, that molecular changes in the brain are the causes of all the states of consciousness of brutes. Is there any evidence that these states of consciousness may, conversely, cause those molecular changes which give rise to muscular motion? I see no such evidence. The frog walks, hops, swims, and goes through his gymnastic performances quite as well without consciousness, and consequently without volition, as with it... The consciousness of brutes would appear to be related to the mechanism of their body simply as a collateral product of its working, and to be as completely without any power of modifying that working as the steam-whistle which accompanies the work of a locomotive engine is without influence upon its machinery. Their volition, if they have any, is an emotion indicative of physical changes, not a cause of such changes." A few paragraphs later, after a short detour on free will in animals, he adds: "Much ingenious argument has at various times been bestowed upon the question: How is it possible to imagine that volition, which is a state of consciousness, and, as such, has not the slightest community of nature with matter in motion, can act upon the moving matter of which the body is composed... Their volitions do not enter into the chain of causation of their actions at all." And near the essay's close, extending the whole argument from brutes to us, he writes: "It is quite true that, to the best of my judgment, the argumentation which applies to brutes holds equally good of men... our mental conditions are simply the symbols in consciousness of the changes which take place automatically in the organism; and... the feeling we call volition is not the cause of a voluntary act, but the symbol of that state of the brain which is the immediate cause of that act. We are conscious automata, endowed with free will in the only intelligible sense of that much-abused term."

I'd like help thinking through three things. First, what exactly licenses the move from the steam-whistle paragraph to the flat claim that volitions "do not enter into the chain of causation" at all — is Huxley entitled to that strong a conclusion from reflex-arc physiology and a handful of decerebration experiments, or is he smuggling in something his evidence doesn't actually support? Second, Huxley arrives at what sounds like textbook epiphenomenalism by an empirical, almost case-by-case route, while Kim reaches the same fallback position by a purely a priori argument from the causal closure of the physical and the rejection of overdetermination — is that a real convergence between two independent arguments, or is Huxley quietly relying on something like a closure premise himself? And third, Huxley seems to leave open whether there's an immaterial "soul" behind consciousness, which makes this sound like a fairly traditional dualist's version of the old interaction problem — so does his reasoning, suitably modernized, actually threaten a contemporary non-reductive physicalist who insists mental properties are nothing over and above the physical, or would such a physicalist have principled grounds for thinking Huxley's worry simply doesn't reach their more modest position?
