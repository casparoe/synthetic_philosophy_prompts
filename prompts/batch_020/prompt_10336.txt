Two members of the hiring committee I sit on got into a disagreement after our department's last search, and I haven't been able to settle it in my own head. Rather than voting simultaneously in writing, the committee goes around the table after each interview and states verdicts aloud in a fixed seating order. For one candidate, the two most senior (tenured) members, who always speak first, gave mildly positive verdicts based on genuinely weak impressions. The third member, untenured, had a specific, well-founded worry about the candidate's job talk, but voted yes anyway, reasoning that two independent positive impressions probably outweighed her one concern. The last two, hearing three yeses, did the same despite private doubts of their own. The vote came out unanimous, though it emerged afterward that three of the five had real reservations.

Owen called this a textbook information cascade of the kind Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, and Welch modeled: once two votes were in, it was individually rational for each subsequent voter to disregard her own signal, even though the resulting "unanimous" vote carried barely more evidence than the first two impressions had. He wants the committee to switch to simultaneous, secret ballots. Nadia agreed something had gone wrong, but doubted this was a genuine information cascade rather than a reputational one: the untenured member wasn't inferring hidden information from two votes so much as declining to be the lone dissenter against two tenured colleagues, and on a five-person committee that meets every year, a "secret" ballot doesn't really give anyone cover. Owen replied that the two stories predict identical behavior here, so nothing actually turns on the distinction. Nadia answered that something does: a genuine information cascade should be fragile against a sufficiently strong private signal, whereas hers was strong by her own account and still got swamped — which favors the reputational story and counts against Owen's proposed fix.
