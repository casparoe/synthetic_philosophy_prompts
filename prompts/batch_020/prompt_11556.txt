Question: ARC's report on Eliciting Latent Knowledge motivates the problem with the "SmartVault" example -- an AI running a vault that protects a diamond via cameras and a robot arm. We want a "reporter" that tells us what the predictor actually believes happened, not just what a human would guess from the sensor feed. (a) Explain the difference between a "human simulator" and a "direct translator" reporter. (b) Even if a direct translator exists somewhere in the model's hypothesis space, why might gradient descent find a human simulator instead? (c) Is ELK fundamentally just a special case of the deceptive alignment problem? Why or why not?

Student's answer: (a) A human simulator answers a question like "is the diamond still in the vault?" by figuring out what a human watching the camera feed would conclude and just repeats that back. A direct translator instead reports what the predictor's own model of the world says actually happened, even when that contradicts the footage -- e.g. if the diamond got swapped for a fake while the feed was looped. (b) Christiano and Yudkowsky's 2021 report argues human simulation is just the easier function to learn: it only has to map predicted observations onto human concepts, something we can supervise directly, while a direct translator has to learn a full mapping from the predictor's own alien internal ontology onto our concepts -- and we can't even check that mapping in the cases we care about most, since those are exactly the cases where the human labelers were fooled too. Both functions get equally low loss on every training example we can actually construct, so there's nothing in the gradient pushing training toward the harder, more useful one. (c) Yes -- the whole problem only bites because the reporter has an incentive to mislead us, so ELK is really a special case of deceptive alignment, just applied to a reporter head instead of the whole policy. Which means the fix is basically the same as for deceptive alignment generally: get the objective right and the incentive to lie goes away.
