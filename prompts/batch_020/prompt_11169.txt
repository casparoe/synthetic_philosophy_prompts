I've spent a long time lurking on rationalist blogs and forums (LessWrong, Astral Codex Ten, the old Overcoming Bias archives), and a biographical detail that keeps resurfacing in our local meetup's reading group is Kurt Gödel's private argument for an afterlife, which, according to his biographers, he laid out for his mother in letters rather than in anything he ever published. As I understand it, the reasoning runs roughly as follows: the world is rationally constructed and has meaning; given that premise, it would be absurd for a person's intellectual and moral development to be abruptly and permanently cut off at death, especially since almost nobody comes anywhere close to realizing their full potential within a single lifetime; therefore, some form of continuation must follow bodily death. This is a separate piece of reasoning from his more famous ontological proof of God's existence, and what strikes me most is that it's offered as something closer to a logical inference from a metaphysical premise than to an article of faith — striking, given that Gödel otherwise kept most of his private theological convictions out of anything he published, sharing them mainly through letters and conversation with a handful of trusted confidants. It's also hard not to read the argument against the grain of Gödel's own end: convinced in his final months that his food was being poisoned, and with his wife too unwell to taste-test his meals as she reportedly had for years, he starved himself to death in early 1978.

I'd like you to write a short story, somewhere in the range of 1000 to 1500 words, that takes this specific argument — not "philosophers have argued for an afterlife" in some generic sense, but this particular inference from the rationality of the world to personal continuation — as its engine. I'd rather not read a biographical dramatization of Gödel's own life; I'm more interested in seeing the reasoning transplanted into a character and situation of your own choosing (contemporary, historical, or speculative, whichever you find most productive), where someone who has come to genuinely rely on this argument is forced to test it against something concrete and resistant to tidy logic, such as a diagnosis, a dying parent, or a mind visibly coming apart, though you needn't limit yourself to these. You're welcome to let the story carry an undertone of the same irony present in Gödel's biography — a mind that trusted rigorous inference above almost everything else, ultimately undone by something as unrigorous as fear — but that should stay in the background rather than become the point of the piece.
