I've spent a good deal of my free time working through decision theory puzzles on my own, and the St. Petersburg paradox is one I keep coming back to. The setup feels strange even before you get to the resolution: you flip a fair coin over and over until it lands heads for the first time, and if that happens on flip number n you're paid 2^n dollars -- 2 dollars if heads comes up right away, 4 if it takes two flips, 8 if it takes three, and so on, doubling each time you have to wait longer. The expected payout of the whole game works out to infinity, yet almost nobody would be willing to pay more than a modest entry fee to play it. When I read about Daniel Bernoulli's proposed fix -- replacing raw dollar amounts with a curved, diminishing-marginal-value utility function, so that the expected utility comes out finite instead -- it made intuitive sense to me, and for a while I considered the puzzle more or less settled.

What's unsettled me again is something I came across while following an old discussion thread on decision-theory paradoxes: apparently there is a variant, which I believe is called the Pasadena game, constructed specifically to show that Bernoulli's fix doesn't fully resolve the underlying difficulty. As best I can piece together, it is still built out of repeated coin flips, but the payoffs now alternate between a gain and a loss as they grow, working out to roughly plus or minus 2^n/n dollars when heads first appears on flip number n. Set up that way, the sum you would naturally use to compute the expected value turns into the alternating harmonic series, which only adds up to a finite number because of the specific order in which its terms are listed, and which can apparently be rearranged to add up to absolutely any value you like, or to no stable value at all. I mentioned this to a friend who studied a lot of mathematics at university, and even she could not quite explain to me how a "sum" could be so sensitive to bookkeeping -- I had always assumed the expected value of a gamble was simply a fact about the gamble, not something that shifts depending on the order in which you happen to add up its outcomes.

Could you help me actually develop an intuitive handle on this, rather than just presenting me with a wall of formal notation? Specifically, I would like to understand what is really going on with a series converging "conditionally" rather than "absolutely" that makes this kind of reordering legitimate in the first place, and then what the construction is supposed to establish philosophically: does it show that expected-utility maximization simply breaks down as a standard of rational choice in situations like this one, or is there some principled way of still assigning the gamble a definite worth despite all this? I am much more interested in feeling why the difficulty arises than in a fully rigorous derivation.
