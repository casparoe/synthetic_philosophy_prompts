I'm reporting on the intellectual pedigree of "AI safety via debate," the scalable-oversight proposal in which two AI systems argue for opposing answers in front of a judge who cannot verify the truth directly, on the theory that a dishonest debater will struggle to survive cross-examination by an honest, equally capable opponent. Researchers in this space sometimes gesture at older defenses of free, adversarial argument as a conceptual ancestor for the idea, and the passage I keep returning to is this one, from Milton's Areopagitica (1644), his pamphlet against pre-publication licensing of the press:

"And though all the winds of doctrine were let loose to play upon the earth, so Truth be in the field, we do injuriously by licensing and prohibiting to misdoubt her strength. Let her and Falsehood grapple; who ever knew Truth put to the worse, in a free and open encounter?"

I follow Milton's immediate target well enough, but I can't quite pin down the actual epistemic mechanism he's relying on: is Truth supposed to prevail because well-formed arguments are intrinsically more persuasive than ill-formed ones, because he's tacitly assuming a reader already competent to judge between the two once both are heard, or because of some theological confidence about the nature of truth that's doing more work than the "encounter" metaphor lets on? That last possibility worries me, because the entire point of debate-based oversight is to help a judge who is precisely not competent to verify the answer alone -- which looks like the one case Milton's "free and open encounter" doesn't obviously cover. Can you help me work out which reading of the passage is most defensible, and whether any of them leaves Milton with resources for the weak-judge case, or whether that's a disanalogy debate researchers should be more worried about than they seem to be?
