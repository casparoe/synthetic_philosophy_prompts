I'm a PhD student in political theory, and I'm giving the toast at my sister's wedding next month. She's finishing a PhD in philosophy, and her dissertation defends an assurance-based account of promising, in the spirit of Scanlon's discussion in What We Owe to Each Other: what makes breaking a promise wrong is that you've deliberately invited someone's trust and then exploited it, not that you've broken some pre-existing social rule. She rejects the Humean, conventionalist picture on which promises bind only because society has found the practice useful, since she thinks that picture makes fidelity sound arbitrary and rule-bound rather than something owed, personally, to the one person who trusted you. She's asked me to avoid the usual wedding-speech language of vows as a "tradition" or a "contract" -- that is exactly the view she rejects -- but also not to make the promise sound binding through some mysterious bond floating free of anything she and her fiancé actually do, since that isn't her view either. About half the guests are her philosophy cohort, who will catch precisely what's being defended and avoided; the rest are relatives who have never read Hume or Scanlon. Please write a five-minute toast, warm and allowed to be funny in places, that uses her actual view of why promises bind to say something true and moving about the marriage, without lapsing into a seminar or quietly smuggling back in the picture she rejects.
