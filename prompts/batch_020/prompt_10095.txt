For a term paper in a formal epistemology seminar, I want to defend a deliberately heterodox reading of van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin problem, and would like you to help me build the strongest version of the argument before I commit to it in writing.

Here is the setup. Judy's prior is uniform over the four cells formed by crossing the Red/Blue partition of her possible location with the Headquarters Company/Second Company partition, so that P(Red) = P(Blue) = 1/2 and, independently, P(Headquarters) = P(Second) = 1/2. A radio report then tells her only that, conditional on her being in Red territory, the odds are 3:1 in favor of the Headquarters Company area. Choosing the posterior that satisfies this constraint while minimizing relative entropy (KL divergence) from the prior sends her credence that she is in Red territory down to about 0.468, and her credence in Blue up to about 0.532 — even though the report is, on its face, silent about the relative sizes of Red and Blue territory. Van Fraassen introduced this as a problem for relative-entropy updating, and it has been treated as at least prima facie embarrassing for that rule ever since.

I want you to argue the reverse: that Judy's credence in Red territory ought to fall, and that the intuition behind the objection — that testimony silent about Red versus Blue cannot bear on the relative credence of Red versus Blue — is not a principle any Bayesian is entitled to hold in the first place. Engage with the strongest form of that objection rather than a weaker one, and state explicitly what your argument commits you to, more generally, about how conditional testimony ought to be represented as evidence.
