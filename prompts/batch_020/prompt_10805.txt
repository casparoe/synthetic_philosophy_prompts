During a long career studying the evolution of animal communication—vervet alarm calls, the honeybee waggle dance—I never had to credit my subjects with beliefs about each other's beliefs. So David Lewis's Convention unsettled me: he defines a convention as a regularity that persists only because it is common knowledge in a population that everyone conforms to it, expects others to conform, and would have conformed just as readily to some different regularity had that come first instead. Brian Skyrms, in The Stag Hunt and the Evolution of Social Structure and Signals: Evolution, Learning, and Information, derives the same phenomena—including full-blown Lewis-style signalling conventions—from evolutionary dynamics or reinforcement learning alone, with no common knowledge or rationality assumed.

Compare Lewis's account of convention with Skyrms's evolutionary account. Does Skyrms's success show that Lewis's common-knowledge clause was never doing real explanatory work, or does it leave out some genuinely normative feature of convention that mere evolved regularities lack? Also say whether Lewis's framework, built for pure coordination problems where every equilibrium suits everyone equally, extends to the stag-hunt cases central to Skyrms's later work, where a safer and a more rewarding equilibrium pull apart. Defend a definite verdict on the first question rather than settling for 'both are partly right.'
