For the past several months I have been doing cognitive behavioral therapy for generalized anxiety, and a recurring exercise involves logging "automatic thoughts" and sorting them into a standard catalogue of "cognitive distortions" — mind-reading, catastrophizing, and the like. Last week I spent an entire weekend convinced that a terse one-line Slack message from my manager meant I was about to be let go. My therapist walked me through the exercise: thoughts of that shape are, statistically, almost always distortions generated by an anxious processing style that isn't tracking the actual evidence, so I should discount my confidence accordingly. I find this persuasive in the abstract, yet here is the strange part: I still believe, right now, that my manager is unhappy with me, and I do not seem able to switch that belief off just because I also accept the diagnostic story about where it came from. So I appear to be stuck holding both "my manager is upset with me" and "the process that produced this belief is known to be unreliable in cases like this one" — which, I've since learned, is close to what epistemologists call epistemic akrasia.

Reading further, I discovered that this is a genuinely live and fairly technical dispute, not just an odd corner case. On one side are philosophers (I've seen the view associated with people like David Christensen and Sophie Horowitz) who defend something like an "enkratic" requirement: rational belief has to answer to your own best higher-order assessment of your evidence, so once you rationally judge that a belief-forming process is unreliable, keeping the first-order belief anyway is a distinctive kind of irrationality, not just bad luck. On the other side, philosophers such as Maria Lasonen-Aarnio are among the best-known defenders of a "level-splitting" view, on which it can be perfectly rational to keep the first-order belief fixed even while correctly judging, one level up, that the process behind it is statistically dubious — otherwise, the worry goes, anyone could talk you out of a perfectly good belief just by asserting, truly or falsely, that your reasoning is compromised. I cannot tell which side is actually right, or whether my therapist is asking me to do something epistemically well-motivated or merely something that is therapeutically useful while quietly violating the norms of rational belief.

I would like to see this fought out properly rather than summarized. Could you write me a dialogue between two epistemologists holding these opposing views, who run into each other during the coffee break of a workshop and end up arguing about a case structurally like mine (or, if it is more tractable, Christensen's well-known sleep-deprived-doctor-style scenario)? I want them evenly matched and genuinely engaging with each other's actual moves rather than restating talking points: the enkratic theorist should press the level-splitter on whether their view secretly grants an unprincipled immunity to any inconvenient higher-order evidence, and the level-splitter should push back on whether the enkratic requirement is one any non-ideal, embodied reasoner could ever actually satisfy, or whether it just mandates permanent, unearned self-doubt. I would especially like the dialogue to help me see whether there is a real fact of the matter here that my therapist's approach is either honoring or flouting, rather than this all being a dispute I can safely set aside as merely academic.
