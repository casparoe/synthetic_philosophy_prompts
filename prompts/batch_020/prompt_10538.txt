I've been down a total rabbit hole this week reading about Thomas Hobbes's decades-long math feud with the Oxford geometer John Wallis -- apparently Hobbes spent basically the last stretch of his life insisting he'd squared the circle (also trisected the angle and duplicated the cube, for good measure), while Wallis kept publicly picking his "proofs" apart, and Hobbes never once backed down, getting increasingly nasty about it in print right up until he died. What struck me is that this wasn't just an old man's vanity project -- Hobbes seems to have genuinely built a big chunk of his philosophy around geometry, treating it as the one truly certain science, precisely becuase geometric objects like lines and circles are things WE construct out of motion and definition, so we can be certain of them in a way we can't be certain of physics or theology. And then he tried to do political philosophy the exact same way in Leviathan and De Cive, deducing stuff about sovereignty and obligation from first definitions like he was proving a theorem.

Which got me thinking: geometry itself didn't stay nearly so tidy after Hobbes died. In the 1800s, mathematicians showed you could reject Euclid's parallel postulate entirely and still get perfectly consistent alternative geometries. Then Gödel came along in the 20th century and showed that no formal system rich enough to do arithmetic can be both complete and provably consistent from the inside. And now we've got AI systems (DeepMind's AlphaGeometry, Lean-formalized proofs, that whole world) grinding out real geometric theorems with nothing like a human mind "seeing" the self-evidence of each step along the way. Given how specific and personal Hobbes's reasons for trusting geometry were -- not just "it's rigorous" but "we author it, so we can be certain of it," plus his idea that reasoning itself is nothing more than reckoning with symbols -- I'm curious what you think he'd have made of all that.

Would non-Euclidean geometry or Gödel's results have wrecked his confidence in using geometry as the template for a certain, dispute-ending science of politics? Or might his weirdly constructivist take on math have actually let him absorb the shock better than a more conventional rationalist could? And would he have counted a machine mechanically proving geometric theorems as genuine reasoning by his own lights, given that he thought reasoning just is computation, or is there some principled Hobbesian reason to deny that a machine can really "construct" anything? Try to work through his actual arguments rather than the flattened "17th-century-guy-who-liked-certainty" version of him -- I want something that gets into the actual texture of his views, not just the greatest-hits summary.
