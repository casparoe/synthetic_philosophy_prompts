I'm working on a magazine feature about why corrective information so often fails to change people's minds, and while researching it I interviewed an epistemologist who introduced me to something called the dogmatism paradox. She traced it to Saul Kripke's unpublished lectures from the 1960s, later developed in print by Gilbert Harman, and sketched the argument roughly like this: if I know that some proposition P is true, then I already know that any evidence I might someday encounter against P has to be misleading, since nothing that counts against a truth can be trustworthy; and if I know in advance that such future evidence will be misleading, it seems I'm entitled to simply discount it whenever it shows up, rather than let it disturb my belief in P.

That struck me as an oddly precise, decades-early philosophical anticipation of what psychologists now call the backfire effect: the finding that confronting people with facts can entrench their original belief even further. I was planning to build part of the piece around the claim that philosophers had essentially proven, long before any empirical research existed, that dismissing disconfirming evidence is the rational thing to do once you "know" something. Before I commit to that framing, I wanted to check it with you: is that a fair way of connecting the dogmatism paradox to the backfire effect, or would I be conflating two arguments that don't actually line up the way I think they do?
