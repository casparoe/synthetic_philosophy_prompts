I spent about 35 years as an experimental physicist, and it's only in retirement that I've actually started reading the philosophy behind the stuff I used to just calculate with. I think I've finally got Newton's bucket sorted out -- the water climbs the sides even once bucket and water are rotating together, so it must be rotating relative to something other than the bucket, which is usually taken as decent evidence for some kind of absolute space or spacetime (substantivalism). But then I fell down a bit of a rabbit hole and found the "hole argument" that comes out of general relativity, which I gather Einstein himself ran into around 1913 and philosophers of science dug back up decades later. As I understand it, if your a substantivalist about spacetime points and you take GR's diffeomorphism invariance seriously, you end up saddled with a weird indeterminism, and the usual moral people draw is basically "don't be a substantivalist," or at least not a naive one. That seems like the opposite lesson from the bucket. Can you compare the two arguments properly for me: what each one actually establishes, whether they're even talking about the same "substantivalism," and whether there's a way to keep faith with the bucket while still having an answer to the hole -- or whether one of them just has to give.
