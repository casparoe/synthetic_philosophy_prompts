I'm taking a seminar this term on Victorian literature and religious doubt, and this week's session paired Middlemarch and Silas Marner with excerpts from Feuerbach's The Essence of Christianity, which Eliot herself translated into English in 1854. The seminar's organizing thesis is that Eliot's fiction works out Feuerbach's "transformative" critique of theology in narrative form: rather than discarding Christian ethics along with Christian metaphysics, she tries to preserve concepts like duty, self-renunciation, and sympathy while relocating their source from God to humanity itself. Our instructor illustrated this with the well-known anecdote, recorded by F. W. H. Myers and retold with minor variations across the secondary literature, in which Eliot, on a walk through Cambridge, is said to have pronounced on the three words "God, Immortality, Duty" — declaring how inconceivable the first was, how unbelievable the second, and yet how peremptory and absolute the third remained.

After the seminar, two classmates of mine, Marcus and Sofia, kept arguing about this over coffee, and I haven't been able to settle in my own mind who had the better of it. Marcus opened by arguing that Eliot's position is secretly unstable: Feuerbach's "transformative method" is supposed to be costless, merely restoring to humanity the predicates — love, justice, wisdom — that theology had alienated into a separate being, so that nothing of value is lost in the transfer. But, he said, the unconditional, peremptory character that Eliot wants duty to keep was always parasitic on those predicates being backed by something beyond us; once "God" is unmasked as nothing but our own projected best self, there is nothing left to make duty bind someone who simply does not feel like complying. Sofia disagreed, arguing that this gets Feuerbach backwards: on his view, the God-shaped guarantee was never doing any real work to begin with — it was a mystifying detour back to a need for other people that is already unconditional, so relocating the source of duty changes nothing of substance, which is exactly what Silas Marner dramatizes when Silas is restored not through doctrine but through his bond with Eppie. Marcus came back that mutual need is a psychological fact, not a moral "must," and that Eliot still wants duty to bind even someone who has, in fact, stopped needing or caring about anyone else — which is exactly the gap that only a theological "must" can plausibly close, so that Sofia's account quietly reintroduces what it claims to have dissolved. Sofia's reply was that this very demand — that ethics bind even a totally indifferent agent, standing in no particular relationship at all — is itself the Christian residue Eliot is trying to shed; she pointed to the narrator's remark in Middlemarch that a full, unmediated feeling for all human suffering would mean we "should die of that roar which lies on the other side of silence," and argued that Eliot's ethics was always meant as a cultivated, particular capacity rather than a context-free absolute, so that its silence about the sympathy-less agent is a predicted feature of the view rather than an embarrassing omission.

Could you go through this exchange move by move and say who has the better argument at each stage, rather than simply declaring an overall winner? I would also be curious whether, in your view, Marcus and Sofia are both overlooking some more basic problem with reading Eliot's fiction as a working-out of Feuerbach's philosophy in the first place.
