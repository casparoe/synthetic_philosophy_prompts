So I'm about a third of the way into a novel set in Red Vienna in the late 1920s, and one of my side characters works at Otto Neurath's statistics museum -- the Gesellschafts- und Wirtschaftsmuseum, if I've got the name right. I've been doing background reading on Isotype (the "Vienna Method" picture-statistics system he's known for), and every summary I find describes it the same way: Neurath set out to build a genuinely universal language made entirely of pictures, so that two people who don't share a word of vocabulary could look at the same chart and walk away with the same meaning -- basically a visual Esperanto. Its a great little detail for the book, except the more I sit with it the more it seems like a strange thing for a Vienna Circle guy to actually believe, since pictures on their own can't handle negation, or "if...then," or really any claim more complicated than "there are this many of these." Feels like the whole language should collapse the second you try to say anything but the simplest sentence.

I've got a scene where my character has to defend the project to a skeptical journalist at a dinner party, and I'd rather the defense be something Neurath could plausibly have argued than something I made up to sound clever. So how did he actually get around the obvious objection that pictures can't carry the logical weight real language does -- did he have some clever answer to it, or did he just kind of wave it away in his more propaganda-ish essays? Any help getting inside his head here would save me from putting words in his mouth he'd never have said.
