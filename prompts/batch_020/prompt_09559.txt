I spent thirty-five years running an experimental rock-mechanics laboratory before retiring, and for the past two years I have been attending a small philosophy discussion group that meets on Thursday evenings at our local library, mostly other retired professionals working through classic texts for the first time. A recent session left me unsettled about an episode from my own working life that I had never previously examined closely, and I would value an outside perspective on it.

Early in my career I relied heavily on the judgment of two people: a postdoctoral researcher named Elena and a senior technician named Marco. Ahead of a set of friction experiments, I asked each of them, independently, to predict which of two rock cores would slip first under increasing load. Elena worked through the loading conditions and the relevant friction laws and produced a prediction she could defend step by step; Marco, who had spent thirty years handling rock cores but had little patience for theory, gave the same prediction based on what he only ever called 'a feeling,' and admitted he could not say why he held it. Both of them turned out to be correct. Years afterward, when a firm consulting on a dam site asked me, in effect, whose judgment they should defer to on a related question, I recommended Elena without a second thought — yet when I try to explain that recommendation to my reading group, I find I cannot give a clear reason why. On the one occasion where I could actually put the two of them side by side, Marco's belief turned out to be exactly as true as Elena's, and the firm never cared about anything beyond getting the right answer that day. What I would like to ask is this: on what grounds, if any, was I entitled to prefer Elena's correctness to Marco's? Or was I really just favoring a style of reasoning that happened to resemble my own, and dressing that preference up as something more principled?
