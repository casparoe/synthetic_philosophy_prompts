For the last several months I have been working through Schopenhauer's *The World as Will and Representation* on my own time, without any formal background in philosophy, mainly because I wanted to understand why he treats music as so different from every other art. Before he even gets to music, he spends a long stretch ranking the other arts against one another according to how directly and completely they reveal what he calls the different "grades of objectification" of the will, with architecture at the very bottom because it reveals almost nothing beyond the conflict between gravity and rigidity, and poetry somewhere near the top. Then, once that whole ladder is built, he argues that music does not really belong on it at all, since it is not a copy of the Ideas the way the other arts are but a direct copy of the will itself, which is supposedly part of why he thought it could, in some sense, still mean something even if the entire visible world ceased to exist.

I brought this up recently with a friend of mine who trained seriously as a violinist, and we ended up in a genuine disagreement. I was not convinced that complexity as such is really what his own reasoning tracks, since what I have read of his remarks on song and opera suggests he cares more about how tightly a piece of music is bound to a specific text or a specific imitated event than about how many voices it has. Neither of us could settle the question from memory alone, and I would rather have it settled by a careful reconstruction of his actual argument than by however our conversation happens to end.
