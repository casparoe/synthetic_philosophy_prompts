I've been reading through Leibniz's Monadology properly for the first time, prompted by yet another LessWrong/Astral Codex Ten comment thread invoking his "mill" argument as though it were the original statement of the hard problem of consciousness -- usually deployed in some argument about whether language models, or whatever succeeds them, could be the kind of thing that has experiences and therefore moral status. Section 17 is short enough to quote in full: "It must also be confessed that perception, and that which depends on it, is inexplicable by mechanical causes, that is, by figures and motions. And in supposing that there were a machine whose structure produced thinking, feeling, and perceiving, one could conceive it enlarged while retaining the same proportions, so that one could enter into it as into a mill. Given this, one would find, on going inside, only parts which push one another, and nothing with which to explain a perception. It is accordingly in the simple substance, and not in the composite or in the machine, that perception must be sought."

Before I lean on this passage in a longer piece I'm drafting on the moral status of digital minds, I want to actually understand what work it does rather than cite it the way those comment threads do. Three things would help. First, reconstruct the argument as rigorously as you can: is its force really carried by the vivid image of walking through an enlarged mechanism and finding nothing but parts pushing parts -- which, taken alone, looks like a fairly weak argument from failure of imagination, no better than objecting that a brain scan doesn't "show" you a thought -- or is the mill just an illustration of a more basic commitment stated elsewhere in the Monadology, namely that only genuine unities ("simple substances," without parts) can bear intrinsic properties like perception, while any composite, however organized, can have only relational ("extrinsic") ones? Second, if it's the latter, I'd like your honest judgment on whether that background premise is doing independent argumentative work or is essentially presupposing the conclusion in different vocabulary. Third, and here I want a real verdict rather than a survey of positions: granting the argument whatever force it has, does it give us any actual reason to doubt that a suitably organized computational system could have genuine perception, or does it only rule out a crude picture of mechanism -- rigid parts transmitting motion -- that no contemporary functionalist or computationalist about mind was ever committed to, such that Leibniz and a proponent of digital sentience could both be internally consistent and simply be talking past one another?
