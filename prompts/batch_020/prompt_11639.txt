David Lewis's "Humean supervenience" has been a recurring touchstone in the philosophy-of-physics threads I've followed for years: it's the thesis, named in Hume's honor, that all facts whatsoever—laws, chances, causation, counterfactuals—supervene on nothing but the spatiotemporal arrangement of local qualities (the "Humean mosaic"). It's a sharp test case in the broader Humean/anti-Humean dispute about laws of nature, because Lewis himself conceded that quantum mechanics might undo the doctrine: particles that violate a Bell inequality seem to be correlated in ways that don't supervene on any arrangement of local properties, a point philosophers of physics such as Tim Maudlin have pressed hard. Lewis's own response was to concede the threat while refusing to revise his metaphysics on the strength of physics he considered interpretively unsettled.

Hume died in 1776, more than a century before J. J. Thomson identified the electron and nearly two centuries before Bell's theorem. Drawing as carefully as you can on his actual arguments about causation and induction in the Treatise and the first Enquiry, reconstruct how he would have reacted to this situation. Would he embrace "Humean supervenience" as the natural heir to his skepticism about necessary connection, siding with Lewis that entanglement poses a real threat to be resisted or somehow absorbed into the mosaic? Or would he see Lewis's mosaic—committed as it is to a privileged manifold of local, separable qualities—as smuggling in exactly the metaphysical baggage that his own, purely epistemic argument against necessary connections was meant to avoid, so that a consistent Humean empiricist should simply shrug at nonlocal correlations rather than see them as posing any threat at all? Stake out one of these readings, or a third if you think Hume would have gone somewhere else entirely, and defend it by appeal to what Hume actually argued—not to what later self-described Humeans have made of his name.
