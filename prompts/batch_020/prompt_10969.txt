I work as a backend engineer specializing in distributed systems and have no formal training in philosophy, so please excuse any mangled terminology below. A debugging session a few months ago sent me down a rabbit hole I still haven't fully recovered from. The bug involved a consensus edge case, which sent me back to the classic coordinated attack problem and, from there, to the epistemic logic framework that Halpern and Moses use to reason about knowledge in distributed systems, where each process's knowledge at a given point is modeled as the set of possible worlds it cannot distinguish from the actual global state, given only its own local information. Somewhere between that and a late-night Wikipedia chain, I ended up reading about David Lewis's modal realism, and I've been stuck ever since. I mentioned my confusion to a friend with a philosophy MA, who told me I was running together two very different notions of "possible world," but when I pushed her to explain why the difference actually matters — given that the underlying formal apparatus (worlds, an accessibility or indistinguishability relation, and a valuation) looks identical either way — she wasn't able to fully satisfy me, and we left it unresolved.

Here is where I've landed, and I'd genuinely like to be corrected if I'm wrong. In distributed computing, nobody treats quantifying over possible worlds, or leaning on the existence of worlds indistinguishable from the actual one, as any kind of controversial metaphysical move; it's just unremarkable formal machinery we use every day to state and prove correctness properties. So when philosophers present Lewis's modal realism — the thesis that merely possible worlds are exactly as concrete and real as our own — as one of the most radical and hardest-to-accept positions in all of metaphysics, I don't see what makes it so different from, or more ontologically committal than, what we already casually assume every time we build one of these Kripke-style models. Is there a substantive distinction I'm missing here, or does philosophers' discomfort with Lewis's view ultimately come down to intuition and theoretical taste rather than to any real difference in what's being posited?
