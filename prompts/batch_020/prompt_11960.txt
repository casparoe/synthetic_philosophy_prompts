Ok so this is a weirdly specific thing but it happened two weeks ago and I still haven't stopped chewing on it. Me and two friends have had a little three-piece band going for about a year, nothing serious, but we finally landed our first real paying gig at a bar downtown. We'd been rehearsing every week and telling everyone we know to come. Then twelve days before the show our guitarist texts the group chat that he's out -- a more established local band offered him a fill-in spot for the same night and he's taking it instead. No call, no sit-down, just a text. I was furious and told him he couldn't just bail on us like that, we had a commitment, and he said (I'm quoting almost exactly) "nobody signed anything, I never promised you a contract, I'm allowed to change my mind." Which is technically true, I guess? But it still feels like he did something to us specifically, not just a generically disappointing thing that happened near us.

I brought this up over dinner with my roomate who's finishing a philosophy PhD, and she got weirdly animated and said this maps onto an actual ongoing debate between two people, Bratman and Gilbert, about what a "shared intention" or "joint commitment" even is. Something like: one of them thinks a group intention is entirely built out of each person's individual intentions interlocking and meshing together, so once you've fully described everyone's private mental states there's no extra moral residue left over to explain; the other thinks that once a group forms a joint commitment, even a completely informal one, each member is owed something real and has actual standing to object, and she brought up some example of hers about two people just walking down the street together where one can't just stop short without the other having a legitimate gripe, contract or no contract. I haven't read either of them myself so I might be mangling this, it was one dinner conversation.

Anyway -- could you write me a dialogue between a Bratman-style philosopher and a Gilbert-style philosopher who really get into it about my situation specifically? I don't want them just each restating their own view past each other, I want actual back and forth where each one has to deal with the other's strongest move. And if they can, I'd love for them to try to settle, using their own theoretical resources rather than just common sense, whether the guitarist actually wronged the rest of us or whether I'm retroactively inventing an obligation that was never really there. Bonus points if the Bratman character has a good answer for why "you can't just do that to us" feels like such a strong reaction, without quietly just borrowing Gilbert's picture to get there.
