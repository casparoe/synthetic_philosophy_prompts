So last night I got into a whole thing with my friend Priya over drinks (she's doing a stats PhD, so she's dangerous in these arguments) about whether "equally likely" actually means anything, and I still haven't recovered. It started because I was defending the idea that a flat, uniform prior can be the genuinely rational default when you truly know nothing about a situation, and she pulled out Bertrand's chord paradox as her big gotcha (the probability one, not his other paradox about price competition in econ — pretty sure it's the same Bertrand). You know the setup: draw a "random" chord in a circle and ask how likely it is to be longer than a side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Pick the two endpoints uniformly on the circumference and you get 1/3. Pick a random radius, then a uniform point along it, and draw the chord through that point perpendicular to the radius, and you get 1/2. Pick the midpoint uniformly inside the whole disc and you get 1/4. Every single method sounds like the obviously fair, symmetric, adds-no-extra-information way to pick a random chord, and yet they flat out disagree with each other. Priya's whole point was that this kills the principle of indifference at the root: "equally likely" isn't a fact about the world, its a fact about whatever parametrization you happened to grab first, so the entire Laplacean project of grounding probability in equipossible cases was broken from day one.

Here's the part neither of us could actually settle, and what I want your help with: Laplace died in 1827 and Bertrand didn't publish this thing until 1889, so Laplace never had to answer it head-on, but he definately wasn't naive about continuous or geometric probability — he's literally the guy who took Buffon's needle problem and generalized it to a grid of two perpendicular sets of lines to squeeze a better estimate of pi out of it. So can you actually try to reconstruct, in real detail and using only concepts he actually had available (not modern measure theory, and not whatever clever symmetry/invariance fix later probabilists came up with, unless you genuinely think he had the pieces to invent that himself), how Laplace would have responded if Bertrand had put the chord problem in front of him? Would his method of reducing everything to equally possible cases give him any principled way to pick one of the three constructions as "the" right one, or would he have had to concede that "choose a chord at random" is simply underspecified until you say more about the physical process generating it? Please don't just hedge with "we can't know" — I want your actual best reconstruction of the reasoning, and I want you to be honest about which parts of his framework you think genuinely survive this and which part is just dead in the water.
