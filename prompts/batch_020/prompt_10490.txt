So this happened in my philosophy of language reading group (part of my quals prep, technically, though it never feels like studying) and its been bugging me all week. We're doing a few weeks on slurs -- working through Anderson & Lepore's prohibitionism alongside Hom's combinatorial externalism -- and somebody in the group pointed out something kind of funny: basically none of the papers on our list ever actually print the slur they're analyzing. Everyone writes "the n-word," or does the first-letter-plus-dashes thing, even in the middle of formally glossing an example sentence where the word is purely mentioned, not used to refer to anyone. Two people in the group, Naomi and Owen, ended up going back and forth for like twenty minutes over what that one fact is actually evidence for, and I'd like a referee who isn't me, since I was too busy taking notes to land on an opinion of my own.

Naomi's first move was that the fact that nobody will even mention the word on the page while transparently just glossing syntax shows the offense can't be tracking speaker meaning, truth-conditional content, or any garden-variety implicature -- it floats completely free of the use/mention distinction, which is exactly what you'd expect if something like Anderson & Lepore's picture is right, where it's a brute prohibition on the word-form and not a fact about what's communicated at all. Owen's reply was that this doesn't actually discriminate between theories, because surviving mention is old news for conventional-implicature- or presupposition-style content too -- that kind of content is famous for projecting past exactly these embedded, quotation-adjacent contexts, so any theory that models slur content that way predicts the identical data, no brute prohibition required. Naomi came back with: fine, but then why don't linguists show anywhere near the same squeamishness printing actual swear words or blasphemy in, say, a phonology paper on obstruents -- if this were really a general architectural fact about content that projects, it should generalize to every expression with that profile, not track this one culturally specific vocabulary, so the asymmetry looks like social practice doing the work, not semantic or pragmatic architecture. Owen's last word was that no asymmetry is needed, because on a Hom-style story the offending content is defined by reference to specific histories of discrimination against a group, so of course only words with that particular content get the typographic kid-gloves treatment, while ordinary taboo words, which don't encode any such history, don't -- the generalization holds, it's just narrower than Naomi thinks.

Neither of them backed down, and honestly I think they're each onto something real rather than just talking past each other.
