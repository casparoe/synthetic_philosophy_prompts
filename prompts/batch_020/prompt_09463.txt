I'm a retired oceanographer -- spent close to forty years doing acoustic and circulation work, a good chunk of it Navy-funded back in the day, which is its own complicated feeling once you sit with it long enough. Since retiring I've gotten embarrassingly into philosophy, mostly through a free continuing-ed seminar and a lot of stubborn re-reading with a dictionary at my elbow. My grandson Eli is graduating from Annapolis next month and getting commissioned as an ensign, and he's asked me -- half as a joke I think, because he knows exactly what I've been reading lately -- to say a few words at the family dinner after the ceremony.

Here's my problem though. I've been stuck for weeks on the bit in Hegel's Philosophy of Right (somewhere around para. 324, don't hold me to the exact number) where he argues war isn't just a tragedy that states have to occasionally manage -- it actually does something ethically necessary for a people, keeps them from going stagnant and self-satisfied the way a becalmed sea goes foul, reminds individuals that their property and their tidy private lives aren't the last word on things. He's also pretty dismissive of Kant's dream of a lasting peace between nations, basically because there's no higher authority standing over sovereign states the way a state stands over its citizens, so its treaties and contingency all the way down. As someone who has spent an actual career on actual bodies of water and knows perfectly well that a calm sea isn't a dying one, I find the whole argument half brilliant and half monstrous, and I don't beleive it, not straight up. But I also don't want to just stand up and say "so proud of you, stay safe out there," not with his favorite philosophy professor from the Academy sitting right there at the table, next to my sister who thinks all this reading has made me strange.

So could you write me a short toast, three or four minutes out loud, that actually deals with what Hegel says honestly, including the uncomfortable part, without me ending up sounding like I'm telling my grandson that war is good for the soul of a nation -- because I don't think that, and I don't want him carrying it into a wardroom somewhere. It still has to work as a toast though: something that lands as a grandfather's love and pride, not a philosophy lecture, for a table where half the people have never heard of Hegel and one or two have read him more carefully than I ever will.
