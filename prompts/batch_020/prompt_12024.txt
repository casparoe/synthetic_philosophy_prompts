I've spent a good deal of time working through the free will question on my own, without any philosophical training, and eventually arrived at an argument along these lines: whatever I do in a given situation, I do because of the way I am—my values, my temperament, my deepest desires—and I never chose to be that way, since any act of self-shaping I could point to would itself just express some earlier way I already was, which I also didn't choose, and so on backward without end. Genuine responsibility for anything would then seem to require having authored myself out of nothing, which looks impossible. I later discovered that this is essentially Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument" against moral responsibility, and two things about it puzzle me. First, Strawson insists the argument holds up just as well under indeterminism as under determinism—that even genuinely undetermined links in the causal chain behind my character do nothing to rescue responsibility—and I don't see why pure chance shouldn't count for something, since it seems to leave room that strict determinism doesn't. Second, unlike other philosophers who reach similar conclusions, Strawson doesn't seem to treat the impossibility of responsibility as a reason to reform how we actually praise, blame, and punish one another. Why not? Could you reconstruct the reasoning behind both of these points?
