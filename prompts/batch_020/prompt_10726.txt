I have spent the past several weeks reporting a feature on the curious echo between today's "biohacking" culture and older traditions that treated diet and daily routine as disciplines of the mind, and it sent me back to a text I had not read closely since graduate school: Nietzsche's *Ecce Homo*. What struck me on this pass was how literally, not decoratively, he meant the claims he makes in the section "Why I Am So Clever." There he insists that questions of nutrition, locale, climate, and recreation matter more than anything philosophy had previously dignified with grand names like "the soul" or "the good," and he happily diagnoses whole nations, not just individuals, by their eating and drinking habits.

For a sidebar to that piece, I would like to hold several of his specific prescriptions up against what nutrition science, exercise physiology, and psychology actually tell us today, rather than let them pass as the charming trivia of a famously immoderate mind. The claims are: (1) "zero alcohol" – his insistence that even one daily glass of wine or beer is enough to be ruinous to a creative mind; (2) "heavy cuisine dulls thought" – his view that dense, meat-and-starch national cuisines (his targets were German and English cooking) blunt the intellect, while lighter, Mediterranean cooking sharpens it; (3) "staple grain, national vice" – his suggestion that a people's staple grain predisposes it toward a matching narcotic, with rice-eating cultures drawn to opiates and potato-and-beer cultures drawn to alcohol; (4) "thinking on foot" – his claim that any idea worth having is conceived while walking or moving outdoors, and that sitting still for long stretches is close to a sin against the spirit; (5) "climate determinism" – his belief that philosophical genius requires dry, high-altitude, or coastal air, and that damp, overcast climates stunt it; and (6) "small, plain meals" – his preference for frequent, light, easily digestible meals over large, heavy ones, with nothing eaten in between.
