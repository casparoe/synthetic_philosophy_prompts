So after forty-odd years doing condensed matter physics I retired three years ago and, like apparently every retired physicist, fell down the philosophy rabbit hole. Lately I've been stuck on Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity as the "real" foundation for Occam's razor, and theres something about it bugging me that I can't quite shake. The whole selling point, as I understand it, is that algorithmic complexity gives you a machine-independent, objective notion of simplicity -- the invariance theorem says any two universal machines only ever disagree by some fixed constant. But that constant is fixed once you've picked the two machines, nobody seems to say how big it typically is for the machines people actually use, and for any finite chunk of data short enough to matter in practice (which is all data, always) that constant can easily swamp the real complexity difference between two competing hypotheses. So it looks to me like the "objectivity" only kicks in in some limit of infinite string length that no real scientist or agent ever reaches, which would make it a nice theorem about idealized reasoners and a non-answer for anyone doing actual induction on actual evidence. There's a seperate, deeper worry sitting behind that one even if you grant the machine-independence: going from "hypothesis A compresses the data better than B" to "A is more likely true than B" seems to smuggle in the substantive assumption that the universe was generated by something like a short program in the first place, which is exactly the kind of thing Occam's razor was supposed to give you a reason to believe, not something you get to assume on the way to proving it.

I brought this up with an old department colleague who dabbles in machine learning now and got a lot of "it works asymptotically, don't worry about it", which didn't really address the bit I was worried about. I even dug up a paper, I think by Hutter and a co-author, that tries to defend Solomonoff induction against more or less this exact complaint, but I'd like an independent take rather than someone grading their own homework. Could you write me a proper essay on it: lay out the strongest version of the case that Kolmogorov complexity genuinely rescues Occam's razor from being a mere aesthetic preference, and the strongest version of the case that it just relocates the arbitrariness (to the choice of machine) and the circularity (to an unproven assumption about the world being algorithmically compressible), and then actually land somewhere. I don't want a shrug at the end -- if the honest verdict is a qualified one that's fine, but make the qualification precise ("it succeeds as a justification for X but not for Y", say) instead of just gesturing at both sides and calling it balanced.
