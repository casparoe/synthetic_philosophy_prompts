Since retiring after nearly forty years in neuroethology, working mostly on the visual systems of frogs and toads, I've spent my time reading philosophy of mind, and I keep coming back to teleosemantics with a nagging dissatisfaction. The basic move is appealing: a frog's bug-detecting retinal ganglion cells (the classic case described by Lettvin and colleagues) represent flies, rather than the disjunction 'fly-or-BB-pellet,' because detecting flies, and not merely small dark moving things as such, is what the mechanism was selected to do. But the very same selection history seems to me equally well described as selection for detecting 'edible prey,' 'nutritious moving objects,' or 'small, dark objects that move the way flies do,' and I cannot see what further fact about the animal's evolutionary past is meant to decide among these glosses rather than the others. This looks like exactly the trouble evolutionary biologists already know from the distinction between selection for a trait and mere selection of traits that travel along with it, as in genetic hitchhiking -- except here the 'hitchhikers' are rival candidate contents, and nothing about the selective process itself seems to privilege one over its rivals. Please argue that this residual indeterminacy cannot be removed by appeal to any further biological fact -- including proposals, of the sort associated with Karen Neander, that try to settle the matter by looking more closely at the fine structure of the sensory-detection mechanism itself -- so that teleosemantics cannot deliver the fully determinate content it promises without covertly relying on an interpreter's choice among equally adequate descriptions.
