I've fallen down the Robin Hanson signalling rabbit hole hard enough that I can't look at any institutional policy anymore without asking "wait, is this actually a costly signal or just costly." Which brings me to an actual problem on a committee I sit on. A few of us run a tiny, self-funded ten week research fellowship for people trying out independent research for the first time -- basically a shoestring, unofficial cousin of stuff like SPARC or the Atlas Fellowship, no endowment, just some of us who care. For the last three cohorts the final round of selection has been an unpaid five day "work sample": finalists draft a mini research proposal on their own time, unsupervised, get feedback from us, but no stipend unless they're actually accepted. Half the committee wants to keep it, on the grounds that (their words, not Zahavi's) "someone who won't put in five unpaid days for a shot at this isn't serious enough for us to want anyway." The other half wants to swap it for something shorter and paid, arguing the current version doesn't really test seriousness so much as it tests who can afford five unpaid days, which just reproduces whatever inequality gave people that free time in the first place.

What I keep getting stuck on is whether the "keep it" camp's argument is actually a handicap-principle argument or just sounds like one. As I understand Zahavi, and then Grafen's cleanup of him (plus Spence's econ version with education signaling), a cost only makes a signal honest if its differentially cheaper for the type you're trying to select for, not just costly in some flat, evenly distributed way. So the real question for our task isn't "is it costly," its whether the people we actually want -- talented, committed, likely to do good independent research -- face a lower effective cost for producing a strong work sample than the people we don't want, or whether the cost differential tracks something else entirely, like who has a flexible schedule or no kids. Honestly I don't know how that shakes out here. Could you write the short memo I'll circulate to the committee before we vote next month? I want an actual recommendation -- keep the task, drop it, or modify it somehow -- plus a rough timeline for whichever one we pick, and I want it to engage with the "keep it" camp's argument on its own terms rather than just asserting that unpaid tasks are regressive and moving on.
