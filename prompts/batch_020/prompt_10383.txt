I don't have any formal background in philosophy, but I grew up fairly religious, and over the past year or so, mostly by working through the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy and a couple of books I picked up on my own, I've been trying to figure out for myself whether the doubts I've developed are actually rationally justified or whether I'm missing something. The argument that has stuck with me the most is the one from divine hiddenness. What I find compelling about J.L. Schellenberg's version, as opposed to the looser complaint that "if God existed, we'd have better evidence," is how narrowly targeted it is: it isn't about nonbelief in general, only about people who are genuinely open to a relationship with God, not resistant to one, and who still don't believe — and as I understand it, even a single clear case of that kind of nonbelief is supposed to be enough on its own to make the argument go through. But then I read some of the responses and found what look like two quite different strategies for saving theism: one says that obvious, overwhelming evidence would actually crowd out the free, uncoerced response God is supposed to want (something like Hick's "epistemic distance," and I believe Swinburne makes a related move about the value of moral freedom), and another, which I've mostly seen attached to Paul Moser, says God deliberately withholds decisive evidence until a person is volitionally and morally ready to receive it as more than just one more fact about the world. I honestly can't tell whether these are two genuinely separate lines that both need answering, or whether the second is really just a dressed-up version of the first, and I can't tell whether either one actually rescues theism from the argument.

Could you write me an essay that works through this properly and actually settles it, rather than just describing the debate? I'd like the hiddenness argument stated precisely enough that the distinction between resistant and nonresistant nonbelief, and the point about a single case being sufficient, are both doing real work in the essay — and then I'd like the free-will/epistemic-distance line and the volitional-readiness line taken on separately and on their own strongest terms, including the best comeback a defender of the argument would have to each, rather than blurring the two together or waving them off. Please don't end on a "there are considerations on both sides" note — I want the essay to reach an actual verdict, something like "the argument goes through against the traditional personal God even given these replies" or "the replies succeed, and at most the argument shows something weaker, namely X," and to argue for that verdict rather than just announce it.
