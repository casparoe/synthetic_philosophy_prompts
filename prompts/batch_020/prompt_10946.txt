In my Theory of Knowledge class, I've been reading about the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda, and I'd like to get past the "blind men and the elephant" story that every summary repeats without ever grappling with the hardest objection to the view. As I understand it, syādvāda requires every assertion to be qualified by "syāt" ("in a certain respect"), since reality has too many facets for any single, unconditioned claim to fully capture it, and an unqualified claim only reflects one naya, or partial standpoint. What I can't work out is how the doctrine avoids a self-referential trap: is the governing claim that "all assertions are only conditionally true" itself meant to hold unconditionally, in which case anekāntavāda seems to break its own rule, or is it just one more conditional, standpoint-bound claim, in which case it's unclear how it can still do the work of refuting rival "one-sided" systems, such as Advaita Vedānta's doctrine of an unconditioned, absolute Brahman? This is essentially the charge that Śaṅkara and the Buddhist philosopher Śāntarakṣita historically pressed against the Jains. Could you explain how Jain thinkers tried to escape this trap—for instance, by appealing to the sevenfold predication (saptabhaṅgī) or to the distinction between a partial standpoint (naya) and a fully comprehensive one (pramāṇa)—and then tell me candidly whether their response actually dissolves the paradox or just relocates it?
