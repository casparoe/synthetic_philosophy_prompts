For three decades I worked as an experimental physicist, extracting error bars from noisy detector data, and the tool I relied on constantly was the statistical bootstrap: resample the one dataset you have, with replacement, many thousands of times, and let the spread of the resulting statistics stand in for a confidence interval you have no other way of obtaining. Efron's asymptotic results assure us that this is legitimate, even though the procedure never leaves the original sample to check itself against anything external. Since retiring I have taken up epistemology, and I keep encountering what Cohen and Vogel call the "bootstrapping problem," or its close cousin the "problem of easy knowledge": the charge that a reliabilist cannot legitimately use a cognitive faculty, such as perception, to certify that very faculty's own reliability, since this amounts to extracting assurance from a single, uncorroborated source. This looks to me like exactly the situation statistics settled in our favor decades ago, so I am struggling to see why philosophers still treat bootstrapping as a live problem rather than a solved case of self-referential inference. Where, if anywhere, does the analogy break down?
