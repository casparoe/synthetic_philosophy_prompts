For the past several months I have been in the revision stages of a novel, an experience that has thrown into relief a distinction I first encountered while reading around the Vienna Circle for background on a character with one foot in that milieu: Moritz Schlick's 1927 essay "On the Meaning of Life." Schlick's central claim, as I understand it, is that purposive activity — anything undertaken merely as a means to some further end — cannot ground ultimate meaning, since a life composed entirely of means to further means only defers the question indefinitely. He locates meaning instead in what he calls play: activity pursued for its own sake, whose value does not depend on any goal beyond the activity itself, and whose clearest instance is the unselfconscious absorption of a child at play. Two features of his account matter for what follows. First, he is explicit that an activity's producing something useful or valuable does not disqualify it from being play, provided that this is not why it is done. Second, he holds that a person well past childhood can recover this condition — can, in his terms, remain or become young again — by coming to perform even ordinary labor purely for the pleasure of doing it rather than for what it yields.

My trouble is with the part of novel-writing that least resembles a child absorbed in play: not the early drafting, which does have something of that fever, but the later, contractually bound revision — cutting a subplot because an editor flags the pacing, rewriting a scene for the fifth time to answer a note, trimming forty pages to meet a word count specified in the contract. This is the stage of the work most obviously undertaken as a means to the end of a finished, publishable, saleable book, and so the stage most exposed to Schlick's own verdict against merely instrumental activity. A friend who works on ancient philosophy, hearing me complain about this over dinner, suggested that the difficulty echoes Aristotle's distinction between kinesis and energeia — between a process like housebuilding, incomplete until some product exists apart from it, and an activity like seeing, complete at every moment it occurs — though neither of us could decide whether the parallel helps my case or simply sharpens it.

What I would like from you is a sustained argument for the position that this constrained, deadline-driven revision work can nonetheless satisfy Schlick's own criterion for play, so that his account does not, contrary to first appearances, confine a writer's access to ultimate meaning to the earliest and freest moments of composition. Please engage directly with the "for its own sake" criterion and the proviso about valuable effects, rather than simply asserting that revision can feel enjoyable in practice, and take seriously the strongest objection available on Schlick's own terms: that any activity performed because a contract or an editor requires it is, by definition, a case of work rather than play, whatever incidental pleasure accompanies it. As part of the argument, please also state explicitly whether the Aristotelian comparison my friend raised strengthens the case for treating revision as play, or instead gives the objection its sharpest form.
