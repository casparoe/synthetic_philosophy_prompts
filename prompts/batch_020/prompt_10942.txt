In a graduate seminar on moral responsibility, a classmate and I could not settle how much work Frankfurt-style cases still do against the Principle of Alternative Possibilities (PAP) once the discussion moves past the original Jones-and-Black vignette and into the more technical exchanges it provoked. I would like to see that later stage of the debate played out properly, as a dialogue between two philosophers of action who run into each other at the coffee break of a conference on free will.

The first character is an incompatibilist who thinks Frankfurt-style cases ultimately fail because of the dilemma associated with Ginet and Widerker: whatever prior sign a counterfactual intervener relies on to know when to step in is either causally sufficient for the resulting decision, in which case determinism (not the intervener) is doing all the interesting work, or it is not sufficient, in which case a residual "flicker of freedom" survives that restores some genuine alternative possibility after all. The second character is a semicompatibilist in the spirit of Fischer who thinks Mele and Robb's "buffer" cases close off that flicker for good, and that what grounds responsibility is guidance control — the agent's own reasons-responsive mechanism issuing in the action — rather than regulative control, the freedom to actually have done otherwise.

Please have the two respond to each other's specific arguments rather than simply restating their own position: the incompatibilist should press on whether a deterministic buffer mechanism merely relocates, rather than resolves, the causal-sufficiency horn of the original dilemma, and the semicompatibilist needs a substantive answer to that challenge rather than a bare reassertion that guidance control suffices. Neither character should be easier to argue for than the other, and the dialogue need not end in a resolution — but it should end with both interlocutors able to state clearly, in terms the other would recognize as fair, exactly where their remaining disagreement lies.
