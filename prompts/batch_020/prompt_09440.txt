I don't have any formal training in philosophy -- I work as a machinist -- but a long-running argument with a coworker about taxation finally pushed me to sit down this year and actually read Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, rather than just seeing bits of it quoted back and forth online. What's stayed with me longest isn't the minimal-state argument or the Wilt Chamberlain passage everybody cites, but a short, oddly tentative stretch near the end where Nozick starts worrying about animals and sketches a view he sums up as "utilitarianism for animals, Kantianism for people": persons are protected by side constraints against being used merely as a means, while animal interests just get fed into an aggregate utilitarian calculation. He then more or less admits that he isn't fully satisfied with the loose ends.

I help run a small monthly philosophy meetup in my town -- nothing formal, just a group of us arguing about a book over coffee -- and next month we have an actual academic coming in for a Q&A who has spent a good part of her career taking that throwaway passage seriously and defending a fuller version of the dual view. Since most of the room will already have done the reading, I don't want to waste the hour letting her simply restate the position back to us. Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that push on its real pressure points rather than inviting a summary?

I'd specifically like at least one question on why species membership is doing the work of sorting beings into the two moral categories, rather than whatever underlying capacity -- rationality, autonomy, or whatever else is supposed to ground side constraints -- actually justifies the split, since that threatens to sort some humans into the "utilitarian" bucket as well; at least one on predation, i.e., whether taking aggregate animal suffering seriously commits her to favoring large-scale human interference against wild predators; and at least one on replaceability -- the idea that if an animal is painlessly killed and, in effect, "replaced" by another equally contented one, nothing of value has been lost, which seems to be exactly what a purely aggregative view of animal welfare is committed to. For each of these, and for whatever other questions you include to round out the set, please add a follow-up that anticipates the answer she's most likely to give and doesn't let her simply retreat to Nozick's own admission that the view is unfinished.
