I co-host a small interview series in which our graduate students in Classical Chinese Philosophy get to hear specialists defend narrow, technical readings of canonical arguments rather than the flattened version they already have from a survey course. Next month's guest has written a monograph defending a broadly Hansen-style mereological reading of Gongsun Long's white horse dialogue: on her account, "白馬非馬" ("a white horse is not a horse") comes out literally true rather than paradoxical once we recognize that classical Chinese count-nouns like "horse" behave semantically more like mass terms than like English count nouns, so that "horse" denotes the mereological sum of all horse-stuff, "white" the sum of all white-stuff, and "white horse" a distinct, only partially overlapping stuff related to "horse" as part to whole rather than as species to genus. She also reads the companion "Discourse on Referring to Things" (指物論) as evidence that this was a deliberate semantic project, not just a display piece for the disputers' circuit.

Could you draft nine or ten interview questions that go well past asking her to restate that thesis? I want at least one question that presses the mereological analysis on its own linguistic merits, since specialists in classical Chinese grammar have pushed back hard on treating count nouns generally as mass terms -- what, precisely, does her reading need from the grammar to go through, and does it survive if that generalization fails? I want one that asks her to reconcile a mereological semantics of overlapping "stuffs" with the much stronger, quasi-Platonist metaphysics of the "hard and white" (堅白) discourse, which treats hardness and whiteness as universals that are separate because they are known through entirely different senses -- can both arguments plausibly belong to one coherent semantic-and-metaphysical program, or is she stitching together texts that later doxographers merely happened to file under the same school? And I want one that takes the ancient critics seriously rather than dismissing them out of hand -- Zhuangzi's send-up of the whole dispute in the "Qiwulun," and the broader charge, central to Xunzi's "Rectification of Names," that this sort of hair-splitting corrupts the fixed correspondence between names and things that good government depends on -- does her rehabilitation of Gongsun Long as a serious semanticist actually answer that charge, or just relocate it one level up? Please skip anything a first lecture on the paradox already covers, add a short follow-up to each question keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, and close with a question asking what would actually have to be true of the text or the language for her to give up the mereological reading and concede the traditional verdict of clever sophistry.
