I'm part of a small probability-and-forecasting club at my school: every week each of us submits a calibrated probability for some low-stakes real-world question and then we compare notes, partly to practice the Bayesian updating we've been doing in our stats elective. Last week's question was the probability that our robotics team would place in the top three at regionals, and two of the seniors in the club, Devon and Priya, ended up at 80% and 30% respectively even after telling each other their numbers and their reasoning. That turned into a much bigger argument that neither of them has really let go of, and it went roughly like this.

(1) Devon said that if they both think the other is basically rational and honest, they shouldn't be able to just sit at 80% and 30% once each knows the other's number: he'd read about Aumann's "agreeing to disagree" result, which he said shows that two Bayesians who start from the same prior can't have common knowledge of differing posteriors on the same event, so a disagreement that survives full disclosure means someone is being irrational or withholding evidence. (2) Priya answered that the theorem is cute but doesn't touch their actual situation, since the "same prior" premise is doing all the work and is basically never satisfied between two real people, whose gut assumptions were shaped by different temperaments and histories long before either of them had seen anything robotics-related. (3) Devon replied that this is a dodge: any honest belief difference has to bottom out in some difference in information, and if you trace two people's views back far enough, calling the residue a "different prior" instead of "different evidence" is just relabeling, not an excuse -- otherwise "we have different priors" becomes an all-purpose way to wave off disagreement with anyone. (4) Priya granted that, but pointed to the theorem's other premise: it needs the posteriors to be common knowledge, in the full "I know that you know that I know" sense, not just stated once out loud, and one exchange of numbers falls well short of that, so by the theorem's own terms nothing had actually gone wrong yet. (5) Devon said she was being too literal, because there's a follow-up result -- he couldn't remember whose -- showing that if two people just keep honestly exchanging updated numbers back and forth, they're guaranteed to converge to the same posterior after finitely many rounds, so a disagreement that survives even a few genuine exchanges is already misbehaving relative to that version, common-knowledge tower or not. (6) Priya's closing point was that this convergence only comes from each side updating on the bare fact that the other has some number, not on the reasons behind it, so at most it shows they'd end up mutually contaminated into the same figure rather than either number becoming more correct -- and besides, the whole framework assumes each side is warranted in trusting the other's rationality and honesty, which is exactly what two people who still can't converge might reasonably doubt about one another.

Could you go through Devon and Priya's six numbered claims and adjudicate them individually rather than just declaring an overall winner? For each one, say whether it's basically correct, overstated, or mistaken, and flag any technical detail either of them got notably right or wrong (including, if you can, filling in the reference Devon couldn't remember). Then tell me who you think comes out ahead once all six points are weighed together, and, separately, name the one important consideration you think neither Devon nor Priya raised.
