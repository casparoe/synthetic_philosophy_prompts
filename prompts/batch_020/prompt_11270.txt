Over the past several months I have been working through a formal autism assessment as an adult, a process set in motion after a supervising clinician at the practice where I am completing my training remarked that several of my own habits closely resembled traits she was screening for in a young patient of hers. Receiving the eventual diagnosis was clarifying in some ways, but it has also left me unsettled, because over the same period a philosophy of science reading group I attend spent two sessions on Ian Hacking's work on "looping effects" — his claim that classifying people, unlike classifying rocks or isotopes, is not a neutral act of discovery, since the classified population can internalize, resist, or reorganize itself around a category, which in turn revises the category, in a feedback loop that never fully settles. Autism is one of the cases Hacking himself kept returning to: the diagnostic criteria have shifted substantially since the 1980s, a large and vocal self-identifying community has grown up around the label and visibly reshaped it, and the "late-diagnosed adult woman" narrative I now find myself part of barely existed as a recognized pathway a generation ago.

What I have not been able to work out is whether taking looping effects seriously should change how much evidential weight I put on my own diagnosis, or on the diagnoses I will soon be making for patients of my own. I would like you to write a dialogue between two people who are equally rigorous and equally well versed in this material: one a clinician who defends a broadly realist view, on which autism, however messy its diagnostic history, tracks a genuine and fairly stable neurodevelopmental kind that exists in people prior to and independently of being named, so that the shifting criteria amount to our improving, if imperfect, attempts to draw a line around something already there; the other a Hacking-style philosopher of psychiatry who argues that autism is a textbook interactive kind, one whose boundaries, population, and even characteristic behaviors (masking, for instance) are partly constituted by the diagnostic practices, advocacy movements, and self-narratives built up around it, such that asking whether the category simply "gets autism right" is, on her view, a bit like asking whether our concept of a "hipster" gets hipsters right. Please have them engage each other's actual arguments rather than talk past one another — the realist should have something substantive to say about the DSM-5 merger of separate diagnoses into a single spectrum, the explosion of self-diagnosis online, and the historical underdiagnosis of women, and the looping theorist should have something substantive to say about twin studies, early infant markers, and cross-cultural prevalence data — and let the dialogue work toward at least a partial answer to the question that is actually keeping me up at night: does taking looping effects seriously give someone in my position real reason to hold their diagnosis more loosely?
