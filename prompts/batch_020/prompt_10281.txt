I've been trying to get my head around the "set-theoretic multiverse" ever since a mathematician friend sent me a podcast episode on it that left me more disoriented than the popular physics multiverse ever has, so before writing anything myself I went back to Gödel's 1947 essay on Cantor's continuum problem and some of the more recent philosophy-of-mathematics literature to catch up. The continuum hypothesis says, informally, that every infinite set of real numbers can be matched up either with the natural numbers or with the full continuum, with nothing of intermediate size in between; Gödel showed in 1940 that the standard axioms of set theory can't refute it, Cohen showed in 1963 that they can't prove it either, and more than sixty years on, set theorists still split into two camps over what that independence actually means. Before I interview anyone for the piece, I'd like to see the disagreement worked out as a real argument rather than the two-sentence version that shows up in every popular account, so could you write me a dialogue between representatives of the two camps?

One holds the universe view: there is exactly one true universe of sets, the continuum hypothesis is simply true or false in it, and the task of set theory is to discover new axioms -- ideally self-evident ones, though a strong track record of fruitful consequences would do -- that a mature field will eventually adopt and that will settle the question, the way stronger axioms of infinity have already settled other things ZFC alone leaves open. The other holds the multiverse view: there is no single background universe for the hypothesis to be true or false "in," only a wide range of equally legitimate set-theoretic universes in which it holds in some and fails in others, so that hunting for the one axiom to crown is a category error -- comparable, on this view, to asking which non-Euclidean geometry is the "real" one now that we know Euclid's fifth postulate, the parallel postulate, was never forced on us.

Please don't let them simply restate these positions at each other. Have the universist directly challenge that geometry comparison: unlike the fifth postulate, which genuinely fails in some perfectly consistent geometries, Zermelo proved in 1930 that any two full models of second-order ZFC agree with one another all the way up to wherever the shorter one stops -- more than enough agreement, for a question that arises as "low" in the cumulative hierarchy as the continuum hypothesis does, to fix its truth value even if we cannot yet prove which way it goes. Then have the multiversist push back on that very appeal to "full" second-order models, on the grounds that invoking them smuggles in the one assumption forcing keeps calling into question -- that there is a single, fixed totality of "all" subsets of the reals to quantify over in the first place -- so that the proof of determinacy is bought by presupposing exactly what it was meant to establish. Let the exchange run eight to ten turns, with each side answering the point just put to it rather than changing the subject, and end with both of them trying, and largely failing, to agree on what kind of discovery -- as opposed to decision -- would even count as evidence that a proposed new axiom for the continuum hypothesis is true rather than merely convenient.
