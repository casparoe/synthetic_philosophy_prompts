"Before Gettier, most philosophers thought knowledge just meant a justified true belief (JTB) - you know something if you believe it, it's actually true, and you have good enough reasons or evidence for believing it. Gettier's 1963 paper attacked this by showing you could satisfy all three conditions and still not really 'know' the thing, because it ends up being true only by luck.

The famous case he used to make the point is the barn facade case: a man is driving through the countryside and sees what looks like a barn, so he believes 'that's a barn.' He's right, and he formed the belief in the normal, justified way, using his eyes in broad daylight. But it turns out the county is full of fake barn fronts and he just happens to be looking at the one real barn. So he has a justified true belief that isn't knowledge, since if he'd glanced at any of the fake ones instead he'd have believed the exact same false thing for the exact same reasons.

One response to this is the causal theory of knowledge, developed by Nozick, which says a belief only counts as knowledge if its connected to the fact in the right way - basically, if the fact weren't true, you wouldn't believe it. This fixes the barn case, because the man's belief isn't really hooked up to the actual barn-ness of the thing he's looking at, its just luck that he picked the real one. I find this pretty convincing, although I think it still runs into trouble with stuff like math, where its hard to say a mathematical fact 'causes' you to believe it."
