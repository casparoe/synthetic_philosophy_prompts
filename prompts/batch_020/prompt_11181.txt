I'm about a third of the way into a novel where a resurrected Nietzsche gets dragged into a present-day physics seminar on the arrow of time (theres a whole device-and-plot-mechanism for how he gets there, my critique group already gave me grief about it, don't ask). Before I write his half of the dialogue I want to actually work out what he'd think instead of just puppeting him into saying whatever I find cool, because the version of "eternal recurrence" everybody half-remembers from a college dorm-room conversation -- the universe just repeats itself on a loop, tape-recorder style -- isn't really the doctrine, and I don't want to write that flattened version.

Here's the actual thing I'm stuck on. The published eternal recurrence (the demon passage in Gay Science 341, the Zarathustra material) is mostly the ethical "can you affirm this life eternally" version, but Nietzsche also left an unpublished notebook argument -- finite force plus infinite time forces recurrence -- that's basically a crude draft of the recurrence theorem Poincaré proved properly the year after Nietzsche's collapse in Turin, so he came agonizingly close to the rigorous version without ever knowing it existed. What I want to know is what he'd make of everything downstream of that, which he obviously never got to see: David Albert's "Past Hypothesis" move, where physicists just posit a wildly low-entropy early universe as a brute law to explain why time even has a direction, and the Boltzmann brain problem, where the statistics say a random thermal fluctuation is way more likely to spit out a brain with false memories of a 13-billion-year history than for that history to have actually happened.

So would he read the Past Hypothesis as exactly the kind of comforting metaphysical story he spent his career being suspicious of, or does it basically hand him the structured, directional universe he wanted against the heat-death crowd? Is the Boltzmann brain scenario a weird demonic cousin of his own recurrence thought experiment, or does that framing completely miss what recurrence was supposed to do to a person, ethically speaking? I'd love a version of his likely take that's actually anchored in the doctrine as he wrote it -- the affirmation-of-life stakes, the running fight with Schopenhauer's pessimism, his allergy to any smuggled-in "purpose" -- rather than the pop-culture time-loop gloss.
