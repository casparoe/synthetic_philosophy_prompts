So I spent close to thirty years as a geophysicist doing seismic event classification - working out whether some signal on the network was a small quake, a mine blast, or something that needed a call to the folks who track that sort of thing for treaty verification. We fused readings from multiple stations using Dempster-Shafer theory: each station handed you a mass function over the hypotheses, and you combined them with Dempster's rule to get a combined belief and plausibility for "quake" vs "explosion" vs "who knows." It always felt like clever engineering, though our resident statistician used to grumble that it "wasn't really probability," and I never got her to spell out exactly what she meant by that.

Now that I'm retired I've been reading a lot more philosophy of science, and I recently worked through Walley's book on imprecise probabilities - lower and upper previsions, credal sets, coherence, all of that. On the surface it looks like the same idea I already know wearing a different coat: refusing to pin uncertainty down to one number. But the more I sit with it the less sure I am whether Dempster-Shafer is basically a special case of what Walley is doing, or whether it's actually a rival framework that only has a surface resemblence. My instinct as an old scientist is to just map the new thing onto whatever I already understand, and I have a feeling that habit is going to get me in trouble here.

Could you write me a genuinely careful comparison of the two? I'm not after the "both use intervals instead of a single number" level of comparison, I already get that part. What I'm after is: (1) is it actually true, in a precise mathematical sense, that Dempster-Shafer belief functions are a special case of the credal sets/coherent lower previsions Walley works with, or does the resemblance break down somewhere; and (2) the old fight over Dempster's rule of combination, which has a bad reputation for going haywire on conflicting sources (there's a famous example with two doctors disagreeing on a diagnosis that stuck with me from a training course years ago), versus the imprecise-probability crowd's insistence that combining independent evidence and conditioning on new information are just different operations that shouldn't be run together. Tell me which side of that fight you find more convincing, and whether it actually matters for someone in the field just trying to fuse noisy sensor data honestly, or if its more a dispute for the philosophers.
