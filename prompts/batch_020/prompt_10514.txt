So I had this whole argument with my friend Dana last night about whether you can actually "see" something that isn't there just because you were primed to expect it, or whether that's just a fancy excuse people trot out after they screw up -- she thinks its basically always the excuse. Turns out this isnt just a bar argument for me though: I sit on my hospital's radiology peer-review committee, and Friday we've got a real case in front of us -- a radiologist read an ambiguous shadow on a scan as a probable mass (patient ended up getting an unnecessary biopsy), right after the referring doctor's note said "strong clinical suspicion of malignancy, please review carefully." Half the committee wants this logged as a systemic issue, since handing radiologists that kind of loaded context before they even look at the images primes them to see what they're told to look for. The other half says a competent radiologist reads the image on its own merits and factors in history after, and if she couldn't do that, it's on her. Can you draft the short memo I'd actually bring to that meeting -- one real recommendation (discipline, retraining, changing what info radiologists get and when, or nothing) with the reasoning behind it, and give the "this is just how perception works" side a fair shake instead of just defaulting to "she should've been more careful"?
