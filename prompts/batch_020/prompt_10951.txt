Been down a total rabbit hole this week re-reading accounts of the classic Sperry/Gazzaniga split-brain experiments for a piece I'm working on — you know, the ones where a patient gets "HEART" flashed across the visual midline so the left half-field (which only the right hemisphere sees) gets "HE" and the right half-field (left hemisphere) gets "ART," and the patient says out loud that they saw "ART" while their left hand, asked to point at what was shown, calmly picks the card that says "HE," with the patient having no idea why they did that. I brought this up with a philosopher friend at dinner and we ended up in a proper argument, partly because I'd also just read about some newer, messier findings (a Dutch group, I think Pinto and colleagues?) suggesting some split-brain patients can still localize or compare stimuli across the two half-fields in ways the classic "two separate minds" story says shouldnt be possible. Neither of us could talk the other out of their view, so now I want to see the argument done properly.

Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers of mind standing there watching one of these tests live, who take the same evidence in opposite directions? One should argue that during tasks like this, the patient really does contain two distinct subjects of experience, each stuck with its own narrow window onto the world, and that our normal sense of being one unified self is something the intact brain actively produces rather than something the callosotomy merely interrupts. The other should argue that's too quick a move from disunified access and report to disunified experience — that there's still a single subject here, whose experience may be more integrated than any single lab task can reveal, with the surgery mainly cutting the machinery needed for verbal report and coordinated bimanual action rather than experience itself. I don't want two monologues talking past each other: make the "two subjects" philosopher answer for why the patient never behaves like two people at breakfast, and make the "one subject" philosopher answer for why the two hands can, in the moment, chase flatly incompatible goals like two wills that don't know about each other. And have them actually deal with the messier newer results instead of each just reaching for whichever old study flatters their side. They don't have to agree by the end, but I want it clear exactly what the remaining disagreement is about — empirical, conceptual, or both.
