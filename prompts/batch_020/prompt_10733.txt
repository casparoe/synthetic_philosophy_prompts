Last week a friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about whether total utilitarianism can even be formulated coherently once you take seriously the possibility that the population of morally relevant beings is infinite in size - a possibility that, as my friend kept reminding me, contemporary cosmology does not rule out. Their clinching move was to invoke the Riemann rearrangement theorem: a series that converges but does not converge absolutely can be made, purely by reordering its terms, to sum to any value whatsoever, or to diverge outright. On this basis they argued that "the sum of everyone's welfare" is not a well-defined quantity for an infinite population unless we are handed, on top of the profile of individual welfares, some further and entirely extra-ethical fact about the order in which those welfares are to be added up - and that this is not a mere curiosity but a fatal defect in any additive, aggregative approach to ethics. I objected that this smelled like an artifact of a badly chosen summation procedure rather than a deep problem for aggregative consequentialism as such, but I could not make that objection stick on the spot.

I would like your help settling this properly. First, construct a concrete toy example: specify an infinite population of individuals, each assigned some finite welfare level, such that the natural infinite sum of their welfares is conditionally convergent, and exhibit two different, equally natural ways of ordering or pairing off the same individuals' contributions that yield different totals - ideally one ordering that converges to a finite sum and another that diverges outright. Second, tell me what, if anything, you think this example actually shows about the prospects for total utilitarianism, or for aggregative consequentialism more broadly, once the population under consideration is infinite. Finally, assess whether any of the standard proposed remedies in this vicinity - for instance, restricting attention to absolutely convergent welfare streams, replacing direct summation with an overtaking or catching-up criterion, or abandoning cardinal aggregation altogether in favor of some other way of ranking infinite worlds - genuinely dissolves the underlying difficulty, or whether each one simply relocates the arbitrariness elsewhere, such as into the choice of a privileged spatial or temporal ordering of persons.
