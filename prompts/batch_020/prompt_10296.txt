I've recently been rereading Kierkegaard's *Two Ages* — specifically the long final section usually published in English as "The Present Age" — partly prompted by a discussion in my rationalist reading group, where someone drew an analogy between Kierkegaard's account of "leveling" and present-day talk of Moloch and egregores: the idea that a genuinely faceless, agentless collective can still exert real coercive pressure on individuals. It's a striking essay to revisit knowing it was written not long after Kierkegaard's own bruising experience of being publicly mocked by the satirical paper *The Corsair*, because his complaint about the press and "the public" turns out to be much more specific than a generic gripe about gossip or conformity: he argues that the press manufactures "the public" as a kind of phantom, a "monstrous abstraction" that can hold opinions and hound individuals while diffusing responsibility so thoroughly that no actual person is ever answerable for what "it" does. What strikes me is how this sits in real tension with Mill's *On Liberty*, published just over a decade later, which shares Kierkegaard's worry about social conformity and the "tyranny of the majority" but draws almost the opposite lesson: for Mill, the remedy for bad or oppressive opinion is not less publicity but more of it, since only the constant friction of open, free contestation keeps any orthodoxy — majority or minority — from calcifying into dead dogma.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between a Kierkegaardian critic of the press and "the public" and a Millian defender of a free and open public sphere, staged around a concrete contemporary case: a private remark, stripped of its context, that has just gone viral and triggered a fast-moving pile-on against the person who made it. The Kierkegaardian should argue that the very phenomenon of a converging "public opinion" about the case is corrosive almost independently of whether the underlying remark deserved criticism, precisely because no individual participant in the pile-on is answerable for the collective verdict it produces; the Millian should push back that suppressing or chilling this kind of open, messy reaction would be worse in the long run for both truth-tracking and liberty than tolerating its excesses. Please make sure each side directly engages with the other's strongest specific point rather than talking past it: the Kierkegaardian has to seriously address Mill's claim that free contestation, rather than silence, is the best available check on entrenched power and bad opinion, and the Millian has to grapple with the claim that "the public" is not actually identical to a set of responsible, opinion-holding individuals. Neither side should get the last, unanswered word.
