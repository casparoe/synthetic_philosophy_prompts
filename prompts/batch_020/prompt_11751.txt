For three years I have been writing a novel built around one irreversible structural choice. The narrator speaks to the reader in a steady, intimate first person from the opening page, dies roughly two-thirds of the way through the story, and is succeeded, without any announcement in the text, by another character who goes on narrating in her voice and her "I" as though nothing has happened; whether a reader ever consciously registers the handoff is left permanently ambiguous. Before sending the finished manuscript to agents, I gave it to the two people whose judgment about my work I have trusted most, and equally, for a decade: a friend from my MFA cohort who has since published two well-reviewed novels of her own, and the freelance editor who made my debut readable in the first place. Both read the whole book closely, both understood exactly what I was attempting, and both came back with careful, entirely sane, and flatly opposed verdicts on that one choice. My friend thinks the unmarked handoff is the reason the ending earns its power, and that signaling it anywhere would flatten it into a gimmick; my editor thinks the ambiguity is a cheat that retroactively hollows out everything the first narrator felt, and wants the switch clearly signaled a chapter before her death. Neither of them has been more right than the other about my previous two books, so I have no track record to use as a tiebreaker.

My own instinct sides with my friend, but a writer I described all this to over dinner said the honest response, given two people I trust that equally, is to treat the disagreement itself as real evidence that I am probably wrong, or at best to call it a wash, rather than quietly keeping the verdict I already wanted. That feels too deflating to accept outright: I know exactly why I made the choice, in a way neither of them can fully get inside of, and it seems like that should count for something. But I notice that "I understand my own reasons better than anyone else can" is presumably what everyone in my position tells themselves, including my friend and my editor, each of whom would say exactly the same thing about their own read of the manuscript. So: is my dinner companion right that the mere fact of this even split gives me a genuine reason to doubt my own sense of the ending? Or can I reasonably hold my ground here, and if so, on what grounds that would not equally license my friend and my editor to simply ignore each other?
