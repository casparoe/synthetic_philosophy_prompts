So this term I'm taking a jurisprudence seminar and we spent a big chunk of it on the Hart-Devlin thing -- the whole argument about whether law can legitimately enforce morality even when nobody's harmed -- and wierdly enough its now directly relevant to something on my actual campus. I'm the elected student rep on our university's Community Standards Board, and we've got a case that's basically a dorm-room version of the Wolfenden-era stuff: a sophomore has been running a paid subscription page where she posts content she films in her room. She's a legal adult, everyone involved is consenting, it's not illegal, and there have been zero harassment or safety complaints -- but a bunch of students found out and are grossed out, a couple of parents emailed the dean, and now people want her sanctioned under this old catch-all clause in our code that lets the Board punish "conduct seriously detrimental to the moral character of the campus community," completely independent of whether there's an identifiable victim.

The Board is split in a way that maps weirdly well onto the reading: half of us are basically just running the harm principle ("if it's legal, consensual, and nobody's hurt, it's none of our business"), and the other half are running something closer to Devlin's line that a community's shared sense of decency is part of what actually holds it together, and that a community is allowed to protect that even when no individual can point to a harm -- otherwise, as one guy put it, "communities have no moral standing of their own at all, they're just a bunch of separate individuals who happen to share a zip code." A philosophy grad student who TAs for us also pointed out that this exact clause was apparently used in the 70s to discipline unmarried couples for cohabiting, which the harm-principle side thinks proves the clause is just whatever the current majority happens to find icky, dressed up as a principle.

We vote next Wednesday. Could you write the memo I'd circulate to the Board beforehand? I want an actual recommendation -- sanction her, don't, or some third option like referring the clause itself for revision rather than applying it retroactively to her -- with a real timeframe attached to whatever you propose. And whichever way you come out, I don't want it to just wave the harm principle around and declare victory, or just wave "community cohesion" around and declare victory either -- it should engage seriously with the strongest version of whichever side it doesn't land on, not the weak version, before explaining why it lands where it does.
