I'm a bioethics PhD student serving as the ethics fellow on my hospital's psychiatric ethics committee. Our policy lets patients with recurrent mood disorders sign a psychiatric advance directive containing a "Ulysses clause": while stable, they pre-authorize involuntary hospitalization and medication for a future crisis and waive the right to have their in-the-moment refusal honored. Such clauses receive "substantial weight," but the policy never says what, if anything, could outweigh one. Two years ago, Ms. T, who has bipolar I disorder, signed one listing her own early warning signs of mania and stating that any refusal she gives once they appear "should not be treated as my genuine wish." She now shows exactly those signs, does not meet our state's imminent-danger threshold for civil commitment, and is lucid enough to argue her case: she insists she feels fine and wants to leave. The committee is split: some would honor the clause as the precommitment device it was written to be, likening it to constitutional rights entrenched against future majorities; others think the analogy fails, since Ms. T is a single person rather than a polity, and no signature should outweigh her present, coherent objection. Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before the committee reconvenes: recommend a concrete course of action with a timeframe, specify what would need to differ for the committee to decide otherwise in a future case, and engage seriously with whichever side you don't adopt.
