Here's the question she got:

"An AI safety team is debating two designs for how their assistant should handle ethically fraught requests. Design A gives the model a small set of hard, exception-less rules ('never do X, full stop'). Design B has the model estimate costs and benefits case-by-case and act on whichever choice maximizes expected value. (a) What's the strongest argument that Design B, even when it picks the better option in an individual case, is worse overall because of what it does to trust and predictability? (b) What's the strongest argument that Design A can produce outcomes that even a committed rule-follower should find unacceptable? (c) Rule utilitarianism is often pitched as a fix that captures the benefits of both. Explain the basic idea, and explain the 'rule worship' objection to it."

And here's what she wrote:

"(a) If the model is just doing expected-value math every time, its behavior stops being predictable to users and to other systems it interacts with -- people can't form reliable expectations about what it will or won't do, and that matters even when a given calculation is 'correct,' because a system that can always be argued into an exception is a system that can be argued into anything, including by bad actors constructing a persuasive-sounding cost-benefit case.

(b) The classic case is Kant's murderer at the door: your friend is hiding in your house and the murderer asks you point-blank where they are. Kant's own view was that since the murderer has forfeited their claim to the truth by asking in bad faith, you're permitted to lie here -- which shows that even Kant recognized hard rules need some flexibility once you actually work through a real case.

(c) Rule utilitarianism says: pick whichever rule's general adoption produces the most utility, then follow that rule even in cases where breaking it looks better in the moment. The 'rule worship' objection is basically the demandingness objection applied to rules: critics say that requiring an agent to follow a rule 'for the greater good' even when it costs them or others in a specific instance is just as psychologically unreasonable as requiring constant self-sacrifice under act utilitarianism -- so the fix doesn't remove utilitarianism's core problem, it just relocates it."
