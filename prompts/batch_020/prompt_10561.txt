Got into a long back-and-forth with a friend last night about imprecise probabilities and neither of us gave any ground. She thinks that when the evidence is genuinely thin -- her go-to case is an Ellsberg urn, where you know its got red and black balls in there but nothing about the proportion -- the honest response is to hold a whole range of credences, like 'somewhere between 20% and 80%,' instead of forcing out one exact number that pretends to know more than you do. I said fine, but the second you actually have to bet on the thing, that range has to turn into a decision, and I argued that any rule for acting on a range either leaves guaranteed money on the table (you get talked into turning down two bets, offered one after the other, that you'd obviously have taken together) or it quietly picks one number out of the range to act on, which just is a sharp credence wearing a costume. She said I was missing a third option: a rule where what's rational at the second bet gets to depend on what you already did at the first, without that counting as changing your mind for no reason. I couldn't tell if that's a real way out or the same problem with extra steps. Can you build the strongest version of her side -- an actual rule for acting on a range of probabilities that gets around both problems -- and walk it through a concrete pair of bets? Then be honest about whether it survives the obvious comeback: that it treats two choice-points differently even though the two situations are identical in everything that actually matters to the person choosing.
