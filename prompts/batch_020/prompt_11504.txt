I sit on the volunteer board of my neighborhood time bank, where members trade hours of service -- tutoring, elder care, home repairs -- using hour-credits instead of money. We have a liquidity problem: some members hoard credits while others run large deficits, so a motion before the board would let members lend surplus credits at interest (10 hours lent, 11 owed back within a year) to keep credits circulating rather than sitting idle. Our treasurer, a classics graduate, opposes this on explicitly Aristotelian grounds. She distinguishes exchange aimed at meeting real needs (oikonomia, in Aristotle's terms) from wealth-getting pursued as an end in itself (chrematistike), argues that the need-bounded kind is naturally self-limiting, and says lending at interest treats the credit -- meant only to measure and facilitate exchange -- as though it could breed more of itself, which is why Aristotle singles out interest as the most unnatural form of wealth-getting (the same shape of complaint a rationalist blog might frame as Goodhart's law). Other board members consider this an interesting historical footnote with no real force once you're not dealing with literal coinage. I need to circulate the board's decision memo before next month's vote: permit, cap, or prohibit interest-bearing loans of credits, with a concrete recommendation and a date for revisiting it. Please draft it, reconstructing the treasurer's argument well enough to actually test it against our liquidity problem, rather than politely acknowledging it and setting it aside.
