So for my Enlightenment political philosophy seminar we're doing a unit on Rousseau and this week's reading was the Letter to d'Alembert on the theatre, which none of us had even heard of before (we'd only done the Second Discourse and bits of the Social Contract). Its a way weirder and more specific text than I expected - like his case against theater isn't just "artifice bad, nature good," he actually does this whole close reading of Molière's Misanthrope where he complains that the play makes Alceste (the blunt, honest guy) look like a ridiculous fool while Philinte the smooth flatterer gets treated as the reasonable one, and he takes that as proof that theater in an already-corrupt society will always mock sincerity and reward hypocrisy, because that's what a corrupted audience finds charming. Then there's his argument about actors specifically, that getting paid to counterfeit passions you don't actually feel is basically a form of self-corruption, plus his alternative vision of Geneva-style civic festivals where you just plant a flower-crowned pole in the town square and get everyone dancing together so there's no separation between performer and audience at all.

My prof's discussion question for this week is whether any modern form of entertainment satisfies Rousseau's conditions better than 18th century French theater did, and my study group got kind of stuck arguing about livestreaming specifically (like Twitch or TikTok live, not social media in general). One friend thinks its basically the festival ideal because its low production, "ordinary" people, and the chat talks back in real time instead of everyone sitting silently in the dark. But I think that's missing the actual argument, since streamers are still very much performing a curated persona for money, and stream chats are infamous for mocking anyone who comes across as too sincere or earnest while rewarding whoever's got the smoothest ironic deflection, which sounds a lot like the Alceste/Philinte dynamic to me.

Could you help me actually work out what Rousseau's verdict on livestreaming would probably be, using the specific machinery of his real arguments (the actor's self-corruption, the Misanthrope critique, the festival ideal) instead of the generic "he loved authenticity and hated artifice" take everyone reaches for?
