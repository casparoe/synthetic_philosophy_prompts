The way my friend described it, Quine's move is to compare moral statements to what he calls "observation sentences" -- the sentences most directly hooked up to sensory stimulation, the ones that, through some long chain, let even really abstract physics face the tribunal of experience, but only holistically, as a corporate body rather than one sentence at a time. His argument (secondhand, so I might be mangling it) is that value statements have no analogue of an observation sentence anchoring them to anything, so moral discourse can never really face that tribunal the way scientific discourse does -- and that's supposedly why Quine didn't think ethics could be "naturalized" into a theory on a par with science, even though he stops well short of just saying moral sentences are meaningless like the old logical positivists would.

I pushed back that this seems to prove too much or too little: tons of ordinary empirical claims (about other minds, or history, or whatever) are pretty far removed from any observation sentence too, and we dont usually say THOSE can't count as knowledge, so why does ethics specifically get disqualified?
