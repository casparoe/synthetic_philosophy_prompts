Now retired after a long career in observational astronomy, I have been spending much of my time reading in the history and philosophy of science, a discipline I neglected almost entirely while working. One figure who has occupied me lately is Johannes Kepler, whom I had always known narrowly, through his three laws of planetary motion. It came as something of a surprise to learn how central astrology was to his actual working life: he held the post of Imperial Mathematician under Rudolf II, whose formal duties included producing the annual astrological prognostications that emperors and their generals expected of it; he cast a horoscope for the military commander Wallenstein that he was later asked to revisit, in a considerably darker direction; and in 1610 he wrote an entire treatise, Tertius Interveniens, positioning himself between skeptics who wanted astrology abolished outright and the almanac-astrologers he regarded as charlatans. What strikes me is how specific his own settled position seems to have been: he rejected the twelve zodiacal signs and the astrological houses as arbitrary human bookkeeping with no reality behind them, yet he continued to hold that the geometric aspects -- the angles the planets form with one another and with the sun, as seen from earth -- produce a genuine harmonic resonance in the human soul, continuous with the cosmic harmonies he later laid out in the Harmonices Mundi.

This has left me wondering how Kepler would judge astrology as it is actually practiced now. Consider one of the personalized astrology apps in wide use today: they draw on precise ephemeris data, ultimately grounded in the very orbital mechanics he discovered, to compute the exact aspects at the moment of a person's birth down to the minute -- yet the interpretive content they hang on all that precision is still built almost entirely around sun signs and houses, exactly the part of the edifice he dismissed as conventional rather than physically real. There is also the separate matter that, owing to axial precession, the sign such an app assigns someone frequently does not match the constellation the sun actually occupied at their birth, an objection Kepler was arguably better placed to evaluate than anyone alive today. Would he regard these apps as the legitimate, if commercialized, heir to the reformed astrology he defended in Tertius Interveniens, or as a relapse into precisely the sign-based superstition that treatise argued against? And, separately, how do you think he would receive the precession point: as a serious problem for astrology as such, or as an objection that a sufficiently careful astrology, including his own, was never really vulnerable to in the first place? I would be grateful if you could reconstruct his likely reasoning in some detail rather than simply delivering a verdict.
