I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but ever since debates about whether AI chatbots could be conscious started showing up in my feed, I've been reading about theories of consciousness on my own, mostly through the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy and some recorded lectures I found online. One theory that keeps coming up is the higher-order thought theory of consciousness: the idea that a mental state is conscious only if there is a further, separate mental state — a "higher-order thought" — representing the subject as being in it. I mentioned this to two friends from a small philosophy discussion group I joined, Leah and Devon, and the two of them ended up going back and forth on it for almost an hour. I've been turning their argument over in my head ever since, and I'd like a philosophically literate outsider's read on it, stage by stage.

Devon opened by objecting that the theory manufactures consciousness out of nothing: if he has a thought about a rock, that plainly does not make the rock conscious, so why should an otherwise-unconscious thought about an otherwise-unconscious mental state make that state conscious? He said the theory just asserts this transformation without ever explaining it. Leah answered that the comparison is unfair, because a rock was never a mental state to begin with, whereas the higher-order thought in question specifically represents its target as a state the thinker is currently in — exactly the feature a thought about a rock lacks, so the two cases are not actually parallel. Devon came back with a sharper version of the same worry: that very same self-directed representation can misfire. Suppose your visual system produces a sensation with all the qualities of red, but the higher-order thought represents you as sensing green instead — or, in a more extreme version, there is no color sensation there at all for the higher-order thought to be about. If what it's like for you is fixed purely by that higher-order content, he said, then in such cases your experience is being generated entirely out of a representation of a state that is not even there, which is a stranger result than the original rock case, since at least the rock exists. Leah replied that this shows nothing on its own: any theory that treats consciousness as a form of representation has to allow for misrepresentation somewhere, and ordinary perception produces hallucinations all the time without anyone concluding that perceptual consciousness is therefore impossible or unreal, so she did not see why the higher-order case deserved a stricter standard.

Neither of them budged, and I honestly cannot tell who came out ahead. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who had the better of it at each stage, rather than just giving an overall winner? I would also like to know whether there is some further consideration both of them are overlooking. I am especially curious whether Leah's second move actually survives once Devon raises misrepresentation in his third move, or whether it only delays the same problem.
