I spent about thirty-five years running a lab that studied cephalopod nervous systems -- octopus and cuttlefish mostly -- before I retired a few years back and started reading philosophy for fun (my wife says its a midlife crisis that showed up twenty years too late). Right now I'm chewing on the classic functionalist argument from multiple realizability: Putnam's old point that pain can't just be identical to some particular brain state, because pain in a human and "pain" in an octopus, or in some hypothetical silicon-based Martian, are realized in totally different physical stuff, so whatever pain is has to be defined by its causal role rather than its wiring. Having spent my career actually staring at octopus brains, which are genuinely alien things -- something like two-thirds of the neurons live out in the arms rather than the central brain -- I always found that example a little too quick and convenient, like it was doing alot of work for a thought experiment nobody had checked against real neuroscience.

So I went digging and found there's apparently a whole counter-literature on this, starting with a paper by Bechtel and Mundale, arguing that the multiple-realizability case is basically an artifact of sloppy bookkeeping: neuroscientists already use functional, psychological criteria to decide that, say, "the visual cortex" in a cat and a monkey count as the same structure, at some coarse grain, and then philosophers turn around and compare that same coarse psychological description against a much finer-grained physical description, which is what makes the physical side look so wildly disunified. I can follow the outline of that but I don't quite see how big a deal it's supposed to be if true, or how it connects to the other objection I keep half-remembering, from Kim, about pain becoming a "disjunctive" property that isn't really a scientific kind at all once you multiply realize it enough. Could you walk me through how these two objections relate to each other and to Putnam's original picture -- ideally with a concrete example of what "grain" even means here -- and then just tell me straight: does functionalism actually survive this, or was the octopus example always too good to be true?
