Ok so I'm putting together a handout for my phil of science seminar this week — we're doing the stats unit and I want something meatier than the cardboard-cutout version of the frequentist/Bayesian fight that everyone already half-knows ("one side uses priors, one doesn't," etc). My students have all vaguely heard of the replication crisis but most of them don't relaize how much of it is actually a fight about what "evidence" even means, and not just about p-hacking being naughty.

Could you write me a dialogue between a frequentist statistician and a Bayesian statistician who are both staring at Daryl Bem's old "Feeling the Future" precognition experiments — the ones that cleared p < .05 using completely standard methods, and that Eric-Jan Wagenmakers and co. later reanalyzed with Bayes factors, concluding the evidence for psi was pretty underwhelming once you looked at it that way. I want the frequentist to have a genuine, non-strawmanned case for why a run of independently significant results is doing real evidential work regardless of what the Bayes factor says, and I want the Bayesian to push back with actual substance — why the sampling plan/stopping rule shouldn't change what the data tell you (this is the likelihood principle stuff my students always find sort of spooky), and why a small p-value and a large likelihood ratio can come apart for principled reasons, and not just because the Bayesian rigged the prior. By the end I'd like them to have actually located the deepest layer of the disagreement — is evidence fundamentally about controlling error rates over hypothetical repetitions, or about the relative likelihood of the data under each hypothesis — rather than just restating their opening positions louder.

It doesn't need to resolve in favor of either side. Honestly a dialogue that ends in a kind of stalemate is fine, so long as its clear why they can't just split the difference — that's usually the part my students skip past too fast.
