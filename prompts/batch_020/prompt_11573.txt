In my upper-level seminar on the metaphysics of causation, we've moved past the standard preemption and trumping cases into the more specialized literature on causation by omission (sometimes discussed under the heading of "absence causation" more generally). Something about the material has been nagging me. Process (or "production") theories of causation -- the sort of view developed by Wesley Salmon, and refined by Phil Dowe into the "conserved quantity" theory -- analyze causation in terms of a continuous physical process connecting cause and effect, along which some conserved quantity (energy, momentum, charge) is transmitted. On the face of it, this seems to make it structurally impossible for an omission ever to count as a cause, since a mere absence cannot be the source of any such process. Yet ordinary causal judgment clearly treats at least some omissions as genuine causes rather than mere background conditions -- we don't hesitate to say, for instance, that a lifeguard's failure to notice a struggling swimmer caused the drowning. If we retreat to a purely counterfactual analysis to accommodate cases like this one (the effect wouldn't have occurred had the omitted act been performed), we seem to run straight into the opposite problem: for almost any bad outcome, indefinitely many agents' omissions will satisfy the relevant counterfactual, threatening to make omissive causation wildly over-generous. A classmate and I got into an argument about this after seminar last week and couldn't settle it between us.

What would actually help me is a single, moderately detailed example that brings out both horns of this tension at once, rather than the two or three stock cases (the unwatered plant, the inattentive lifeguard, the unsounded warning) that everyone in my class has already had drilled into them from the reading. Could you construct such a scenario -- specific enough that I could actually adjudicate the two theories against it -- in which (a) a committed process theorist is forced to deny that an intuitively obvious omission is a cause of the outcome, while (b) a naive counterfactual theorist is simultaneously committed to an implausible glut of further "causes" by omission for that very same outcome, implicating agents we would not ordinarily hold responsible at all? It would also help if you could say a little about how a more sophisticated counterfactual theorist might try to restrict the profusion of causes in (b) -- for instance, by appealing to norms of proper functioning, or to some default/deviance distinction -- without thereby collapsing back into simply denying that omissions are ever causes.
