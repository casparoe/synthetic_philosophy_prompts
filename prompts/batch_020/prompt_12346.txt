Here's the question they were given:

"In 'Logic and Conversation,' Grice discusses a case where A writes a job reference for a former student, Mr. X, who's applying for a philosophy post. The letter says only: 'Dear Sir, Mr. X's command of English is excellent, and his attendance at tutorials has been regular. Yours, etc.' (a) What does A implicate, and how is the implicature generated -- which maxim is in play and how is it being exploited? Is this implicature generalized or particularized, and why? (b) Grice holds that conversational implicatures are cancellable. Explain what that means using this example, and say what cancellability shows about the relationship between what's implicated and what the speaker means."

And here's the answer sitting in my queue:

"(a) What A actually says is only that Mr X has good English and turns up to tutorials -- nothing about whether he's any good at philosophy. But everyone reading a job reference expects it to speak to the applicant's suitability for the job, so by leaving out any comment on Mr X's philosophical ability, A gets across the further, unstated claim that Mr X isn't much good at philosophy. This is a conversational implicature rather than part of what is said, since it doesn't follow logically -- Mr X could in principle be a great philosopher and it would still be true that his English is excellent and he attends tutorials.

The maxim being violated is the first maxim of Quantity, 'be as informative as is required,' since A gives way less information than the situation calls for. Crucially he does this quietly -- he doesn't draw attention to the gap, and it's exactly because the violation is covert rather than announced that the reader has to do the work of figuring out why so little was said, which is where the implicature comes from. It's a particularized implicature rather than a generalized one, because it depends on the specific context of a job reference -- if A had written the exact same sentence about Mr X in a letter to a friend, with no job on the line, there'd be no implicature that he's a bad philosopher.

(b) Cancellability means an implicature can be called off without contradiction, unlike an entailment. So A could've added 'though his philosophical work is actually excellent too' and there'd be nothing contradictory about the letter, whereas he couldn't coherently write 'his attendance has been regular, though actually he never showed up' -- that's a straight contradiction. Because the implicature can be cancelled like this while the literal content can't, it shows that the implicature isn't really something A means or intends by his utterance -- it's more like a bonus inference the reader is free to draw or not, over and above whatever the speaker actually commits to. That's why Grice calls it merely 'implicated' rather than 'said': it belongs to the reader's interpretation rather than to the speaker's meaning."
