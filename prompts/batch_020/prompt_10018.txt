For an independent project in my AP Biology class, I read Gould and Lewontin's original 1979 "spandrels" paper and mentioned it to two friends, which somehow turned into a running disagreement about whether human musicality itself is a spandrel (an evolutionary byproduct) or a genuine adaptation. The argument spilled over into our group chat for several days, and I've tried to reconstruct it faithfully below. Neither of them budged, and I'd like an outside, informed opinion on who had the better of each exchange, not just who "won" overall.

(1) Maren opened by arguing that music is a byproduct rather than an adaptation: it doesn't reliably improve survival or reproduction on its own, and is more economically explained as a pleasant side effect of faculties we were already selected for, namely language, auditory scene analysis, motor planning, and the reward system. She compared it to Steven Pinker's description of music as "auditory cheesecake," a happy accident that piggybacks on capacities built for other purposes.

(2) Owen replied that music is universal across every documented culture, emerges spontaneously in infancy well before any formal exposure, and reliably engages ancient reward circuitry, and that these are exactly the signatures biologists use to diagnose genuine adaptations elsewhere. Waving all of that away as coincidence, he said, looks less like parsimony and more like refusing to count evidence that's inconvenient for the byproduct story.

(3) Maren answered that universality proves nothing on its own, since it's equally consistent with a universal byproduct of cognitive machinery every human already has. As for the reward circuitry, she said that's exactly what you'd expect if music were parasitizing systems built for something else entirely, the way a virus hijacks a cell's existing machinery rather than building its own.

(4) Owen countered that this is precisely the situation Gould and Vrba's own follow-up work was designed to handle: once a byproduct gets co-opted for a new use, their taxonomy calls it an "exaptation" rather than a mere spandrel, and nothing then stops it from being further shaped by selection for that new role. So even by the spandrel camp's own framework, Maren couldn't just point to music's origin and call the debate settled; what matters now is whether music does real, selection-relevant work today, such as cementing group coordination or signaling mate quality.

(5) Maren shot back that "cementing group coordination" and "signaling mate quality" are exactly the kind of untestable, after-the-fact adaptive storytelling the original spandrel critique was written to block. You can always invent some plausible-sounding current function for a trait, she said, and that's precisely the move Gould spent his career warning against.

(6) Owen said that charge is out of date: there's real physiological evidence for the coordination story (synchronized singing and music-making measurably raise pain thresholds and social bonding, a proxy for endorphin release, in ways that haven't been clearly documented in our closest primate relatives), and even committed selectionists who feuded openly with Gould, like Daniel Dennett, have argued that the original spandrel paper overstated just how untestable adaptationist hypotheses generally are.
