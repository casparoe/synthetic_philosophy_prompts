I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind, and this week's session was devoted to David Chalmers's zombie argument against physicalism. The discussion took an interesting turn when one of my strongest students raised what struck the whole room as a rather decisive empirical rebuttal. Her reasoning ran roughly as follows: cognitive neuroscience has made genuine progress in identifying neural correlates of consciousness -- global workspace integration, thalamocortical binding, and so on -- so it is now a live empirical hypothesis that any being physically identical to a conscious person, right down to those correlates, would, as a matter of natural law, also be conscious. On her view, this means the zombie scenario isn't just far-fetched; it is actually being ruled out by what we are learning about the brain, so Chalmers's conceivability argument is quietly being overtaken by cognitive neuroscience even as philosophers keep debating it in seminar rooms like ours.

I have to admit her argument struck me as more persuasive than I expected, and several other students seemed to feel the same way: a few asked, reasonably enough, why the zombie thought experiment is still treated as a live issue in the philosophy of mind literature if empirical science is closing in on an answer to the very question it poses. Before I tell the class next week that Chalmers's argument has essentially been overtaken by neuroscience, and explain the remaining philosophical resistance to that conclusion as a kind of disciplinary conservatism, I would like to make sure that is actually the right way to frame things. Could you help me work out whether the neuroscience genuinely does the argumentative work my student thinks it does against the conceivability argument, and, if it doesn't, what exactly it fails to establish?
