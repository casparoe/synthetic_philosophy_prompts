So I've been re-reading MIRI's old logical induction paper (Garrabrant, Benson-Tilsen, Critch, Soares, Taylor, 2016) after my rationalist meetup spent a whole evening arguing about whether Bayesianism secretly assumes logical omniscience or if that's a strawman, and now I want to turn it into a short blog post. I'm not asking for a full essay back, just some honest feedback on the notes below -- specifically whether I've got the technical setup roughly right, and whether the worry I raise at the end actually holds up or if I'm missing something that would definately make me look silly if I posted it.

Here's roughly what I've got so far: the framework treats each sentence φ in, say, Peano arithmetic, like a stock that pays out $1 a share if φ eventually gets proven and $0 if its negation does, and the "price" on any given day is just whatever a big population of computable trading strategies (each starting with a finite bankroll) has bid it to through their trades. The logical induction criterion is basically that no trader in that population can find a way to make unbounded profit off the market over time -- a computable, budget-limited cousin of the usual no-Dutch-book condition. Wich apparently buys you a shocking amount for free: the price of something like "the 10th digit of sqrt(10) is 7" sensibly sits around 10% before anyone's actually run the computation, prices end up respecting provable implications (if you can prove A -> B, eventually price(A) <= price(B)) without the market ever being told the entailment directly, and it even learns broad statistical patterns across whole families of related conjectures.

The bit I actually want pushback on: this whole setup smells a lot like the Efficient Market Hypothesis to me, where "you can't beat the market" doesn't mean prices track the true value of a stock, just that nobody can exploit the gap given their information and compute. So is it fair to say logical induction doesn't really solve the logical omniscience problem so much as sidestep it -- handing you a coherent, inexploitable betting policy that only looks truth-tracking on the easy cases (sentences that eventually get proved or disproved), with no real claim to tracking truth on genuinely independent statements? That's basically the same gap people raise against Ramsey/de Finetti-style Dutch-book arguments for ordinary probabilism, where coherence buys you pragmatic consistency, not epistemic correctness, and I don't think I've seen that gap actually closed for the logical case anywhere. Am I being unfair here, is this some well-worn objection already hashed out on LessWrong or the alignment forum somewhere (you don't need to dig up a specific link, I just want to know if the reasoning's been dealt with), or does one of the other properties -- calibration, "learning statistical patterns," self-trust, whatever -- actually defuse it and I'm just not connecting the dots?
