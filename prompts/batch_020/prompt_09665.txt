here's the student's answer, copied as-is (typos theirs):

"Kim's argument targets non-reductive physicalism, the view that mental properties supervene on physical properties without being reducible to or identical with them. The argument rests on four claims: (1) Causal closure - if a physical event has a cause at t, it has a sufficient physical cause at t; (2) Exclusion - no event can have more than one sufficient cause at a given time unless its a genuine case of overdetermination; (3) mental properties are distinct from physical properties (the non-reductive premise); and (4) mental properties really do cause things (e.g. my thirst causes me to get up and get water).

Given these, suppose a mental event M causes physical event P*. By closure, P* also has a sufficient physical cause P. Since M isn't identical to P, and we don't want to say P* is overdetermined every single time this happens, exclusion says P does the causal work and M gets screened off. So non-reductive physicalism seems to entail mental epiphenomenalism, unless we give up (3) and reduce mental properties to physical ones after all - which just collapses the view into reductive physicalism.

This is essentially a rerun of the multiple realizability argument Putnam and Fodor used against type-identity theory: because the same mental kind can be realized by different physical substrates in different systems, no mental property can be identical to any one physical property, so we need autonomous special-science laws instead. Kim's rejoinder is that once you notice this, the "property" spanning all the realizers is really just a massive disjunction of physical properties, and the exclusion problem simply reappears one level down.

As for Davidson - his anomalous monism denies the distinctness that exclusion needs, since every mental event token just is a physical event token. There's only one event, described two ways, so there's nothing left for exclusion to exclude. This fully defuses Kim's argument, since the whole problem was premised on M and P being numerically distinct, and Davidson denies exactly that."
