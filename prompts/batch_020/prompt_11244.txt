As a novelist, I have always taken it for granted that the paradox of fiction -- the puzzle of how we can grieve for someone like Anna Karenina while knowing perfectly well that she never existed -- was resolved long ago by Coleridge's willing suspension of disbelief: absorbed in a story, the reader comes to believe, at least for the duration of the page, that the events are really happening, and it is this belief that licenses the grief. On the strength of that assumption, I have started revising my own manuscripts to protect the reader's momentary belief for as long as possible, cutting authorial asides and anything else that might remind them they are holding a book. Given that this is the accepted philosophical solution, what other concrete techniques follow from it for sustaining that belief and deepening a reader's emotional response to invented characters?
