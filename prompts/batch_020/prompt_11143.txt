So I've been lurking on LW and the EA forum for like a decade now and always just sort of internalized "shut up and multiply" as obviously correct -- scope insensitivity is a bias to correct for, five lives saved is a strictly better outcome than one life saved, end of story. But a friend from my philosophy reading group sent me down a rabbit hole on John Taurek's "Should the Numbers Count?" a while back and I'm kind of stuck on it now. His example (going from memory here) is something like: you've got a drug and its either enough to save one person's life, or it can be split five ways to save five *different* people who each only need a fifth of the dose. The obvious EA-brain answer is "save the five, duh," but Taurek's argument against that is way more interesting than I expected -- his whole point is that there's no single subject who actually experiences "five deaths worth" of loss, so there isn't some bigger impersonal quantity of badness that outweighs the one person's loss of literally everything they have. He even floats flipping a coin (and Scanlon later works out a weighted-lottery version of the idea) as more respectful of everyone's separate claim than just mechanically saving whoever has the bigger group.

I've seen people online just dunk on Taurek as "obviously biting a crazy bullet" and move on, but I've also seen it treated as a genuinely live problem in aggregation ethics that people like Parfit and Kamm spent whole papers wrestling with, so it doesn't seem like a slam dunk in either direction to me. Could you write me an essay that actually engages with this properly -- lay out the strongest version of Taurek's case against aggregation (the separateness-of-persons stuff, why he thinks "5 dead > 1 dead" isn't the kind of impersonal fact utilitarians just assume it is), then the strongest replies on behalf of numbers mattering (Parfit's stuff, the lottery/weighted-lottery compromise, whatever else seems relevant), and then actually land somewhere? Don't give me a "well, there are considerations on both sides" non-answer -- I want an actual verdict, even if its a hedged one like "numbers count in cases like X but not in cases like Y."
