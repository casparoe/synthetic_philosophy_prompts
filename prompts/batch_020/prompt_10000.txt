Question: (a) State the Condorcet Jury Theorem and the assumptions needed for it to hold. (b) Suppose a jury of 3 people each independently has a 0.7 probability of voting for the correct verdict, and the jury decides by majority rule. Compute the probability that the jury reaches the correct verdict. (c) Real juries deliberate rather than voting independently and in isolation. Explain what this means for whether the theorem's guarantee applies to real juries.

Student answer: "(a) The theorem says that if you have a group of voters who are each independently more likely than not to pick the correct answer between two options, then as the group gets bigger, the probability that a majority vote lands on the correct answer approaches 1. The two assumptions are (i) independence - no voter's vote affects or is affected by anyone else's, and (ii) competence - each voter does better than a coin flip, i.e. p > 0.5. This is basically Condorcet's version of the wisdom of crowds, and it's related to his other famous result, the Condorcet paradox, which is part of why we want juries to be reasonably large - to smooth out the kind of cyclical inconsistency the paradox identifies. (b) P(majority correct) = P(exactly 2 correct) + P(all 3 correct) = [3 x 0.7^2 x 0.3] + 0.7^3 = 0.441 + 0.343 = 0.784. So the 3-person jury is right about 78% of the time, which is higher than any individual member's 70%. (c) Deliberation means jurors influence each other, which breaks the independence assumption. Since the theorem depends on independence to get that convergence-to-1 result, real deliberating juries will be systematically less accurate than the theorem predicts - which is actually a pretty strong argument for polling jurors privately and combining their votes mechanically instead of letting them deliberate at all."
