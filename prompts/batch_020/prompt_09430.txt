Last spring, a close friend of mine, Elena, started spending several nights a week at my house because her stepfather has a serious temper and things at home had become unpredictable enough that she no longer felt safe there. Elena and I are both juniors, and my parents quietly agreed to let her stay without making it official with anyone else, since we worried that drawing outside attention to the situation might make things worse for her rather than better. About three weeks into this arrangement, her stepfather called my cellphone directly and asked, point-blank, whether I had seen her recently. Without much hesitation, I told him no, that I hadn't seen her in over a week, even though she was sitting on my bed only a few feet away as I said it.

I mentioned all this to a couple of friends afterward, and one of them raised a point that has stuck with me since: she argued that I didn't need to state an outright falsehood in order to protect Elena, since saying something like "you'd have to ask her yourself" or simply declining to answer would have kept her stepfather just as much in the dark, without my having to assert something I knew to be false. That distinction feels almost like a technicality to me, a way of resolving the dilemma too neatly, but I haven't been able to explain why it shouldn't count. So I'd like help thinking through two connected questions. First, is there an actual moral difference between telling a flat lie and merely withholding the truth or being deliberately vague, given that both options were fully available to me and would have produced exactly the same protective outcome? Second, since my lie seems to have harmed no one and clearly helped my friend, was there really anything wrong with telling it, or does a lie like that simply stop counting as a moral failing once it "works out" for the better?
