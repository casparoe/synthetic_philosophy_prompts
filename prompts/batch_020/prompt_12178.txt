I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I co-run a small podcast where graduate students interview visiting speakers the evening before their colloquium talk. Next month's guest is a philosopher who has built her reputation defending a strong version of cognitive penetrability: the claim that a perceiver's antecedent beliefs, desires, and expectations can alter the content and phenomenal character of perceptual experience itself, rather than merely biasing attention, memory, or the judgments formed afterward. Whenever an interviewer presses one of her showcase examples -- fear making a hill look steeper, a good throw making the target look bigger, a face coming to look angry once you already suspect its owner is angry -- and suggests this might be a confound rather than a genuine change in perception, she tends to retreat to some version of "the phenomenology settles it." Our reading group has spent the term working through the literature that grew out of Fodor's and Pylyshyn's encapsulation picture, and for this interview I'd like to get past that stalemate rather than reproduce it.

Could you draft eight to ten questions that make the "phenomenology settles it" retreat progressively harder to sustain? Please include at least one question, plus a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, on each of the following: first, how she rules out that her showcase cases are really attention, memory, or post-perceptual judgment effects rather than perceptual ones, given that this is exactly what Firestone and Scholl's methodological critique of the "top-down effects" literature claims is going on; second, whether predictive-processing models of perception as inference from priors trivialize her thesis by making some top-down shaping of content just what perception is, and if not, where she wants to draw the line between a merely "perceptual" prior and a fully cognitive one; third, the epistemic-downgrade worry that if a prior belief can shape what you visually experience, and that altered experience is then treated as fresh evidence for the belief, her view licenses exactly the kind of self-confirming perceptual "evidence" that makes biased threat-perception so hard to correct; and fourth, what is actually at stake in resolving the dispute one way rather than the other -- whether something substantive about the modularity of mind or the objectivity of observation hangs on it, or whether the disagreement is largely terminological. Please close with a question asking what specific pattern of results, if it came in tomorrow, she would accept as evidence against cognitive penetrability, since her published replies tend to treat every proposed counterexample as one more confound to be explained away rather than a genuine test of the view.
