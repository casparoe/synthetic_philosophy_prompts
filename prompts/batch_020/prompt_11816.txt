I'm doing an independent study this term on formal epistemology, and part of the requirement is a short discussion note on a technical result of my choosing. I picked logical induction (the Garrabrant et al. framework for assigning probabilities to mathematical statements before they've been proved or refuted), since the reading group I'm part of has already worked through the main construction and theorems, so I don't need to motivate the basics. Before extending this into a full write-up, I drafted the paragraph below, which tries to press on what I think is an underappreciated gap between the informal "no efficient trader can Dutch-book this reasoner" slogan that gets attached to the result and what the non-exploitability criterion actually delivers.

Here's the paragraph as it stands: "Logical induction is often summarized as showing that a reasoner can maintain reasonable credences about undecided mathematical statements, because no efficiently computable trading strategy can exploit its sequence of prices for unbounded profit. I don't think this framing supports the confidence people place in it. The criterion only forbids a trader's total profit from diverging to infinity across the whole infinite sequence of days; it imposes no cap on how large that profit may be on any particular finite day, and no requirement on how many days must pass before a given mispricing gets corrected. A trader could therefore extract an arbitrarily large one-time profit using a fixed, efficiently computable strategy, or slowly milk a shrinking-but-persistent mispricing for an arbitrarily long finite stretch, and the belief sequence would still qualify as a perfectly good logical inductor, since none of this amounts to the trader's profit literally going to infinity. So what 'no efficient trader can Dutch-book this reasoner' actually rules out is just unbounded-as-time-goes-to-infinity exploitation, which is fully compatible with the reasoner being badly and persistently miscalibrated, relative to a strategy we could write down today, for as long as anyone is likely to be around to consult it."

I'd like your comments on a few things: first, whether I've characterized the logical induction criterion correctly, or whether I'm missing some feature of the trader or market setup that actually blocks the scenario I describe; second, whether this point is substantively the same as the "no guaranteed convergence rate" limitation that's sometimes flagged as an open problem for logical induction, or whether it's making a sharper or different claim than that; third, what the strongest reply on behalf of logical induction would be, if there is one; and finally, whether the paragraph itself reads clearly or could make its point more economically.
