Until a few months ago I had never heard of the diary Frege kept for a few weeks in 1924 -- discovered only decades after his death, and full of anti-Semitic and anti-democratic political reflections. It came up almost incidentally in a reading group connected to my dissertation on his philosophy of logic, and it has unsettled me ever since. This term I am grading the take-home portion of our department's qualifying exam for the first time, and one of the questions happens to bear directly on this same material, which makes me concerned that my own recent reaction to the diary might color how generously or harshly I read the answers. Before I assign a grade, I would like a disinterested outside assessment of the response below.

The question was: "A diary Frege kept for a short period in 1924, not published until long after his death, contains anti-Semitic and anti-democratic reflections, including sympathy for dictatorial rule and contempt for the Weimar constitution he lived under. Does this diary give us any reason to revise our assessment of Frege's mature philosophical views, in particular his rejection of psychologism and his logicist project? Defend your answer with reference to specific features of his philosophy, and address at least one consideration on the other side."

Here is the answer I need graded:

"The discovery of Frege's 1924 diary was a genuine scandal in the historiography of analytic philosophy: it shows a man who wanted Jews stripped of political rights or expelled from Germany altogether, admired dictatorial rule, and detested the parliamentary institutions of the Weimar Republic he was living under just as the Nazi party was consolidating the popular support that would soon carry it to power. When the diary was eventually published, Michael Dummett, Frege's most influential twentieth-century interpreter, wrote that it forced him to ask whether he could go on admiring a thinker capable of such views.

I think Dummett is right to keep the two apart, though not simply because condemning the diary while praising the logic would be a version of the genetic fallacy: inferring the falsity of a claim from a discreditable fact about the person who asserted it. The deeper point is that Frege's central technical achievements -- the concept-script, the definition of number in terms of extensions of concepts, the reduction of arithmetical truth to logical truth -- are topic-neutral in a way his politics are not: anyone can check their validity regardless of what they think about the Reichstag.

Where things get harder is Frege's anti-psychologism. He returns to this theme throughout his career, but it is in the Grundgesetze that he first develops the full picture: because logical laws must be objective and mind-independent, thoughts (Gedanken) cannot be identified with private mental episodes, so they must belong to what he calls a 'third realm', distinct from both the physical world and the inner world of individual sensation. It is tempting, and some commentators, including Hans Sluga, have taken this route, to see a family resemblance between this hostility to the merely subjective and Frege's hostility to individual political rights and liberal pluralism: both elevate an impersonal, hierarchical order over the standpoint of the individual. Even granting more than a biographical coincidence here, though, this would show only that the metaphysics and the politics spring from the same cast of mind, not that the third-realm picture is false."
