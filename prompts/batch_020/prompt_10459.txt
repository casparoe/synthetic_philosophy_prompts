I'm a philosophy PhD student working in formal epistemology. During a recent session of our decision theory reading group on Gibbard and Harper's "Death in Damascus" case, two of the senior students got into a real dispute about what the case actually establishes. Quick reminder of the setup: a man in Damascus is confronted by Death, a perfect predictor of where the man will be tomorrow; whichever city he ends up in, Death has already arranged to be there too, so on the standard reading he dies whether he stays in Damascus or flees to Aleppo.

(1) Elena opened by arguing the case is worse for causal decision theory than Newcomb's problem ever was. In Newcomb, two-boxing is ratifiable -- it would still maximize expected utility even if you were certain you were about to do it -- because two-boxing causally dominates regardless of the predictor's forecast. In Death in Damascus, she said, neither pure act is ratifiable: getting confident you'll stay raises your credence that Death is in Damascus, which makes staying look bad, and the mirror-image happens for fleeing, so CDT-plus-ratifiability just cycles and delivers no verdict at all.

(2) Sam answered that this only refutes ratifiability among pure acts. Once the agent is allowed to randomize, some specific mixing probability is a fixed point of exactly the destabilizing dynamic Elena described, so it is ratifiable; CDT survives, and the case just shows that randomizing can be rationally mandatory, which is surprising but not incoherent.

(3) Elena replied that the fix only works if Death, despite being stipulated as a perfect predictor, somehow cannot predict the outcome of the randomizing device itself. If Death can, mixing buys nothing: whatever probability you pick, Death conditions on that very device and matches you with the same certainty as any pure act, so the "fixed point" is merely tied with the alternatives, not better than them.

(4) Sam came back with the point that standard defenses of two-boxing already tolerate a predictor who can't foresee last-second randomization, so it seems inconsistent to treat that assumption as illegitimate here but not there.

(5) Elena's last word was that the two cases aren't parallel. Two-boxing is ratifiable purely by dominance, in every state, regardless of anything about randomizers, so that assumption is never actually invoked in defending it -- whereas the mixed act in Death in Damascus is ratifiable only because of it. The assumption is doing real work in one case and none in the other.

Neither of them would concede the point, and I don't know this literature well enough to referee it myself. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of each one, not just who ends up ahead -- including whether either of them reaches a correct conclusion for the wrong reason? I'd also like your take on what, if anything, both of them are missing, and on what it would really take for the mixed-strategy defense to succeed.
