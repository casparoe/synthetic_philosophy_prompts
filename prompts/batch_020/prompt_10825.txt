In my high school's economics elective, we each have to write a short independent piece on a game-theory topic that goes beyond what's covered in the textbook, and I chose cooperative bargaining after our teacher mentioned, almost as an aside, that the Nash bargaining solution is not the only serious way to model how two rational parties should split a surplus. Digging further on my own, I came across the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, which was apparently proposed specifically as a rival to Nash's. I can see that the two agree on several axioms (Pareto optimality, symmetry, and invariance to how you rescale utility), but that they part ways on exactly one point: Nash's solution preserves independence of irrelevant alternatives at the cost of individual monotonicity, while Kalai–Smorodinsky's preserves monotonicity at the cost of independence of irrelevant alternatives. What I still don't have a good feel for is why anyone should actually care about that trade-off in practice -- that is, what's really lost if you give up either axiom.

Could you write me a dialogue between two game theorists -- one a committed defender of the Nash bargaining solution, the other of the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution -- who have been called in, half-jokingly, to help two co-founders who can no longer agree on how to divide the future profits of the company they built together? I would like each theorist to defend their own solution and go after the other's, ideally by each constructing a concrete change to the founders' set of possible deals that makes their own axiom look indispensable and makes the rival axiom look like it licenses an unfair or irrational recommendation. Please make the two theorists equally sharp, so that neither one is a pushover or misses an obvious counterargument, and have them genuinely engage with each other's specific examples and objections rather than talking past one another by simply repeating their own side's case.
