I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, and together with a few other students in my program I run a colloquium series in which we invite a speaker for a talk followed by an extended audience Q&A. Next month's guest is a philosopher who has built a reputation defending a strong, ontological version of emergentism: on his view, certain higher-level properties in biology and neuroscience — and perhaps consciousness above all — involve genuinely new causal powers that are not fixed by the arrangement and interactions of their underlying physical parts, and not merely hard for us to predict or derive in practice. I've sat through two of his talks already, and I know from experience that if we let the conversation drift, it settles into a comfortable rehearsal of multiple realizability and the claim that "physics doesn't have the last word," without ever quite meeting the sharpest objections in the literature.

Could you draft eight or nine questions for the session that push past a restatement of his position? I want at least one question that presses on how his account is supposed to escape Jaegwon Kim's causal-exclusion problem: if the physical level is already causally closed and sufficient to produce every physical effect, what causal work is left for an emergent property to do that doesn't amount to either duplicating that work through systematic overdetermination, or being epiphenomenal? I want another that tests whether his usual examples of multiple realizability really establish new causal powers in the world, or only show that a higher-level description resists being matched one-to-one with any single physical kind — which sounds like a weaker, more epistemic claim than the one he actually needs. And since he invokes Anderson's "More Is Different" constantly as backing, I'd like a question that makes him locate his own view on the spectrum between Anderson's fairly modest point — that deriving higher-level regularities from lower-level laws is impractical, not impossible in principle — and the much stronger metaphysical claim he wants to defend.

Finally, close with a question, in the spirit of asking what would change his mind, about what kind of empirical or theoretical finding — as opposed to one more failure of derivation from a lower level — would actually count as evidence against his position. For each question, please add a short follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give, the way a good moderator would if he tried to sidestep the hardest part of the question.
