I'm drafting a scene for a novel set in the last years of Émilie du Châtelet's life, not long after she completed the private essay she called her Discours sur le bonheur, and I'd like a genuine philosophical exchange rather than a mere summary of her position. In the scene she defends her claim that happiness depends on cultivating strong passions and on protecting our illusions—in love, in ambition, in the hope of glory—against a visiting Stoic philosopher who argues that no passion or illusion can be trusted, and that only a mind purged of both can attain durable tranquility. Write their dialogue so that each presses the other on the concrete costs of their position—what is lost, psychologically or practically, by suppressing the passions, and what is risked by knowingly indulging illusions—and make sure neither side is allowed a final, uncontested word.
