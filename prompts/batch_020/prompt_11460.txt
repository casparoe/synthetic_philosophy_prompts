I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm drafting a feature on why people routinely turn down information that is free, relevant to a decision they are already making, and available just for the asking — genetic test results, prognoses, even unopened financial statements during a downturn. The spine of the piece is a result I hadn't encountered before this year: I.J. Good's 1967 theorem on the value of information, which decision theorists treat as close to a proof that this kind of refusal is a mistake. I ran an early draft of the key section past a statistician friend, who confirmed I had the mathematics right but said the philosophical stakes were "above his pay grade" and that I should find a philosopher instead. Below is that section, more or less as it stands. I'd be grateful for a careful read before it goes to my editor, because I'm asking the theorem to do real argumentative work and I don't want to either overstate what it proves or spend six hundred words demolishing a position nobody actually holds.

I.J. Good spent the Second World War as one of Alan Turing's statisticians at Bletchley Park and much of the rest of his life as a working Bayesian, which perhaps explains why, in 1967, he published three pages that seem to settle a question moral philosophers still argue over as though it were open. What is now called Good's theorem says this: take an expected-utility maximizer with a fixed menu of possible actions and a prior over the states of the world on which their payoffs depend. Offer this person a free, costless glimpse of some piece of evidence before they must choose, on the understanding that they will update by ordinary Bayesian conditionalization and remain free to act however the evidence moves them. Then looking is never worse than not looking, in expectation, and is strictly better unless the evidence could never have changed what they were going to do anyway. You cannot be harmed, by your own lights, by free truth. Good offered the proof as a way of deriving something Carnap's inductive logic had needed to assume outright: that a rational inquirer should always want the total evidence.

Set that next to a fact from genetic counseling. Direct testing for Huntington's disease has been possible since the gene was located in 1993: a single blood draw tells an at-risk adult, with certainty, whether they will develop a fatal neurodegenerative disease, usually in mid-life, for which there is still no cure and no treatment that delays onset. The test is close enough to free, it is squarely decision-relevant — it bears on whether to have children, what to tell them, how to plan a career or a retirement that may not happen — and it is about as clean as evidence gets. And the great majority of people eligible for it decline, a finding that has held since testing began and that no longer surprises genetic counselors, however much it surprised the clinicians who designed the original protocols expecting high demand.

There are two ways to describe what the decliners are doing, and the piece I want to write turns on keeping them apart. The first says they are simply wrong: whatever their reasons, they are doing exactly what Good proved a rational agent has no reason to do. The second says they are not violating the theorem at all, because the theorem only ever governs a utility function fixed over the outcomes of one's actions — and for these people, knowing is itself among the outcomes. Twenty years lived under an unresolved coin flip may have a different expected shape, for many people a worse one, than twenty years lived under a definite sentence, quite apart from anything the sentence would let them do differently. Economists who model "anticipatory feelings" formally — Caplin and Leahy's psychological expected-utility theory is the standard reference — take exactly this route, folding beliefs themselves into the utility function so that dread becomes something a rational agent can be maximizing against.

I find the second story more humane and probably closer to right, but I can't shake the sense that it buys its humanity too cheaply. If any observed refusal of free evidence can be redescribed, after the fact, as evidence of a cost that was there all along, then Good's theorem stops predicting anything: it becomes a piece of bookkeeping rather than a constraint on rational behavior. So the question I want to leave the reader with is not "were the decliners irrational?" but "what would it even take for that question to have a determinate answer" — and I don't yet have one myself.

What I'd most value from you: first, a check on whether I've stated Good's theorem, and its scope conditions, accurately enough that a decision theorist wouldn't wince. Second, your honest reaction to the closing worry — is the "theorem becomes bookkeeping" problem a real threat to expected-utility theory, a well-worn point I'm dressing up as fresher than it is, or is there a principled way to distinguish legitimately enlarging the outcome space from simply rescuing the theory by fiat? Third, whether Huntington's disease is earning its place as the essay's central case or is too singular a data point to hang an argument on. And finally, the blunter question: does the philosophical payoff in the last two paragraphs justify the technical setup that precedes it, for a reader who is never going to check the math?
