For the last couple of months I've been doing part-time research assistance for a small effective-altruism-aligned organization that studies long-term cooperation problems, and my current assignment is to draft an internal primer on "evidential cooperation in large worlds" (ECL). The core idea, which traces back to the researcher Caspar Oesterheld's work on multiverse-wide cooperation via correlated decision-making, is roughly this: if the universe (or multiverse) is sufficiently large or infinite, it almost certainly contains enormous numbers of causally disconnected agents who arrive at their decisions through relevantly similar reasoning processes. Because my choosing to act cooperatively — giving real weight to the values of agents very different from myself, rather than acting solely on my own — is evidence about what such agents decide on their own, proponents argue that evidential (or "superrational") decision-making recommends cooperating with the whole ensemble, even though no causal signal, trade, or communication ever passes between any of the parties involved.

While drafting this primer, I've also been finishing a seminar paper on Leibniz's Monadology for my philosophy degree, and a parallel has been nagging at me ever since I reread its later sections. Leibniz's monads are explicitly "windowless": no causal influence passes between them at all. Yet their internal states unfold in perfect correlation across the entire universe, because God, in selecting this world as the best of all possible worlds, pre-established a harmony among every monad's history from the very outset. It is hard to shake the feeling that Leibniz had already sketched the logical shape of ECL's problem three centuries early, just with a deity doing the coordinating work that correlated decision procedures are supposed to do for us now.

When I floated this comparison to my supervisor and to my seminar professor, both waved it off as too cute to be philosophically load-bearing, so I would like a more serious attempt. Suppose Leibniz himself were given a full and rigorous briefing on modern decision theory, on the cosmological assumption that the universe or multiverse is extremely large, and on the fact that no deity does any coordinating in this story. Would he conclude that ECL is essentially pre-established harmony rebuilt on secular foundations, with the principle of sufficient reason now doing the justificatory work his God's choice of the best world used to do? Or would he hold that the analogy fails at exactly the point where the deity disappears, since mere similarity between causally isolated agents' decision procedures cannot, on his own principles, supply a sufficient reason for the correlation that ECL needs — leaving what looks like cooperation actually resting on unexplained coincidence? Please commit to one of these two readings, or stake out a clearly different third option, and defend it using Leibniz's actual metaphysical commitments rather than simply observing that both readings are available.
