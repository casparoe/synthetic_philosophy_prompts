I spent close to thirty years as a fisheries biologist, and a good chunk of that career was mark-recapture population surveys - you tag a sample of trout, let them go, net another sample a while later, and use the ratio of tagged-to-untagged fish (the old Lincoln-Petersen index, more or less) to work out how many fish are actually in the river. Since retiring I've fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole, mostly stuff on probability and epistemology, and my wife thinks I've properly lost it, going on about dead French mathematicians over breakfast.

Anyway, a while back a podcast walked me through the "Doomsday Argument" - the idea that since you're presumably a fairly random member of all the humans who will ever be born, you can use your own rough birth-rank (something like the hundred-billionth human or so) exactly the way I'd use a tag number, to put a probabilistic bound on how many people will ever exist, and it tends to spit out a much shorter future for our species than we'd like to believe. It hit me almost immediately that this is basically my old trout survey, run on the whole species across all of time, which is a genuinely unsettling thing to realize before you've even finished your coffee. Whats been rattling around in my head since is Laplace - I recall reading that he did something quite close to this for real, estimating the population of France from the birth registers plus a sample-census ratio, and working out the uncertainty on that estimate using his own inverse-probability methods. He's also the "rule of succession" guy (the sunrise-probability business), and the one who dreamed up the great demon who could deduce the entire future of the universe from the present position and momentum of every particle in it.

So here's my question: sit Laplace down and actually explain the Doomsday Argument to him properly - what do you think he'd make of it? Would his comfort treating ignorance as probability make him just nod along, as another inverse-probability exercise no different in kind from the France census? Or would his hardcore determinism make him balk at putting a probability distribution over something like "total humans who will ever exist," on the grounds that the number is already fixed by present causes even though we can't know it - and if he did balk, is that actually a good objection to the argument, or just a red herring dressed up as metaphysics? I'd also be curious whether someone as careful as he was about defining a fair sample would've spotted anything resembling the modern "reference class" worry - why privilege "all humans" as your reference class rather than, say, "all primates," or "all observers with memories exactly like yours." Really keen to see you reason it through properly rather than just describe the argument back to me, since I already get the basic shape of it.
