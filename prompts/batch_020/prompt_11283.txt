I trained for several years in a cognitive-science PhD program before moving into journalism covering science and ideas, and the interdisciplinary institute for mind and brain sciences at my graduate university has kept me on its small internal grants committee ever since -- partly, I suspect, because they want at least one voice in the room thinking about how a finding will land once it is out of the seminar room and into headlines. We are deadlocked over an application I would like your help thinking through before we vote at Friday's meeting.

The applicant, one of the institute's postdocs, wants a seed grant for a small, pre-registered, professionally back-translated pilot study across four or five countries, meant as groundwork for a larger external grant application. The idea is to replicate and extend a cluster of early-2000s findings -- Weinberg, Nichols and Stich's work on Gettier-style knowledge judgments, and Machery, Mallon, Nichols and Stich's work on Kripke's Gödel/Schmidt reference case -- that first suggested ordinary philosophical intuitions vary by culture and socioeconomic status, a result those authors used to argue that philosophers cannot simply treat their own intuitions as stand-ins for universal human judgment. The trouble is that the last two decades of follow-up work have been genuinely inconclusive: some replications reproduce the original splits, others do not, and even the more careful multi-country efforts have been accused of just swapping one WEIRD sample (American undergraduates) for several more (undergraduates elsewhere), rather than getting at whatever "culture" was supposed to mean in the first place. Half the committee thinks funding yet another pass at this, however well designed, is a well-worn rut that will not move the debate either way; the other half thinks that precisely because the record is such a mess, even a modest, methodologically careful pilot is the most useful thing we could fund this cycle. Underneath that sits a second, more parochial dispute over our own guidelines, which require every proposal to include "a public-communication plan appropriate to the significance of the study's likely findings" -- some members want to hold the applicant to that clause hard, given how readily outlets turn any hint of cross-cultural difference in "concepts of knowledge" into a trend piece about some culture simply not understanding truth; others think that letting anticipated bad journalism shape what counts as fundable philosophy sets an ugly precedent.

Could you draft the short memo I will circulate ahead of the vote? I need a clear recommendation -- fund, reject, or fund with specific conditions -- with reasoning the applicant and my fellow committee members could actually engage with, and a genuinely fair-minded treatment of the strongest case against whichever way you come down. If you recommend conditions, please spell out what they should be and by when they would need to be met.
