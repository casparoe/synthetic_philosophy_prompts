I spent my career as an evolutionary biologist, and since retiring I have been auditing a philosophy of biology seminar at the local university. The instructor, aware of my background, has asked me to help grade a batch of take-home exam scripts this term, and before I return marks I would welcome a second, philosophically rigorous opinion, since I suspect my own scientific instincts about what counts as a "function" may not track the finer distinctions the course is testing for.

Here is the question, followed by one student's answer.

Question 4 (20 points). A point mutation has just arisen in a single bacterium in a laboratory population and already confers measurable resistance to an antibiotic, though the population has not yet been exposed to the antibiotic for long enough for any differential survival or reproduction to have occurred. Separately, consider feathers, which most evolutionary biologists believe originally evolved for insulation and were only later co-opted for flight. (a) Explain how a causal-role (Cummins-style) theorist and an etiological (selected-effects) theorist would each assess whether conferring resistance is a function of the mutation, and whether flight is a function of feathers. (b) State which account you find more defensible, and why.

Student's answer: Cummins' causal-role account defines a function as whatever capacity of a component contributes to some wider systemic capacity that the analyst is antecedently interested in explaining; it makes no reference to selection or history. On this view we simply ask whether the mutation causally contributes to the bacterium's capacity to survive the antibiotic. Since it plainly does, it already has the function of conferring resistance, regardless of the fact that no selection has yet taken place.

The etiological account, first defended by Larry Wright and later refined by Karen Neander into the "selected effects" theory, instead holds that a trait's function is whichever effect explains why the trait exists and continues to be reproduced, i.e. the effect it was selected for. Because the mutation has not yet undergone any differential reproduction on account of the resistance it confers, the etiological theorist is committed to saying that conferring resistance is not yet its function, however useful it obviously is. This strikes me as a real cost of the etiological view.

The feathers case brings out the same contrast from the other side. Since feathers are used for flight today, the etiological theorist counts flight as their function; the causal-role theorist, by contrast, is stuck with whatever feathers were originally selected for, namely insulation, and has no principled way of ever updating that verdict even after millions of years of subsequent flight. The causal-role view also has the further advantage of not depending on the analyst's interests in the way its critics allege: whether the mutation causally contributes to survival is a perfectly objective matter, not a question of who happens to be doing the analysis or why.

For these reasons, I find the causal-role account more defensible overall.
