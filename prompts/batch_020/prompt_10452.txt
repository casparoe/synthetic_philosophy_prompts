So this came up in my philosophy of math seminar last week and it's been bugging me since. We'd just finished the incompleteness theorems (as one does around week 9) and I made some offhand comment that nobody can prove ZFC consistent from principles more secure then ZFC itself, and one of my students got visibly rattled and asked what would actually happen to mathematics if it turned out ZFC really is inconsistent. The whole room just sort of assumed, without argument, that this would be an unmitigated catastrophe -- math as we know it collapsing overnight. But I've got this nagging half-memory from grad school of the reverse mathematics crowd (Friedman, Simpson, that lot) showing that the vast bulk of ordinary math -- arithmetic, most of classical analysis, basically anything a working scientist would ever touch -- goes through fine in absurdly weak subsystems, way below the full strength of ZFC. So the doomsday reaction seems at least overstated to me, at least for the scenario where the rot turns out to be up in power set or replacement or choice somewhere, rather than down in the arithmetic itself (a PA inconsistency really would be a different order of disaster, I'll grant that much up front).

Can you actually build the strongest version of this argument for me? Basically, argue that a ZFC-but-not-PA inconsistency would be far less catastrophic for mathematics than the standard doomsaying reaction assumes. I want something I could basically hand my students as a provocation to tear apart in section, so dont just wave at "reverse math shows weak systems suffice" -- actually name the systems doing the real work (PRA, RCA0, ACA0, whichever) and say something substantive about whether Gentzen's old consistency proof for PA counts as genuine reassurance here or is beside the point. Then take on the obvious comeback: a bare proof that Con(ZFC) fails doesn't come labeled with which axiom is to blame, so we wouldn't actually know which "safe" fragment to retreat to -- and there's at least one famous case, Wiles leaning on a Grothendieck universe to get Fermat's Last Theorem (later shown removable by McLarty and others), suggesting working mathematicians quietly use more firepower than they think they need, and might not enjoy finding out how much of their toolkit they'd have to disown.
