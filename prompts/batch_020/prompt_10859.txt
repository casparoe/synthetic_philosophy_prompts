At my company we run "blameless" postmortems after production incidents: the official line is that a decision should be judged by how reasonable it was given what was known at the time, not by how it happened to turn out, since two equally sloppy deploys can differ only in whether some downstream dependency happened to be under heavy load that day. In practice, though, the engineer whose change actually took down the payment system still gets a noticeably harder time in the retro, and a lingering reputational hit afterward, than the one whose equally under-tested change happened to slip through without incident. I recently came across the philosophical literature on moral luck, including Nagel's distinction between resultant, circumstantial, and constitutive luck, the "control principle" that we should only be assessed for what is within our control, and Williams's notion of "agent-regret," and it struck me as a far more precise description of what's actually happening than anything I had worked out on my own. I have no formal training in philosophy, so I'd like you to lay out what you see as the most important implications of the moral luck literature for how a team like mine should handle accountability after incidents — in particular, whether the arguments suggest a genuinely outcome-blind postmortem culture is even achievable, or whether resultant luck is doing real, and perhaps even legitimate, work in our judgments even when we insist we're being blameless.
