The question is on Dummett's *On Immigration and Refugees*:

(a) Outline the distinction Dummett draws between the moral claims of refugees and those of voluntary economic migrants.
(b) Outline his argument that immigration restriction in Britain has, in practice, been driven more by racial prejudice than by the reasons officially given for it.
(c) Is Dummett's overall position best understood as a version of the "open borders" view defended by philosophers such as Joseph Carens? Explain your answer.

And here's Priya's answer:

"Dummett's book comes out of decades of anti-racist campaigning as much as out of academic philosophy, and I think that shows in how he sets up the argument. On (a), he separates refugees, who are fleeing persecution or a well-founded fear of serious harm and so have a claim grounded in basic humanitarian need, from economic migrants, who are looking for a better life but aren't in comparable danger. Because refugees can't get protection from their own state, Dummett argues other states come under some obligation to take them in -- he links this to the 1951 Refugee Convention definition of a refugee. Economic migrants, on the other hand, can legitimately be controlled by the receiving state, though Dummett is careful to say that 'legitimately' doesn't mean a state can do whatever it wants -- restriction still has to be for genuine reasons, not just because the migrants are unwanted.

On (b), Dummett traces the shift in British law from the 1948 British Nationality Act, which gave Commonwealth citizens an almost unrestricted right to enter and settle, to the 1962 Commonwealth Immigrants Act, which brought in the first real controls. He argues that the 1962 Act (and the Acts that followed it) were written in racially neutral language but were designed, and understood at the time, to cut down specifically on Black and Asian Commonwealth immigration, while barely affecting white immigrants from places like Australia or Canada. So the official reasons given -- pressure on housing, jobs, and so on -- were mostly a pretext for restricting immigration on racial grounds. I think this connects to his rejection, earlier in the book, of the idea that a country just 'belongs' to whoever already lives there: it's basically the same move as his anti-realism about the past, where he denies that claims about the past are made true or false by facts that outstrip anything we could ever verify. In the same way, he's denying that a nation's right to keep people out can outstrip anything the current inhabitants could actually justify to the people being excluded.

Given how thoroughly Dummett dismantles the standard justifications for restriction, I'd say his overall position ends up being a version of the open borders view that Joseph Carens defends: since almost none of the reasons usually given for restricting immigration survive his scrutiny, the conclusion he's committed to is that borders should, in general, be open, with only narrow exceptions. He and Carens arrive there by slightly different routes, but they're basically arguing for the same conclusion."
