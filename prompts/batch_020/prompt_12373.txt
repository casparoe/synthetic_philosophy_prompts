On LessWrong and the EA Forum, Adam Elga's "Subjective Probabilities Should Be Sharp" (Philosophers' Imprint, 2010) tends to get invoked as having settled the case against genuinely imprecise, interval-valued credences — the sort of thing people reach for when discussing deep or Knightian uncertainty. Elga's engine is a sequential pair of mirror-image bets on some proposition H (one that costs $10 if H is true and pays $15 if false, the other reversed), constructed so that accepting both guarantees a $5 profit regardless of H, with the result that a rational agent offered them one after the other must accept at least one. He then argues that every way an unsharp credence could govern that choice fails: treating each bet as merely optional licenses walking away from the guaranteed money; collapsing to the interval's midpoint quietly reinstates a sharp number; and each of his three "global" policies for letting earlier or later choices bear on the present one — NARROW, PLAN, and SEQUENCE — fails too: NARROW forces belief revision absent any new evidence, while PLAN and SEQUENCE both require different treatment of two choices that are alike in the agent's beliefs and in every outcome she cares about.

I've never seen this answered by anything more than a vague appeal to "sophisticated" choice, and I'd like an actual argument in its place. Please argue against Elga's conclusion, defending the claim that a rational agent can retain a genuinely imprecise credence in H and still get through his betting case intact — engaging concretely with the specific bind he thinks PLAN and SEQUENCE are caught in, rather than just asserting that sophistication dissolves it.
