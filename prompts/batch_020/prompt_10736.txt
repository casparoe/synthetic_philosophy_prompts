I'm plotting a novel where two of my characters -- exes, naturally, because what's a good intellectual rivalry without a bit of old heartbreak -- are decision theorists who run into each other at a conferance bar and can't resist picking their old fight back up. The fight is about money pumps. One of them takes the hard line: if your preferences can be pumped -- cyclic, or even just incomplete -- then you're irrational, period, and it doesn't matter what clever commitment strategy you bolt on afterward, because incoherent preferences are incoherent right then and there, at a time. The other is defending something closer to resolute choice (McClennen's idea, if I'm remembering the name right) -- the claim that an agent can have preferences that look "wrong" by the usual axioms of completeness and transitivity and still never actually get pumped in practice, so long as they commit to a plan up front and have the will to stick with it even when a later self, taken on its own, would rather defect.

Could you write me the dialogue itself? Something like 6-10 exchanges, real back-and-forth where each of them actually responds to the other's specific move instead of just restating their own view louder. I want the "you're just irrational, full stop" character to land the obvious objection -- that resolute choice is basically a patch that stops you from being exploited without making your underlying preferences any less incoherent -- and I want the resolute-choice character to actually have something to say back. My hunch is their best move involves the difference between synchronic and diachronic rationality (whether what gets assessed for rationality is your preferences at an instant, or the rationality of a whole plan across time), but I'm honestly not sure that move works, so feel free to work out for yourself whether it holds up or whether the other character has a good reply to it. Don't let either of them fully concede at the end -- I'd rather it end the way these arguments actually do among people who've read all the same papers and still don't agree.
