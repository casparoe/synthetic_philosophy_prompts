So I'm sketching out a new novel and my protagonist is a sculptor who's spent the last fifteen years grinding toward one big commission, and I want a real philosophical backbone for what that's slowly hollowed out of her. Digging around old meaning-of-life essays for research I landed on this bit from Moritz Schlick's 1927 essay "On the Meaning of Life" (Peter Heath's translation, from his Philosophical Papers):

"Unremitting stern fulfillment of duty in the service of an end eventually makes us narrow and takes away the freedom that everyone requires for self development. A life that is constantly focused only on distant goals eventually loses all power of creation whatsoever. It is like a bow that is always bent: in the end it can no longer loose off the arrow, and with that its tension becomes pointless."

I keep tripping over the bow image though. Isn't a drawn bow the one thats got all the power stored up, thats the whole point of pulling it back? So why does Schlick treat "always bent" as the thing that kills the power, instead of the thing that creates it? Is he actually leaning on some real fact about bows (wood losing its spring if you leave it strung too long, or whatever), or is this just a metaphor that doesn't hold up under its own weight? And whichever it is, what's the actual philosophical claim the image is supposed to be doing work for, and does it fit cleanly with his bigger "meaning is found in play, not work" argument from earlier in the essay, or is it more just a stray swipe at duty-for-duty's-sake that doesn't fully connect to the rest? Trying to actually understand the argument before I put a version of it in my sculptor's mouth.
