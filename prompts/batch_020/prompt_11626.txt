Two months ago I accepted a new job that came with a large signing bonus, paid out in full on my start date, in exchange for a clause committing me to stay at least two years or repay a prorated portion of it if I leave sooner. Since then, people at other companies have told me that firms almost never actually pursue departing employees over these clawbacks, since litigation would cost more than they'd recover, so mine would likely go uncollected if I left early. When I mentioned this to a friend finishing a philosophy PhD, she said my situation is basically a live version of Kavka's toxin puzzle: an eccentric billionaire will pay you a fortune the moment you sincerely form the intention, tonight, to drink a toxin tomorrow that will make you painfully sick for a day but do no lasting harm, and he pays up whether or not you actually follow through -- so that by tomorrow you have no self-interested reason left to go through with it. She thinks the puzzle shows something genuinely troubling about whether an intention like mine can be rationally formed at all, given that I already know I'll have no real reason to stay once a better offer comes along. I'd like to understand this properly rather than just take her word for it. Could you write an essay that restates the puzzle precisely (I may well have garbled a detail), weighs the strongest arguments on each side of whether such an intention can be rational, and closes with a definite verdict rather than an even-handed shrug? A conditional verdict -- e.g., "yes, if you count as what decision theorists call a resolute chooser, no otherwise" -- would count as definite; simply concluding that both sides have a point would not.
