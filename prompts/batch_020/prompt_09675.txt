I have no formal background in philosophy or mathematics, but in the course of reading on my own about rational belief and decision-making, I keep coming back to the Dutch book argument for why credences should obey the probability axioms: the idea that if your degrees of belief do not fit together as a probability function, a bookmaker could construct a set of bets that you would each accept individually but that are guaranteed to lose you money overall. That much makes good sense to me. What I have not been able to work out is why the further, "diachronic" version of the argument -- meant to show that failing to update your beliefs by strict conditionalization is similarly irrational -- is treated with so much more suspicion, even by people who find the original, single-moment version fully convincing. Could you explain what is supposed to make the across-time case different from the single-moment case, and whether, in your own assessment, that difference actually holds up under scrutiny?
