I've spent most of the past decade doing research in social psychology, where a result's scientific legitimacy is, in practice, settled by whether it survives peer review at a reputable journal. A senior colleague recently argued that this is not merely our field's best available proxy for a finding being methodologically sound, but constitutive of it: a result simply is sound once qualified reviewers accept it, and doubting this, on her view, is rather like asking whether a bachelor could secretly be married. The question isn't purely academic for me, since I'm deciding whether to spend the next year of my postdoc trying to replicate a result that, on her view, is already beyond doubt simply because it cleared review. I resist her position, partly because it seems to render our field's periodic replication scandals conceptually impossible rather than merely common and embarrassing, yet I can't identify any reviewer-independent standard of soundness that reviewers are supposedly trying, and sometimes failing, to track. I notice I'm confused: is she right that I'm reaching for a distinction without a difference, or is there a real crux here that her view dissolves too quickly?
