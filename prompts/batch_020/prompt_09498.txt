"Fitch's result, first published by Frederic Fitch in 1963 (though Joseph Salerno's later archival work traces essentially the same argument to an anonymous 1945 referee report by Alonzo Church), targets two theses that verificationists and semantic anti-realists have wanted to hold together. The knowability thesis (KT) says every truth is knowable: ∀p(p → ◇Kp). The non-omniscience thesis (NO) says not every truth is actually known: ∃p(p ∧ ¬Kp). Fitch shows that KT alone, together with a few weak assumptions about K, entails the negation of NO: KT collapses into full omniscience.

The derivation needs three ingredients: factivity (Kp → p), distribution of K over conjunction (K(p ∧ q) → (Kp ∧ Kq)), and positive introspection, i.e., the KK principle (Kp → KKp), which is what licenses treating 'it is known that p is unknown' as a coherent target for the knowability thesis in the first place. Instantiate KT with the Moore-style sentence (p ∧ ¬Kp): if that conjunction is ever true, KT says it is possible to know it, i.e., ◇K(p ∧ ¬Kp). But if K(p ∧ ¬Kp) were true, distribution would give us Kp ∧ K¬Kp, and factivity applied to the second conjunct would give ¬Kp, so we would have Kp ∧ ¬Kp — an outright contradiction. So K(p ∧ ¬Kp) is impossible, i.e., ¬◇K(p ∧ ¬Kp), which contradicts what KT told us. Since the only assumption in play was that (p ∧ ¬Kp) is true, we can discharge it: for every p, ¬(p ∧ ¬Kp), i.e., p → Kp. Every truth is known.

Most commentators, including Dorothy Edgington, have taken this to be a decisive refutation of anti-realism: since no anti-realist wants to be committed to omniscience, and the argument shows KT entails exactly that, the knowability criterion of truth has to be rejected outright rather than merely patched. This is generally regarded as one of the cleanest examples of a substantive metaphysical thesis being refuted by a purely logical argument."
