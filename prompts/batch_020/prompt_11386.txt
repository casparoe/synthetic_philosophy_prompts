Write a dialogue between two philosophers arguing about the 'wrong kind of reasons' problem for buck-passing / fitting-attitude accounts of value -- the view that something is good just in case there's reason to have some pro-attitude (desire, admiration, etc) toward it. One character defends the view, the other keeps pressing a demon-style case: some being threatens to torture you unless you admire a totally worthless object, which gives you a reason to admire it that's got nothing to do with the object actually being admirable. Have the defender try out an actual fix -- e.g. distinguishing reasons that are about the object itself from reasons that are really about the state of having the attitude, or some fancier higher-order move -- and have the critic push on whether that fix can be drawn without quietly presupposing the very facts about value the theory was suppose to explain. I want real back and forth, not two people just restating their opening position.
