Me and a buddy who's doing a PhD in molecular bio got into this argument again last weekend (we do this every time we hang out, it's kind of our thing) about whether it's literally true that "DNA is a code," and neither of us can actually pin down what would settle it. As a software engineer, when I call something a code -- ASCII, Base64, a cipher, whatever -- I mean there's an arbitrary mapping between symbols and meanings that could've been set up totally differently and still worked, plus some actual mechanism that decodes it on the other end. My buddy says the codon-to-amino-acid mapping fits that pretty well: the specific assignments are basically a "frozen accident" from early evolution (it could've gone differently, it's not fixed by chemistry the way an acid turning litmus paper red is), and ribosomes plus tRNA are doing something that looks a lot like decoding rather than just one chemical directly causing another.

Where I get lost is the next move people make, where they say stuff like "your DNA has the information for your eye color" or "the code for building a whole organism." That feels like a way bigger claim than "codon AUG maps to methionine," and I want to know if it's actually the same kind of claim or if its just riding on the credibility of the narrow, legit case. Like, is someone saying something as precise as a cipher there, or is "code"/"information" doing more of a loose, "the brain is a computer" vibes-based metaphor thing at that point?

Can you walk me through, in an actually precise way, what criteria people use to decide when biological "code"/"information" talk is doing real explanatory work versus just borrowing the vocabulary -- and specifically where the codon table itself lands on that spectrum versus where the bigger "information for a trait/organism" claims land? I don't want the "well it's sort of both, language is fuzzy" non-answer -- I want the actual considerations for and against on each side.
