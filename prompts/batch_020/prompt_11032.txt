Over dinner last week, a friend and I ended up in a long, occasionally heated argument about how a panel of judges — or any committee bound to give reasons — ought to reach a verdict when the outcome depends on several logically connected premises. We had both been reading about the "doctrinal paradox" that Kornhauser and Sager described for collegial courts, which Christian List and Philip Pettit later folded into a general theory of judgment aggregation under the name "discursive dilemma." The case we kept returning to is the standard one: three judges must decide whether a defendant is liable for breach of contract, where liability holds if and only if (a) a valid contract existed and (b) it was breached. Judge One finds both (a) and (b) true, and votes liable. Judge Two finds (a) true but (b) false, and votes not liable. Judge Three finds (a) false but (b) true, and also votes not liable. Vote issue-by-issue on the two premises, and a majority affirms both (a) and (b), which by the panel's own rule entails liability. Vote instead directly on the bottom-line verdict, and a majority says not liable. Same three judges, same case, opposite outcomes, purely as a function of which procedure is used to count the votes.

My friend thinks the conclusion-based, case-by-case procedure is obviously correct, since deciding the verdict — not legislating about abstract premises — is the court's actual job, and letting the premise-based procedure win would impose a finding of liability that a majority of the very judges hearing the case regard, on reflection, as mistaken. I think this gets the direction of justification backwards: the premises are the judges' actual reasons, the verdict is supposed to answer to them, and a conclusion-based vote can enshrine a result that contradicts what a majority of the panel collectively believes about the facts and the law — which strikes me as the deeper irrationality. Neither of us budged after the better part of an hour, and I don't think we were simply talking past one another. Could you write a substantial dialogue between two judges on this same panel, still in their robes, arguing in the corridor just after the verdict is handed down, each defending one of these procedures with real conviction and technical sophistication? I'd like them to actually engage each other's strongest objections rather than restate their own position, and at some point to confront whether the impasse is a symptom of something deeper — an Arrow-style impossibility result for judgment aggregation — rather than a puzzle that a sufficiently clever third procedure could simply dissolve. I would rather the exchange end in a genuine, hard-won stalemate than in a tidy synthesis that lets us both off the hook.
