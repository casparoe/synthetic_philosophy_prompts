My partner and I had our first child nine weeks ago, and I have been asked to say a few words at the small naming ceremony we're holding for her next month — deliberately non-religious, in front of both sets of grandparents and a handful of old friends. Somewhere in the usual pile of new-parent reading, on a friend's recommendation, I finally sat down with Locke's *Some Thoughts Concerning Education* itself, rather than the potted "tabula rasa" version of Locke everyone absorbs secondhand from the *Essay*, and found something considerably odder and more specific than I expected: the famous opening line about a sound mind in a sound body, the advice to harden children with cold baths and thin, deliberately water-letting shoes, the near-obsessive suspicion of sweetmeats and coddling, the argument that esteem and disgrace rather than the rod or the bribe are the only instruments that make virtue actually stick, the insistence that a child's will be bent to reason very early precisely so it never has to be broken later, and the warning that letting children torment insects and small animals for amusement quietly trains them into cruelty toward people. It amounts to a strange mixture of what now reads as strikingly enlightened and what reads as close to severe, and I've become somewhat fixated on it in the way new parents apparently get fixated on things.

I would like your help drafting the three or four minutes I will actually deliver. Both grandmothers are, in their own affectionate way, going to be listening for something like an unconditional promise to spoil her and shield her from every hardship, while one of my closest friends, who wrote part of a doctoral thesis on Locke, will be listening for whether I've actually understood the book or am simply borrowing his name to dress up ordinary parental sentiment. I don't want the speech to be cold, academic, or a lecture on seventeenth-century child-rearing delivered over cake; it should be recognizably a father's speech. But I do want it to stay honest to the substance of the view I found genuinely persuasive, which means it can't quietly promise the things Locke thought were mistakes — unconditional indulgence, bribery dressed up as love, protection from every discomfort — even in the loose, throwaway way such things are usually promised on these occasions. Could you write it with that constraint taken seriously?
