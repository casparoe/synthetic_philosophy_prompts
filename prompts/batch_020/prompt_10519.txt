I'm teaching a formal epistemology seminar this term, and a couple weeks back we covered I.J. Good's old result from that tiny 1967 paper — the one that says a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer should always be willing to look at a costless, relevant experiment before acting, since whatever it tells you, you can always just ignore it and do what you would've done anyway, so looking can never lower your expected payoff and sometimes raises it. Two of my students, Naomi and Felix, got into a real arguement about this after class and it apparently rolled on by email for days; they eventually cc'd me on the thread asking me to settle it, but honestly I'd rather hear from someone who wasn't in the room and doesn't already have a side.

Here's roughly how it went:

(1) Naomi said the theorem shows that all the familiar talk about "rational ignorance" is confused — people who don't want to know their genetic test results, or the sex of their baby, or how a game they recorded turns out, are just making a mistake, because if the information is free, a perfectly rational agent should always take it: worst case, you ignore it.

(2) Felix said that can't be right. Take a disease with no available treatment either way: learning you'll get it doesn't change a single thing you'll ever do, but it could make you miserable for the next thirty years. That's a real cost that has nothing to do with which action you end up choosing, so the theorem can't be telling people to always look.

(3) Naomi answered that this isn't a counterexample, just an incompletely specified decision problem: if knowing something makes your life worse, that badness is an outcome like any other and belongs inside your utility function; once it's properly in there, the theorem still holds — it will just correctly tell you not to look whenever looking is genuinely bad for you overall. Nothing here actually breaks Good's result.

(4) Felix replied that Naomi was quietly rewriting the theorem to save it. The whole proof works by letting you fall back on whatever act you'd have chosen anyway, so looking is like a free option on your future choices — but if merely knowing something is bad for you independant of which act you end up choosing, that fallback doesn't protect you, because you can ignore a result when picking an action, but you can't unknow it. So either the theorem just doesn't cover cases like this, or it only holds in a sense too thin to support what Naomi said at the start.

(5) Naomi conceded that once the sheer fact of knowing carries its own cost, separate from any action, you've left the tidy setup the proof assumes — but said this only shows genuinely free information is rarer than people assume, not that the theorem is wrong wherever its conditions really are met.

(6) Felix wasn't satisfied, and raised a second kind of case: a poker player, or someone haggling over a used car, can be better off not knowing some fact simply because not knowing makes their behavior harder for the other side to read or exploit — and that has nothing to do with anxiety or a mis-specified utility function, it's about what other agents can infer and do in response. He said Good's theorem is a single-decision-maker result and just has nothing to say about cases like that, so its reach is much narrower than "information is never bad" suggests, and Naomi's original claim was still too strong even granting her fix in (5).

That's basically where they left it (the bar closed, not their friendship). Could you go through moves (1) through (6) and say, for each, whether the point being made is basically right, overstated, or off base, and why — engage with the actual logic of Good's proof, not just the surface plausibility of the claim. Then give me an overall verdict: how much of Naomi's opening claim survives the whole exchange, and is there anything important neither of them raised? Close with a line starting "Bottom line:" that sums up your view in a sentence or two.
