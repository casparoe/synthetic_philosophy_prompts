Question: Simone Weil's wartime essay on political parties argues that parties are corrupting even when they stand for a genuinely good cause. Explain the specific mechanism she thinks makes this true, and explain how her diagnosis differs from Madison's treatment of "faction" in Federalist No. 10.

Student's answer: Weil's argument in "On the Abolition of All Political Parties" is that a party has three defining features: its a machine for generating collective passion, its a machine for putting collective pressure on the thinking of its own members so they stop reasoning things through for themselves, and its real goal, underneath whatever platform it claims to have, is just its own growth without limit. Because of that last feature, she thinks every party, even one built around a genuinely good cause, is totalitarian in embryo -- an organization whose only actual aim is unlimited expansion can't help absorbing everything else, truth and justice included, along the way. That's a much stronger claim than the usual complaint that parties cause gridlock or corruption, since those are contingent problems you could fix with better rules, while for Weil the sickness is built into what a party structurally is, so campaign finance reform or term limits wouldn't touch it. She wrote the essay right after the war, reacting to the return of fractious multi-party politics under the Fourth Republic, and it fed pretty directly into the constitutional debates of the time. Theoretically she's basically making Madison's move in Federalist 10: both think a faction, once formed, will pursue its own interest against the common good, and Madison's fix -- a big, diverse republic that dilutes any one faction's power -- is really just the moderate, liberal-constitutional version of the same solution Weil's proposing, just less drastic.
