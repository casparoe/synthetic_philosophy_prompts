For an assignment in my philosophy elective, I had to read an essay I'd never encountered before: Moritz Schlick's "On the Meaning of Life" (1927). It felt like an odd fit with the rest of his reputation, since Schlick is mostly known as a hard-nosed logical positivist, yet here he was arguing that most of what fills adult life -- careers, long-term projects, five-year plans -- is merely "work" in his technical sense: activity whose value is always borrowed from some further end, so that the payoff keeps getting deferred and, strictly speaking, never actually arrives. He thinks genuine meaning shows up only in what he calls play: activity that is worthwhile purely in and for itself, in the present moment, which is why, on his view, children, and adults who manage to keep something of a child's relation to their pursuits (he has certain kinds of artists in mind), have a real claim to meaningful lives that the rest of us tend to lose once purposive "work" takes over. When I mentioned this to a friend, she pointed me toward a more recent theory of meaning, Susan Wolf's "fitting fulfillment" view, on which a life is meaningful only when subjective attraction (finding something engaging or fulfilling) lines up with objective attractiveness (the thing being genuinely worth engaging with, not merely feeling that way to the person doing it) -- which sounded to me like an attempt to fix exactly the sort of problem Schlick's theory seems to invite.

Could you write an essay comparing these two accounts precisely enough to make the actual disagreement visible, rather than just noting that both reject the idea that meaning is nothing more than instrumental usefulness? Please use the comparison to settle one specific test case: someone who spends most of their free time utterly absorbed, in a playful and entirely present-tense way, in something most people would call trivial -- say, obsessively perfecting their technique in a simple mobile game, with no audience, ambition, or effect on anyone else, just pure engrossment in the activity itself. Does Schlick's criterion of play commit him to calling this a meaningful life, and if so, is that really a cost of his view? Does Wolf's added requirement of "objective attractiveness" handle the case more plausibly, or does it risk being too demanding in the opposite direction, denying meaning to a child's play or a private hobby merely because no one else has to value it? Please close with a definite verdict on which theory comes closer to getting cases like this right, or on how one might need to be revised in light of the other, rather than leaving things open-ended.
