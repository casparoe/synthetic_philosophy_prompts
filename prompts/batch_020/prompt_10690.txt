So in my political philosophy seminar we just covered the Nozick chapter on distributive justice, and after class two people in my discussion group got into it about the Wilt Chamberlain example. Been chewing on it since, here's roughly how it went:

(1) Dana said the argument basically kills any patterned theory of justice (Rawls, strict equality, whatever): start from a distribution D1 everyone agrees is just, let people freely transfer stuff (like paying an extra quarter each to watch Wilt play), and you get D2, which breaks the pattern. So keeping the pattern means either blocking those transfers or clawing the money back afterwords -- either way you're constantly overriding what people chose to do with their own stuff.

(2) Marcus said that's not really a problem, since no patterned theory (not even Rawls's) claims the pattern has to hold every single instant -- they're fine with periodic redistribution, like taxing income every April and calling it done. So all Wilt Chamberlain shows is that patterns need upkeep, which is just what taxes already are, not some knockdown objection.

(3) Dana said "periodic upkeep" is interference by another name -- you're still taking money specifically because of a free transfer someone made, again and again, forever. Calling it "annual" instead of "continuous" doesn't change what its actually doing.

(4) Marcus said that picture quietly assumes people are already entitled to those extra quarters, which is the actual thing in dispute -- the example only feels forceful because we're told to just accept D1 as justly acquired, but Nozick's own account of just initial acquisition is famously thin, so the case smuggles in exactly the historical entitlement picture it's supposed to be proving.

(5) Dana's last word: even setting D1 aside, Nozick's still shown a real liberty cost to any patterned scheme, and Marcus hadn't shown that cost is worth paying, only that its payable -- not the same thing.
