In Guide of the Perplexed III:32 and III:46, Maimonides argues that God commanded animal sacrifice not because such worship is intrinsically valuable, but as a concession to a people too habituated to the sacrificial cults of the surrounding idolatrous culture to abandon them outright: he compares this to weaning an infant gradually rather than changing its diet all at once, and explains a number of the specific sacrificial laws — which species may be offered, for instance — as designed to redirect idolatrous impulses rather than satisfy them. Yet in the Mishneh Torah (Hilchot Melachim 11:1) he rules flatly that the Messianic king will rebuild the Temple and restore sacrificial worship exactly as the Torah prescribes, with no hint that this restoration is merely provisional. Nahmanides, in his Torah commentary (most famously on Leviticus 1:9), took this as exposing a real failure in the theory: sacrifice long predates the idolatry it is supposed to be curing, and in any case elevating precisely the animals that pagans revered to the status of offerings would flatter rather than uproot their error.

Write an essay on whether Maimonides holds a coherent, defensible position on the enduring value of animal sacrifice, or whether his philosophical and legal writings are in genuine, unresolvable tension on this point. Weigh the leading strategies for reconciling the two texts against the possibility that no reconciliation succeeds, and reach and defend a definite conclusion about what his most considered view actually is.
