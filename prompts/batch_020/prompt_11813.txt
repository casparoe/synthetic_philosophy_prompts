So I fell down a rabbit hole this week that started with an argument with my brother-in-law about whether Bitcoin counts as "real money," and ended with me reading about George Berkeley's economic writing, which nobody ever mentions in intro philosophy classes because they're too busy with the "esse est percipi" stuff. Turns out that late in life, as Bishop of Cloyne, he wrote this bizarre pamphlet called The Querist that's literally just hundreds of numbered questions instead of normal paragraphs, and in it he argues that Ireland doesn't need to hoard gold and silver to be wealthy -- that money is really just a ticket or counter for recording who's owed what, and that actual wealth is people working and producing things. He even pitched a national bank that would issue paper currency instead of coin, though it apparently went nowhere politically. And years before that, after watching the South Sea Bubble implode, he wrote a whole separate essay blaming the crash on speculation rather then genuine industry.

Given all that, what do you think Berkeley would actually say about Bitcoin if you sat him down and explained it to him? Would he treat it as basically vindicating his big point -- that money never needed to be a "real" physical commodity, just an agreed-upon token -- or would he say it's missing the one part of his proposal he actually cared about, some accountable institution standing behind the tokens, and lump it in with the ungrounded paper speculation he clearly despised? I'm also curious whether the fact that he was a bishop, not just an economist, changes your answer at all, since he seemed just as concerned with the moral effects of speculation on a society as with the technical mechanics of currency.
