I spent close to forty years as a field geologist before I retired, and for the last year or so I've been auditing philosophy classes at the community college near me, mostly epistemology, just for the fun of it. Something that happened over Thanksgiving has been rattling around in my head ever since, though, and it's turned into a genuine puzzle for me rather than just a family annoyance, so I wanted to run it by you.

My brother-in-law, who used to be a totally normal guy, got heavily involved with a new church a few years back and has become a committed young-earth creationist. Over dinner I made the mistake of bringing up radiometric dating and ice cores as evidence the earth is billions of years old, and he calmly informed me that God created the whole world about six thousand years ago already "mature" - starlight already partway here so distant stars would be visible right away, rock strata already layered, trees already grown with rings in them, the works, all built with a built in appearance of history. Every single piece of evidence I threw at him just got absorbed into the theory instead of denting it even slightly. There's genuinely no observation I could bring up that he couldn't fold back in as "well, that's just how God made it look."

Here's the part that's actually been keeping me up at night: when I tried to work out how I would PROVE to him that the rock record really does reflect a real deep past, rather then a world that popped into existence looking old last week (or six thousand years ago), I realized I don't have an argument for that which doesn't eventually just assume the very thing that's in question, namely that physical traces reliably track real elapsed time. Every justification I reach for bottoms out in something I'm not actually defending, just taking for granted. And yet it sure doesn't feel to me like I'm merely "assuming" it the way I'd assume a working hypothesis - it feels far more solid than that, nothing like the genuinely open question of, say, the exact age of some specific rock formation. So is there any real difference between his bedrock assumption and my bedrock assumption, given that neither of us can actually prove ours from more basic premises? Am I quietly in the same boat as him and just too used to my own boat to notice it, or is something actually different going on? And practically speaking - is there even any point in continuing to argue evidence with him, or does that completely miss what's really going on?
