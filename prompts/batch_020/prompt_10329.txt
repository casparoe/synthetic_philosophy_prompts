So there's this old argument, due to a mathematician named Chris Freiling, thats supposed to refute the continuum hypothesis using a dart-throwing thought experiment, and I want you to argue that it doesn't actually work.

Setup: if CH is true, the reals can be well-ordered so that every real has only countably many predecessors. Define f(x) as the set of predecessors of x under that well-order, so f(x) is always countable. Now imagine throwing two darts at the real line independently, landing on x and y. Since f(x) is countable (measure zero), y misses it with "probability 1" — and by identical reasoning x misses f(y) with probability 1 too. But under a well-order exactly one of "y precedes x" or "x precedes y" has to hold, i.e. exactly one of y∈f(x) or x∈f(y) — so both misses happening at once is actually impossible. An impossible event can't have probability 1, so something's gone wrong, and Freiling takes this to show that CH is the thing that has to give.

Argue that this "axiom of symmetry" argument gives us no real reason to doubt CH. Don't just retreat to "CH is independent of ZFC so nothing could refute it anyway" — actually engage with the specific probability/measure move being made and pin down where exactly it fails.
