Been down a weird rabbit hole this week and could use some outside perspective on it. I stumbled on an old essay about the letters Godel wrote to his mother in 1961, where she basically asked him point-blank whether he thought they'd meet again after death, and instead of just saying something comforting and vague he lays out this whole little argument for why he actually does believe in some kind of afterlife. The gist: the world is rationally organized and everything in it has a reason (this is very explicitly borrowed from Leibniz's principle of sufficient reason, nothing to do with any actual religion or church), so it would basically be a contradiction of that rationality for the world to bring about a mind with this huge field of possible development -- memory, personality, moral and intellectual growth -- and then just cut it off for good after it's realized like a tenth of a percent of that potential. So there's an afterlife (or at least some kind of continuation), because otherwise the books don't balance. This is separate from his ontological proof of God btw, that's its own whole mess, this is just the mother-letters argument specifically.

What's bugging me is who's making this argument. This isn't some crank, it's arguably the most careful, rigorous logical reasoner of the century, a guy whose entire claim to fame is proving hard limits on what formal systems can even prove about themselves, and he beleived this enough to write it to his own mom as a real answer instead of a comforting lie. I'd have described myself as pretty committed to Bayesian-style epistemics and a boring default-naturalist about death, so this feels like exactly the kind of "someone way smarter and more careful than you took this seriously, so update" situation that's supposed to actually move a person like me.

So here's what I want your take on: what are the most important implications of Godel's argument (and of Godel himself endorsing it) for someone who tries to take rationalist epistemics seriously but is otherwise a pretty committed naturalist? Does this have to update me at all on how much independent weight to give "clean" a priori arguments in metaphysics generally -- the same style of reasoning that shows up in stuff like the simulation argument or infinite ethics, which I'm usually a lot more willing to entertain -- or is there some principled, non-question-begging place where his specific argument breaks down that doesn't threaten the a priori reasoning I actually want to keep trusting?
