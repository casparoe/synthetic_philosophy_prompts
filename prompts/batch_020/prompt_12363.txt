I've been rereading Alan Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" for a reading group, and I keep getting stuck on the part that most popular discussions of the Turing test quietly skip over: his ninth and final objection, "The Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception." Turing doesn't wave it away -- he writes that "this argument is to my mind quite a strong one," states that "the statistical evidence, at least for telepathy, is overwhelming," and proposes running the imitation game inside a "telepathy-proof room" so a psychic interrogator couldn't cheat. Most commentary on this passage agrees that he had in mind the card-guessing experiments Samuel Soal ran in the early 1940s -- experiments that a statistical analysis by Betty Markwick, decades later, showed had almost certainly been manipulated. So one of the most careful statistical thinkers of the century built a permanent structural safeguard into his single most influential paper out of deference to evidence that turned out to be fabricated.

What nags at me is that this isn't an isolated lapse. It has the same shape as Daniel Kahneman's insistence, in Thinking, Fast and Slow, that "disbelief is not an option" regarding the social-priming literature -- not long before most of that literature failed to replicate -- and it's an instance of the general phenomenon Scott Alexander used parapsychology to illustrate on Slate Star Codex: a field can produce methodologically competent-looking, statistically significant results for decades without the underlying effect being real.

I'd like an essay that uses the Turing/Soal case as its anchor for working out a general answer to this pattern: when a careful, quantitatively sophisticated thinker meets evidence they judge statistically overwhelming, but which contradicts the rest of their worldview and resists mechanistic explanation, what should they do? Weigh the case that Turing behaved reasonably against the case that a sufficiently careful Bayesian should have discounted the statistics far more heavily than he did, independently of any later fraud revelation. In his favor: he hedged rather than converting outright, built a safeguard into his argument rather than dismissing the objection out of hand, and was working in a milieu where this evidence had genuine institutional credibility -- the Society for Psychical Research, founded by Cambridge philosophers, had counted the likes of Henry Sidgwick and C. D. Broad among its presidents. Then commit to a specific verdict on whether Turing's epistemic conduct was defensible given what was knowable in 1950, and state the general heuristic you think this episode teaches for handling "overwhelming but mechanistically inexplicable" statistical anomalies encountered today, without the benefit of hindsight. I don't want "it's complicated" -- something with the specificity of "defer to the field's consensus unless the claimed effect would violate a sufficiently load-bearing physical law, in which case suspect the statistics before the law" is the grain of answer I'm after, whether or not it's the one you end up defending.
