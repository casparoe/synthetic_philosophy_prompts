I'm working on a longread about the claims, revived every time a new large language model makes headlines, that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show machines can never fully match human mathematical insight — the argument originally due to J.R. Lucas and developed at much greater length by Roger Penrose. I've done enough reading to know that the most immediate rebuttal is considered too quick by specialists: a mind reasoning about some formal system F can only conclude that "if F is consistent, then its Gödel sentence is true," and that conditional is itself something F can typically already prove, so no unconditional advantage over F has actually been demonstrated unless the mind also somehow knows, categorically, that F is consistent. That already looks doubtful, and matters get worse once you add that nobody has ever been able to say which formal system is supposed to capture a given person's mathematical powers in the first place.

What I can't reconstruct cleanly for the piece is the next move in this back-and-forth. Penrose was well aware of objections along these lines, and in Shadows of the Mind he tried to build a more careful, "upgraded" version of the Gödelian argument specifically meant to survive them, without simply assuming we know our own consistency or can point to our own underlying formal system. Could you reconstruct, in some technical detail, how that upgraded argument is supposed to work, and then explain where exactly it is generally thought to break down? I'd especially like clarity on one interpretive question a colleague and I keep disagreeing about in our email exchanges: is the flaw in Penrose's upgraded argument really just the old "which formal system even is my own reasoning" problem resurfacing in a cleverer disguise, or does his construction introduce a genuinely separate logical gap that has nothing to do with self-knowledge at all? I'd like to represent that disagreement accurately in print rather than flatten it into a soundbite.
