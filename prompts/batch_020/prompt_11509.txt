Okay so I live in a group house with six other people (pretty rationalist/EA-adjacent crowd, we literally have a Discord bot that runs polls for house decisions), and last month we ran into something that's been bugging me since. We had to decide whether my housemate's partner could get a key, and the house rule is he qualifies only if he clears two seperate conditions: stays over enough nights, and actually pulls his weight on chores/groceries. So instead of one big vote we ran it as two sub-polls. Sub-poll one passed 4-3. Sub-poll two also passed 4-3, but with a totally different coalition of yes-voters, and when someone cross-referenced the actual ballots it turned out only two of us had voted yes on both conditions. If we'd just put "should he get a key" to a straight yes/no vote, it would've failed 2-5. Someone in the house recognized this as basically the doctrinal paradox from Kornhauser and Sager's old legal theory papers on collegial courts, which Pettit later generalized into the "discursive dilemma," and which List and Pettit apparently turned into a full-blown impossibility theorem for judgment aggregation that's structurally a cousin of Arrow's theorem, just for judgments instead of preferences. I went down a rabbit hole reading about it and now I can't stop side-eyeing every vote our house has ever taken.

Can you give me the most important implications of the discursive dilemma / judgment-aggregation impossibility results for how a small group like mine should actually structure its collective decisions? I'm especially trying to get my head around three things: (1) whether there's any principled way to choose a premise-based (issue-by-issue) procedure over a conclusion-based (straight up-or-down) one, given that whoever controls that choice basically controls the outcome; (2) what we really give up if we abandon the "systematicity" condition the impossibility result leans on — treating every proposition the same way regardless of context — and whether relaxing it is a genuine fix or just relocates the arbitrariness into whoever sets the agenda or the voting order; and (3) whether "deliberate more before voting" is an actual solution, on the theory that it makes people's judgments correlated enough that the bad coalition patterns stop showing up, or whether that's in real tension with the Condorcet-jury-theorem-style story about why majority votes track truth in the first place, which kind of needs everyone's errors to be independent. Basically: is there a defensible way to run a household (or a jury, or a committee) here, or does the math just say we're screwed no matter which procedure we pick?
