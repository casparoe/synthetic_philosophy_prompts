For forty years I worked as an experimental physicist; only in retirement have I had the time to read philosophy, and I have become quite taken with, yet also unsettled by, David Lewis's Humean account of objective chance: the idea that the chance of an event is simply whatever the single best systematization of the entire history of occurrent fact — the system striking the ideal balance of simplicity, strength, and fit — says it is, with nothing further standing behind it. Lewis himself worried that such chances can be "undermined": a low-chance outcome that a given system permits might actually occur, and when the whole history of the world is reconsidered in light of it, some rival system might fit that history better, retroactively unseating the very chances that were supposedly in force when the event happened. Anti-Humeans point to exactly this predicament as evidence that chance, and lawhood generally, cannot simply be a matter of patterns in what happens, however economically summarized. I would like you to write a short story that makes this predicament felt rather than merely explained — centered on a character, or even an entire world, that lives through (or gradually comes to suspect) such an undermining event, so that the very identity of the laws it depended on is thrown into doubt after the fact. Please resist tidying the metaphysical unease away by the end.
