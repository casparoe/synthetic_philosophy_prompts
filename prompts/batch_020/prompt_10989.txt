I'm currently writing my IB History extended essay on Marx's critique of industrial capitalism, and while I feel reasonably confident about his concept of alienation, I've hit a snag with his concept of exploitation that I haven't been able to resolve on my own. In class, we read passages from Capital where Marx explains that capitalists extract "surplus value" from their workers, and my teacher presented this as a structural feature built into the capitalist mode of production itself, not just an occasional abuse that happens to occur. However, one of the secondary sources I found while researching online, a fairly polemical essay responding to contemporary socialist arguments, claims that this analysis really only holds up in labor markets distorted by monopsony power (basically, where a worker has only one realistic employer to sell their labor to) or outright coercion. According to this essay, once you have a genuinely competitive labor market in which employers have to bid wages up to match what a worker actually contributes, the kind of exploitation Marx describes simply stops occurring.

Since this would seem to imply that a sufficiently competitive capitalist economy could pay workers "fair" market wages and thereby cease to be exploitative in Marx's own terms, I've been considering arguing in my essay that Marx's theory of exploitation is really an empirical claim about the specifically uncompetitive labor markets of nineteenth-century industrial Europe, rather than a claim about capitalism as such. Is that a defensible way to read Marx, or am I misunderstanding something more fundamental about how his argument is actually supposed to work? I raised this question with a couple of friends from class, and we went back and forth for a while without really settling it, so I would really appreciate a clearer explanation before I commit to this as one of the central claims of my essay.
