My grandma Mimi died about two weeks ago at ninety-six, and it turns out she was one of the last people alive who'd actually worked as a "transformer" at the Isotype Institute in Oxford, back when it was still basically Marie Neurath and a handful of others carrying on what she and Otto had built. Grandma trained under Marie in the years after Otto died, spent the next four decades turning raw statistics into picture-charts for health ministries and school textbooks, and never called herself a philosopher, but she'd absorbed the whole Neurath worldview from the inside, in a very literal way thats now become my problem. (I'm the closest thing the family has to a philosopher — I'm ABD on an HPS dissertation about the Vienna Circle, which she found endlessly funny.)

She left a typed page of funeral instructions that is very her. Rule one: nobody says she's "watching over us" or that her "spirit continues," because to her that's exactly the kind of ungrounded, unverifiable pseudo-statement that Otto's physicalism existed to rule out of serious talk — if you can't point to something a stranger could in principle walk up and check, it doesn't belong at her funeral. Rule two, in her own words, bans "Ballung eulogy-speak": fuzzy cluster-phrases like "she touched so many lives" or "a light has gone out," which sound weighty but that nobody could ever actually cash out. She also used to say, apropos of nothing, that you never get to climb off the boat and rebuild it from scratch on dry land, you just replace one plank at a time while everyone's still standing on it — which I think is supposed to be about tonight, somehow, even though she meant it about protocol sentences. And, half joke and half not, she left behind an Isotype-style chart of her own life — years married, grandkids, picture-books published, languages they were translated into, all as rows of identical little repeated icons, never once resized, exactly how she'd have corrected a student for doing it — with a note that says something like: a picture unites, a eulogy just divides the people who actually knew you from the people being polite. She wanted it "read into the record" somehow and never said how.

I'm giving the eulogy this weekend, five minutes, tops. The room's going to split right down the middle — half family and neighbours who've never heard of Otto Neurath and never will, half a scarily well-read contingent of design historians and philosophy-of-science people who worked with her or have published on Isotype and will absolutely notice if I fudge the physicalism or quietly work around her rules to make my life easier. Could you actually write the eulogy? It has to comfort the room and function as a normal, moving thing to say out loud to people who could not care less about logical empiricism, but it also has to take her stipulations completely seriously rather than dodge them — including, ideally, doing something real with "pictures unite, words divide" even though a spoken eulogy has nothing to work with but words.
