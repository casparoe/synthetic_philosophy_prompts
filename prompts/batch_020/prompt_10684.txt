In my formal epistemology seminar we've spent the last two weeks on the Pasadena game, Nover and Hájek's variant of the St. Petersburg paradox: a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads, on some toss n, which pays out (-1)^(n-1) * 2^n/n. Summed in the "natural" order — toss 1, then toss 2, and so on — the expectation is the alternating harmonic series 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which converges to ln 2. But because the series is only conditionally convergent, the Riemann rearrangement theorem lets us reorder these very same outcomes to sum to any real number we like, or to make them diverge to +∞ or -∞ — which is why the authors insist that the game's expectation is undefined rather than simply equal to ln 2. They anticipate the objection that the toss-by-toss order is just the privileged, canonical way of adding up the outcomes, and they reject it, but their reply is fairly compressed. Could you unpack, in intuitive rather than purely formal terms, why we shouldn't just privilege the natural ordering here, and why this makes the Pasadena game a deeper problem for expected utility theory than the ordinary St. Petersburg paradox, whose infinite expectation is at least order-independent?
