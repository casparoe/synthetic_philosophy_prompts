I've been reading David Lewis and Saul Kripke for a philosophy club project on how individuals relate across possible worlds, and I've run into a tension between two arguments that I can't work out by myself. Lewis argues that genuine transworld identity — the view that one and the same individual is literally a part of several different possible worlds — forces us to say that properties like shape are relations to worlds rather than the plain, non-relational intrinsic properties they seem to be (this is the problem of accidental intrinsics), since otherwise the very same individual would have to bear incompatible intrinsic properties at once. His solution, counterpart theory, avoids this by saying the individual exists in only one world, while what exists in the other worlds are merely similar "counterparts," each having its own properties outright, so that no contradiction ever arises. But this looks like exactly the move Kripke targets in his famous footnote about Humphrey: if "Humphrey could have won the election" comes out true only because some distinct, similar person wins in another world, the claim no longer seems to be about Humphrey at all, and Humphrey would have no reason to care how it turns out. Can the counterpart theorist keep the fix for the first problem without paying the price Kripke describes, or are the two so closely linked that avoiding one guarantees running into the other? I'd like an explanation of whether and how this gets resolved in the literature, rather than just separate summaries of each argument on its own.
