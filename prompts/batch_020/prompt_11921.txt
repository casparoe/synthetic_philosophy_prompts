So I've been down a bit of a rabbit hole on Madhyamaka lately (a friend in my philosophy reading group brought up Nagarjuna's tetralemma a few weeks back and it kind of spiraled from there). Here's the thing that's been bugging me: in the Mulamadhyamakakarika, Nagarjuna doesn't just decline to answer certain questions the way the Buddha famously did with the "undeclared" points -- he goes further and actually negates all four corners of the catuskoti at once for stuff like the existence of the Tathagata or the status of nirvana (not real, not unreal, not both, not neither). But then in a seperate, shorter text, the Vigrahavyavartani, when a critic points out that his own claim "everything lacks inherent existence" should, by his own logic, be just as empty and therefore have no more force than any other statement, Nagarjuna's response (verse 29, i think?) is basically "I have no thesis, so that objection doesn't even apply to me." My friend thinks this is a total cop-out, since the guy wrote an entire treatise absolutely packed with arguments that sure look like theses to her.

I want to know if she's right. Can you write me an essay that really digs into whether "no thesis" names an unresolvable contradiction at the heart of Nagarjuna's project, or whether there's a genuinely defensible way to reconcile it with all the arguing he actually does? I want you to seriously engage with the main moves people make here -- e.g. the two-truths defense (he's only refusing theses about ultimate/inherent existence, not conventional ones), Candrakirti's idea that his negations are purely ad hominem reductios against an opponent's own commitments rather than independent theses of his own, the "non-implicative negation" idea that denying something doesn't automatically smuggle in some positive alternative, and the more extreme option some people (Garfield and Priest, maybe?) take of just biting the bullet and saying Nagarjuna is knowingly asserting a genuine, coherent contradiction at the limits of what language can express. Don't just lay these out as a neutral menu -- actually weigh them against each other and commit to whichever reading (or combination of readings) you think best survives scrutiny, the way you'd defend a verdict rather than staying diplomatically on the fence.
