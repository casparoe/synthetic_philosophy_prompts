Now that I'm retired after thirty-five years doing fieldwork on seal and seabird populations in the Canadian Arctic, I've had a lot of time to go down internet rabbit holes, and lately it's been the reports of grizzly-polar bear hybrids - "pizzlies," people are calling them - turning up more and more as the ice keeps retreating and the two animals' ranges start to overlap. This came up over dinner with an old grad-school friend a few weeks back and turned into a real argument. She's convinced that since a polar bear and a grizzly can mate and produce cubs that themselves go on to breed, this basically proves polar bears were never a genuinely seperate species from grizzlies to begin with - that the whole category is just a line humans drew for convenience and nature never respected it. I pushed back that they look different, hunt totally differently, one's essentially a marine specialist at this point and the other isn't, and a bit of interbreeding where their territories happen to bump into each other doesn't erase a few hundred thousand years of separate adaptation. She thinks that's just me being sentimental about a label. Neither of us budged.

It's not purely an academic spat either, since as I understand it the legal protections polar bears get partly depend on their being recognized as their own species rather than, say, a northern variant of the brown bear. Since then Ive gotten a bit obsessed and started reading around in philosophy of biology (my daughter, delighted I've finally found a hobby that isn't the boat, keeps sending me articles), and it turns out even professional taxonomists apparently work with quite a few different, sometimes conflicting definitions of what makes two populations count as separate species. So now I'm less sure than when I started: was my friend basically right, was I, or is this one of those arguments where both sides end up talking past each other because the concept itself is doing less work than we assumed?
