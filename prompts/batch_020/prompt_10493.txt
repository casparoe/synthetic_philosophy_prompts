Ok here's a fun one: what do you reckon Leibniz would've made of Benatar's asymmetry argument? You know the one -- presence of pain is bad, presence of pleasure is good, but absence of pain is good even when nobody's around to enjoy it, while absence of pleasure isn't bad since theres no one being deprived. Benatar uses that to argue coming into existence is always a net harm, even for a happy life. Leibniz obviously never heard of any of this, but he's got that whole apparatus where existence/reality is basically convertible with goodness and perfection -- the "best of all possible worlds" stuff, the privation theory of evil, etc -- which seems like it should clash pretty hard with the idea that mere absence can just be "good" with no subject there to enjoy it. Can you actually reconstruct his response instead of just going "well he's an optimist so obviously he'd disagree"? Like, would he reject the whole asymmetry structure, or accept the four-part setup but deny one specific premise -- and if so which one, and why. Try to ground it in actual bits of his metaphysics (Theodicy, the Ultimate Origination of Things essay) rather than vibes.
