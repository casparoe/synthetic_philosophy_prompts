So I'm working on a piece about this odd little revival of interest in paraconsistent logic — the idea that some contradictions can be true without the whole system exploding into nonsense — and a philosopher I talked to for background kept steering me toward Nagarjuna's catuskoti (the "tetralemma": for any claim, is it true, is it false, is it both, is it neither) in the Mulamadhyamakakarika. From what I've read since, especially Garfield and Priest's paper on this, it sounds like the consensus among philosophers working on Madhyamaka these days is that Nagarjuna wasn't just using the tetralemma as some rhetorical or meditative device — he was a genuine proto-dialetheist, asserting actual contradictions (like in that verse, 18:8, where he says everything is real, unreal, both, and neither, and calls it the Buddhas teaching), and that 20th century logic basically just caught up with him 1800 years later.

Here's the bit I can't get past, though: doesn't that sit kind of weird next to Nagarjuna's other big claim, that everything is "empty" of svabhava (any fixed, determinate nature)? To genuinely assert "A and not-A" as a true contradiction, rather than just gesturing at a paradox, doesn't A need some pinned down content for the contradiction to actually bite? It feels almost backwards for a philosopher whose entire project is dissolving fixed essences to also be the one guy who proved you can rationally assert an essence and its negation at the same time. Am I missing some distinction that resolves this, or is that tension basically the price of admission for reading him this way? Would love a sanity check before I write "ancient Buddhist monk beat Western logicians to the punch" as my nut graf.
