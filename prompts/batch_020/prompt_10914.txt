so for this "history of science" elective i'm taking (it's this small special-topics class instead of a study hall), we have to write a final paper on a scientist's religious beliefs and how they connect to their actual work, and i picked Newton because i already knew the basic "secretly obsessed with alchemy and Bible prophecy" thing, but going down the research rabbit hole for this paper has been way weirder than i expected. like, i knew he privately rejected the Trinity his whole life and had to hide it (Cambridge fellows were technically supposed to get ordained, and he got some special dispensation to avoid it because he couldn't honestly swear to Trinitarian doctrine), but i didn't realize how directly that fed into his physics writing. In the General Scholium and in some letters to this guy Bentley, Newton argues that the reason all the planets orbit the sun the same direction in basically the same flat plane can't just be gravity doing its thing — he says gravity alone wouldn't produce that setup, so God must've specifically arranged it. And separately, in the Opticks, he argues that the little gravitational tugs planets and passing comets exert on each other will build up over time until the whole system needs a "reformation," basically God has to periodically swoop in and retune the orbits. Leibniz (well, technically it was this guy Samuel Clarke arguing Newton's side, but everyone just calls it the Newton-Leibniz fight) thought this was hilarious and said it makes God sound like a bad watchmaker who can't even build a watch that runs on its own.

here's the part i can't stop thinking about for my paper: decades later, Laplace and Lagrange actually did the math (perturbation theory) and showed those little orbital wobbles mostly cancel out over long timescales instead of piling up forever, so the "needs periodic fixing" thing basically wasn't true. and Laplace even came up with his own naturalistic explanation (the nebular hypothesis, the solar system forming out of a big spinning cloud) for why the planets share a rotation direction and orbital plane in the first place, which was Newton's OTHER big design argument. so both of Newton's specific "this requires God" arguments about the solar system basically got dissolved by later science.

what i'm trying to actually figure out for my paper is: what would Newton have said if he'd lived to see this? like would it have been a genuine crisis for him, since he was adamant (this is basically straight from the General Scholium) that a God who doesn't actively rule and intervene isn't really God at all, he was NOT into the "distant watchmaker" idea — or would he have just shrugged it off since his real personal religious obsession was prophecy and biblical chronology, not this astronomy stuff, and the design argument was more for public consumption anyway? or maybe he pivots to some totally different design argument? i don't want the generic "well Newton lived in a religious era so he believed in God" answer, i already know that much going in — i want something that actually engages with his specific, kind of heretical theological commitments and reasons out how a guy like that would react.
