I'm drafting the second half of a novel whose protagonist receives a formal diagnosis of dissociative identity disorder roughly two-thirds of the way through the story, and I want the diagnosis to do real work in the remaining chapters rather than simply supplying a retroactive label for what the reader has already seen. I've been reading Ian Hacking on the looping effects of human kinds -- his distinction between indifferent and interactive kinds, the "making up people" thesis, and his own case studies of multiple personality -- and I suspect this framework could give the back half of the book some genuine intellectual ballast. Please describe the most important implications of Hacking's looping-effects idea for how I write the months following her diagnosis: in particular, how the label itself might reshape her self-narration and conduct, how the clinicians around her might in turn revise the category in light of how she and patients like her respond to it, and how all of this should differ from the simpler, non-looping story in which a diagnosis merely names something that was already fully formed beforehand. I'd rather this went straight to the specific mechanics of the loop than offered a general primer on social constructionism.
