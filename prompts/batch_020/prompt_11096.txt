ok this has been rattling around in my head for a while and I cant find anyone who's actually written about it. Frank Ramsey has this passage from his notebooks (written not long before he died in 1930, aged 26) where he lays out his whole take on meaning and death: he says he doesnt feel remotely humbled by the vastness of the universe, because sheer size just isn't the kind of thing that matters morally -- stars are huge but they can't think or love, so who cares how big they are -- and that the eventual heat death of everything is so far off that, discounted properly, its present value to him right now is basically nothing. Basically the idea that his picture of the world is drawn in perspective rather than to scale -- humans and what they care about blown up big in the foreground, stars and deep time shrunk down small in the background. Rationalist/EA people love trotting this passage out as basically the definitive anti-cosmic-significance take.

Here's the thing though: Ramsey is also, more or less, the guy who invented subjective expected utility theory -- his 1926 paper on truth and probability gets you most of the von Neumann-Morgenstern/Savage apparatus decades early. That's the exact machinery longtermist arguments run on: tiny probabilities times astronomically large numbers of future people, so the far future ends up swamping every other consideration even though it feels abstract and distant.

So if you sat 1920s Ramsey down and actually walked him through, say, Bostrom's astronomical waste argument, which side of him wins -- the guy who flatly refuses to be moved by cosmic scale, or the guy whose own math is the engine that makes the longtermist case go? Don't just say "we can't know" -- actually reconstruct how that argument would've played out inside his head, and tell me where you think he'd have landed and why.
