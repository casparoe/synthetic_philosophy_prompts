As a software engineer, I spend a lot of my time thinking in terms of orderings and comparator functions. So when a friend sent me Ruth Chang's TED talk on how to make hard choices, what caught my attention was her claim that in genuinely hard cases, two options are neither better, worse, nor exactly equal, but instead stand in a further relation she calls being "on a par" -- a fourth possible verdict alongside the usual three. That sent me down a bit of a rabbit hole, including the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry on value incommensurability, and I learned that this "parity" idea is actually contested rather than a settled description of what's going on in hard choices. Some philosophers think Chang has genuinely identified a comparative relation we'd otherwise miss. Others, in a line of thought about incommensurable values that goes back at least to Joseph Raz, think this is an unnecessary complication, and that the honest description of hard cases is simply that no comparison between the options can be made at all -- full stop -- and that this absence of any comparison is exactly what makes the choice hard.

I can restate both positions, but I don't yet have a good feel for whether "there's a comparison here, it's just not better/worse/or-equal" and "there's no comparison here at all" are really different claims with different consequences, or just two vocabularies for the same underlying situation -- a bit like the difference between two elements being incomparable under a partial order versus being related by some further rule that just isn't a strict ranking. What I haven't seen anywhere is the two views actually going toe-to-toe against each other's strongest objections.

Could you write a dialogue between two philosophers -- one defending Chang's parity view, the other defending the rival "incomparabilist" position -- who have been asked by a mutual friend to help her think through a genuinely hard choice she's facing, say, between two very different jobs she's been weighing for weeks without ever feeling that one comes out ahead or that they're a precise tie? I'd like the parity defender to address the classic puzzle about why making one job just slightly more attractive doesn't obviously tip the balance in its favor, and I'd like the incomparabilist to press hard on whether "being on a par" names a genuine further comparative relation or is just a label for vagueness or for our own ignorance, and on whether the two views actually cash out differently once you're the one who has to decide. Please make both philosophers equally sharp, and have them respond to each other's actual arguments as the exchange develops rather than talking past one another -- there's no need for either one to concede by the end; I'd rather see exactly where, and why, they get stuck disagreeing.
