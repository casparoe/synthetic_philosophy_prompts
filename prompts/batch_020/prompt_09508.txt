I'm a backend engineer by trade, with no formal training in philosophy, but I've been turning an idea over in my head for the past few weeks after a friend who's finishing her philosophy PhD brought it up in connection with my wife and me expecting our first child. The term she used was "transformative experience," which she credited to the philosopher L.A. Paul. As I understand it, the core idea is that some experiences are "epistemically transformative" in that they teach you something about what it's like to undergo them that you could not have grasped in advance no matter how much testimony or data you gathered beforehand, while others are "personally transformative" in that they reshape your core preferences and point of view, so that the self who makes the decision and the self who has to live with it aren't even judging the outcome by the same standards. Many of the biggest decisions people make (with having a child as her central example) are apparently both at once, which is supposed to be a real problem for the ordinary picture of rational choice as imagining possible outcomes and weighing how much we'd like them, since here the weighing cannot be done in advance even in principle. The example I keep coming back to, apparently a favorite of hers, involves being offered an irreversible transformation into a vampire: everyone who has undergone it swears it is wonderful, but there is no way to assess that claim except by becoming the sort of creature that would agree with it.

I'd like to see this turned into short fiction rather than another argument, something closer in spirit to Ted Chiang than to plot-driven sci-fi, if that's a useful reference point. Here is a setting in case it helps, though please feel free to depart from it entirely: imagine a near-future clinic offering a single, irreversible outpatient procedure, something like "the Shift," which permanently rewires perception and affect into a state that everyone who has undergone it describes as immeasurably richer, invariably in terms that are useless to anyone who hasn't had it done ("like describing color to someone who has never had eyes" is the sort of line the marketing department has learned to love). Nobody who has gone through with it has ever asked to have it reversed, yet all of them also lose interest in actually persuading anyone else to do the same, which different observers read as either serenity or a warning sign. The protagonist might be someone occupationally entangled in the dilemma, an engineer who built the clinic's informed-consent software and therefore knows better than anyone how little the disclosures actually disclose, or simply someone whose sibling or partner underwent the procedure and came back changed in ways that resist description.

If you're willing, I'd love an original short story of roughly 1000 to 1500 words built around this bind. I'd rather it not resolve neatly into either "they went through with it and regretted it" or "they went through with it and it was straightforwardly wonderful," since either resolution would quietly smuggle in exactly the kind of advance knowledge the premise is supposed to rule out. What I'm hoping for instead is something that stays honest to the actual epistemic trap, ideally giving us some real sense of both the "before" and "after" perspectives without ever letting them fully understand one another, since that gap seems to be the whole philosophical point.
