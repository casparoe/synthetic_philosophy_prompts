I've spent a lot of time in rationalist and EA-adjacent corners of the internet where suffering-focused ethics keeps resurfacing, and a thread I was following recently circled back, as these things always do, to David Benatar's asymmetry argument for antinatalism (from Better Never to Have Been). Someone in the comments asserted that Benatar was really just formalizing a move Schopenhauer had already made a century and a half earlier, and pointed to "On the Sufferings of the World" as evidence. That sent me to the primary text instead of trusting the gloss, and I've found myself stuck on how much weight the parallel can actually bear.

Here is the passage (T. Bailey Saunders' translation, from the collection published in English as Studies in Pessimism) that I keep turning over:

"I know of no greater absurdity than that propounded by most systems of philosophy in declaring evil to be negative in its character. Evil is just what is positive; it makes its own existence felt. [...] It is the good which is negative; in other words, happiness and satisfaction always imply some desire fulfilled, some state of pain brought to an end. [...] every state of welfare, every feeling of satisfaction, is negative in its character; that is to say, it consists in freedom from pain, which is the positive element of existence. It follows, therefore, that the happiness of any given life is to be measured, not by its joys and pleasures, but by the extent to which it has been free from suffering—from positive evil."

What I can't pin down is exactly what kind of claim this "positive/negative" distinction is meant to be—a metaphysical thesis about the will and lack, a purely definitional point about what "satisfaction" means, or an empirical-psychological generalization about how pleasure and pain actually feel—and how, if at all, it maps onto Benatar's own four-part asymmetry: the presence of pain is bad, the presence of pleasure is good, the absence of pain is good even when there is no one around to enjoy it, and the absence of pleasure is bad only when someone is thereby deprived. Is Schopenhauer's passage doing the same logical work as Benatar's asymmetry, just in cruder form, or is it a different and considerably stronger claim—one that would deliver a straightforward "pain outweighs pleasure in every life" verdict without needing any of Benatar's careful hedging about absences and non-existent subjects? And if it is the latter, does Schopenhauer's simpler route dodge the standard objections about nobody being "worse off" for never having existed, or does it just quietly assume its way past them? I would like a close reading of the passage on its own terms first, and only then a verdict on how it actually relates to Benatar's argument, since I suspect the two get conflated more often than is warranted.
