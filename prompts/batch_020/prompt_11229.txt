I'm moderating the Q&A after a colloquium talk by a philosopher who has spent much of her career defending a broadly Kantian account of moral worth — the view, rooted in the Groundwork, that an action has moral worth only if it is done from the motive of duty rather than merely in accordance with duty, refined along the lines of the "limiting condition" reading: duty need not be the agent's sole or even most salient motive on a given occasion, so long as it is disposed to take over whenever her other motives would otherwise lead her astray. She likes to illustrate this with the image of duty as a thermostat — silent most of the time, but ready to engage the moment something drifts out of range — and in her talk she leaned heavily on the standard cases: the shopkeeper who gives correct change from self-interest versus one who does so from duty, and the sympathetic helper who acts from bare compassion.

Since most of the audience will already know these textbook cases, I'd like the questions to move past them quickly. Please draft eight to ten questions for the Q&A, each aimed at a real pressure point in the view rather than an invitation to restate it, and include at least one question — with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give — on each of the following: (1) the "cold-hearted" charge that her picture ranks a reluctant, dutiful helper above someone who helps from warm and spontaneous sympathy, and whether the limiting-condition move genuinely escapes this or merely relocates it to a background disposition; (2) Michael Smith's fetishism worry, and specifically whether having duty stand guard over one's other motives doesn't itself amount to a fetishistic, de dicto concern with rightness rather than a direct, de re concern for the person being helped; (3) how the thermostat metaphor is supposed to survive the fact that the relevant counterfactual — what she would have done had inclination pointed the other way — is never actually tested in the sequence of events that occurred, so that "the thermostat was set correctly" looks unfalsifiable in any given case; and (4) how the account is meant to extend to cases — a small child's spontaneous kindness, or a Huckleberry-Finn-style agent who does the right thing while explicitly believing it wrong — where the action seems clearly admirable despite the agent's apparent lack of anything like the concept of duty. Arrange the questions so each builds on the last rather than reading as a checklist, and end with whichever of the four you think leaves the least room for a comfortable answer.
