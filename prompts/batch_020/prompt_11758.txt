I'm several drafts into a novel built on a "parallel lives" conceit: every significant fork in my protagonist's life is rendered not as a hypothetical "what if" but as a fully realized, ongoing world running alongside the one she actually inhabits. Wanting the device to rest on firmer ground than the usual hand-wavy talk of multiverses, I started reading David Lewis's modal realism, since it strikes me as the most serious philosophical attempt on record to argue that other possible worlds are exactly as concrete, populated, and real as our own, and not merely useful fictions or abstract shadows of it. The trouble is that the more carefully I read Lewis, the more I suspect his actual view quietly undermines the emotional effects I'm after instead of grounding them.

Three features of his position worry me specifically. First, because Lewis analyzes cross-world modal talk through counterpart theory instead of strict identity, the person living out an unchosen branch is never literally my protagonist under a different history, only a distinct individual who merely resembles her closely enough to count as her counterpart — which threatens to turn every "road not taken" scene into a story about a stranger. Second, because worlds are, on his view, causally and spatiotemporally isolated from one another, nothing that happens on one branch can affect, or even be perceived from, another; this leaves me unsure whether the longing, guilt, or rivalry I want my protagonist to feel toward her other selves is even a coherent state for her to be in, on this picture, or merely something I've imported from softer, more mystical multiverse stories. Third, because Lewis treats "actual" as an indexical term on a par with "here," no branch — including the one she currently occupies — turns out to be metaphysically privileged over any other, and I can't decide whether that ought to read as liberating or simply deflating for a character trying to make peace with the life she ended up living.

Could you set out what you take to be the most important implications of taking Lewis's modal realism seriously, as opposed to some looser everyday notion of parallel worlds, for a novel built on exactly this premise? I'm especially keen to know whether regret, rivalry, and felt significance across branches can be made sense of at all within his picture, and whether there is a principled way of writing around these difficulties short of quietly smuggling back in the trans-world identity and cross-world interaction that the story wants but that his view seems to rule out.
