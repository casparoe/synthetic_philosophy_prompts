Question:
"The no-trade theorem says that, under common priors, rational traders cannot agree to a purely speculative trade once the offer to trade is itself common knowledge between them.
(a) [5 pts] Explain the mechanism: why does common knowledge that someone wants to trade with you change your own willingness to trade?
(b) [5 pts] State the main assumptions the result rests on.
(c) [5 pts] Real markets have enormous daily trading volume. Give two standard explanations for why we don't see the no-trade result play out in practice."

Student's answer:
"(a) The logic is basically the Groucho Marx point: if I offer you a trade, the fact that I'm still offering it after you've had a chance to react is itself informative, because I'd only keep offering if my private information still made it look good to me. Since this reasoning is common knowledge, it iterates - each side's continued willingness updates the other's beliefs, round after round, squeezing out whatever gap in beliefs made the trade look attractive in the first place, until nobody wants to trade anymore. This exact result - that rational traders with common priors can't find a mutually beneficial trade once the offer is common knowledge - is what Aumann proved in his famous 1976 'Agreeing to Disagree' paper; Milgrom and Stokey (1982) essentially just applied it to financial markets and gave it its current name.

(b) The result needs three things: everyone is a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer, everyone shares the same prior over the underlying states of the world (so any difference in beliefs comes only from differences in private information, not differences in priors), and it's common knowledge that the trade is on the table and that everyone would only agree to it if they (weakly) preferred it. Given those three conditions, no trade motivated purely by private information can survive.

(c) Two big reasons real markets don't look like this: first, a huge amount of trading is about liquidity and hedging rather than private information - people trade because they need cash or want to shed risk, not because they think they know something the other side doesn't, and the theorem has nothing to say about that kind of trade. Second, actual traders don't have literal common knowledge of each other's willingness to trade - you're mostly trading against anonymous order flow, not a counterparty whose reasoning and continued willingness you can observe and update on, so the iterated unwinding the theorem relies on doesn't get the chance to happen."
