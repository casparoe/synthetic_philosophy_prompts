I'm a junior on my high school's Honor Council, a student-run body that hears cases under our school's honor code, and I need help drafting the recommendation memo for the hardest case we've had all year before our vote this Thursday. Our code includes a "non-toleration" clause: if you learn that a classmate has committed a violation, such as cheating or plagiarism, you are yourself in violation if you don't report it, and the policy carves out no exceptions. The student in front of us, Reese, found out that her closest friend had used a hidden phone to look up answers during a proctored final after the friend broke down and begged her not to tell anyone, since a report would mean expulsion right as college decisions were coming out. Reese kept the secret for about a month, until the same friend was caught a second time through an unrelated slip-up, and the ensuing investigation revealed that Reese had known the whole time. She's now charged with violating non-toleration herself, and by every account, including her own, she has clearly been miserable about the choice since well before anyone found out.

The council's disagreement isn't really about the facts anymore; it's about a question my ethics elective happened to raise the same week, namely whether a person can be caught in a genuine moral dilemma, where two real obligations collide with no way to honor both, or whether every apparent dilemma dissolves once you reason it through carefully enough, the way Kant apparently thought a well-ordered set of duties never truly conflicts. Half the council wants to read Reese's lingering guilt the way a philosopher like Bernard Williams might, as evidence that a real duty went unmet no matter what she chose, and to sanction her more lightly than someone who simply broke the rule out of indifference; the other half thinks "genuine dilemma" is just a flattering name for a promise that was never valid in the first place, since nobody can really be obligated to help a friend conceal wrongdoing, so Reese should get the standard non-toleration sanction like anyone else. Could you write the memo I'll circulate to the council? It should recommend a specific sanction or sanction range for Reese, propose a standard the council could apply to future "conflicting duties" pleas so we aren't improvising every time, and seriously engage the strongest version of whichever side's argument your recommendation doesn't adopt.
