I'm plotting a chapter in a novel in which a present-day classicist, somewhat against her better judgment, finds herself drawn into a long argument with a crypto-enthusiast friend, and I want her side of the exchange to rest on an honest reading of Aristotle rather than the thin "the Philosopher frowned on greed" gloss that turns up in most popular treatments. Before I draft the dialogue, I've been rereading the relevant stretches of Politics, Book I, and Nicomachean Ethics, Book V, and would like to work through the substance with you.

Aristotle separates household management (oikonomike), which acquires only as much as a household needs and so has a natural limit, from wealth-getting as such (chrematistike), which he treats as suspect exactly insofar as it slips loose from any limit tied to genuine use. Within that second category he singles out interest (tokos) as the most unnatural species of acquisition, since it generates money directly from money with no exchange of goods intervening at all — a kind of "breeding," trading on the fact that the Greek word for interest is also the word for offspring. I'd like to extend this apparatus, as faithfully to Aristotle's actual arguments as you can manage, to two structurally different mechanisms in decentralized finance: (1) proof-of-stake staking, in which a participant locks up tokens and is issued newly minted tokens simply for holding and validating, with no counterparty on the other side of a trade, and (2) supplying tokens to an automated market-making liquidity pool, where the return instead comes from fees paid by third parties who are actually swapping assets through the pool.

Could you work out which of these two practices Aristotle would most plausibly treat as a modern instance of tokos, and which, if either, he might instead assimilate to ordinary exchange or to the "measuring" role he assigns to currency in Nicomachean Ethics V.5 — and are there features of either mechanism, such as impermanent loss, or the fact that a staking reward is typically denominated in an asset with no independent use-value at all, that would genuinely strain his categories rather than sliding neatly into them? I'd rather avoid a pat verdict along the lines of "Aristotle would have hated crypto" and instead see exactly where his own distinctions do and don't carry over.
