"Clifford's argument starts from his famous shipowner case: a man owns an old emigrant ship, has nagging doubts about whether its seaworthy, but talks himself out of them by trusting in Providence and sends it to sea anyway. When it sinks and everyone drowns, Clifford says the owner is guilty of their deaths, even though he sincerely believed the ship was fine. The point is that the rightness or wrongness of a belief isn't about whether it happens to be true, its about whether you earned the right to hold it given your evidence. This is why Clifford's famous formulation is, as he put it, that a wise man 'proportions his belief to the evidence' - it's always wrong, for anyone, to believe on insufficient evidence, no exceptions. His deeper reason is social rather than just personal: beliefs aren't private, because acting on unearned beliefs makes us a more careless believer generally, and that carelessness spreads through a community, so credulity is basically a communal harm rather than just a personal risk.

James's 'The Will to Believe' is usually read as a direct reply to Clifford. James introduces the idea of a 'genuine option' - a choice between two hypotheses that is live (both are real possibilities for the person), forced (you can't avoid deciding by staying neutral), and momentous (the stakes are big and the chance won't come again). His central example is religious belief: you can't put off deciding whether to live as though God exists forever, because declining to decide already functions as deciding against it in practice, and the potential loss is just as big either way. Where the evidence genuinely can't settle the question, James says our 'passional nature' is allowed to tip the balance, because Clifford's advice to just withhold belief isn't actually the neutral, safe option it pretends to be - its a choice too, one that guarantees you lose out on the truth if the religious hypothesis happens to be correct. Basically James is arguing that since we can't escape betting one way or the other in cases like this, and reason has run out of things to say, it's fine to let hope or desire make the call - which is the same basic logic he later uses to argue that truth itself is just whatever works best for us to believe.

I think James wins this one. Clifford's principle sounds airtight in the abstract, but it quietly assumes we always have the luxury of waiting for more evidence, and James shows that in enough real cases (love, trust, religion, even everyday risk-taking) that assumption is false. So James isn't really disagreeing with Clifford about ordinary beliefs, like what a thermometer reads - he's pointing out that Clifford's rule assumes every belief is like a science question, and once you notice that some beliefs aren't, the 'always, everywhere, for anyone' part collapses into something much narrower."
