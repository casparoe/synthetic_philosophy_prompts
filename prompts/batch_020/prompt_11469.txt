I've been working through the McMahan–Walzer debate on the ethics of killing in war, and one particular move keeps bothering me. McMahan grounds combatants' liability to lethal defensive force in their being morally responsible for contributing to an unjust threat, deliberately decoupling this from culpability, so that even a non-culpably mistaken unjust combatant — someone who reasonably but falsely believes their cause to be just — remains liable to attack, while combatants on the just side generally retain their right not to be attacked. Please argue against this responsibility-without-culpability move and defend a conception of liability on which non-culpably mistaken unjust combatants are not liable to be killed, such that something like Walzer's moral equality of combatants is preserved at the level of deep morality rather than surviving merely as a practical or legal convention.
