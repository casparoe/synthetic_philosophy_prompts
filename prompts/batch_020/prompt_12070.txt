I'm an Army captain with an undergraduate philosophy background, which is presumably why my battalion keeps appointing me investigating officer whenever a soldier applies for conscientious objector discharge. This time I've drawn a genuinely hard file. The applicant, a specialist in my company, does not claim to oppose war as such: under questioning by the chaplain he said plainly that he would take up arms tomorrow if the country were invaded. What he says he can no longer do in good conscience is keep fighting in the current deployment, a position he traces to a specific change in the mission's character a few months in, from securing the population to something closer to punitive raids. He was careful to tell the chaplain that he isn't certain the deployment is unjust, only that he can no longer meet the level of confidence in his cause that he has come to believe killing requires, and that almost no real war short of unambiguous self-defense against invasion clears that bar for him. Current doctrine is unambiguous that objection to a particular war, however sincere, doesn't qualify for 1-O or 1-A-O status -- only objection to war "in any form" does -- and on its face this looks like exactly the selective objection that Gillette v. United States put outside the exemption. But I've spent the last few years reading rationalist- and EA-adjacent writing on moral uncertainty, alongside Walzer's case for the moral equality of combatants and McMahan's revisionist argument against it, and it seems to me this soldier has arrived at something close to McMahan's position entirely on his own, which makes his objection look less like war-weariness in philosophical dress and more like a genuine, generalizable commitment that simply happens to authorize almost no wars.

Please draft the investigating officer's report and recommendation that I'll forward to our service's conscientious objector review board before the packet closes early next week -- concise enough that the board will actually read it in full. I need a clear bottom line: grant full objector status, grant noncombatant status only, deny, or return the case for a further hearing, with the reasoning framed in terms the board, who are not philosophers and will be working through dozens of these files this cycle, can actually apply here and to the next borderline case. Whichever way you come down, take the losing side as seriously as the file supports: if you recommend granting, explain why this isn't simply selective objection dressed in the language of moral philosophy; if you recommend denial, explain why a soldier who reasons his way, unprompted, to something like McMahan's conclusion shouldn't get more consideration than current doctrine gives him.
