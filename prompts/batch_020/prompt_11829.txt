I began a PhD in theoretical cosmology this past year, and something that used to strike me as fun pop-science trivia — the idea that a rare thermal fluctuation could spontaneously assemble a brain, complete with false memories, out in the depths of an eternally expanding de Sitter universe — has turned into something I now actually have to hold a considered opinion about. My advisor works on the measure problem in eternal inflation, and for the last few weeks I've been helping prepare a response to a referee who objects that the measure my advisor favors implies our own vacuum is "Boltzmann-brain dominated": that is, that on this measure, the overwhelming majority of observers with experiences just like mine, across the eternal future, arise as short-lived statistical flukes rather than through ordinary stellar and biological evolution. One student in the group wants to modify the measure so as to avoid this consequence at almost any cost; a postdoc thinks that's a waste of effort, since she doubts the "problem" is anything more than a confusion about comparing infinite populations of observers and about which self-locating assumptions are doing the work. I have to help finalize our response in the next couple of weeks, and would like a careful outside opinion before I commit to a side.

Could you write an essay that tackles the underlying question head-on, rather than simply cataloguing the positions: is Boltzmann-brain domination a genuine theoretical defect, one that gives us a legitimate reason to disfavor a cosmological model, vacuum, or measure, or is it better diagnosed as an artifact of applying self-locating, anthropic-style reasoning where it doesn't actually apply? Please engage seriously with at least one strong argument on each side. On the side that takes the problem seriously, there is the point that a theory on which we are typical members of an ensemble overwhelmingly composed of Boltzmann brains seems to undercut its own evidential support, since a typical Boltzmann brain has no business trusting either its apparent memories or its apparent grasp of physics. On the skeptical side, there is the worry that relative frequencies of observers are not obviously well defined over infinite, measure-dependent populations, and that the whole argument may covertly presuppose one specific and contestable resolution of the reference class problem in anthropic reasoning — something in the spirit of Bostrom's Self-Sampling Assumption, rather than, say, the Self-Indication Assumption or no such principle at all. Please don't leave it at "reasonable people disagree": I need an actual verdict, defended, even if it has to be a qualified one along the lines of "this counts as a real problem only under assumption X."
