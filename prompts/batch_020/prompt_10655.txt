I'm a software engineer who follows an online reading group organized around effective altruism and longtermist philosophy. Last week's shared reading was a short write-up on "evidential cooperation in large worlds" (ECL): roughly, the idea that a large or infinite universe or multiverse almost certainly contains vast numbers of agents facing decisions structurally like ours, some of whom reason about them in relevantly similar ways. Because our decision procedure is correlated with theirs, acting cooperatively toward other value systems now -- rather than single-mindedly pursuing our own -- is evidence that similarly-minded distant agents do the same, so everyone ends up better off than under unilateral optimizing, even though none of us can causally affect one another. Two regulars, Owen and Nadia, spent the evening arguing about this in the comments, and I've reconstructed the exchange below. Neither has formal training in philosophy, but both clearly know the material, and I honestly can't tell who's getting the better of it.

1. Owen: This is really just Hofstadter's superrationality at cosmic scale. In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against your own psychological twin, you should cooperate, because your reasoning and theirs are similar enough that your choice is strong evidence of theirs, with no causal link required. A large or infinite universe almost certainly contains many agents reasoning about this very argument in relevantly similar ways. If we act cooperatively toward other value systems today, that's evidence agents like us elsewhere do too, so everyone ends up better off than under unilateral optimizing.

2. Nadia: That only bites if you've already accepted evidential decision theory -- choosing whichever action is the best news about the world, rather than whichever action actually causes the best outcome. A causal decision theorist will say your cooperating today has zero causal effect on some agent you'll never meet, so treating your choice as though it "brings about" their cooperation just rewards you for good news without making anything true. You're building a cosmic ethics on a decision theory most philosophers still treat as a contested minority view.

3. Owen: But the twin case is exactly where causal decision theory looks worst: almost everyone's gut says you should cooperate with your twin, and causal decision theory can't explain that without extra help. If evidential decision theory gets the twin case right and causal decision theory gets it wrong, that's not some idiosyncratic commitment doing the work in ECL -- it's the same widely shared intuition, just applied at a larger scale.

4. Nadia: The twin case earns that intuition by stipulation: we're simply told the twin runs your exact algorithm, so the correlation is total and given. Nothing like that is available for agents scattered across a large or infinite universe -- we can't observe them, so we can't check whether their disposition to reason about ECL specifically, rather than merely resembling us in some vague general way, actually correlates with ours. Borrowing the twin case's plausibility for a premise that can't be checked even in principle is where the argument overreaches.

5. Owen: You don't get to check what your twin does before you act, either -- the whole point of superrationality is that the correlation has to be justified beforehand, not verified afterward. Whether it's justified for far-flung agents is a real question, and maybe the honest answer is "weaker than in the twin case, but not zero." That's a reason to estimate the correlation, not to discard the framework.

6. Nadia: Grant some correlation, then. You still have to decide which slice of the vast space of possible value systems to cooperate with and how heavily to weight each one. Nudge that weighting slightly and the compromise you're supposedly bound to shifts wildly, especially once rare-but-numerous value systems enter the mix. So even a successful defense of the correlation doesn't hand you a determinate policy -- it just relocates the arbitrariness from "should we cooperate at all" to "how exactly do we weight it," which is where the real disagreement actually lives.
