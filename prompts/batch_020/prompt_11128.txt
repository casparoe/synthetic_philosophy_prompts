A friend of mine and I spent much of the walk home from our decision theory reading group last night arguing about whether the "coherence arguments" for expected utility maximization really deliver what their proponents claim. The occasion was Eliezer Yudkowsky's essay "Coherent decisions imply consistent utilities," which my friend reads as showing that any agent facing a long enough sequence of choices will be dragged, on pain of leaving free money on the table, toward acting as if it maximizes some real-valued utility function. When I asked why an agent couldn't simply decline to have any preference at all between certain pairs of options, rather than being forced to rank everything, my friend retreated to a narrower and admittedly more careful line of defense: the money-pump argument for the completeness axiom specifically, of the sort developed at length in Johan Gustafsson's 2022 book on the subject. There, an agent with a genuine gap in its preferences (the example under discussion was a career as a clarinettist versus a career in law) can apparently be walked through a short sequence of trades, none of which its incomplete preferences give it any standing to refuse, that leaves it strictly worse off than where it started.

I remained unconvinced, because the whole procedure seemed to rely on treating each offered trade as a fresh, memoryless choice rather than as one step in an ongoing policy. An agent that keeps track of which options it has already turned down, and simply refuses ever to accept something ranked below one of those, appears to escape the entire sequence without needing a complete preference ordering anywhere. My friend regarded this as a kind of evasion: a rule invented on the spot to defuse one particular pump is suspicious by its very design, and in any case Gustafsson devotes a whole section of the book to arguing that this general style of history-sensitive, "resolute" response to money pumps does not ultimately work. We went around in circles for the better part of an hour and never settled it.

I would like your help making my side of the case properly. Could you argue that the money-pump argument for completeness fails to show that rational agents must have complete preferences, on the grounds that its force depends on a substantive and independently unmotivated assumption about which choice rules an agent is permitted to use across a sequence of decisions, rather than following from any minimal requirement of rationality on its own? I would especially like you to engage with my friend's strongest objection – that a choice rule custom-built to survive a known pump is question-begging in exactly the way any ad hoc rescue is – rather than brushing it aside.
