I sit on my university's academic-integrity appeals panel, and we've got a case that's turned into a weird methodology fight instead of a factual one. An instructor flagged a student for cheating, but the only real evidence is the proctor's fuzzy recollection: "I'm not certain, but for maybe half a minute the student's eyes kept drifting toward their neighbor's exam, and if you made me put a number on it, I'd say 65-70% that it was deliberate." Seperate from that, we've got the fact that the two students landed on the exact same four wrong multiple-choice answers out of five questions -- suspicious, but each question only had a couple plausible wrong options, so it's not damning by itself. Two panel members have locked horns over how to even combine these two bits of evidence, and it's actually swinging what we'd recommend.

One member wants to just plug the proctor's "65-70%" in as the probability that "copying happened" and run ordinary Bayesian updating from there. Another member -- she's auditing a formal epistemology seminar this term -- says that's sneaking in more certainty than we actually have, since the proctor never became sure of any one proposition, and that what we should really be doing is something more like Jeffrey conditionalization on the whole space of what-happened possibilities. She's also pointed out, and this is the part that's thrown the room, that if we weigh the wrong-answer overlap before the proctor's testimony instead of after, we don't obviously land on the same confidence level, which bothers her a lot for a panel that's supposed to treat similarly-evidenced students the same regardless of which folder got read first. I don't need a lecture on probability theory, I need an actual memo I can circulate before we reconvene: a recommendation on this student under our "clear and convincing evidence" standard, plus a call on whether the panel should adopt some standing method for weighing this kind of hedged, partial-confidence testimony going forward, or whether that's a fool's errand given how made-up a proctor's "65-70%" really is. Please actually engage with whichever side you don't recommend -- I don't want a memo that just pretends to consider the other view before brushing it aside.
