I've been reporting on the weirder corners of the AI safety world for a longer piece, and one idea that keeps coming up in the rationalist/MIRI-adjacent writing is "acausal trade" -- the notion that two agents who can never causally interact with each other (they're in causally seperate regions of an infinite universe, say, or different Everett branches, or one only exists as a simulation the other is running of it) can still end up cooperating. Neither ever sends the other a signal; each one just reasons that the other, being a similar kind of reasoner, would predict its behavior and act accordingly, so it's rational to act as though a deal has been struck even though no message and no cause ever actually passes between them. I first ran into a version of this through the whole Roko's Basilisk saga a few years back, and it turns out that's just the ugliest, most clickbaited special case of a much bigger idea that people trace back to Hofstadter's "superrationality" and Newcomb's problem.

The more I sit with it though, the more it reminds me of something much older: Leibniz's monads. Famously "windowless," zero causal traffic between them whatsoever, and yet the whole created universe apparently ticks along in perfect coordination anyway, because God pre-established the harmony between all of them back at creation. No monad ever pushes on any other monad, but the outcomes line up regardless. My editor thinks I've lost it trying to work 17th-century metaphysics into a piece about AI decision theory, but I can't shake the feeling theres a real structural resemblance here and not just a cute "no interaction, yet coordination" pun.

So here's what I'm actually asking you: if you explained modern acausal trade to Leibniz in full -- agents modeling each other across regions of reality they can never reach, cooperating purely because of what their shared reasoning implies about one another -- would he recognize it as basically his own system with God quietly pulled out of the middle? Or does something in his actual commitments, like the principle of sufficient reason or his idea that our world was picked out as the best among the infinite possible worlds God surveyed, make him resist that framing? I'd love the version of this answer that actually engages with how his system works mechanically, rather than one that just notes the surface-level similarity and moves on.
