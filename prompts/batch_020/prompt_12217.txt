In the closing sections of the Philosophy of Right, Hegel argues that because no power stands above sovereign states, a genuine dispute between them can ultimately be settled only by war; he then goes further, insisting in a well-known passage (§324) that war has a positive ethical moment rather than being merely destructive, and comparing its effect on the "ethical health" of nations to the way wind keeps the sea from stagnating during a prolonged calm. He turns this same analysis against Kant's proposal, in Perpetual Peace, for a federation of states that would secure lasting peace among its members: on Hegel's view, any such federation still rests on the particular, contingent will of each sovereign member, so the very problem it was meant to solve simply reappears one level up. A friend of mine who works on Kant's political philosophy thinks this argument is little more than an organic metaphor dressed up as philosophy; I suspect Hegel has actually put his finger on a real structural problem for any Kantian-style federation, and neither of us has managed to convince the other.

Could you write a dialogue of roughly 700 to 1000 words between a committed Hegelian and a committed Kantian cosmopolitan who are trying to work through exactly this disagreement? The Hegelian should reconstruct Hegel's case for why an international federation cannot do the adjudicating work Kant wants from it, and why war nonetheless plays a positive role in the ethical life of a people, without letting this collapse into simply asserting that war is good. The Kantian should respond using the actual resources of Kant's essay — the link between republican constitutions and reluctance to go to war, the idea of the federation (foedus pacificum) as a surrogate for a world republic rather than a world state, and the roles of cosmopolitan right and commercial interdependence in making war gradually less likely — and should press directly on whether the stagnation metaphor is doing real argumentative work or is simply question-begging naturalism in tension with treating individual citizens as ends in themselves. Each side should engage with the other's strongest point rather than talking past it, and the dialogue should end with the two interlocutors agreeing on what, at bottom, their disagreement really comes down to — for example, whether it is a narrowly institutional dispute about international law, or a symptom of the deeper quarrel between Hegel and Kant over whether a moral "ought" that lacks full actuality can do any genuine normative work.
