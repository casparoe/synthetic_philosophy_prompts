At a dinner party last month, two friends of mine from grad school, Jonah and Camille, got into a long, increasingly technical argument about the Doomsday Argument that outlasted the meal. Roughly:

(1) Jonah made the standard self-sampling case for doom: you should reason as a random draw from everyone who will ever live, so finding yourself this early -- among the first hundred-odd billion humans -- is far less surprising if the total human population turns out modest than if it runs to trillions more, which should shift your credence toward an earlier end.

(2) Camille said that only follows under the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA). Its rival, the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) -- your existence favors hypotheses on which more observers exist, since there were more "slots" for you -- exactly cancels the shift, leaving you with whatever risk estimate ordinary evidence supports.

(3) Jonah answered that SIA buys this at an absurd price: Bostrom's "presumptuous philosopher," who tells physicists choosing between two equally supported cosmological theories -- one with a trillion trillion observers, the other a trillion trillion trillion -- to skip their planned experiment, since SIA alone makes the more populous theory overwhelmingly likely. Out-arguing a physics experiment from an armchair is a worse cost than the one SIA is meant to avoid.

(4) Camille said: bite the bullet -- we already accept this move to dissolve fine-tuning surprise, since no observer could find itself in a universe incompatible with its own existence, and the presumptuous philosopher just runs that logic on bigger numbers. She then turned Jonah's complaint back on him: SSA needs a reference class (all humans? all observers? all observer-moments?), and the force of the doomsday shift -- perhaps its very existence -- depends on that choice, which looks more like an artifact of how finely you carve up "observers relevantly like me" than a fact about the world. Even Bostrom ended up swapping "observers" for "observer-moments" to dodge related paradoxes.

(5) Jonah granted the reference class problem is real but called it a problem of precisification, not principle: there's presumably a best way to set the class, and the shift survives many reasonable candidates. The presumptuous philosopher isn't like that -- it's what SIA says on any setup, and it hands cosmology to headcounts. Something hard to fully specify is a lesser sin than something confidently wrong.

We got to dessert and never resolved it. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move?
