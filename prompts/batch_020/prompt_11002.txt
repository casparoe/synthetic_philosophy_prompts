I have no formal training in philosophy, but over the past year or so I have become quite drawn to the idea of Ubuntu, the Southern African concept often summed up as "a person is a person through other persons." I first came across it in a book by Desmond Tutu that a friend lent me, and I have since read around the topic on my own. What has really stuck with me is a stronger version of the idea that I believe comes from the Nigerian philosopher Ifeanyi Menkiti, though I may be getting some of the details wrong: the claim that personhood is not something you simply possess from birth, but something that has to be earned gradually, by being incorporated into a community and coming to live up to its moral expectations, so that infants, and anyone who does not yet (or no longer) participate fully in communal life, count as persons only in an incomplete sense. I gather that other African philosophers, Kwame Gyekye among them, pushed back against this with a more moderate view that still grants individuals some standing independent of the community, but I have never really understood how that response is supposed to work in detail.

The reason this has stayed with me is quite personal. My grandmother's dementia has now progressed to the point where she no longer recognizes most of her family and can no longer really take part in the back-and-forth of relationships that Ubuntu seems to treat as central to being a person. If personhood really is constituted relationally in the strong sense Menkiti seems to describe, it is hard to avoid the uncomfortable thought that someone in her situation, or a newborn baby, or someone permanently cut off from their community through exile or illness, would have to count as less of a person than someone fully embedded in a thriving social network. That conflicts sharply with the instinct most of us have that these are exactly the people whose moral status we should be most careful to protect.

Could you give a concrete example, real or a carefully constructed hypothetical, that brings out this tension as sharply as possible, and then say how serious you think the resulting problem really is? I would like to know whether this counts as a genuine, fatal difficulty for strong relational views of personhood of the kind Menkiti defends, or whether there is a good way for defenders of Ubuntu (perhaps along the lines Gyekye was trying to draw) to accommodate cases like these without simply abandoning what makes the relational view distinctive in the first place.
