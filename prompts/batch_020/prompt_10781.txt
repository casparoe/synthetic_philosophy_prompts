I'm a PhD student in philosophy working mainly on comparative and Confucian ethics, and I've been asked to give a short talk in my department's work-in-progress series, where the audience spans several subfields and can't be assumed to know the primary texts. Below is the draft. I'd like your comments before I send it to the organizers.

Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont have argued, across several books, that Confucian ethics is best understood not as a variant of virtue ethics or consequentialism but as a distinct "role ethics": a person just is the sum of their particular roles and relations — son, father, ruler, friend — rather than an autonomous individual who happens to stand in those relations. On this view there is no self, and no principle, that exists prior to or independent of one's roles; what is normatively basic is always something like acting well as a father toward this particular son, not acting on a rule that fathers-in-general should follow. Rights talk, and any impartial standpoint from which roles could themselves be ranked or overridden, are treated as imports from a liberal individualism foreign to the tradition.

I want to press this view with a case Mencius himself raises. His student Tao Ying asks what Shun, as Son of Heaven, should do if his father Gusou killed a man, given that Gao Yao, Shun's minister of justice, would have to enforce the law (usually numbered Mencius 7A35, though I'd want to double-check that before this goes anywhere public). Mencius's answer has two parts. First, Gao Yao goes ahead and arrests Gusou; Shun has no standing, simply as ruler, to stop him. Second, Shun does not thereby just let the law run its course and remain on the throne. He abdicates — gives up being Son of Heaven entirely — and then, no longer ruler but only a son, spirits his father away into hiding and lives out his days with him, by Mencius's account perfectly content, the empire forgotten.

What strikes me is that neither role simply wins. The ruler-role doesn't override the father-son bond (Shun never uses the throne to shield Gusou), and the father-son bond doesn't override the ruler-role either, at least not while Shun still holds it (he doesn't obstruct Gao Yao). Instead Shun dissolves the conflict by exiting one role and only then acting, extralegally, from the other. That looks like a different kind of move than "acting well within a role": it is a judgment about which role to go on occupying, forced by the fact that the two can no longer be jointly honored. I don't see how to state the content of that judgment in role vocabulary alone — "a good ruler steps down when..." can't be filled in without smuggling in exactly the cross-role comparison that role ethics denies we ever need.

The obvious reply is that knowing when to withdraw is itself a familiar role-virtue, so nothing outside the roles is doing the work. But this only relocates the question: elsewhere Confucian exemplars are expected to let public duty override private feeling without withdrawing at all, so something is sorting cases into "withdraw" and "stay and prioritize" that role-content alone doesn't supply. The strongest version of the reply, I think, appeals to something like Mencius's own notion of quan (權), situational "weighing" — explicitly not a further rule but a kind of case-sensitive discretion, and arguably congenial to role ethics precisely because it is already anti-principle in spirit. But then I want to know whether quan is itself just a role-appropriate skill, filled in relationally like everything else, or whether invoking it quietly concedes a locus of judgment — whoever is doing the weighing across roles — that isn't constituted by any one of the roles being weighed. I can't tell whether that's a real problem for role ethics or just a restatement of it in more dramatic language, and that's more or less where the draft currently ends.

What I'd find most useful:

1. Whether I've represented Ames and Rosemont, and the Mencius case, fairly — I'm working from memory of the secondary literature rather than a fresh rereading, so flag anything I've flattened.
2. Whether the quan-based reply actually rescues role ethics, or whether my closing worry about it is on the right track, or whether I'm missing a better reply altogether.
3. Whether this reads as a real problem for Ames and Rosemont specifically, or as a more general problem for any ethics that tries to do without role-independent standards — I keep going back and forth on how large a claim to make, and fifteen minutes isn't long enough to hedge my way through both.
