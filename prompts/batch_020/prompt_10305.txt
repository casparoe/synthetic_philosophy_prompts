A friend and I argued for hours last night about whether the Löbian obstacle marks a genuine, permanent limit on self-trusting formal agents, or merely reflects an unnecessarily rigid notion of trust. I defended the pessimistic view: Yudkowsky and Herreshoff showed that, on pain of Löb's theorem, a proof-based agent can only certify a successor running on a strictly weaker system than its own — a limitation structurally akin to Gödel's second incompleteness theorem — and every fix I know of relocates the problem rather than removing it: descending hierarchies of ever-weaker successor theories continue only by conceding that trust always flows downward in strength, and Christiano et al.'s attempt to sidestep proof altogether — a real loophole in Tarski's classical result on the undefinability of truth — was later shown to be unsound, reproducing the same difficulty as a "procrastination paradox." My friend countered that I was mistaking a defect of one idealized formalism — unbounded, exact, proof-theoretic self-trust — for a fact about self-trust as such, citing Critch's bounded reformulation of Löb's theorem for resource-limited agents as evidence that realistic self-trust doesn't require the impossible guarantee I was chasing.
