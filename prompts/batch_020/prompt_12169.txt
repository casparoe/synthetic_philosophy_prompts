Here's the question she set:

"Zermelo's 1904 proof of the Well-Ordering Theorem, which relied on what came to be called the Axiom of Choice, provoked immediate controversy. (a) State the Axiom of Choice and sketch why it's equivalent to both Zorn's Lemma and the Well-Ordering Theorem. (b) Describe the objection raised against Zermelo's proof in the 1905 'Cinq lettres' exchange between Baire, Borel, Hadamard, and Lebesgue, and say which of the four defended Zermelo and which opposed him. (c) Gödel (1938) and Cohen (1963) later showed that AC is independent of the other axioms of ZF. Does that result vindicate the objection from part (b)? Discuss with reference to Gödel's own view of the matter."

And here's the student's answer:

"(a) The Axiom of Choice says that for any collection of nonempty sets there is a function picking out one element from each of them, even when the collection is infinite and there's no rule specifying the choices. It's equivalent to the Well-Ordering Theorem because given a choice function you can use transfinite recursion to keep picking a 'next' unchosen element, and this process well-orders the whole set (it has to terminate rather than running past the set, by a Hartogs-type argument); conversely a well-ordering hands you a choice function for free, just take the least element of each set under that order. Zorn's Lemma follows the same way: run the same recursion to build a chain in the poset, and if it never reached a maximal element you could always extend it, contradicting the fact that the recursion has to run out of room eventually; the converse direction treats partial choice functions as the poset and shows a maximal one has to be total.

(b) In 1905 Baire, Borel, Hadamard, and Lebesgue published an exchange of letters about Zermelo's argument. Borel and Lebesgue actually thought the proof was fine, comparing an arbitrary infinite sequence of choices to things analysts already accept without complaint, like the existence of a supremum nobody constructs term by term. Hadamard was the skeptic of the group: he argued that a 'choice' made all at once over an infinite collection, with no rule for singling out any particular element, wasn't a legitimate mathematical operation, just an act of faith dressed up in symbols.

(c) Gödel showed in 1938 that ZF plus AC is consistent if ZF is, and Cohen showed in 1963 that ZF plus the negation of AC is too, so AC is independent: neither it nor its negation follows from the rest of ZF. That looks like a vindication of Hadamard's objection from (b) - if the full strength of ZF can't settle the question, there may just be no further fact about whether AC is 'really' true, only a choice of whichever system is more convenient for the job at hand. That's more or less the moral Gödel drew from his own result: having proved the consistency half of the independence himself, he took it as evidence that statements like AC don't have a determinate truth value beyond what we stipulate, which is why he spent his later years treating new axioms as useful conventions rather than candidate truths."
