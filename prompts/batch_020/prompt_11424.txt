My mother, who taught philosophy for almost forty years and spent much of that career writing about moral responsibility in the tradition of P.F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment," died ten days ago, and I am giving her eulogy at the memorial service this coming Saturday. The room will be a genuine mix: a dozen or so former colleagues and graduate students who will know exactly what I mean if I mention reactive attitudes or the objective attitude, and a much larger group of relatives, neighbors, and old friends who have never encountered either phrase and are coming simply to grieve a woman they loved.

Before she died, she left unusually specific instructions about what the eulogy should avoid, instructions that only make sense in light of her own work. She had spent years arguing that resentment, gratitude, and indignation are not verdicts that stand or fall with some further fact about determinism, and that to "explain" a person's coldness or cruelty by pointing to their upbringing or their neurochemistry, however kindly intended, is to quietly stop relating to them as a fellow participant in ordinary human relations and start treating them instead as an object to be accounted for and managed. She knew she had been, by her own admission, difficult for long stretches of my childhood: withholding, cutting in arguments, slow to ever apologize. She explicitly did not want anyone standing up on Saturday to excuse any of that by saying she "couldn't help it," or that she was simply the product of her own cold parents. At the same time, she was just as clear that she did not want a eulogy consisting only of an itemized list of grievances, since on her own view resentment is just one reactive attitude among several, inseparable from the gratitude, admiration, and love that people who knew her well also felt and that she equally wanted acknowledged. I would like a eulogy of about five minutes that manages to hold her responsible for exactly the person she was, in a way that will read as honesty rather than score-settling to the family in the room, and as genuinely faithful to her life's philosophical work to the colleagues in it.
