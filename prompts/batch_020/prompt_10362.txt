I'm reporting a feature on how philosophers and AI researchers model the process of updating beliefs on conditional statements — claims of the form "if A, then probably B" — as opposed to flat, categorical observations. One scenario keeps coming up whenever I press specialists for a genuinely hard case: Bas van Fraassen's "Judy Benjamin problem," from the early 1980s. As I understand it, Judy Benjamin is a soldier dropped by helicopter into a region split evenly into Red and Blue territory, each further divided into two company areas — First and Second in Red, Third and Fourth in Blue — so that her prior credence in each of the four areas is one quarter. Before she can get her bearings, a garbled radio message tells her that if she is in Red territory, she is considerably more likely to be in the Second Company's area than the First. The trouble, several of my sources insist, is that the seemingly natural way of folding that conditional into her prior — updating to the "closest" distribution consistent with it — ends up moving her credence that she is in Red at all, even though the message appears to say nothing about Red versus Blue. Almost everyone I've spoken to finds that suspicious, but they disagree sharply about why, and my own grasp of the math isn't sharp enough to referee between them on their say-so.

For the piece, I'd like to actually work through the two main families of response rather than just gesture at "the problem" and move on, so I'm hoping you can compare them directly. The first is updating by minimizing relative information, or cross-entropy, with respect to the prior subject to the new conditional probability as a constraint — essentially a generalization of Jeffrey conditionalization, grouped under the label "probability kinematics," that produces the puzzling shift in the first place. The second is updating by imaging in something like David Lewis's sense: instead of redistributing probability via a global divergence-minimizing calculation, you shift the probability mass of each disfavored possibility onto whichever surviving possibility counts as "closest" to it, borrowing the same similarity-based machinery ordinarily used to evaluate conditionals and counterfactuals. Please compare these two approaches specifically as responses to the Judy Benjamin case. I'm especially interested in whether imaging actually secures the "no news about Red versus Blue" verdict that critics of cross-entropy minimization want, or whether it just relocates the same difficulty under a different name. And, standing back from the example, I'd like to know what the disagreement between the two suggests about whether conditionals should be treated as propositions we conditionalize on, as constraints we fit a distribution to, or as something that calls for its own distinct updating rule. Please justify whichever verdict you reach.
