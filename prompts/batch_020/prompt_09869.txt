Since retiring from a career in experimental physics two years ago, I have been filling my time with philosophy, and I recently came across I. J. Good's 1967 result that decision theorists call Good's theorem: for an expected-utility maximizer who is certain to use new evidence correctly — update on it, then choose the best available act — the expected value of a cost-free look at that evidence can never be negative, because at worst she can simply act exactly as she would have without it. This came up at dinner with two former colleagues, a biostatistician (Elena) and an actuary (Marcus), who ended up arguing about a mutual friend's refusal to open a sealed, already-completed predictive genetic test for a late-onset, currently untreatable neurodegenerative condition.

Elena said the friend was making a decision-theoretic error: opening the envelope costs nothing further, and Good's theorem guarantees a rational agent cannot be worse off in expectation for looking, since she could always act as if she hadn't. Marcus objected that the theorem's proof assumes utility attaches only to states of the world and to the actions taken in them; here no action changes either way, since there is no treatment, so the entire effect of opening the envelope falls on the friend's psychological state itself — exactly the sort of thing the theorem is silent about. Elena replied that this was a distinction without a difference: Good's argument is fully general over any utility function defined on final outcomes, and living the next thirty years believing herself a carrier is as much an outcome as any physical state, so once the outcome space is specified richly enough the mathematics goes through unchanged — Marcus had shown only that the psychological costs need estimating, not that the theorem fails to apply. Marcus answered that this move saves the theorem by emptying it: its interest always lay in the substantive, instrumental claim that extra facts cannot hurt you because you can act as though ignorant of them, and once "the sheer fact of now knowing" is folded into the outcome being evaluated, that instrumental claim has quietly been abandoned in favor of a tautology, which vindicates nothing about their friend's supposed error.

Please go through the exchange move by move, saying who has the better of it at each stage, and close by stating whether Elena's original charge — that leaving the envelope sealed is a decision-theoretic mistake — survives Marcus's rebuttal, or whether both of them are missing something.
