"Peter van Inwagen's Consequence Argument is often presented as the strongest argument for incompatibilism about free will. Explain (a) the basic structure of the argument, (b) how a classical (Humean, conditional-analysis) compatibilist would try to resist it, and (c) how a Frankfurt-style compatibilist would try to resist it. My classmate's answer was:

"The Consequence Argument holds that if determinism is true, our present actions are simply the necessary consequences of the laws of nature together with events in the distant past. Since no one has any choice about the laws of nature, and no one has any choice about what happened before they were born, no one has any choice about the actions that follow from these either. So if determinism is true, no one ever truly has a choice about anything they do.

The classical compatibilist resists this by giving a conditional analysis of 'could have done otherwise': to say that I could have done otherwise just means that I would have done otherwise if I had chosen or wanted to. On this reading, determinism poses no threat, because even in a fully determined world it can still be true that I would have acted differently had my desires been different.

The Frankfurt-style compatibilist is really making the same move in a different guise. Frankfurt's cases, where a counterfactual intervener stands ready to force the agent's choice but never actually has to act, are designed to show that the agent could still have done otherwise in the relevant conditional sense — if circumstances had been slightly different, the agent would have chosen differently. So both compatibilist strategies ultimately come down to the same idea: unpack 'could have done otherwise' conditionally, and show that determinism does not rule out the conditional being true.

Because van Inwagen accepts the Consequence Argument, he concludes that free will is an illusion: since determinism rules out free will, and the world is, as far as physics tells us, deterministic, no one is ever really morally responsible for what they do."
