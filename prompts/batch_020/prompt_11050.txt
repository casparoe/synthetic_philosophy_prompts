Last night, at what was meant to be a quiet dinner before tomorrow's wedding, my friend Priya and I fell right back into the argument we've been having since our first year of graduate school. She has spent her career defending Margaret Gilbert's account of shared intention: that when two people form a joint commitment, they constitute a genuine plural subject, a "we" bound by obligations that neither party can rescind alone. I've spent mine defending the rival, more deflationary line associated with Michael Bratman: that so-called shared agency is nothing but a stable structure of individual intentions, meshing sub-plans, and ongoing mutual responsiveness between two people who never stop, metaphysically, being two people. Neither of us budged, and I'm giving the toast at her wedding tonight. About a third of the guests are philosophers of action who will know exactly what's happening if I bring this into the speech; the rest are family who have never heard the phrase "joint commitment" and should still laugh, and maybe tear up, without knowing why the other third is laughing too. Write me a five-minute toast in which this actual disagreement -- plural-subject theory against reductive individualism about shared intention -- does the real work of the speech rather than sitting on top of it as decoration, earns one genuine laugh from the theory itself rather than from a generic wedding joke, and stays honest to both positions while still landing somewhere warmer than a draw.
