I spent about thirty years as a research physiologist working on growth hormone signalling, and now that I'm retired I've been making my way through the bioethics literature for fun (yes, I'm aware every retired scientist thinks their pet case is the profound one, bear with me). The case that wont leave me alone is the old growth-hormone-for-short-kids one: a kid with an actual GH deficiency gets hGH and it's called treatment, but a kid who's just as short, with completely normal hormone levels (what they call "idiopathic short stature"), gets the identical drug for the identical height gain, and that's supposedly "enhancement" -- somehow only the first kid's care counts as owed to him as a matter of justice. I know Boorse's idea of health as normal species functioning, and Daniels leaning on it to draw his justice line, but I cant tell if that idea actually survives contact with a case like this. Could you write me a dialogue -- say, two bioethicists hashing it out over drinks after a conference panel -- between one who thinks the normal-function line can be patched up and still do real moral work, and one who thinks the short-stature case shows it's hopeless and that the ethics needs to be grounded in something else instead (need, welfare, fair shares of opportunity, whatever). I want genuine back and forth here: each one's best comeback should actually land on the other's point, not just restate their own opening position.
