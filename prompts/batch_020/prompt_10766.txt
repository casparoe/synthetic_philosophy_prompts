I've spent much of the last decade writing about research misconduct and the replication crisis for a general-interest science magazine, and this year I rotated onto my newsroom's editorial standards committee, which has to sign off on any investigative piece before it runs. We're currently deadlocked over a story about a well-known social-psychology lab, and I'd like an outside opinion before I have to circulate a recommendation of my own.

Here's where things stand. Our reporter has four threads to work with. A former postdoc who left the lab on bad terms (call him Source A) describes specific irregularities in a dataset behind two of the lab's published papers, but plainly has a grudge. A current PhD student (Source B) backs up part of that account, though under questioning she admits that most of what she knows she heard from A before he left, plus one anomaly she says she noticed herself. A lab manager (Source C) won't confirm or deny anything on the record, but a colleague who was there recalls C joking about "creative" data cleaning at a conference two years ago. Separately, our reporter re-ran the numbers in the supplementary files of both papers herself and found statistically implausible duplicate participant IDs -- a finding that doesn't depend on any of the human sources. The committee has split three ways over whether this adds up to a publishable story. Our managing editor says we shouldn't run anything until we have one source who can stand behind the central claim on their own footing, independent of the others -- otherwise, she argues, this is really just A's account echoing through B, with a hearsay anecdote about C thrown in, dressed up to look like corroboration from three directions. Our investigations editor thinks that demand is unworkable: no single thread needs to be bulletproof on its own, he says, if several imperfect threads -- including the reporter's own independent statistical work -- all point the same way and none contradicts another. Our newest fact-checker isn't satisfied by either answer: she points out that if we always insist on verifying the source behind the source behind the source, we will never publish anything, since every account eventually bottoms out in somebody's word -- but she also won't go so far as to say any mutually consistent set of claims is good enough.

The university's own inquiry into the lab won't conclude for another six weeks, and the lab head is due to present the disputed results at a major conference in ten days, so waiting them out isn't really an option. Could you draft the memo I'll bring to Thursday's meeting? It should come down on one concrete path -- publish, hold, or publish with a specific reframing of how the claims are attributed -- along with a specific timeline, and it should engage the managing editor's worry on its merits rather than dispatching it with the observation that investigative journalism always involves imperfect sources.
