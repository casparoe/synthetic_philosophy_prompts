Hey, I'm putting together some notes for a talk I'm giving to the grad reading group next week and want a sanity check before I embarass myself in front of everyone. Nothing polished, just the core argument so far:

Herbert Simon's whole "bounded rationality" idea is that real agents can't exhaustively search for the optimal option, so they set some aspiration level and stop as soon as they find something that clears it -- satisficing instead of maximizing. Fine, that tracks. Now Michael Slote comes along later and defends "satisficing consequentialism," the view that an act can be right even when it doesn't produce the best available outcome, so long as it's good enough. My working hunch is that Slote's view is basically just Simon's bounded rationality dressed up in normative-ethics clothes -- once you take seriously that real moral agents are cognitively limited, satisficing just falls out as the sensible thing to defend, and Slote isn't really adding much thats philosophically new.

Can you poke holes in that for me? I've got a nagging feeling there's a reason people treat these as importantly different claims, something to do with the fact that Slote's own cases are rigged so the extra bit of good is basically free, no search costs involved -- but I can't quite articulate why that should matter, or whether there's a classic objection along these lines I should be citing instead. Tell me if I'm being unfair to Slote.
