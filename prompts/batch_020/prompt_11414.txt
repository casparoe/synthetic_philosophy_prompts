Question: "In Anarchy, State, and Utopia, Nozick briefly floats the idea of 'utilitarianism for animals, Kantianism for people.' (a) Explain why Nozick, given his general hostility to utilitarian reasoning, is even tempted by this combination. (b) State the reason Nozick himself gives for thinking the combination is unsatisfying."

Student answer: "Nozick's whole system is built on side constraints: rights function as boundaries on what may be done to a person, not as goals to be maximized, because there is no 'social entity' that benefits from a sacrifice — there are only individual people, each with their own separate life. That's why he rejects utilitarianism for humans, and it's also why this section is best read as his attempt to make room for the utilitarian case for animal liberation that Peter Singer had just laid out in Animal Liberation, without letting animal interests swamp human rights the way Singer's equal-consideration-of-interests principle threatened to. Animals, on Nozick's view, lack the reflective self-awareness that grounds a right against being used as a mere means, so ordinary side constraints don't obviously apply to them. But Bentham's question, 'can they suffer,' still seems to matter morally, since animals clearly can suffer, hence the temptation to import a utilitarian calculus just for them. Nozick ultimately backs away from the hybrid view for essentially the same reason he rejects utilitarianism generally: it's his utility monster problem again, just relocated — if animal welfare simply gets weighed against human benefit, a large enough aggregate of trivial human benefits could in principle outweigh even extreme animal suffering, which is exactly the swamping-by-aggregation result the utility monster was meant to make vivid."
