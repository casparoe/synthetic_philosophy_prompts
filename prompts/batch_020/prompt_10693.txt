So this happened at one of those recurring dinners a bunch of us from the local rationalist-diaspora meetup do, and I haven't been able to let it go since. Backstory: our friend Priti got a modest inheritance and is deciding whether to just put it in a normal total-market index fund or eat the higher fees on one of those funds that screens out factory farming and weapons manufacturers. She'll tell you shes like 85% on the view that buying shares on the secondary market doesn't actually do anything - no marginal causal effect on the company either way - so the screening is basically cosmetic, but she's got real, non-trivial credence (15-20%, by her own estimate) in a view where merely profiting off those industries makes you complicit in something bad, independent of whether your specific trade changes anything. She wasn't even in the room for the argument that followed though - it was two other friends of mine, Owen and Mira, who spent a good forty minutes fighting about how she *should* be reasoning about that split, not about which fund is actually correct. Both of them have actually read MacAskill's book on this stuff, not just absorbed the vibes secondhand, which is probably why neither one folded.

Here's roughly how it went. (1) Owen opened with: this is exactly what "maximize expected choiceworthiness" is for - weight what each theory says about the options by how confident you are in it and go with whatever comes out highest, same move as expected value reasoning under ordinary uncertainty. (2) Mira said thats fake rigor, since you can't add "15% times how bad complicity is" to "85% times how good the extra return is" when the two theories don't even agree on what's being measured - that's averaging a temperature with a distance and calling it a number. (3) Owen's comeback was the standard fix: normalize each theory onto its own 0-to-1 scale, best option scores 1, worst scores 0, so you're never comparing raw units. (4) Mira said that's exactly where the sleight of hand happens, because a theory that just says "complicity is wrong" doesn't hand you a cardinal scale to normalize in the first place, so whoever picks the scale has already picked the winner. (5) Owen admitted that's a real bruise, but said the alternative - just acting on whichever theory you personally find most plausible and letting the rest count for nothing - is worse, since it means treating a 15-20% credence exactly like a 0% one, which we'd call reckless if it were an ordinary probability instead of a moral one. (6) Mira replied that "reckless" smuggles in the very comparison that's in dispute, a second time, and that at least favoring your top theory means acting on something you actually think is true, whereas hedging means acting on a blend of views you think are probably false. (7) Owen fell back on the seatbelt case: we hedge against low-probability bad outcomes under ordinary uncertainty constantly, without anyone saying that means we don't "really believe" the car won't crash, so why is this different. (8) Mira's answer was that crashing and not-crashing are still measured in the same currency, your own survival and welfare, either way, but the two moral theories here don't even agree on the currency, so the seatbelt case just assumes the very thing they were arguing about.

Neither of them budged and it kind of broke up the table. Could you go through it move by move and say who's got the better of it at each point, not just an overall winner? I'm way more interested in the individual moves being right or wrong than in who "won," though if you do think one of them comes out ahead overall, feel free to say so at the end.
