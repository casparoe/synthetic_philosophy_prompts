As a journalist covering science and ideas, I'm working on a piece about laws that restrict "harmless wrongdoing" on essentially moralistic grounds, and I keep returning to the Hart–Devlin debate on legal moralism for a vocabulary to think with. Please write a short dialogue between a present-day defender of Patrick Devlin's position and a present-day defender of H.L.A. Hart's position, arguing over a case that sits awkwardly for both of them: Manuel Wackenheim, the French performer with dwarfism whose consensual, safety-padded "dwarf-tossing" act was banned by French authorities and, when he challenged the ban, upheld by both the country's highest administrative court and the UN Human Rights Committee, on the grounds that the act offended human dignity — a value the courts held he could not waive even by his own consent — notwithstanding his own objection that the ban simply cost him one of the only well-paid jobs open to him.

The Devlin character should draw substantively on the disintegration thesis and on Devlin's "reasonable man" test for the limits of toleration, and should address whether a dignity-based rationale is simply Devlin's argument in modern dress. The Hart character should press Hart's own objections — the positive/critical morality distinction, and Dworkin's worry that such tests cannot separate genuine moral conviction from prejudice — while also honestly confronting the fact that Hart himself accepted a degree of legal paternalism that Mill would have rejected. Have each side engage directly with the other's strongest move rather than a caricature of it, and let the dialogue end in genuine, unresolved disagreement.
