Me and my friend Theo got into a whole thing last night after a few beers about free will, and it ended in a draw cause neither of us could land a knockout blow, so now I want a tiebreaker. We were going back and forth on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility -- the one where you act the way you do because of the way you are, and you can never be truly responsible for the way you are, because to shape yourself that way on purpose you'd already need some earlier character doing the shaping, and that earlier character is just as unchosen as the one you ended up with, so you either regress backward forever or hit a starting point you never picked. Theo, who's doing a physics postdoc and loves bringing this stuff up, argued that the whole arguement secretly just assumes determinism, and since we now know from quantum mechanics that the universe has genuine indeterminism baked into it, premise 1 doesn't actually hold with the same force Strawson needs, so the regress never really gets going and real, robust moral responsibility survives after all. He was pretty smug about it, and by 2am I'd basically caved and agreed with him.

Thinking about it again this morning tho: if indeterminism sinks Strawson's argument that easily, why do free will researchers still treat the Basic Argument as one of the toughest live challenges to moral responsibility in intro courses and anthologies, instead of just filing it under "refuted by 20th-century physics," the way you'd file an old philosophical argument that assumed a flat earth? Am I missing something about how the argument is actually built, or about what indeterminism would even have to do to rescue responsibility, that makes Theo's "gotcha" less devastating than it felt at 2am?
