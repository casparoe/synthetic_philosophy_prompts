I went down a rabbit hole a while back reading Scott Garrabrant's "geometric rationality" posts on lesswrong, and one bit of it still bugs me. I'm a backend dev, not a philosopher, but I already knew Kelly betting from poker circles, and the logic makes sense there: you maximize E[log(wealth)] instead of plain E[wealth] so one bad bet doesn't wipe out a string of good ones, and the geometric mean of your wealth multiplier just handles that better then the arithmetic one does. Fine, got it. Then he turns around and uses basically the same move (maximize a geometric mean / expected log instead of an arithmetic one) to justify the Nash bargaining solution between different people, treating each person's utility kind of like a "possible world" you might turn out to be behind a veil of ignorance. That feels like a totally different kind of problem to me: one agent making repeated bets over time vs. combining different people's interests in a single one-shot decision. Is there an actual structural reason the geometric-mean trick legitimately carries over from the first case to the second, or is this more of a slick mathematical coincidence (logs and products popping up in both) that's been stretched further than it can really support? Would love the gut-level version of this, not just the equations.
