This term I'm taking a seminar on formal epistemology, and this week's session covers the Judy Benjamin problem and what it shows about updating credences on conditional information. In van Fraassen's original case, a soldier's prior is uniform over four possibilities generated by two partitions -- Red Territory versus Blue Territory, and First Company's area versus Second Company's area -- and she then receives a garbled radio message that pins down only the conditional probability of being in Second Company's area given that she is in Red Territory, set at 3/4 -- a message that seems, intuitively, to be silent on the question of Red versus Blue as such. Yet updating by minimizing cross-entropy relative to the prior, subject to that single constraint, shifts credence in Red Territory itself away from one-half, a result many have read as evidence that minimum cross-entropy updating illegitimately manufactures information the evidence does not contain.

Write an essay that weighs the main lines of response to this puzzle: (a) accepting the cross-entropy-minimizing update and arguing that the "irrelevance" intuition is simply mistaken; (b) rejecting minimum cross-entropy as the correct general rule for updating on conditional evidence, in favor of some alternative, such as the conditioning-based approach defended by Grove and Halpern or the revised rule proposed by Douven and Romeijn; and (c) any other diagnosis of the puzzle you find compelling. The essay should reach and defend a definite verdict on which response is closest to correct, rather than ending on a non-committal survey of the options.
