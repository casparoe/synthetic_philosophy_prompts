"Popper introduces verisimilitude to solve a problem generated by his own fallibilism: since no theory is ever verified, and some of the theories that matter most to the history of science — Newtonian mechanics, superseded by relativity — are ones we now take to be false, Popper wants a sense in which a later theory can still be 'closer to the truth' than an earlier one without smuggling in inductive confirmation. This has to come apart from probability: for Popper, a theory's content and its logical probability vary inversely, so the theory with the most content, and hence the best candidate for high verisimilitude, is thereby the least probable one, not the most.

Formally, let the content of a theory a, Ct(a), be the class of its non-tautological logical consequences. This splits into a truth-content, Ct_T(a), consisting of a's true consequences, and a falsity-content, Ct_F(a), consisting of its false consequences, so that Ct(a) = Ct_T(a) ∪ Ct_F(a). Popper's comparative definition (1963) then says that a has at least as much verisimilitude as b just in case Ct_T(b) ⊆ Ct_T(a) and Ct_F(a) ⊆ Ct_F(b), and strictly more verisimilitude just in case at least one of the two inclusions is proper.

Tichý and Miller independently showed, in 1974, that this cannot work. A false theory a is trivially among its own consequences, so a ∈ Ct_F(a); if Ct_F(a) ⊆ Ct_F(b), then a ∈ Ct_F(b) too, which means b entails a, so every consequence of a, true or false, is a consequence of b as well. Pushed through, this makes it impossible for a to come out strictly more verisimilar than b whenever a and b are both false. Since the comparisons Popper actually cares about — Newton versus Kepler, relativity versus Newtonian mechanics — are precisely comparisons between theories taken to be false, the result shows that Popper's definition cannot do the job it was designed for, and the natural conclusion is that the whole project of explicating closeness to truth in these terms was misconceived from the outset.

Popper accepted the criticism, and in his later writing he repaired the definition himself by replacing content-inclusion with a similarity measure, computing verisimilitude from the 'distance' between a theory's claims and the relevant true state-descriptions; it is this likeness-based approach that subsequent work on truthlikeness has continued to develop."
