So this came up at my rationalist-adjacent reading group (we'd been going through some s-risk stuff) and it turned into a proper argument between two of the regulars that I haven't stopped thinking about. The topic was "surrogate goals" as a defense against acausal threats -- basically, the idea that you publicly commit to reacting to a threat against some goal you don't actually care about exactly as you would to a threat against something you do care about, so a threatener who's only in it for leverage might as well target the fake goal, and you lose nothing if they follow through.

Jonah's take: this is basically a free lunch. If it works, you've deflected real disvalue onto something you're indifferent to; if it doesn't, you're no worse off than if you'd never bothered. Mira pushed back that the scheme never gets off the ground, because any threatener smart enough to be worth worrying about will notice you don't terminally value the surrogate and will just threaten the real thing anyway, or threaten you specifically for trying to dodge.

Jonahs reply: the threatener doesn't need your inner states to line up, only your behavior -- if it's common knowledge you'll respond identically either way, that's all the incentive structure needs, functional equivalence rather than sincerity. Mira: fine, but then the threatener just threatens both goals at once, which costs them almost nothing extra and fully defeats the deflection, so "free lunch" was never the right description.

Jonah came back with: a compound threat isn't actually free to verify and carry out, and the point of the whole move was never "this defeats every possible adversary" -- it's that it shifts the expected-value math against threateners who care only about leverage, the same way a home alarm doesn't stop every burglar but still changes the calculation for most of them. Mira's last word was that this concedes too much: once surrogate goals are common knowledge, any threatener worth worrying about will just refuse to treat a threat as "real" unless it hits something the target couldn't plausibly have deflected, which pushes everyone toward escalating, costlier commitment displays that Jonah's original "free lunch" framing never priced in.
