In 1715 and 1716, Leibniz and Samuel Clarke exchanged a series of letters that are best read as an argument with Isaac Newton conducted at one remove: Clarke was writing as Newton's defender and mouthpiece, and is generally thought to have shown Newton at least some of the letters before sending them. I've been working through this correspondence for a seminar on early modern philosophy of religion, and two of its threads strike me as unusually rich material. First, Newton had suggested in the Opticks that space might be something like the sensorium of God, the medium through which an omnipresent deity intimately perceives everything happening within it; Leibniz pounced on this, asking whether describing God's perception in terms of a sense organ doesn't demean him by making his perception dependent on something outside himself, the way a creature's perception depends on its body. Second, Leibniz argued that a God who must periodically intervene to correct irregularities accumulating in the planets' orbits resembles a watchmaker whose mechanism is imperfect and needs occasional mending, whereas his own picture of a pre-established harmony has God get the whole design right at the outset, with no need for after-the-fact repairs. Clarke countered that Leibniz's tidy, self-sufficient world leaves no real room for God's continuing government of nature, turning providence into an empty formality and pushing the whole picture toward the kind of blind necessity associated with Spinoza.

Could you write me a short dialogue between Leibniz and Clarke that opens in the middle of this exchange and works through both threads? I want real engagement rather than two monologues: Clarke should have more to say about the sensorium charge than simply retreating from it, and Leibniz should be pushed to explain whether pre-established harmony leaves room for any meaningful sense in which God acts freely and providentially in the world, rather than having merely front-loaded a fixed plan at creation. Keep both sides sharp and evenly matched, and resist the temptation to map the disagreement onto a simple "religious traditionalist vs. modern scientific rationalist" axis — if anything, the correspondence scrambles those categories, since it is Clarke who insists on God's ongoing, active involvement in nature while Leibniz's universe runs perfectly well without any further divine tinkering.
