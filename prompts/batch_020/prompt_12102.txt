I'm a philosophy PhD student finishing a dissertation chapter on Moore's Principia Ethica, and a few weeks ago a reading group I attend (mostly people working on population ethics and effective altruism) spent a session on Eliezer Yudkowsky's "torture vs. dust specks" dilemma: is it worse for one person to be tortured horribly for fifty years, or for an inconceivably large number of people, standardly written 3^^^3 in Knuth's up-arrow notation, each to receive a single, barely noticeable dust speck in the eye, given that on a simple additive hedonic calculus the summed disutility of the specks comes out larger? Most of the group treated this as a contest between "scope-sensitive" consequentialism and squeamish intuition, but it struck me that Moore, of all the classical figures I work on, had the most directly relevant theoretical resources for resisting the aggregative step itself, even though he obviously never considered anything resembling this example.

I'd like you to reconstruct, as rigorously as you can, how Moore would most plausibly have responded to this dilemma. Please draw specifically on (a) his principle of organic unities and what it implies about assuming that the disvalue of an enormous aggregate of harms is simply the sum of the disvalues of its parts, and (b) his rejection of hedonism in favor of what is often labeled, after Rashdall, his "ideal utilitarianism," on which goods and evils are plural and only partly commensurable rather than reducible to quantities of pleasure and pain. On balance, do these commitments push Moore toward preferring the torture, toward preferring the dust specks, or toward rejecting the dilemma as posed altogether? Please also state the strongest reply a committed aggregative utilitarian could make against the Moorean position you reconstruct, and say whether, on Moore's own principles, he has the resources to meet it.
