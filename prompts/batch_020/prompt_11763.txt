I spent most of my career as an experimental physicist doing precision gravity measurements, and now that I'm retired I've been filling the hours working through philosophy of science books -- currently a used copy of a Bayesian epistemology text my niece (she's doing a philosophy PhD) left behind after visiting over the holidays. I hit the chapter on what they call "the problem of old evidence" and it's been nagging at me since, so I brought it up with a couple of old colleagues over coffee and none of us could agree on what it actually shows.

Here's the setup as I understood it: Mercury's perihelion precession was a known, measured anomaly for decades before Einstein came along, so by the time General Relativity matched the number, our credence in E ("the precession has this value") was already sitting at 1 for everybody involved. On plain Bayesian conditionalization, P(T|E) = P(E|T)P(T)/P(E), and if P(E) is already 1 then conditioning on E is a no-op -- your credence in T literally cannot move. So it seems to me the philosophers have shown, using relativity's own signature success story as the textbook case, that the perihelion match never actually counted as Bayesian evidence for GR at all -- physicists just felt reassured by it psychologically, while the real evidential weight should fall entirely on the later novel predictions, like the light-bending result from the 1919 eclipse expedition or the orbital decay of the Hulse-Taylor binary pulsar.

Is that a fair reading of what the problem of old evidence actually establishes -- that retrodicting a known anomaly is basically confirmationally worthless, and only genuinely novel predictions should ever move our credences? Before I go around telling my old colleagues that half the "great confirmations" in the physics textbooks don't really count for much, I'd like to know whether I'm getting the philosophical upshot right, or whether I'm overreacting to what might just be a technical wrinkle in the Bayesian formalism rather than a real dent in how we ought to think about evidence.
