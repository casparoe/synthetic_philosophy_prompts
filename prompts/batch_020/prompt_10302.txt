I am drafting a courtroom chapter for a novel set in West Germany in 1949. The scene turns on an appellate judge who must rule on a woman charged with unlawfully depriving her husband of his liberty, after she had denounced him to the Nazi authorities in 1944 for making disparaging remarks about Hitler while home on leave; he was sentenced to death under the wartime statutes then in force, though the sentence was never carried out. The case is closely modeled on the real one that anchors the Hart–Fuller debate, and I don't want to flatten the philosophical dispute into its usual thumbnail version ("positivists think unjust laws are still laws; natural lawyers disagree"). As I understand it, Hart's considered position was not that the wife should go unpunished, but that the honest course was to leave the wartime statute standing as valid law and, if she were to be punished at all, to do so openly through a new, frankly retroactive criminal statute — rather than have the court simply declare the Nazi-era law void from the outset, as the actual court did. Fuller resisted exactly this move, holding that a regime as morally corrupted as the Nazi one could forfeit its claim to have been making law at all, at least with respect to statutes of this kind. Write an essay setting out the strongest version of each position and then tell me, so that I can give my judge's ruling real philosophical integrity, which side actually has the better argument. I want a defended verdict, not a survey of the debate.
