My friend and I spent most of last Saturday night arguing about whether large language models understand anything they produce, and, as these conversations tend to do, we ended up back at Searle's Chinese Room. I defended some version of the systems reply—that even if the man inside understands no Chinese, the system as a whole, room, rulebook, and man together, might—while my friend held the harder line that no amount of syntactic shuffling could ever amount to genuine understanding. Eventually we realized we were really re-litigating the Turing test, since Searle's whole thought experiment is aimed at showing that passing a behavioral test of exactly the kind Turing proposed is not sufficient for understanding.

What struck me afterward is that Turing himself never got to answer any of this: he died in 1954, twenty-six years before Searle published "Minds, Brains, and Programs." Yet Turing's own 1950 paper was not naive about the underlying worry. Among the objections to machine intelligence he considers is what he calls the argument from consciousness, in the form pressed by the neurosurgeon Geoffrey Jefferson in a widely discussed 1949 lecture: that a machine could never be credited with really composing a sonnet or a concerto out of felt thought and emotion, and, more importantly, with knowing that it had done so, rather than merely producing the right symbols by chance. Turing's reply combined a solipsism-flipping move—we only ever grant that other people think by a kind of polite convention, so why not extend the same convention to a suitably behaving machine—with a proposed remedy: a probing oral examination, illustrated with an imagined exchange in which an interrogator presses a machine that has offered a line of verse about a "summer's day" on why a "winter's day" would not have done just as well.

Given that Turing had staked out this position before Searle ever wrote a word, I'd like your best reconstruction of how he would have responded, point by point, to the Chinese Room. Does his polite-convention move, together with his approach to probing questions like the sonnet exchange, actually blunt Searle's argument, or does Searle expose a weakness in Turing's position that Jefferson's cruder objection never touched? And once you've settled that, tell me what verdict this leaves Turing with on the question my friend and I couldn't resolve: whether a system like a modern large language model, probed as searchingly as his imagined interrogator probes the sonnet-writer, would count for him as something that understands, or only as something that has become very good at the polite convention.
