A friend and I recently finished a slow, several-months-long read of Anna Karenina for the two-person book club we've kept going since university, and when we reached the scene of Anna's death under the train, we were both, a little sheepishly, close to tears over the fate of a woman who has never existed and never will. That admission turned into a long, unresolved argument on the walk home. My friend maintained that whatever we'd felt couldn't have been real grief or pity, since neither of us believed for a second that Anna, her marriage, or that train were anything but fiction; at best, she said, we'd felt something like make-believe sadness, on the model of a child shrieking in mock terror while being chased by a parent pretending to be a monster. I objected that this seemed a strange thing to say about a feeling that had just put us both on the verge of tears, and that a thought, even an unbelieved one, ought to be perfectly capable of moving someone. We went back and forth for the better part of an hour without either of us landing a decisive blow, and afterward I discovered, to my slight annoyance, that we had merely been badly rehearsing a genuine, long-running dispute in aesthetics known as the paradox of fiction.

I'd like to see the argument conducted properly, by two people equipped to actually defend their side. Could you write a dialogue, built around this same case of Anna's death, between a philosopher of art who holds something close to Kendall Walton's make-believe theory of fiction — on which what we feel toward Anna is at best "quasi-pity," an emotion generated inside a game of make-believe in which the novel serves as a prop — and one who holds something closer to the rival "thought theory" associated with Peter Lamarque (and, in a related way, Noël Carroll), on which merely entertaining the thought of Anna's situation, without believing a word of it, already suffices to produce a perfectly genuine instance of pity.

I don't just want each side to restate its own slogans. I'd like the Waltonian to press the classic objection that nobody who is supposedly grieving for Anna gives a moment's thought to anything resembling appropriate real-world action — contacting anyone, treating the news as real, and so on — and I'd like the thought theorist to have an actual answer to that, plus a pressure point of their own about why symptoms like a racing pulse or genuine tears turn up right on schedule even when no belief, fictional or otherwise, seems to be doing any causal work to produce them. Feel free to give them a concrete occasion, such as two colleagues in a reading group closing the same final chapter, but the setting matters far less to me than getting the real back-and-forth right, since that is precisely the part my friend and I couldn't manage for each other.
