I did not expect to need actual philosophy for my job this year, but here we are. I'm an immunology professor, and back in the fall I got roped onto my university's Research Integrity Committee, which just got reactivated and given more teeth after -- well, you've probably seen the same headlines about manipulated data that I have, it feels like a new one breaks every few months. My actual first case landed on my desk two weeks ago, and it's a lot messier than the onboarding session made it sound.

Short version: a postdoc in a cancer biology lab manipulated, and in a couple of spots outright fabricated, the quantification behind two of six figures in a paper (western blots and mouse tumor counts). Nobody disputes she acted alone or that the paper is getting retracted -- that part's easy. The fight is about the other three co-authors: the PI, an outside collaborator at another university who ran the statistical analysis on data as it was handed to him and never touched a raw blot, and a grad student whose own experiments in the same paper are totally clean. Our authorship policy just says authors "share responsibility for the accuracy and integrity of the reported work" and leaves it at that, which is not alot of help when the whole question is what that's even supposed to mean for three people who had no realistic way of knowing.

Half the committee wants a finding only against the postdoc, with the other three simply asked to sign off on the retraction and otherwise cleared. The other half, led by our chair (she did a philosophy phd before moving into research administration), keeps insisting that co-authoring a paper is itself a joint act, not just four individual contributions stapled together, and that this creates a real, if lesser, form of shared responsibility that doesn't just reduce to who-knew-what -- she threw out some names, joint commitment, shared agency, Gilbert or Bratman or maybe both, I wrote it down wrong and don't have time to go chase down the actual texts before we reconvene a week from Monday. Could you draft the memo I'd actually bring to that meeting? I need a real recommendation on what, if anything, happens to the three non-fabricating co-authors, a rough timeline for next steps, and reasoning solid enough that the "only punish the person who did it" side of the room would have to actually engage with it rather than just being told they're wrong -- I think they've got a genuinely strong point, and I don't want the memo to just bulldoze past it.
