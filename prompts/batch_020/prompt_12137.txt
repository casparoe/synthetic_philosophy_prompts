I spent about thirty-five years doing experimental condensed-matter physics before I retired, and I fell into philosophy almost by accident: I was laid up for two months after a hip replacement with nothing to do but read, and a friend handed me a copy of Gödel, Escher, Bach to keep me occupied. That got me hooked, and I've since joined a small reading group, half retirees and half grad students, that meets over Zoom every few weeks and works through logic-adjacent stuff. Last session we went through some of the old MIRI material on self-modifying agents and what people call the Lobian obstacle -- I think it traces back to a paper by Yudkowsky and Herreshoff on "tiling agents," though I might be misremembering the names -- and two of the sharper members, Nadia and Warren, got into a running argument about how much it actually proves. It's been bugging me since, because I think they're each onto something real, but I've only got about eighteen months of amateur reading behind me and I can't referee it myself.

Nadia's opening claim was that Löb's theorem is a genuine, structural wall: if a formal system could prove, for some given statement, that "if I can prove this then it's true," Löb's theorem says the system could already prove that statement outright, full stop -- so a system can never bolt trust in its own future proofs onto itself as a free safeguard. Applied to a self-modifying agent, she said, this means a system that only lets itself be replaced by a successor once it's proved the successor is safe can't get that guarantee in full generality, no matter how the proof search is set up, as long as the successor reasons in a similar system. Warren agreed the theorem itself is solid but said Nadia was overselling it's scope: this is a fact about one specific way of cashing out "trust," namely a provability predicate obeying the usual derivability conditions, and MIRI's own later work on logical induction and reflective oracles exists precisely because people moved away from that model -- so calling it an obstacle for self-trusting agents in general mistakes the limits of one formalism for a law of nature. Nadia's comeback was that swapping proof for probability relocates the problem instead of dissolving it: no consistent system, proof-based or probabilistic, can hand itself unconditional certainty that its own future outputs of some given kind are trustworthy without risking the same sort of self-referential collapse that Gödel's second incompleteness theorem already predicts for plain soundness claims, and the fact that some version of this keeps turning up across totally different formalizations is itself evidence something structural is going on. Warren's last word was that "it keeps recurring so it must be deep" proves too much, since almost any sufficiently expressive self-referential system eventually hits some Tarski- or Gödel-flavored limit -- that's much older and broader than Löb's theorem specifically -- and that actual humans, and probably actual deep-learning systems, don't manage self-trust by deriving internal guarantees about their own future outputs anyway; they lean on track records and outside checks, so whatever wall exists for an idealized proof-writing agent might not be the constraint that matters for anyone real.

Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's got the better of it at each stage, rather than just handing down an overall winner? I'm especially curious whether Nadia's "it keeps recurring across formalizations" move is actually good evidence for something structural, or whether Warren's "that proves too much" objection really deflates it, and whether there's some consideration neither of them raised that ought to be front and center.
