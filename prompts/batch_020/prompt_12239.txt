I write about science and ideas for a living, and I was at dinner after a small workshop on the history of analytic philosophy (mostly historians and philosophers of logic) when two of the guests got into a genuinely heated arguement about Gottlob Frege that I haven't been able to stop thinking about since. Everyone at the table knew Frege as the guy who basically invented modern formal logic and the sense/reference distinction, but things kicked off because someone brought up his private diary from the last year of his life -- the one that surfaced decades after he died and turned out to be full of hard-right, anti-Weimar, antisemitic political opinions, including wanting Jews stripped of political rights. Apparently even Michael Dummett, who did more than anyone to make Frege central to English-language philosophy, admitted the discovery rattled him.

Here's roughly how the argument went, as best I can reconstruct it afterward. (1) Mara said the diary is basically irrelevant to Frege's philosophy: the validity of the sense/reference distinction, or his account of logical objects, doesn't depend one bit on the character of the guy who came up with it, and treating it otherwise is a textbook genetic fallacy -- like refusing to use a theorem because you dislike the mathematician. (2) Dev agreed the logic obviously isn't affected, but said that's not the interesting claim -- Frege's whole philosophical persona was built around policing the border between objective 'thought' and mere subjective psychology, and in the diary that exact move gets turned on people: certain groups, in his view, just weren't capable of properly objective reasoning and so didn't deserve full civic standing. That's not nothing, Dev said, even if it doesn't touch the theorems. (3) Mara pushed back that this is a just-so story: plenty of hardline Platonists about truth are politically progressive, and plenty of relativists have been reactionaries, so you can't retroactively read Frege's politics into his anti-psychologism just because you already know how the story ends. (4) Dev said fair, it's not a general law connecting Platonism and politics, but a narrower, biographical point about Frege specifically -- that the habit of sorting who counts as a 'real' reasoner shows up both in his polemics against rival logicians and in his private politics, and that's worth knowing about the man even if it doesn't refute a single one of his proofs.

Nobody budged and I went home honestly unsure who'd had the better of it. Could you go through moves (1) through (4) and say who's right about what at each step -- not just who wins overall -- and also flag anything you think both Mara and Dev are missing?
