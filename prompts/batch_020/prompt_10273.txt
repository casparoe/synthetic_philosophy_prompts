I help organize my university's graduate philosophy colloquium, and this term we're running a session in which one of our postdocs will spend an hour defending David Lewis's modal realism: the thesis that absolutely every way a world could have been is a way some world actually is, that these other worlds are exactly as concrete and real as ours while being spatiotemporally and causally disconnected from it, and that "actual" is merely an indexical term picking out whichever world the speaker happens to inhabit, on a par with "here" and "now." She has rehearsed the talk with a few of us already, and her go-to move whenever an objection lands is to concede its force and then retreat to Lewis's own fallback: that she has no non-question-begging argument against the "incredulous stare," only a cost-benefit case that the ontological extravagance buys enough theoretical unification -- of modality, of properties as sets of possibilia, of counterfactuals, of the content of thought and language -- to be worth the price.

Could you draft nine or ten Q&A questions that keep her from resting on that cost-benefit shrug every time? I'd like at least one question that presses the epistemological problem -- if other worlds are causally and spatiotemporally isolated from ours, what could our justification for believing anything about their contents even consist in, given how much fine-grained modal knowledge ordinary counterfactual reasoning seems to presuppose -- one on whether counterpart theory quietly changes the subject when it cashes out "Humphrey could have won the election" as a claim about some other, merely similar individual rather than about Humphrey himself, and one on how she squares individuating worlds by spatiotemporal unity with the "island universe" cases Lewis himself worried might undermine that very criterion. Please also include a question on whether the view has any practical or ethical bite at all, given that Lewis insists the plurality of worlds is causally inert with respect to ours, and end the sequence with a question that asks her to say what observation or argument could actually count against modal realism, given that the view seems constructed so as to be immune to ordinary empirical disconfirmation. For each question, note briefly what response you'd expect from a well-prepared Lewisian, and design the scripted follow-up so that it closes off that escape route rather than inviting a mere restatement of the theory.
