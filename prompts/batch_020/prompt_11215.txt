So I spent almost forty years as a marine biologist studying siphonophores -- things like the Portuguese man o' war, which isn't actually one animal but a colony of thousands of genetically identical zooids that behave like a single organism. It always bugged me that nobody could really say whether that's "really" one organism or many, and now that I'm retired and reading philosophy for fun, I think I've found out why, and I don't love the answer. There's this old thought experiment from Eli Hirsch: imagine a language with no word for "car," just "incar" (whatever part of a car is inside a garage at a given moment, counted as one object) and "outcar" (the complementary object, made of whatever's outside); as a car pulls out, incar-speakers say the incar shrinks away while an outcar grows in its place, and they'd never disagree with us about a single observation, only about how to carve up the same particles into objects. Hirsch's moral is that a lot of metaphysics, including what philosophers call the "special composition question" (when do some things add up to a further thing), is a merely verbal dispute dressed up as something deep -- and my gut says hes basically right about my zooids too, that its semantics, not biology. I want you to talk me out of that. Make the strongest case you can that "do these zooids really compose one further object" has a real, non-verbal answer -- that the universalist and the nihilist about composition aren't just talking past each other the way incar-speakers and we do -- and get specific about what would have to be true of the world, or of the word "exists" itself, for that to hold up, rather than giving me some on-the-one-hand-on-the-other-hand answer.
