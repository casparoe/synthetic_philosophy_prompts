Our son Theo, who just turned nine, was recently diagnosed with idiopathic short stature: he sits below the third percentile for height, but every test that might explain it — growth hormone levels, IGF-1, thyroid function, karyotype, bone age — has come back essentially normal. His pediatric endocrinologist described daily growth hormone injections as a "treatment option" that could add several centimeters to his adult height, and asked us to think it over. I've spent the past few weeks doing something I didn't expect: reading bioethics papers instead of sleeping. Below is the essay I've drafted to work out my own thinking, and I'd value your comments before I take it any further.

The obvious first move is to ask whether this would be treatment or enhancement, and the obvious framework is something like Christopher Boorse's biostatistical account of health, as Norman Daniels adapted it for exactly this kind of case: health care corrects departures from species-typical functioning because such departures narrow a person's fair share of what Daniels calls the normal opportunity range, so if Theo's growth plates, pituitary axis, and hormone levels are all doing precisely what they're supposed to do, and he is simply constitutionally at the low end of the height distribution, then raising his height is enhancement, not treatment, however clinical the delivery mechanism looks. But this framework has a soft underbelly that Daniels himself points to: the statistical cutoff that makes someone "abnormally" short — the third percentile, or two standard deviations, or whatever threshold a given guideline uses — isn't read off nature the way the existence of a pituitary tumor would be. Some normative choice about where mere deviation becomes dysfunction is doing real work there, and that choice looks a lot more like the kind of thing enhancement critics worry about than like ordinary disease diagnosis.

My second thought was Michael Sandel's argument in The Case Against Perfection, which I'd always found a little too aesthetic to take seriously until now. His complaint isn't that intervening is unsafe or unfair to other children; it's that the impulse to intervene expresses an anxious refusal to accept our children as given, at some cost to the humility, responsibility, and solidarity that Sandel thinks that acceptance sustains. But actually facing the decision, I noticed an objection to this that I don't think I'd have taken seriously before: declining the injections is not obviously the "unchosen" option. It is a choice too, made on Theo's behalf, expressing our values rather than his, and no less an exercise of parental will than opting in. If both paths are impositions, giftedness talk doesn't obviously favor either one.

The third thread, from the positional-goods discussion that runs through Buchanan, Brock, Daniels, and Wikler's work on genetics and justice, is that height's payoff is mostly relative rather than absolute: a few centimeters help you only to the extent that others don't have them, and are worth much less once height enhancement becomes routine. That's a serious worry about a world in which everyone does this, but it sits oddly as a reason for any particular parent to refrain, since our decision does approximately nothing to the arms race either way. It feels like a policy argument wearing the costume of a personal-ethics argument, and I'm not sure I'm entitled to borrow its weight for this decision.

The essay doesn't reach a conclusion so much as clear some underbrush, and I'd like your comments on three things specifically: whether I've represented the Boorse/Daniels line and its "soft underbelly" fairly, or whether there's a way of drawing the statistical cutoff that isn't as normatively loaded as I'm claiming; whether my objection to Sandel — that inaction is also an imposition — actually works, or whether there's a natural reply on his behalf that I'm missing; and whether the positional-goods point really is as inert at the individual level as I've suggested, or whether it should bear on our decision after all.
