I'm teaching a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of causation this term, and this week's session sets David Lewis's counterfactual analysis against the interventionist, structural-equations tradition associated with Woodward and Pearl. My students pick up the official contrast quickly enough — dependence among possible worlds versus dependence under hypothetical interventions on variables in a causal model — but they tend to treat it as a difference in formalism rather than a source of real disagreement. I want a text that shows the two views actually straining against each other on a hard case, rather than each side politely restating its own definitions.

The case I have in mind is Jonathan Schaffer's "trumping preemption" scenario. A major and a sergeant simultaneously give a soldier the order "Charge!" The soldier is trained always to obey the highest-ranking officer whose order he hears, and he charges. Had the major alone given the order, he would have charged; had the sergeant alone given it, he would equally have charged. The two orders reach him in exactly the same way at exactly the same instant, so there is no temporal or mechanical sense in which either order screens off or interferes with the causal process running from the other to the charge — unlike ordinary preemption, nothing here is cut short or arrives too late. Yet the intuitive verdict is unambiguous: the major's order caused the charge, and the sergeant's did not.

Please write a dialogue of roughly 700–900 words between two equally capable philosophers: L, who defends a Lewisian counterfactual analysis (L may reach for whatever refinements — chains of stepwise dependence, or the later "influence" account — are needed to handle ordinary cases of preemption), and W, who defends an interventionist analysis cast in terms of directed graphs and structural equations. Have them work out, responding to each other's actual proposals rather than delivering parallel monologues, whether either framework has principled resources for explaining, rather than simply stipulating, why rank settles the matter here. L should press W on whether building the soldier's deference to rank into the structural equation for his behavior explains the asymmetry or just encodes an answer fixed in advance; W should press L on what, exactly, in the machinery of dependence and influence could possibly distinguish the major's order from the sergeant's, given that the two are, by construction, counterfactually and even "influence"-wise identical. Neither should straightforwardly win, but each should end having identified precisely where they think the other's theory runs out of road.
