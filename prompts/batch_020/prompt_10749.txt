Ok, this is going to sound like a weird question but I went down a rabbit hole this weekend re-reading old LessWrong posts about logical induction (the Garrabrant-and-friends MIRI paper from a few years back), and I think I finally understand the mechanism well enough to explain it: you set up an idealized market of traders betting on logical and mathematical sentences, you require that no trader running in polynomial time (or whatever the relevant efficiency bound is) can Dutch-book the market forever, and out of that one constraint alone you get an explicit, fully computable sequence of "probabilities" over things like unproven conjectures or whether some absurdly large number is prime -- probabilities that are provably guaranteed to converge to the right answers as more of the deductive process unfolds, without ever assuming anything except the axioms of math themselves.

But if that's right, doesn't logical induction basically just solve the problem of induction? Hume's whole point was that you can't rationally justify assuming the future will resemble the past without either begging the question or arguing in a circle, but here's a fully rigorous, computable procedure thats guaranteed to converge on correct beliefs over time, and it never has to sneak in an assumption about "the uniformity of nature" anywhere, since it only leans on facts about logic and computation that aren't up for grabs the way empirical regularities are. I brought this up with my roommate, who's doing an actual philosophy PhD, and she gave me this look and said something like "those are just completely seperate kinds of induction, you're equivocating," but when I pushed on it she couldn't quite spell out where the disanalogy actually was. On the surface both problems seem to be about the exact same thing, justifying confident generalization beyond what you've strictly already nailed down, so I'd love an actual explanation of where the parallel breaks down, rather than just being told that it does. Am I missing something basic, or is this a more interesting question than she's giving it credit for?
