I don't have any formal background in philosophy or law, but over the past year or so I've been reading my way through older texts on the ethics of war, mostly after a documentary on drone strikes got me wondering how we're supposed to judge soldiers who end up fighting on the wrong side of a conflict through no real fault of their own. That's how I ended up with a copy of Emer de Vattel's "The Law of Nations" from 1758. As far as I can tell, Vattel's position is that because there is no higher authority standing above sovereign states able to rule definitively on who actually has justice on their side, what he calls the "voluntary law of nations" requires that, once a war is formally underway between recognized sovereigns, soldiers on both sides be treated as equally entitled to fight and kill under the same rules, regardless of whose cause is actually just. He doesn't seem to think both sides really can be equally right in some deeper sense, only that the practical business of war has to proceed as if they might be, since no outside party is in a position to certify otherwise. I later learned this same intuition has a name in more recent philosophy, the "moral equality of combatants," and that people like Michael Walzer have defended versions of it while others have tried to tear it down, but Vattel seems to be a much older ancestor of the same basic thought.

What I can't stop chewing on is what he would have made of drone warfare, where the "soldier" on one side is sitting at a console on a base with no possibility of being shot at, while the people on the receiving end have no way of striking back at him at all. My rough sense is that part of what made Vattel's picture of equal treatment feel fair, even to someone like me reading it centuries later, was some background assumption that both sides were mutually exposed to violence, so that treating them as equals wasn't just a legal fiction but tracked something real about their situation. Once that mutual exposure disappears completely for one side, I don't know whether his own reasoning still holds up on its own terms, or whether I'm smuggling in an assumption he never actually needed.

So here is what I would like your help with: reconstruct, as carefully and honestly as you can, what Vattel's actual principles commit him to in the case of remote, risk-free drone warfare, and then tell me whether, by his own lights, a drone operator facing zero personal risk should still count as the moral equal of the combatant on the ground below. Please be explicit about which parts of your answer are a faithful extension of what he wrote and which parts are your own extrapolation into a situation he never anticipated.
