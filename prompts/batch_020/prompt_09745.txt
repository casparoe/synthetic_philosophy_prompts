A few of us in my high school's philosophy club have been working through classic problem cases for causal decision theory, and we keep getting stuck on the "Death in Damascus" puzzle devised by Allan Gibbard and William Harper. As I understand it, an agent in Damascus learns that Death — an excellent predictor — will appear the next night wherever he expects the agent to be, whether that is Damascus itself or Aleppo, so that meeting him is fatal and avoiding him means survival. Because any fixed, deterministic rule for choosing where to go can be anticipated and matched, no such rule is safe, which is why some decision theorists claim that only a genuine coin flip between the two cities can be rational, though that escape route only works if Death's predictive powers stop short of covering the coin itself.

Could you write a dialogue between two causal decision theorists who accept the same basic framework but disagree sharply about this case? One argues that randomizing 50/50 is the uniquely rational response, since it is the only option that is "ratifiable" in Richard Jeffrey's sense — an act the agent would still endorse even after coming to expect that she will actually perform it — whereas committing to either city outright is self-undermining once Death's accuracy is factored in. The other argues that leaning on ratifiability here is an unmotivated patch, since nothing in the requirement to maximize expected utility forces an act to be self-endorsing in this way, and treating the coin flip as a safe haven does not really take Death's predictive powers as seriously as the case is supposed to. Please have the two characters engage directly with each other's strongest objections rather than talking past one another, and feel free to let the exchange end without a tidy resolution if the disagreement does not actually admit of one.
