A decision I made last month has turned an abstract puzzle from the testimony seminar I'm auditing this term into something uncomfortably concrete. I had been offered a well-paid contract to build recommendation-engine features for an app that several people I respect consider close to predatory — the kind of product that periodically makes the news for how compulsively teenagers use it — and I genuinely could not tell whether taking the job would be wrong. Rather than work through the arguments myself, I asked my friend Renata, whose moral judgment I trust more than my own on almost everything, what she thought. She said it was fine, and I essentially adopted her verdict wholesale and signed the contract. When I mentioned this at dinner, two other friends, Simon and Dana, ended up in a long argument about whether that was an acceptable way for me to have settled the question — an argument that, once I noticed it, tracked almost exactly a debate from my seminar readings about what philosophers call "moral testimony," except neither of them has read a word of it.

Their exchange went, roughly: (1) Simon said there was nothing wrong with it — we take advice from people better positioned than us constantly, nobody re-derives their mechanic's diagnosis from first principles, and Renata is about as good a moral judge as I know, so treating her verdict as decisive was simply rational. (2) Dana said moral questions are not like that: a diagnosis is a fact you can just receive, but a decision about what you should do is supposed to express your own values, and if you import someone else's verdict outright you have not made a moral judgment at all, you have subcontracted one — which matters even if the verdict happens to be correct. (3) Simon replied that we subcontract moral judgments to authority all the time without a second thought: we tell children that lying is wrong and expect them to believe it long before they grasp why, and nobody thinks this leaves their later moral agency defective. (4) Dana said the child case is disanalogous twice over — we expect children to eventually grow into the reasons themselves, and they are being handed a general rule, not a verdict on a single, self-interested, one-off dilemma, where the temptation to go shopping for a convenient answer is itself grounds for suspicion. (5) Simon answered that this only shows we should worry about cherry-picking whom to ask, not about deference as such: if I would have walked away from the contract just as readily had Renata said it was wrong, the motivated-reasoning worry disappears, and there is nothing left for Dana's objection to bite on. (6) Dana said that even granting that, something is still missing — moral understanding is not just a matter of landing on the right verdict but of being able to track the relevant reasons yourself in future, similar cases, and a borrowed verdict, however sincerely accepted, does not give you that, so my choice might not have been irrational, exactly, but it was still a worse way of settling the question than working it out myself would have been.

I could see the force of both sides and honestly could not tell you, by the end of dinner, who had actually won. Could you go through the exchange move by move, (1) through (6), and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I would also like to know whether either of them is quietly resting on a premise about testimony that you think is simply false, whether Dana's point in (6) actually survives Simon's challenge in (5) or just restates (2) in fancier language, and whether there is some consideration neither of them raised that you think should bear on the case.
