And here's the student's answer, in full:

(a) The centipede game is a two-player extensive-form game, first introduced by Robert Aumann in 1981, in which the players alternate moves and each has the choice, at their turn, to either "take" the current pot (ending the game immediately) or "pass" it to the other player, at which point the pot grows larger. If a player passes all the way to the final round, both players end up much better off than if either had taken early. Backward induction reasons from the last node backwards: at the very last node, the mover knows the game is about to end, so they should take, since there's no future round left in which passing could pay off. Given that the second-to-last mover can anticipate this, they're better off taking at their own node rather than passing into a situation where they'll just get taken advantage of, and so on back to the first move. So the backward induction solution is for the very first player to take immediately, ending the game before it even properly begins.

(b) This is paradoxical because the backward-induction outcome is Pareto-dominated by outcomes involving even a little cooperation -- both players would be strictly better off if they passed for a few rounds instead of the first player taking immediately. It also doesn't match the experimental evidence: in the famous experiments by McKelvey and Palfrey, most subjects don't take at the first opportunity, and instead cooperate for a number of rounds before the game unravels, suggesting either that people aren't perfectly rational in the game-theoretic sense, or that a bit of uncertainty about whether your opponent is the "irrational"/altruistic type can support cooperation even among otherwise rational players.

(c) Aumann's 1995 paper tries to show that the backward induction outcome doesn't need any special assumptions about payoffs or altruism to fail. He proves that if there's common knowledge among the players that both of them are rational (i.e., each always best-responds to their beliefs at every node), this actually generates a contradiction once the game has more than a couple of rounds, since a player who sees their opponent pass can't rationally go on believing that opponent is rational and will keep behaving rationally later on. So Aumann's real conclusion is that common knowledge of rationality can't be consistently upheld throughout the centipede game -- which is actually a deeper problem for backward induction than people had previously realized, since the very assumption that's supposed to justify it ends up undermining itself.

(d) A related worry, raised by Ken Binmore among others, is about what a player should infer if the game somehow reaches a later node than backward induction predicts, i.e. off the equilibrium path. If player 2 sees that player 1 passed, does that mean player 1 is irrational (in which case maybe player 2 should deviate too), or does it mean player 1 has some other reason to pass that's compatible with being rational? Since backward induction implicitly assumes common knowledge of rationality at every node, including nodes that the theory itself says will never be reached, critics argue the argument is committed to reasoning about counterfactual situations that undercut its own premises -- which is why some philosophers think backward induction is a much less secure solution concept than it first looks, even though it remains the unique subgame-perfect equilibrium of the game.
