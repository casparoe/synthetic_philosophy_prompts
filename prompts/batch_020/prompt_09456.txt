A friend and I got into a long argument after our philosophy reading group last week about Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument in *Knowledge and Its Limits*, and I still haven't managed to resolve it in my own head. As I understand the argument from chapter 4, Williamson asks us to picture a subject whose felt temperature rises so gradually over the course of a morning that no two temporally adjacent instants are discriminable to her, even though she is clearly cold at the first instant and clearly not cold by the last. He argues that for her belief, at any instant, that she feels cold to count as knowledge, it has to be safe: true not just at that instant but also across a margin for error covering every instant too close to that one for her to tell them apart. Now suppose, for reductio, that feeling cold is luminous — that being in that state always puts you in a position to know you're in it. Since she does feel cold at the first instant, luminosity says she knows it; the margin for error then forces her to also feel cold at the next indistinguishable instant; luminosity lets her know that too; and so the margins chain together across the whole series until she ends up knowing she feels cold long after she's actually stopped. Since that's absurd, Williamson concludes that feeling cold isn't luminous after all.

The disagreement started over what Williamson does next. Since nothing in the reductio turns on any special feature of temperature sensations beyond their being the kind of thing that can be gradually gained and lost, he treats the argument as a template applying to any condition with that structure — and because knowing that p looks like exactly such a condition, he runs a structurally analogous reductio in chapter 5 to conclude that the KK principle fails as well: that knowing p doesn't put you in a position to know that you know it. My friend thought that accepting the temperature case while resisting its extension to KK was just special pleading. I disagreed, but under pressure I couldn't articulate, beyond a vague hunch, what actually breaks the analogy between the two cases.

I'd like you to help me turn that hunch into an actual argument. Please argue against generalizing Williamson's anti-luminosity argument for gradually-varying perceptual states like feeling cold into an argument against the KK principle for knowledge. I'm not looking for a rundown of everyone who has already published a reply to Williamson on this — I'd rather you build the strongest version of this specific objection yourself, engaging with details like how "closeness" between cases and the relevant margin for error are even meant to be fixed once the condition in question is a knowledge relation rather than a sensation, instead of just insisting that higher-order knowledge intuitively feels more secure than knowledge of one's own sensations.
