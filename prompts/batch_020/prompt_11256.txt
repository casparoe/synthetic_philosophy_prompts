As a novelist, I keep returning to Lon Fuller's parable of King Rex, the well-meaning monarch whose reign collapses through eight successive failures to govern by law: secret decrees, retroactive punishments, contradictory rules, and the like. Fuller uses Rex's failures to argue that the eight virtues he lacks—generality, publicity, prospectivity, clarity, non-contradiction, possibility of compliance, constancy through time, and congruence between announced rule and official action—add up to an "inner morality of law" that any genuine legal system must largely satisfy. This was already the crux of his 1958 dispute with Hart over the postwar "grudge informer" prosecutions, where Fuller argued that Nazi Germany's rules had decayed too far to count as law at all. Hart's rejoinder was that Fuller mistakes a contingent correlation for a conceptual truth: tyranny is often sloppy about clarity and prospectivity, but need not be, so a regime could in principle satisfy all eight desiderata with scrupulous care while pursuing thoroughly wicked ends—which would show the desiderata to be conditions of law's efficacy rather than of its morality. Write an essay adjudicating this dispute: does compliance with Fuller's eight principles carry genuine, if minimal, moral weight in its own right, or is Hart correct that it is really just morally neutral craftsmanship that happens to correlate with decency? Engage with the strongest form of each position before defending a definite verdict, rather than settling for a diplomatic split-the-difference.
