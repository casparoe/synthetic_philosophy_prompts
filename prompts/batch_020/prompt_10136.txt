As a PhD student working on formal epistemology, I've been reading I. J. Good's 1967 result that a Bayesian agent who will go on to act optimally on whatever she learns can never be made worse off, in expected-utility terms, by cost-free evidence obtained before she acts. I'd like to genuinely stress-test the scope of this claim rather than simply cite it in my dissertation. Please construct a detailed, realistic scenario in which a fully rational agent is strictly worse off in expectation for having received some piece of free evidence before acting, without leaning on overused moves such as "the evidence wasn't really costless" or "she simply prefers not to know how the film ends." Then diagnose exactly which of Good's premises—for instance, that only a single decision-maker is involved, that the agent conditionalizes correctly on the evidence, or that utility attaches only to outcomes rather than to epistemic states—the example is implicitly exploiting, so that it becomes clear why the theorem itself survives the counterexample.
