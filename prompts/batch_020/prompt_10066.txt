I'm a junior taking a philosophy elective at my high school, and we just finished a unit on Augustine's writings on lying, including excerpts from On Lying (De Mendacio) and Against Lying (Contra Mendacium). The main thing our teacher emphasized is that Augustine thought lying is sinful no matter how good the motive behind it is: he ranks lies from most to least serious depending on why they're told, but even the gentlest category — what's sometimes called an "officious lie," told purely to help or protect someone, without malice — still counts as a real sin for him, just a minor one.

This came up at lunch the next day because of something that had actually happened at school the week before. Our friend Sam skipped fourth period for a family emergency and texted our other friend Devon to let him know where he'd gone. When the assistant principal stopped Devon in the hallway and asked whether he knew where Sam was, Devon said, "I haven't actually seen him since first period" — which was true, since Sam had only texted him and they hadn't actually crossed paths that day — and the assistant principal let it go. Two of my classmates, Nora and Marcus, ended up arguing at lunch about whether Devon had done anything wrong by Augustine's own standards. (1) Nora said Devon had obviously lied in exactly the sense Augustine condemns: he meant well, but Augustine's whole point is that even a well-intentioned, protective lie is still a lie and still a sin, so Devon was in the wrong. (2) Marcus said that's too quick, because Augustine's own definition of lying isn't about creating a false impression in general — it's about asserting something contrary to what's actually in your own mind, with the intention of deceiving someone — and Devon's sentence matched exactly what he believed, so by Augustine's own standard it doesn't even qualify as a lie, whatever else it might be. (3) Nora replied that this can't be the right reading, because then Augustine's supposedly airtight, no-exceptions position would have an enormous loophole built into it: anyone could excuse any "officious lie" whatsoever just by phrasing it in words they happen to technically believe, which would make his two whole treatises on the subject pretty pointless. (4) Marcus answered that Nora was conflating "is a lie" with "is wrong" — Augustine can still condemn Devon's sentence as some other kind of sinful deception without calling it a lie specifically, because what makes lying uniquely bad for Augustine is the corruption of the link between your words and your own mind, not every possible way of misleading someone. (5) Nora shot back that if Augustine's deeper reason for condemning lying is that it betrays the God-given purpose of speech, which is to communicate your actual thoughts to other people, then Devon's sentence betrays that same purpose just as much as an outright falsehood would, so Marcus's "technical" distinction doesn't really do the work he needs it to do.

Neither of them would budge, and I honestly don't know Augustine's texts well enough to referee this myself. Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say, for each one, whether the point being made is basically right or mistaken, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like to know whether you think Nora, Marcus, or both of them are missing something important about Augustine's actual view that would change the verdict.
