My friend from undergrad is getting married in a few weeks and I'm giving the maid-of-honor speech. She and her fiancé met through early LessWrong meetups back around 2013, and he's spent most of his career since then at a small alignment research shop working on stuff like tiling agents and the Löbian obstacle -- the problem that you can't get a proof-based system to just blanket-trust "whatever my future self (or a successor running the same axioms) concludes is fine," because Löb's theorem means that kind of unconditional self-trust either collapses into the system already proving the thing outright or wrecks its consistency. At the engagement dinner he'd had two glasses of wine and gave this whole unsolicited mini lecture where he compared "I promise to love you forever" to exactly that kind of ungrounded self-trust clause, and argued, mostly joking but not entirely, that what a good marriage actually needs is closer to the tiling-agents move of each version of you proving something weaker but sufficient about the next version, rather then one big unconditional promise you can't ground out. The four of us at the table who'd also spent our early twenties on OB and LW thought this was the funniest and most romantic thing we'd ever heard; both sets of parents just kind of nodded along politely.

I want my speech to answer that bit instead of just repeating it back at him -- something built around the Löbian obstacle that the handful of alignment people in the room will recognize as actually getting the logic right, not just "these two are nerds haha," while still working as a genuinely warm toast for my grandma and everyone else who will never hear the words "Löb's theorem" in their life and doesn't need to. The hard part is he already used up the naive move: I can't just get up there and unironically promise they'll love each other forever, since that's the exact step he already flagged as the sketchy one, so it needs to find its way back to something sincere without pretending his objection never happened. Something like 400-500 words, and it should read like an actual toast people would cry a little at, not a logic seminar with "congratulations" tacked onto the end.
