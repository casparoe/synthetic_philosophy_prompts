I've spent the last few sessions of my graduate seminar on practical rationality working through Derek Parfit's "hitchhiker" case from *Reasons and Persons*, and a running disagreement has broken out between me and a colleague who sits in on the seminar. Both my students and my colleague are quite happy to reach for the label "rational irrationality" as though it settled the philosophical question; I'm not convinced it does, and I'd like your help thinking through why. Before I put the question to you, let me set the case out precisely, since a great deal turns on the details. You are dying of thirst in the desert. A driver happens by who is willing to take you into town, but only if he is convinced you will pay him a fee once you arrive safely; suppose he is a highly reliable, though not infallible, judge of sincerity, so that a promise from someone who does not really intend to keep it will not convince him, while a genuine intention will. You have no way of paying him in advance. Once you are safely in town, however, paying no longer seems to serve any purpose for you: the ride is already behind you, the driver has no further leverage, and a purely self-interested agent reasoning about what to do at that later moment, in isolation, seems to have every reason to keep his money. Hence the usual moral drawn from the case: it can be rational, beforehand, to make yourself into the sort of agent who will, when the moment comes, do something that is -- at that later moment -- irrational.

I would like you to resist that moral rather than simply restate it. Please construct the strongest argument you can for the claim that there is, in the end, no irrationality anywhere in this story: that paying on arrival is simply the rational act, full stop, once the rationality of an act is properly assessed relative to the policy or disposition it instantiates rather than in isolation. I would especially like the argument to engage with the following complication, rather than just gesturing at "good dispositions": the reason paying looks irrational at the moment of arrival is that the driver's earlier uncertainty about you has, by then, already been resolved one way or the other. But evidential decision theory's recommendations are likewise sensitive only to what an act reveals about states that were uncertain at the time of choice -- so it is not obvious that evidential decision theory fares any better than causal decision theory once you are already standing safely in town. Is the case, then, really best understood as an objection to causal decision theory specifically, as it is usually presented? Or is its true target any theory, evidential or causal, that assesses the rationality of an act by reference only to the situation as it stands at the moment of that act? Please defend your answer against the strongest objection you can construct against it.
