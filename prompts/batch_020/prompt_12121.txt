I'm plotting a novel built around the rivalry between a celebrated welfare economist and the doctoral advisor she long ago eclipsed and estranged. The economist has staked her public reputation on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, treating it as something close to a mathematical proof of utilitarianism: if individuals and a benevolent social planner are all rational in the von Neumann–Morgenstern sense, and the planner never overrides a preference everyone happens to share, then the planner's ranking of outcomes must be a weighted sum of everybody's utility — so, as she likes to put it in interviews, any sufficiently rational and impartial observer of the world is simply compelled to become a utilitarian. In the novel's climactic scene, at a symposium convened in her honor, I want the old advisor — diminished, faintly bitter, but still formidably sharp — to rise from the audience and dismantle this certainty using what I've seen called the Sen–Weymark critique: the charge that the theorem only goes through if one silently equates two things with no obvious right to be treated as the same — the cardinal utility function that represents a person's preferences over gambles, and whatever it is that actually measures that person's well-being for the purpose of comparing one life against another.

I know enough to sense that this is supposed to be a serious problem rather than a pedant's footnote, and my partner, who has an economics doctorate, insists it's fatal — but I don't yet feel the argument in my own gut well enough to write the advisor's speech so that it lands as devastating rather than merely clever. Could you explain, as concretely as you can, why these two senses of "utility" might come apart — ideally with a simple example of two people whose attitudes toward risk and whose actual welfare pull in different directions — and why Harsanyi's derivation needs the two to coincide before its conclusion carries any ethical weight? I would also value your own assessment of whether the economist has a genuine comeback available to her once the room has heard the objection, or whether, once the equivocation is stated plainly, her confidence really ought to collapse.
