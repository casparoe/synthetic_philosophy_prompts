I got engaged two months ago, and the ring has become an unlikely source of intellectual conflict in my family. At the engagement dinner, my cousin Sana and her partner Mateo spent a good twenty minutes arguing about whether an expensive ring really is a "costly signal" in the handicap-principle sense, and I haven't been able to stop turning over who had the better of it.

(1) Sana opened by calling the ring a clean case of a Zahavian handicap: because it's expensive, a half-hearted or unreliable partner couldn't or wouldn't pay for one, so the expense itself is what makes the gesture credible.

(2) Mateo objected that this isn't quite what Zahavi's principle, as later formalized by Grafen, actually requires. The claim was never that cost as such buys credibility; it's that the marginal cost of signaling has to fall more heavily on low-quality signalers than on high-quality ones -- the same "single-crossing" condition that makes education a credible signal of ability in Spence's job-market model. A price tag that's identical for every buyer doesn't obviously satisfy that, so the ring looks more like undifferentiated money-burning than a genuine handicap.

(3) Sana replied that the condition still holds, just in utility rather than dollar terms: a genuinely committed partner finds the sacrifice easier to bear than an uncommitted one going through the motions, so the marginal cost is lower exactly where the theory needs it to be, even though the sticker price is the same for both.

(4) Mateo answered that this only rescues the argument if willingness-to-sacrifice tracks ability-to-pay, but ring price in practice tracks income far more reliably than devotion -- which is presumably why the custom is pegged to salary rather than left as a freely chosen handicap. And once the threshold is set by social convention and enforced through comparison and expectation, rather than by cost that intrinsically bites harder on the less committed, it starts to look like what Maynard Smith and Harper called a conventional signal rather than a handicap signal -- a different category, with honesty kept in place by outside sanction instead of intrinsic differential cost.

Neither of them budged, and I'd like a more rigorous outside opinion than I can give myself. Could you adjudicate moves (1) through (4) individually?
