I am drafting a historical novel set between Córdoba and Baghdad in which two secondary characters — a jurist-theologian modeled loosely on al-Ghazālī and a court physician modeled loosely on Ibn Rushd (Averroes) — spend a long central chapter arguing about whether fire can truly be said to burn cotton. I am aware the two men never actually crossed paths (Ibn Rushd was born some fifteen years after al-Ghazālī died), so I am knowingly taking the novelist's license of putting fictionalized versions of both in the same room, decades out of true, because their real exchange — al-Ghazālī's attack on causal necessity in the Tahāfut al-Falāsifa and Ibn Rushd's point-by-point rebuttal in the Tahāfut al-Tahāfut — is too good a piece of philosophical theater to leave alone. I want my theologian's lines to track the actual argument rather than caricature it as "believers think logic simply does not apply to God," so I have been working through the Seventeenth Discussion in Michael Marmura's translation, and I have run up against a passage I cannot fully unpack on my own.

The passage, in relevant part, runs as follows: "The connection between what is habitually believed to be a cause and what is habitually believed to be an effect is not, in our view, necessary. Take any two things where neither is the other, and where neither the affirmation nor the negation of the one is entailed by the affirmation or negation of the other: the existence of the one does not necessitate the existence of the other, nor does the nonexistence of the one necessitate the nonexistence of the other. Such is the case with the quenching of thirst and drinking, satiety and eating, burning and contact with fire, light and the rising of the sun, and death and the severing of the head. It is repeated observation of things conjoined that fixes in us the belief that the conjunction is necessary, and not any demonstration that it must be so. Consider the burning of cotton at the point of its contact with fire. We do not deny that the cotton burns; we dispute only who the agent of the burning is. The philosophers hold that fire alone, acting through its own nature and not through choice, is the agent; we hold that the agent is God, whether through the mediation of angels or without mediation, since fire is an inanimate thing possessing no action at all. Their only evidence is the observation of burning at the moment of contact — but observation proves no more than that the one occurs together with the other, not that it occurs by means of it and has no other cause."

I can follow the general shape of the move — no logically necessary tie between the two, hence no barrier to God suspending the pattern — but I mentioned the project over dinner to a friend who did her doctoral work on classical Islamic philosophy, and she warned me that most popular summaries flatten this into exactly the caricature I am trying to avoid, missing whatever is actually doing the argumentative work. So before I put anything further in my theologian's mouth, I would like help with three things. First, what precisely is the "necessity" being denied here: is it narrowly logical or conceptual necessity, of the kind a contradiction would violate, or is al-Ghazālī committing himself to the far stronger claim that there is no binding of any kind between successive events, so that strictly anything could follow from anything? Second, when he insists that fire, being inanimate, "has no action at all," is he saying fire is causally idle in every sense whatsoever — no different in principle from a cold stone lying beside the cotton — or is there some weaker sense in which it remains a necessary condition, an occasion, or an instrument, short of being a genuine efficient cause? Third, I recall someone in a seminar years ago drawing a straight line from this passage to Hume on constant conjunction; I would like to know whether that comparison is actually sound, or whether it papers over a real difference in what each man is trying to establish, given that one is defending God's power to break a pattern at will and the other is questioning whether we ever had grounds for calling the pattern necessary in the first place. Any clarification you can offer on these three points, even partial, would help me get the scene right.
