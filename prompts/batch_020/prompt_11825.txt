ok so weird TA situation: I'm a CS grad student but I somehow got roped into grading finals for a philosophy of mind / AI-ethics seminar this term (long story, dont ask). one of the essay questions was:

"Explain Chalmers's fading qualia and dancing qualia thought experiments, and the principle of organizational invariance they're meant to establish. Then explain what further premise would be needed before that result could tell us anything about whether a digital mind has moral status."

and here's the answer from one of the stronger students in the class (cleaned up slightly, not verbatim):

"Chalmers asks us to imagine replacing your neurons one at a time with silicon chips that are functional duplicates - each chip takes the same inputs from its neighbors and produces the same outputs as the neuron it replaces, so your overall functional organization never changes during the process. Fading qualia: suppose your experience gradually dims or greys out over the course of the replacement even though nothing about your functioning has changed. That would mean at some point you're having a much poorer experience than before while behaving, reacting, and even reporting on your experience exactly as you always did - you'd have no way to notice or say anything about the fading, since your report-generating machinery is working exactly as before. Chalmers thinks this kind of silent fading is absurd. Dancing qualia is the same idea but for a sudden qualitative swap (red experience flipping to blue, say) instead of a gradual dimming, again with no accompanying functional change and hence nothing the subject could notice. Since both scenarios are absurd, Chalmers concludes that experience is fixed by fine-grained functional organization rather than by whatever substrate is doing the organizing - two systems with the same functional organization can't differ in what it's like to be them. That's organizational invariance, and its main job is to rule out a functional duplicate of you having dimmer or absent experience.

To get from there to moral status you need a separate premise, since organizational invariance is a claim about when experience is the same, not a claim about ethics - you'd need something like "having the relevant kind of qualia, especially the capacity to suffer or flourish, is what grounds moral status." Put those together and you get: any system whose functional organization is sophisticated enough to produce genuinely human-like behavior - holding a conversation, responding appropriately to what's said to it, and so on - has to be having the kind of experience a human in that conversation would have, and so has the same moral status. So the real bottleneck for digital minds isn't philosophical, it's just an empirical question of whether current or future AI is actually running that kind of organization rather than something cruder that just produces similar outputs."
