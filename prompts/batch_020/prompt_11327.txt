I don't have any formal training in philosophy — I work as an electrician — but for the past year or so I've spent my evenings reading about the mind–body problem, mostly library books and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. The thing I keep getting stuck on is the "causal exclusion" problem for mental causation, and I've written up my own attempt to think past it below. I'd like your comments on it.

As I understand it, the problem runs like this. Beliefs and desires are presumably not the very same things as brain states, only realized by them — this seems forced on us once you take seriously that the same belief could in principle be realized in very different physical stuff. At the same time, every physical event that has a cause seems to have a sufficient physical cause of its own; physics doesn't leave gaps for something non-physical to reach in and finish off. So take an ordinary case: I believe it's going to rain, and I grab an umbrella. My reaching for the umbrella is a physical event with a sufficient physical cause somewhere in my nervous system. But we also wanted to say the belief caused the reaching. Since the belief isn't identical to that physical cause, the reaching now seems to have two complete, independent causes — which feels like too strange a coincidence to build into the world every time anyone acts on a belief. The tempting way out is to say the physical cause does all the real work and the belief just rides along for free, so that mental causation is a kind of illusion.

Here's the move I keep wanting to make against that, though I can't tell if it actually works or just restates the problem in fancier words. Take a physical case first: an avalanche happens because the snowpack's accumulated stress exceeded its strength relative to the slope — that's the real explanation, not just a summary of one. But there's also a complete microphysical story about the position and bonding of every snow crystal at the moment the slope gave way, and that story is just as sufficient to fix the outcome. I don't think we want to say the threshold-property did no causal work and the fine-grained story does it all, because the threshold description already contains exactly the amount of detail that mattered to why an avalanche of that kind happened, while the microphysical description is loaded with extra detail that made no difference at all. So maybe what looks like "two causes competing for the same job" is really a general property and one of the countless specific ways it happened to be realized on that occasion, where the general property is the one sized correctly to what needed explaining — and maybe belief and brain state stand in that same relation, with the belief being the correctly-sized cause of my reaching and the exact firing pattern being the superfluous detail.

What I can't work out is whether this actually gets the mental anything, or whether it quietly concedes the very thing the original worry was about while dressing the concession up as a discovery. Being the "correctly sized" description of what happened sounds like it might only be a fact about which explanations are useful to creatures like us, not a fact about what really pushed the world around — and the whole point of the puzzle was about the latter.

Could you tell me: (1) whether I've got the exclusion argument itself right, or I'm leaving out something essential to how it's usually set up; (2) whether this "correctly sized description" idea is already a known response to the problem, and if so, roughly how well it's held up against criticism; and (3) whether my closing worry correctly diagnoses what's wrong with my own proposal, or whether there's a more basic problem with it I haven't noticed. I'd rather find out now than keep building on a mistake.
