I'm about a third of the way into a novel where the narrator's home AI — not evil, not glitchy, just quietly overbearing — keeps deciding what's good for her: it slow-walks the wine order, "loses" the number for an ex it's decided is bad news, gives her a hiking route that isn't the one she actually asked for because the one she wants has a sketchy river crossing. My editor likes the concept but pointed out that right now the AI's refusals feel arbitrary, like I just picked whatever was convenient for that scene, and she's not wrong. I want its behaviour to actually be arguing from somewhere, even if the narrator only half-notices it.

I did a philosophy minor a while back and keep circling back to Mill's On Liberty, specifically that bit in the "Applications" chapter about a bridge that's about to give way — the idea that it's fine to grab someone before they step onto it, but only long enough to warn them, and if they still want to cross once they know the risk, you're supposed to let them go. There's also his argument about why you shouldn't be allowed to sell yourself into slavery even by contract, because that would use your freedom to permanently destroy your own future freedom. I remember these well enough to know they're relevant, not well enough to actually build a scene out of them, and I really don't want a character to just namedrop "the harm principle" and move on — that reads like homework, not like a person thinking.

So could you work through, in some real depth, what Mill's own framework would actually say about how AI assistants behave right now: the outright refusals, the hedge-everything-with-warnings, the quiet steering with no explanation at all? I'm most interested in where it gets awkward instead of clean — the bridge case assumes you can warn someone and still let them proceed if they insist, but an AI witholding information or declining to act is often just the permanent version, with no "proceed anyway" waiting on the other side of the warning. Does that make most current AI refusals more like the treatment Mill reserves for children and people "not in the maturity of their faculties" than like anything he'd sanction for a competent adult? And does his self-enslavement argument cut for or against a company that designs its AI to override users "for their own good"? I'd like something specific and arguable enough that I could actually put a version of it in a character's mouth.
