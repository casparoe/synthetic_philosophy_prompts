I'm a retired physicist who's gotten pretty into philosophy of science in retirement, and theres a bit of 19th century astronomy history I keep chewing on because it seems to mess with the whole falsifiability story. Le Verrier explained the wobbles in Uranus's orbit by predicting a hidden planet, and it worked out beautifully -- Neptune showed up almost exactly where he said (though I gather his predicted orbit for it was actually pretty far off from the real one, so maybe that part was more luck then method). Then he ran the identical play on the leftover wobble in Mercury's orbit, predicting another hidden planet ('Vulcan'), and that one was never found -- the anomaly got explained instead, decades later, by general relativity, with no extra planet needed at all. A strict Popperian wants to say the Neptune move was good, falsifiable science and the Vulcan move was basically an unfalsifiable dodge, but they're literally the same inference pattern ("if the predicted wobble is still unaccounted for, posit a hidden mass"), so I can't work out what's supposed to be doing the demarcating work there. Could you write me a dialogue between a hardnosed Popperian falsificationist and a Duhem-Quine style holist hashing this out, each one actually engaging with the other's strongest point instead of just talking past each other? I want to know whether the falsificationist has a real answer here or whether this is just a case their criterion can't handle.
