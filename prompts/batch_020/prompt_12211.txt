Here's the question as given: "In 'Should the Numbers Count?' Taurek denies that we are always required to save the greater number when we can save only one of two groups of strangers, each facing equally serious harm. (a) Explain the argument he gives for this conclusion. (b) Explain what the proposed coin flip is supposed to accomplish, and the range of cases Taurek thinks it covers. (c) Briefly explain how Parfit responds to Taurek in 'Innumerate Ethics.'" Worth 20 points, roughly even across the three parts.

And here's the student's answer, copied exactly:

"Taurek's basic move is to deny that we can add up harms across different people the way we add up harms to a single person. If five strangers will each die and one other stranger will also die, the deaths of the five aren't automatically five times as bad as the one death, because there's no single subject for whom all five losses are experienced together -- there's no "we" that suffers the combined loss. This is basically the same separateness-of-persons point that Rawls uses against utilitarianism, just relocated from a distributive-shares case to a rescue case. Since summing harms across persons is illegitimate, the numbers on each side give you no automatic reason to prefer the larger group, so in a case with no special relationship to either side the fair thing is to flip a coin -- and this holds no matter how lopsided the numbers get, so a thousand against one gets the same coin flip as two against one. The point of the coin flip is to express equal concern and respect for everyone involved, since it's the only way to give each person a genuine, equal chance of survival. Parfit's response in "Innumerate Ethics" basically agrees with Taurek that we shouldn't think of it as literally adding up welfare the way classical utilitarianism does, and Parfit ends up conceding that Taurek was right that nothing obligates us to save the larger group -- Parfit's complaint is just about the framing, not the conclusion."
