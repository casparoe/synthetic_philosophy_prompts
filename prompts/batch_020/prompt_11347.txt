I have no formal training in philosophy or Jewish studies, but for the past year or so I have been working through Maimonides on my own time, alongside the Torah portions his arguments are anchored to. I have written up some notes on a tension I cannot get past, and I would appreciate your comments on it.

In the Guide for the Perplexed (III:32), Maimonides argues that the sacrificial cult was never something God wanted for its own sake; it was a concession to a people raised amid idolatrous sacrifice who could not have been expected to abandon that whole mode of worship at once, so the practice was redirected toward Him rather than abolished outright. A little later (III:46), he goes further and grounds specific details of the sacrificial laws -- which animals, which procedures -- in point-by-point rebuttals of particular star-worshipping customs he had read about in his sources on ancient idolatry. On this reading, sacrifice is medicine for a disease, and the disease is idolatry.

Here is my problem. In the Mishneh Torah, in the Laws of Kings, Maimonides describes the Messianic king rebuilding the Temple and gathering the exiles as one continuous, confidently expected event, and elsewhere in the same code he treats the sacrificial commandments as still fully in force and simply waiting on the Temple to resume. But by his own account, the Messianic age is also when idolatry finally disappears from the earth and everyone comes to recognize the one God. So the cure is scheduled to return in exactly the period when the disease is supposed to be gone for good. It looks like sacrifice has to be both a purely historical accommodation and a permanent institution, and I do not see how it can be both without one of the two texts giving way.

I also came across, secondhand, the fact that Nahmanides raises something related in his commentary on Leviticus 1:9: he points out that Abel's offering, and then Noah's after the flood, are both described in the text as pleasing to God, long before there was any surrounding idolatrous cult for God to be weaning anyone off of. That seems to weaken the accommodation story even for the past, which only makes the future problem worse.

The closest thing I have to a resolution is this. A little further on in the same stretch of the Guide (III:34, if I have the reference right), Maimonides argues that divine law is legislated for what benefits people in general, not tailored case by case, so a law does not bend just because some individual's circumstances no longer match its original rationale. Maybe sacrifice works the same way on a civilizational scale: once instituted for humanity at its idolatrous stage, it stays law regardless of whether a later generation, even the Messianic one, still needs the original medicine. That would explain the permanence, but it comes at a cost I am not sure Maimonides can afford: it makes the reason for a commandment essentially irrelevant to whether the commandment holds, which sits oddly with a thinker who spends dozens of chapters hunting for exactly those reasons.

Could you tell me whether I am representing Maimonides fairly here, whether my proposed way out earns its keep or just relocates the problem one level up, and whether the Nahmanides point actually strengthens my case or is a separate difficulty I am wrongly folding in? I would rather hear where this breaks than be told it holds together.
