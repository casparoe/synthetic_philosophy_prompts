Over the past year I have gotten fairly serious about trading on prediction markets such as Polymarket and Kalshi, mostly on political and economic events, and I have been trying to read more of the underlying theory so that I actually understand what I am doing when I place a bet. In the course of that reading I came across something called the "no-trade theorem," which I believe originates with Milgrom and Stokey. As far as I can tell, it shows that if two people share the same prior beliefs, are both rational, and each knows the other to be rational, then they should never be willing to make a purely speculative bet with one another on the basis of private information, because the mere fact that the other side is willing to take the trade is itself evidence that should make you want to back out of it. This strikes me as a strange result given how much speculative trading obviously happens, including among people who seem perfectly rational and well informed about whatever they are betting on, so I assume there is something about how the theorem's assumptions map onto real markets that I am not appreciating.

What I would find most useful is for you to lay out what you take to be the most important practical implications of this result for someone like me who trades on these platforms as a hobby, rather than a restatement of the theorem itself. In particular, I would like to understand which of its assumptions are doing the real work and are most obviously false of actual prediction markets, such as differing priors, hedging or liquidity needs, or plain overconfidence, and what I should conclude from that about what a market price is really telling me and about how much weight to give my own reasoning when I notice what looks like an attractive bet that nobody else has taken. If it is relevant, I would also be curious how this connects to the broader idea that two genuinely rational people who trust each other's rationality should not be able to knowingly go on holding opposite beliefs about the same question, since that seems to bear on how much confidence I should place in my own contrarian market calls.
