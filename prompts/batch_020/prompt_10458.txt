I do Lincoln–Douglas debate, and this year's resolution turns partly on whether citizens are ever obligated to obey unjust laws, so my coach had us read the actual 1958 Hart–Fuller exchange in the Harvard Law Review instead of just working from summaries, so we'd have real framework material instead of secondhand paraphrases. I've gotten a bit stuck on one specific move in it. Fuller claims a legal system has to satisfy an "inner morality of law" — things like generality, publicity, non-retroactivity, and making sure officials actually apply the rules as written — to count as a legal system at all, not just a "good" one. Hart's response, which people on my circuit just call the "poisoner" objection, is that these principles are really only what any purposive activity needs in order to be efficient at whatever it's doing, good or evil alike: a poisoner wants clear, consistent, well-publicized dosing instructions too, and nobody would call good recipe-writing a "morality." (This is the same exchange where Hart brings up that postwar German case about a wife convicted for denouncing her husband under a Nazi-era statute, but that's not the part I'm stuck on.)

Most of my teammates just treat this as a knockout blow against Fuller and move on. But rereading Fuller's actual reply, it looks like he's gesturing at something more specific than "rule-following is nice": something about the eight principles only making sense on the assumption that the people being governed are capable of knowing the rules in advance and shaping their own conduct around them — which seems like a very different relationship than the one between a poisoner and either the poison or the victim.

Could you work out the strongest version of that argument for me? Specifically, I want you to argue that Fuller's inner-morality-of-law thesis survives Hart's poisoner objection, on the grounds that the eight principles are bound up with treating the governed as reasoning agents in a way that has no real analogue in the poisoner case. Please commit to defending Fuller rather than surveying both sides, and really press on exactly where the disanalogy with the poisoner is supposed to bite, since that part never gets spelled out in anything I've read. I'd also appreciate it if you flagged whether this "respect for agency" move costs Fuller something in the end — for instance, whether it quietly turns his supposedly "procedural" natural law into something that depends on substantive moral premises about autonomy after all, which would be a bigger concession to Hart than Fuller intended.
