Eccentric billionaire tells you he'll wire a huge sum into your account tonight if, by midnight, you've formed a sincere intention to drink a nasty-but-harmless toxin at noon tomorrow. You don't actually have to drink it to get paid — he just needs to verify that you really intend to (assume he's got some flawless way of checking, a perfect lie detector or whatever). And then everyone's favorite follow-up: once midnight passes and the money's sitting in your account, you have literally zero reason left to go through with drinking the stuff, so in what sense could you have "genuinely" intended to do it in the first place, knowing all along you'd bail the second the incentive disappeared?

I keep seeing people on rationalist forums wave this away with "oh that's basically just Parfit's hitchhiker" — you know, the one where you're stranded in the desert and a driver says he'll only give you a lift if he's confident you'll pay him once your safely in town, he drops you off, and now (under plain causal decision theory anyway) you have no causal reason left to actually pay up, which is the kind of case that gets used for the whole "why ain'cha rich" style jab against CDT in the functional decision theory literature. And sure, on the surface the two puzzles have the same shape: something checks your disposition in advance, rewards you for having the "right" one, and then later on there's a moment where following through does you no further good whatsoever.
