Anyone who has spent time on LessWrong or in the old MIRI workshop write-ups will recognize the basic shape of the Löbian obstacle: Löb's theorem says a formal system can prove "if I can prove P, then P is true" only for those P it can already prove outright, which seems to block a self-modifying agent from ever fully vouching for a successor that reasons exactly as it does. What's harder to find is a serious argument about whether the obstacle still bites. Write a dialogue, set at a whiteboard still covered in modal-logic notation from an earlier argument, between two rationalist-forum regulars who are equally fluent in the material, agree on the theorem, but split sharply on its upshot. The pessimist holds that the obstacle marks a permanent, in-principle ceiling on formal self-trust: a successor can be vetted only by sacrificing either full generality or full certainty, and no amount of engineering changes that. The optimist counters that the tiling-agents trick of a descending tower of proof systems — where each agent certifies its successor safe for only some fixed number of future steps rather than safe as such — together with probabilistic approaches like logical induction, gives an agent everything it could reasonably want from self-trust, so the "obstacle" was only ever an artifact of insisting on one unnecessarily rigid formalization of trust. Have them push each other toward concrete cases rather than resting on slogans, with neither side simply conceding.
