I write about science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and I'm working on a feature about how statistical "paradoxes" get deployed in public argument. Simpson's paradox is the one I keep running into. Every popular treatment I can find — including the two examples that turn up in nearly every textbook, the 1973 Berkeley graduate-admissions figures and the 1980s British kidney-stone-treatment study — lands on the same tidy moral: the pooled numbers were misleading, the numbers disaggregated by department or by stone size are the real picture, and the lesson for the reader is to distrust aggregates and go looking for the hidden grouping variable. A causal-inference researcher I spoke to for background complicated that moral in a way none of the popular write-ups I've read even mention. Her point was that whether you should trust the pooled figure or the split-by-group figure depends on the causal role of the variable doing the splitting: if it is a confounder sitting upstream of the "treatment," stratifying removes a genuine bias, but if it is a mediator that the treatment itself helps bring about, stratifying can just as easily bury a real effect as expose a fake one — and in a case like Berkeley, where department choice is plausibly shaped by the same gendered social pressures that the discrimination question is trying to get at, deciding which number to trust is a substantive causal assumption dressed up as a routine statistical correction.

I'd like you to build that argument out properly, for readers who have absorbed "when Simpson's paradox shows up, trust the disaggregated data" as a settled rule of statistical hygiene. Please argue against that general slogan: use the distinction between a confounder and a mediator (bring in colliders too, if it sharpens the point) to show that deciding which level of analysis is the causally and normatively relevant one is a modeling choice the data cannot settle by themselves. Support the argument with a short toy example that you construct yourself — not Berkeley, not the kidney stones — in which stratifying on the lurking variable conceals a genuine overall effect rather than exposing a spurious one, and then use the same reasoning to say something substantive about whether "Berkeley shows no sex bias once you control for department" is really as settled as it is usually presented. Close by addressing the obvious rejoinder: that in the large majority of everyday invocations of the paradox, the lurking variable really is a garden-variety upstream confounder, so "disaggregate first" remains decent practical advice even if it is not a theorem.
