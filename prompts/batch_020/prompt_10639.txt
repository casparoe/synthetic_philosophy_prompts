Ok so this whole thing kicked off because of a really dumb fight with my sister two weekends ago. I'd just had my wisdom teeth out and was still pretty deep into the leftover hydrocodone, and we got into it over whether my mom's layover in Denver was actually long enough for her to make her connecting flight. I did the arithmetic out loud like three seperate times and got the same answer every time, and felt completely sure of it. My sister kept saying "you literally cannot trust your own math right now, you're on painkillers" - which, fair, opioids mess with people's heads, that's not controversial - but from the inside my reasoning didn't feel foggy or uncertain at all, each recheck just confirmed the same clean number. She wasn't even claiming to have found a mistake, just that I was in a bad position to know whether there was one.

That fight sent me down a rabbit hole (my housemate is doing a philosophy PhD and pointed me toward some of the reading) into stuff about "higher-order evidence," and apparently the specific state I was in even has a name - "epistemic akrasia" - believing something at the first-order level while also, in some sense, accepting a higher-order verdict that you shouldn't trust your own belief-forming process right then. And there's a real live disagreement about whether that combination can ever be rational: on one side there's something like a conciliationist/enkratic view (I think this is basically the Christensen/Elga line) that says no, if you get genuine evidence your faculties are compromised you're rationally required to lower confidence no matter how compelling things still seem from the inside, and that any view which lets you shrug that off is really just letting you bootstrap your own reliability. On the other side there's a "level-splitting" view (I think this one's mostly associated with Lasonen-Aarnio) that says sometimes the right move is to keep full first-order confidence exactly because your faculties are, in fact, fine, and that caving to misleading testimony about your own unreliability just means throwing away perfectly good knowledge you'd otherwise have.

Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers defending these two opposing sides? I'd like it set in basically my situation - someone who's just re-derived the same answer for the third time in a row, with a friend standing there who (based on solid outside information) keeps telling them not to trust it. I want both characters to be genuinely sharp and to engage with each other's actual arguments rather than just re-asserting their own view - in particular I want the level-splitter to have to directly answer the bootstrapping charge (why doesn't their view let you dismiss literally any evidence of your own unreliability, ever?), and I want the conciliationist to have to directly answer the "you're just throwing away real knowledge" charge (why should misleading testimony be able to rationally strip someone of a belief they reached through perfectly sound reasoning?). It doesn't need to end in a tidy resolution - I'd honestly rather it didn't - I just want it to feel like watching two people who really know this material go a few genuine rounds with each other.
