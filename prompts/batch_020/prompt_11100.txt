ok so over drinks this weekend my friend Dana and I got into it about that old thought experiment with the evil neuroscientist -- Black wires into Jones's brain so that if Jones ever shows a flicker of not deciding to shoot his rival, the chip takes over and forces his hand, but as it happens Jones goes ahead and decides to do it entirely on his own, so the chip never even has to switch on. Dana's whole point was that Jones can't really be blamed since, chip or no chip, he literally could not have done otherwise. I said that's beside the point -- blame should track the actual story of why he pulled the trigger, not some other story that never happened. We went back and forth for like two hours, nobody won, and I mostly forgot about it until Tuesday, when a case landed on the desk of our state board of professional engineers (I'm one of the public members) that has almost the exact same shape, minus the murder and plus a lot more paperwork.

Short version: an engineer, R., certified that a parking garage repair met code when, per the later audit, it didn't quite. R. reviewed the incomplete work herself, had real doubts on record in her emails, and signed off anyway under deadline pressure from the client. Turns out her firm had quietly adopted a policy where if the engineer of record doesn't submit a certification by the contract deadline, the project software auto-applies that engineer's stamp to a pre-drafted sign-off so the job doesn't stall -- and the logs show that if R. had simply done nothing, her stamp wouldve ended up on basically the same certification anyway, without her lifting a finger. She swears she didn't know the fallback existed, and nothing in the record contradicts that. Half the board says the fallback is a total red herring, she made a real professional judgment call and should be held to it, full stop. The other half says that if her stamp was going on that document no matter what she decided, its weird to hang the entire weight of professional responsibility on her personal choice, and that we should go easier on her and go after the firm for the policy itself instead.

I have to bring a short written opinion to our meeting next week -- something with an actual recommendation for what should happen to R.'s license, not "further review needed," and reasoning that takes the side I don't land on seriously instead of dismissing it in a sentence. Can you draft that for me?
