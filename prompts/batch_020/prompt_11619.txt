As a longtime reader of Less Wrong and Slate Star Codex, I keep encountering the same rhetorical set-piece: Daryl Bem's 2011 "Feeling the Future" experiments, which used entirely conventional null-hypothesis significance testing to report "significant" evidence for precognition and became one of the opening shots of the replication crisis in psychology, are cited as proof that frequentist statistics cannot be trusted, with the Bayes-factor reanalysis by Wagenmakers and colleagues — applying a skeptical prior to show the evidence for psi was actually weak — offered as the corrective. I have yet to see this argument actually fought out rather than simply invoked as a parable.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between a frequentist statistician and a Bayesian statistician re-litigating the Bem case at a methods conference. The Bayesian should defend the Bayes-factor reanalysis as capturing something p-values structurally cannot: that an extraordinary claim requires a correspondingly strong likelihood ratio, not merely a threshold crossed. The frequentist should counter that the detour through priors was unnecessary — that identifying and correcting for the exploratory, multiple-comparison-laden way Bem collected and analyzed his data would have deflated the result within the frequentist framework alone — and should press the Bayesian on whether the "skeptical" prior used against Bem was principled, or reverse-engineered to yield the conclusion everyone already favored. Have the Bayesian answer that challenge on its merits rather than retreating to restating Bayes' theorem, keep both debaters equally sharp, and make sure each responds to the other's actual arguments rather than talking past them.
