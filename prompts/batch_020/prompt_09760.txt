ok this has been bugging me for a while, and in like a decade of reading LW/ACX-adjacent stuff I don't think I've ever seen anyone spell it out: the backward induction argument in the centipede game and the surprise exam paradox seem to be running the exact same trick. Both start from an obviously-settled last case (final round, final day) and chain backward through a common-knowledge-style argument to a conclusion that nobody actually believes once their in the situation -- defect turn one, the exam can never be a surprise -- and that real behavior falsifies anyway (people keep cooperating for many rounds in actual centipede experiments, the test does end up surprising people). Compare the two for me: is the logical structure actually identical once you spell out each step, or does the resemblance break down somewhere? And of the standard fixes on offer -- denying common knowledge of rationality / worrying about off-path beliefs for the centipede case, vs. the self-reference objections usually raised against the exam paradox's notion of 'surprise' -- which move do you think genuinely resolves its own paradox, and does that same move carry over to the other one at all?
