I've been working through Leo Tolstoy's 1897 treatise *What Is Art?* (in Aylmer Maude's translation) for a small reading group, partly out of curiosity about what a fully worked-out anti-formalist theory of art looks like when it isn't run through the signaling/status machinery that gets applied to almost every other cultural practice on rationalist blogs (Hanson's writing on art and status comes to mind). Tolstoy's alternative rests on his concept of "infection": a work counts as art exactly insofar as it transmits a felt emotion from artist to audience, uniting them in that feeling, and the degree of infection is supposed to be the sole measure of a work's merit as art, quite apart from its subject matter. That part I can follow without much trouble. What trips me up is the very next move, in Chapter XV, where Tolstoy lays out three conditions that are each supposed to govern how infectious a work is, and then tries to collapse two of them into the third. Here is the relevant passage, lightly trimmed (the cuts are marked and don't change the shape of the argument):

"And the degree of the infectiousness of art depends on three conditions:— (1) On the greater or lesser individuality of the feeling transmitted; (2) on the greater or lesser clearness with which the feeling is transmitted; (3) on the sincerity of the artist, i.e. on the greater or lesser force with which the artist himself feels the emotion he transmits. … I have mentioned three conditions of contagiousness in art, but they may all be summed up into one, the last, sincerity, i.e. that the artist should be impelled by an inner need to express his feeling. That condition includes the first; for if the artist is sincere he will express the feeling as he experienced it. And as each man is different from everyone else, his feeling will be individual for everyone else; and the more individual it is,—the more the artist has drawn it from the depths of his nature,—the more sympathetic and sincere will it be. And this same sincerity will impel the artist to find a clear expression of the feeling which he wishes to transmit. Therefore this third condition—sincerity—is the most important of the three. It is always complied with in peasant art, and this explains why such art always acts so powerfully; but it is a condition almost entirely absent from our upper-class art, which is continually produced by artists actuated by personal aims of covetousness or vanity."

The crux for me is that middle inference. Tolstoy needs sincerity to entail, not merely correlate with, both individuality and clearness, since he is about to lean on sincerity alone to explain why "peasant art" reliably beats sophisticated upper-class art elsewhere in the book. But the entailment from sincerity to clearness looks straightforwardly false to me: I can picture an artist who is maximally sincere, genuinely driven by inner need, and yet simply lacks the technical facility to express the feeling clearly - and, symmetrically, a cold, calculating technician who fakes sincerity convincingly enough to infect an audience anyway. Is there a more charitable reading of "that condition includes the first" than a flat (and false) logical entailment - some idealized notion of sincerity that already smuggles in expressive competence, perhaps, or a causal-tendency claim dressed up as a conceptual one? Or is this just a seam in the argument that Tolstoy needs to paper over in order to reach the populist conclusions he wants later on? I would appreciate a close, charitable-but-honest reading of exactly what is going on, logically, in that paragraph.
