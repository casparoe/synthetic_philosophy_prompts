I am a fingerprint examiner at a forensic laboratory, and last month I re-examined a set of prints that I had marked inconclusive six months earlier; knowing this time that the suspect had already confessed, I was unsettled to find what looked to me like a clear match in the ridge detail. My supervisor wants to cite this incident in a training memo as evidence that contextual information can genuinely change what an examiner perceives, rather than merely biasing the final match-or-non-match judgment, and I would like to know whether that is a fair way to describe what happened before the memo goes out. Could you give a concrete example, drawn from forensic pattern-matching or closely analogous expert perception, of a case that would satisfy strict criteria for genuine cognitive penetration of perceptual experience, in the sense Pylyshyn or Fodor intended, paired with a superficially similar case that critics such as Firestone and Scholl would instead attribute to a shift in attention, judgment, or memory rather than to perception itself?
