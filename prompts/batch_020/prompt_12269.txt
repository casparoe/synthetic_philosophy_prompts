I spent my working life as a geophysicist, and it is only since retiring that I have had the leisure to read philosophy with any care, rather than in odd hours on airplanes. My reading group has lately been working through William James's "The Will to Believe" alongside the Clifford essay it answers, and at our last meeting we were asked to test James's argument against a hypothetical case: a geologist who, decades ago, staked her career on a heterodox hypothesis about deep-mantle convection that the consensus of the time regarded as unsupported by the evidence available, and who insisted afterward that she could not have sustained years of unglamorous, often-discouraging work on it without genuinely believing the hypothesis, rather than merely "accepting" it provisionally for the sake of argument. Two of the other members, Renate and Oskar, ended up in a real disagreement about how to apply James's framework to this case, and the rest of us mostly just listened.

Renate opened by arguing that the geologist's case met all three of James's conditions for what he calls a genuine option: sustaining belief was forced, since years of suspended judgment would in practice have amounted to abandoning the research program; it was momentous, since a career and a research direction that would not come again were at stake; and it was live, since serious colleagues, unlike flat-earthers, still took the hypothesis seriously. She added that James's Alpine climber needs actual confidence, not mere hope, to make his leap, and that the geologist's stamina through years of discouraging results plausibly required the same kind of genuine conviction rather than a merely provisional, evidence-tracking stance. Oskar replied that James, earlier in the very same essay, treats ordinary scientific hypothesis-testing as paradigmatically a "trivial" option, picturing a chemist who spends a year testing an idea and simply counts the year lost if it fails, no great harm done; the reason, Oskar said, is that professional and reputational stakes are not the unique, irreversible stakes James has in mind, and treating career investment as momentous in his sense just smuggles in the very self-interested motive that James and Clifford agree ought to be kept out of belief-formation. Renate answered that Oskar was trading on an ambiguity: James's own definition of "momentous" turns on whether an opportunity is unique and a decision irreversible, not on whether the stakes are professional or metaphysical, and a scientist's one real chance at a research direction before funding or a field moves on plausibly meets that definition on its own terms, so the chemist example does not settle the case either way. Oskar then shifted ground: the Alpine leap and the case of a friendship that warms because one trusts it will, he said, work for James only because belief there is partly self-fulfilling, whereas nothing about mantle rock cares what the geologist believes, so even a genuinely forced, live, and momentous option lacks the mechanism doing the real justificatory work in James's strongest examples. Renate's rejoinder was that James extends the identical license to the religious hypothesis itself, which he treats as no more obviously self-verifying than the geologist's case and which is in fact his real target throughout the essay, the Alpine climber and the anxious lover being offered only as warm-up cases before he turns to religion -- so if Oskar wants to disqualify the scientific case for lacking a self-verifying mechanism, he owes an account of why the religious case is not disqualified on the same grounds. Oskar's closing move was that James does have something to say here with no analogue in geology: he holds that withholding assent from the religious hypothesis forfeits a real good by the very act of withholding, in a way that withholding assent about a rock stratum does not -- so the two cases can still be prised apart, just not by appeal to self-verification alone.

I would be grateful if you would go through this exchange move by move and tell me, for each of the six turns, who had the better of it and why, tying your judgment to the specifics of what James (and, where it bears on the point, Clifford) actually argues rather than to the general shape of the dispute. I would also value your own sense of whether Renate and Oskar are, between them, overlooking some further consideration that would settle the matter more cleanly than any of their six moves manages to.
