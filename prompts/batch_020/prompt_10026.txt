Three months ago my aunt passed away and, somewhat to my surprise, named me executor of her estate, a role I had never anticipated and have found considerably more taxing than expected. Her will was drafted nearly twenty years ago and never revised; the clause now at issue leaves "my house at 14 Birch Lane" to my cousin Elena, to whom our aunt was especially close. The complication is that our aunt sold that property eleven years ago and, combining the proceeds with a substantial mortgage, bought a larger house in a different town, which is where she was living when she died.

My other cousins maintain that the will has to be honored as written: since the specific property described in that clause was no longer hers at death, the bequest simply fails, and its value should instead be folded into the residuary estate and divided equally among all the grandchildren. One of them even points out that elsewhere in the will our aunt refers more loosely to "my property" and "my belongings," and argues that her decision to specify a street address in Elena's clause, rather than something like "my home" or "wherever I am living," shows she meant that particular building and nothing else. Elena thinks this reads far too much into an elderly woman's drafting habits and treats an incidental fact about which house our aunt happened to own as though it overrides what she plainly intended; she notes that our aunt never updated the will after moving because she assumed, reasonably enough, that the gift would simply carry over to wherever she ended up living. Our estate attorney has declined to take a side, saying only that courts in our state have decided similar cases both ways and that the outcome would likely depend on a probate judge's reading if my cousins choose to press the matter formally.

Having turned this over many times, I no longer trust my own sense of who has the stronger position, and I would welcome a more principled way of thinking about the dispute than simply guessing which way a judge might lean. It strikes me that the same underlying disagreement, whether to hold to the words actually used or to give weight to what their author was evidently trying to accomplish, keeps resurfacing in other situations I encounter involving contracts, policies, and rules, and I would like a firmer grip on how to reason about it in general, and not just in Elena's case.
