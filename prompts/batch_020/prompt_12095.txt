As a novelist, I find that a philosophical argument only really shows its shape once someone stands to lose something by making it. I'd like a short story about Gongsun Long, the Warring States-era sophist of the School of Names, built around the semi-legendary anecdote in which he tries to lead his white horse across a border checkpoint that, at the time, is barred to horses. Rather than having him toss off the slogan "a white horse is not a horse" and leave it at that, I want him to actually work the case on the guard, using the real substance behind it: the claim that "horse" names a shape while "white" names a color, and the observation that anyone wanting a horse would accept one of any color while anyone wanting a white horse would not. I'm less interested in whether he talks his way through than in what the scene reveals about the gulf between an argument that is watertight on its own terms and the categories the world actually holds you to. Keep it to roughly 1,000 words, and make sure the logic is doing real work in the dialogue rather than sitting in the background as local color.
