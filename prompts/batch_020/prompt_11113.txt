I'm writing a dissertation chapter on the conditional analysis of dispositions and have gotten stuck on a distinction that keeps recurring in the literature. C. B. Martin's electro-fink case is generally taken to refute the simple conditional analysis, and David Lewis's reformed version — which requires a disposition's intrinsic causal basis to persist through the stimulus — is supposed to neutralize finkish counterexamples. Yet masking/antidote cases (a fragile glass shielded by protective packaging, or Bird's poison rendered harmless by an antidote) are supposed to survive that very fix, even though the causal basis looks just as intact in a mask as in any finkish case Lewis's analysis handles correctly. Could you explain, in intuitive terms, exactly what structural feature distinguishes a fink from a mask so that requiring basis-persistence rules out the former but not the latter, and whether any standard response in the literature manages to draw that line without covertly presupposing it — or whether the mask/antidote problem instead shows that no purely counterfactual analysis of dispositions can be salvaged?
