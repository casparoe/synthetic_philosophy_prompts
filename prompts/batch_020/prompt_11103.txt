After forty years as an experimental physicist, I have spent much of my retirement reading philosophy of science, and Larry Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction has proven particularly hard to shake: because many past empirically successful theories — phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether, among others — were later abandoned as false, the argument concludes that we have little reason to trust the approximate truth of our own best current theories. What troubles me, in the way a poorly specified sample troubles an experimentalist, is that Laudan's list is a curated set of failures for which no reference class of mature, successful theories, and no corresponding base rate of survival within it, is ever specified. Please construct the strongest argument you can that the pessimistic meta-induction, in its standard form, commits precisely this kind of base-rate (or reference-class) fallacy, and that, once corrected for, the historical record gives us little genuine reason to doubt the approximate truth of our best current theories.
