I spent close to thirty-five years doing low-temperature scattering experiments before I retired, and I've since gotten hooked on philosophy of science through a book club my daughter roped me into (her revenge, probably, for years of dragging her to conference banquets). Last month we did Ian Hacking's *Representing and Intervening*, and the chapter on experimentation really got its hooks in me — his argument that you can be a realist about an entity like the electron without being a realist about whatever the current best theory of the electron says, because we can grab electrons and use them as tools to do things, like spraying electrons and positrons onto a niobium ball to hunt for leftover fractional charges from quarks. The line I remember (probably mangled slightly) is something like "if you can spray them, they're real" — which honestly is close to how I always felt about my own gear. I trusted the electrons in my detector even on days I didn't fully trust the theory paper explaining them.

Now, here's the reason I'm actually writing. My old postdoc, who I still have dinner with occasionally, left academia for one of the AI labs and works on "mechanistic interpretability" — poking around inside neural networks looking for "features" and "circuits." She was telling me about the well-known case where Anthropic found a direction inside Claude that lights up for the Golden Gate Bridge, and when they clamped it to a huge value the model started insisting it WAS the bridge. Her camp treats things like this (activation patching, steering vectors, sparse autoencoders pulling apart "superposed," overlapping features) as basically Hacking-style manipulability evidence that these representations are real. But apparently plenty of people in her field think its a mirage — that the "features" are just a convenient, basis-dependent story draped over a big pile of uninterpretable vector geometry, and that clamping a direction and getting the behavior you named it after isn't much of a discovery at all.

So here's what I'd like from you: compare Hacking's manipulability argument for entity realism to this realism-about-interpretability dispute, and actually adjudicate it rather than just laying both out neatly side by side. Does causally steering a "feature" and getting the predicted output really do the same epistemic work as spraying electrons to shift a charge by a specific, independently checkable amount, or is there a disanalogy — something about circularity in how the features get named in the first place, or about there being no privileged decomposition into "real" parts, or about novel predictions versus after-the-fact fitting — that breaks the comparison? Please land somewhere definite. I've had enough "both sides have a point" non-answers from journal referees to last a lifetime.
