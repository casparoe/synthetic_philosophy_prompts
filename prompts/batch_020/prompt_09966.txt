For some thirty years I worked as an experimental physicist, and a good part of that career was spent worrying about what has to be true for a reading on one instrument, or by one observer, to be treated as commensurable with a reading on another: calibration, invariance under change of apparatus, operational definitions, and so on. Since retiring I have turned to reading philosophy, and I recently found myself back on oddly familiar ground while working through some welfare economics. The question there is whether one can meaningfully compare how well-off one person is with how well-off another person is - whether interpersonal comparisons of utility mean anything beyond each person's own private ranking of their own preferences - and it struck me as the calibration problem again, except that here nobody can point to any procedure, however indirect, that would settle it. Lionel Robbins and the ordinalist economists who followed him concluded that such comparisons are not scientific claims at all: there is no observation that could tell you whether an extra measure of relief means more to one person than to another, so any policy that trades one person's loss against another's gain is dressing up an ethical judgment as a fact. What unsettled me was then reading John Harsanyi's attempt to rescue the idea, using an "impartial observer" who does not know in advance which member of society they will turn out to be, and who therefore compares lives the way an ordinary decision theorist compares risky prospects, using expected-utility reasoning to do the work that direct observation cannot. I cannot decide whether this actually meets Robbins's challenge or merely relocates the same unverifiable assumption one step back, into the claim that my hypothetical betting behavior "as you" tells us something about your satisfactions and not merely about mine.

The question stopped being purely abstract for me a few months ago, when I followed a local hospital ethics committee's public deliberations over which of two interventions to fund from a fixed budget: one that would give a large number of patients modest relief from chronic pain, the other that would give a much smaller number of patients a substantial extension of life. Whichever way the committee decided, it seemed to me that they were implicitly weighing one kind of benefit to one set of people against a different kind of benefit to a different set of people - exactly the sort of comparison Robbins says can never be more than a value judgment in disguise.

I would like you to write a dialogue between two people serving on such a committee: one who holds, in the spirit of Robbins, that interpersonal comparisons of utility are never more than value judgments dressed up as facts, and that the committee should say so honestly rather than claim a precision it cannot have; the other who holds, in the spirit of Harsanyi, that such comparisons can be given real content and that refusing to make them explicit does not avoid the underlying ethical work but only conceals it. I would like the two of them to engage with each other's strongest moves rather than talk past one another: in particular, the Harsanyian should have to answer the suspicion that the impartial-observer construction shows something only about one person's coherent preferences under uncertainty, not about the comparability of two different people's inner lives, while the Robbins-style skeptic should have to answer the charge that his own refusal to compare is not a neutral default but a substantive comparison in disguise. It would help me if, by the end, they had at least clarified for themselves whether their disagreement is really about whether interpersonal comparisons exist as facts, about whether such facts, if they exist, could ever be known, or about something more practical that neither of those questions need settle.
