The question was: "In 2–3 paragraphs, explain the orthogonality thesis and the instrumental convergence thesis, how the two theses relate to each other, and briefly assess whether the orthogonality thesis has any bearing on the truth of moral realism." Here's the answer I got back, copied word for word:

"The orthogonality thesis, popularized by Nick Bostrom, holds that intelligence and final goals are independent axes -- roughly any level of cognitive ability could in principle be paired with roughly any final goal, so a superintelligent agent could just as easily be optimizing for curing cancer as for producing paperclips. This is meant to knock down the intuitive idea that getting smarter naturally makes an agent more moral, since on this view 'smart' just describes how good an agent is at achieving its goals, whatever those happen to be.

Instrumental convergence, an idea Bostrom introduces in Superintelligence, is the claim that although final goals can vary arbitrarily, a wide range of agents with very different final goals will still converge on pursuing certain instrumental sub-goals, like self-preservation, resource acquisition, and cognitive enhancement, simply because those sub-goals are useful for almost any final goal. So the two ideas slot together neatly: orthogonality explains why we can't predict an AI's final goal from its intelligence, and instrumental convergence explains why we can predict quite a lot about its behavior anyway, because the instrumental means it reaches for will look similar regardless of the end. That's the whole 'paperclip maximizer' scenario -- it's not that the AI hates us, it's that self-preservation and resource acquisition come bundled with basically any sufficiently ambitious goal.

On moral realism, I'd actually say the orthogonality thesis settles the matter pretty decisively against it, at least against the version of moral realism that's supposed to be relevant to AI safety. If there were objective moral facts that any sufficiently rational agent would necessarily be moved by, you couldn't coherently describe a fully rational, arbitrarily intelligent paperclip maximizer -- but the orthogonality thesis says you can. So either there are no such motivating moral facts, or -- which comes to the same thing in practice -- whatever 'rationality' means for an agent's intelligence level has nothing to do with the kind of rationality that's supposed to track moral truth. Either way, you shouldn't expect a superintelligence to be good just because it's smart, which is really the point of the thesis in the first place."
