Two people in the experimental philosophy reading group I sit in on got into a running argument a few weeks back that's been stuck in my head ever since. (Context: I'm actually a cog sci PhD student, not philosophy, but the group overlaps with some concepts-and-categorization stuff I work on, so I audit it.) We'd just read the old Weinberg, Nichols and Stich paper where they ran Gettier-style vignettes on Western and East Asian subjects, plus a socioeconomic-status split within the US, and found the groups gave noticeably different verdicts on whether the guy in the case "really knows" the thing or just happens to have a true belief. The next meeting covered the fallout literature -- some failed replications, plus the "expertise defense" stuff -- and that's what set Marcus and Leah off. It blew way past our end time and then kept going over email, where I got cc'ed for some reason despite not saying a word in the actual room.

(1) Marcus's opening move was the restrictionist line: if ordinary people's Gettier intuitions split along cultural and class lines, that's bad news for the standard epistemology method of leaning on intuitions to build and check a theory of knowledge, since it suggests those data points aren't tracking one shared thing. (2) Leah's comeback was that this is too fast -- maybe the groups aren't actually disagreeing about whether the same concept applies to the case, they might just have somewhat different concepts of "knowledge" to start with (translation issues, different everyday uses of the nearest word in Mandarin, whatever), in which case there's no real first-order disagreement to worry about, any more than two people "disagreeing" about whether a stool counts as a chair. (3) Marcus shot back that this move is suspiciously convenient: you can always rescue a favorite intuition from an inconvenient result by declaring that whoever disagrees with you must mean something different by the words, and nobody would accept that dodge if it were used to explain away a philosopher's own inconvenient intuition. (4) Leah then pivoted and said the deeper issue is that the original effect might not even be real -- she brought up the much bigger replication attempts (Kim and Yuan, and I think seperately Seyedsayamdost?) that reran the same vignettes and didn't find the cross-cultural or SES gap at all, so arguing over what the effect means is premature. (5) Marcus granted the point but said it doesn't save business-as-usual either, because the fallback move -- the "expertise defense," where only trained philosophers' judgments count as real evidence since training presumably filters out noise -- has also been tested directly and hasn't held up great, since a couple of studies found professional philosophers show similar order and framing effects on other classic cases as random undergrads do. (6) Leah's closing shot was that this is just a different argument than the one they started with, and that Marcus had quietly swapped "the specific cross-cultural Gettier result" for "a grab bag of unrelated worries about intuitions generally" the moment his original evidence got shaky.

Could you go through this move by move and tell me who's actually right at each step, not just who wins overall? Give me a quick JSON block scoring moves 1 through 6 from -2 (clearly wrong) to 2 (clearly right), like {"1": 0, "2": 0, ...}, then explain each score in a sentence or two. I'm especially torn on whether move (2) is a legitimate methodological point or exactly the unfalsifiable dodge Marcus accuses it of in move (3), and whether Leah's move (6) charge actually sticks given that Marcus's move (5) arguably still concerns whether anyone's intuitions -- expert or not -- deserve evidential weight. And if you think there's a bigger point neither of them is seeing that would actually settle this, I'd like to hear it.
