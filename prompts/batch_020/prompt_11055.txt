In my graduate seminar on philosophy of mind this term, we have been working through the family of projects that try to naturalize intentional content: that is, to explain, in broadly causal or nomological terms, how a mental representation can be about something and, crucially, how it can misrepresent that thing. Two of the most carefully worked out proposals are Ruth Millikan's teleosemantics, on which content is fixed by the evolved "proper functions" of the systems that produce and consume a representation together with the "Normal conditions" (a technical term, not a statistical one) under which those functions were historically performed, and Jerry Fodor's asymmetric dependence theory, on which content is fixed instead by a network of current counterfactual-supporting causal laws, with no appeal to selective history at all.

Several of my students have gotten stuck on the question of which theory actually earns its keep as a solution to the disjunction problem: the worry that, because a representation is sometimes tokened by its intended target and sometimes by lookalikes, decoys, or degraded stimuli, nothing in a purely causal story stops us from saying its content is simply the disjunction of everything that actually causes it, so that misrepresentation becomes conceptually impossible. I would like you to compare Millikan's and Fodor's ways of blocking this conclusion, and then to test both accounts against the Swampman case: a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a person that is assembled by sheer chance (the standard version has lightning striking a swamp) and so has no evolutionary lineage or history of selection, even though it is physically and behaviorally indistinguishable from the original at the moment it comes into being. Does either theory deliver a verdict about Swampman's mental states that its own proponents should be happy with? On balance, which of the two theories gives the more satisfying resolution of the disjunction problem, and does it buy that success at some hidden cost elsewhere, for instance by making what a state means depend on facts, historical or nomological, that seem to have no bearing on what is going on inside the head at the moment of tokening?
