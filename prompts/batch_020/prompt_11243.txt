I only really got into philosophy after I retired, and these days I go to a little Tuesday discussion group -- I'm the token ex-physicist in the bunch (35 years doing spectroscopy, so margins of error are basically my whole personality). Last week two of the regulars, Paul and Marge, got into a real scrap over Williamson's "anti-luminosity" arguement, the one that starts with feeling cold on a slowly warming day. Paul laid it out roughly like this: if you know you feel cold at some instant, then since you can't tell that instant apart from one a millisecond later (the temperature's barely moved), you must still feel cold a millisecond later too -- and chaining that all the way across the day would mean you're still "cold" even once you're sweating, which is absurd, so somewhere along the line you felt cold without knowing it. He took this to mean you don't always know when you know something, so the KK principle is false, full stop. Marge agreed with the argument itself but said Paul was overselling the conclusion: all it really shows is introspection breaking down right at the borderline, not some sweeping collapse of KK -- there are still tons of ordinary cases where you obviously know that you know. Paul said that's beside the point, KK is stated as a universal principle, so one genuine counterexample anywhere kills it outright, no hedging -- and worse, since you can never be fully sure you're not in one of those borderline cases yourself, right now, that basically infects every claim you'd make about your own knowledge. Marge shot back that he was mixing up "this specific principle has a counterexample" with "introspection is globally worthless," and that of all people a scientist should get the difference between an instrument having a margin of error and an instrument being junk. Nobody at the table, me included, could settle it. Could you go through their exchange move by move, say who's got the better of it at each step, and flag anything important both of them might be missing?
