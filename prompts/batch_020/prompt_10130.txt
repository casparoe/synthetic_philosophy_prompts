So I've been going down a philosophy-of-mind rabbit hole for a couple months now (no formal background in this, just late nights on wikipedia/SEP and random youtube lectures), and I keep running into this thing called the "causal exclusion argument." As I understand it, the idea is: every physical event, like a neuron firing, already has a fully sufficient physical cause, so there's no causal room left over for whatever mental property is supposedly riding along with it — the mental stuff gets "excluded" and doesn't actually do anything on its own. I brought this up with my sister, who's doing a psych degree, and she just kind of shrugged it off, but it honestly freaked me out a bit, because it seems to basically prove that stuff like willpower, therapy, or deciding to change a habit is an illusion. Like your brain chemistry was always going to do what it was going to do regardless, and the "decision" was just along for the ride with zero actual causal oomph of its own.

So I guess my question is: if the causal exclusion argument is this solid, why isn't it treated as a way bigger deal outside of philosophy departments? Why do psychologists, psychiatrists, self-help books, etc, keep talking like thoughts and decisions cause behavior change, when this argument seems to show thats not really possible? Am I missing something here, or is basically the entire mental health field just quietly ignoring a pretty devestating result?
