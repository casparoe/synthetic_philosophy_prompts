The question everyone answered was: "Explain what Sartre means by 'bad faith,' and why he thinks it raises a philosophical problem that ordinary lying to another person does not. Then, using the café waiter and the woman on her date as your two examples, show how each exploits the relationship between facticity and transcendence in a different way. Finally, explain briefly how Sartre's own account of self-deception is meant to improve on Freud's theory of repression."

Here is the response I've been assigned to grade:

"For Sartre, bad faith (mauvaise foi) is a form of self-deception, and he distinguishes it sharply from ordinary lying. When I lie to you, there are two consciousnesses involved: I know the truth and deliberately conceal it from you, so the deception is something I inflict on another person from a position of secret knowledge. Bad faith is stranger, because the deceiver and the deceived are the same person: I must somehow know the truth in order to hide it from myself, which makes it hard to see how the hiding could ever succeed, since 'I' would have to be aware of exactly what I am not supposed to be aware of.

Sartre thinks this puzzle is possible because human reality has two sides that can never simply coincide: facticity (everything about me that is simply given, such as my body, my past, and my concrete situation) and transcendence (my freedom to go beyond any given fact about myself toward what I am not yet). Bad faith exploits the fact that I am never purely one or the other. The café waiter throws himself into his role with exaggerated, mechanical gestures, trying to actually be a waiter the way a thing simply coincides with what it is, so that he does not have to face the anguishing truth that he is always free to set down his tray and walk out. The woman on her date does exactly the same thing: faced with a choice she does not want to make, she turns herself into a passive object in exactly the same way the waiter turns himself into a thing, so that she is not responsible for leaving her hand in her companion's. In both cases the strategy is to disappear into one's facticity so that one's transcendence, and the responsibility that comes with it, never has to be acknowledged.

Sartre's account is meant to improve on Freud's theory of repression, which explains this kind of self-ignorance by dividing the mind into a conscious ego and an unconscious id policed by a censor. Sartre keeps this basic division but relocates it: instead of splitting the mind into genuinely separate systems, he keeps everything within a single person's awareness, so that it is one consciousness, rather than a hidden homunculus, that both knows and hides the truth from itself."
