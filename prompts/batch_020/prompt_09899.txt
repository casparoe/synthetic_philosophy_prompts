I'm a retired physiologist, and since retiring I somehow ended up as the unaffiliated member on my university's IACUC -- the committee that signs off on animal research protocols. We've got a new proposal from a cephalopod lab that wants to run nociception experiments on octopuses (minor induced injuries, to study how pain sensitization works) with no anesthesia at all. As far as I know there's nothing in the regulations stopping them, since cephalopods aren't covered by the Animal Welfare Act the way vertebrates are, so this is really a judgment call for the committee, not a compliance question. The PI's justification for skipping analgesia rests partly on the claim that octopuses almost certainly lack "genuine" phenomenal consciousness -- just nociceptive reflexes -- so the welfare cost is low. Here's my problem: I've been reading about illusionism (Frankish, Dennett) since I retired, and if illusionism about phenomenal consciousness is correct, even human pain doesn't involve the "raw feel" we introspect -- thats supposedly a trick introspection plays on all of us too. So "lacks genuine phenomenal consciousness" can't be doing the moral work here unless it also shows human pain doesn't matter, which nobody on the committee would accept. I don't want to just wave this arguement around at Thursday's meeting though. Can you draft the short memo (under a page) I'll circulate beforehand -- recommending approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions (and if conditions, what they should actually be) -- that deals with this fairly, including the best reason someone might still give for treating octopus pain and human pain differently even granting illusionism?
