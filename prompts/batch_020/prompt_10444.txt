I teach a seminar that pairs Madhyamaka with a bit of analytic philosophy of logic -- we spend a week on paraconsistency and dialetheism early on as a foil, then spend the back half of the course on Nagarjuna, using Garfield's translation and commentary on the Mulamadhyamakakarika (MMK). Just started working through the take-home midterms, and I like to get an independant read on the free-response questions before I lock in a grade rather than just trusting my own first pass, so figured I'd run one past you.

Here's the question as I posed it: "Chapter 25 of the MMK runs nirvana through the four alternatives -- it is not existent, not nonexistent, not both, and not neither. In 2-3 paragraphs: (a) explain how this fourfold negation is supposed to work as an argument for nirvana's emptiness rather than as the claim that nirvana is a contradictory object; (b) explain what work the two truths distinction does to block the inference from 'x is empty of svabhava' to 'x doesn't exist'; and (c) say something about who Nagarjuna is actually arguing against here."

And here's the students answer, copied exactly:

"Nagarjuna's treatment of nirvana in chapter 25 runs through the catuskoti, or tetralemma: for any predicate there are four possible stances you could take toward a subject -- it applies, it doesn't apply, both, neither -- and Nagarjuna rejects all four in turn for nirvana. This matters because rejecting all four alternatives is not the same as asserting the third one; Nagarjuna isn't claiming nirvana both exists and doesn't exist, which would just be a garden-variety contradiction. The point of the fourfold negation is that none of the four descriptions get a grip on nirvana, because predication as such presupposes svabhava, and nirvana, like everything else examined in the text, lacks it. So the tetralemma shows the limits of a certain kind of discourse, not a paradoxical object.

Given that nirvana fails to be existent, though, the natural reading is that Nagarjuna ends up an eliminativist about nirvana, and about everything else: if lacking svabhava means a thing lacks inherent existence, then strictly speaking it doesn't exist at all, full stop. The two truths distinction is really just a concession to ordinary usage layered on top of that: conventional truth is the name for the useful fictions unenlightened people mistake for reality, while ultimate truth is that none of it is real.

Nagarjuna's target throughout the text is the Vedantic picture, familiar from the Upanishads, of an eternal and unconditioned substance -- atman/Brahman -- underlying the flux of appearances. The tetralemma is his tool for showing that no such self-existent substance survives scrutiny, whether we're talking about the self or about nirvana itself."
