A few months ago I started reading through David Hume's essays on economics on my own — "Of Money," "Of the Balance of Trade," and "Of Public Credit" in particular — after realizing there was a lot more to him than the handful of ideas, like the problem of induction and the is-ought gap, that show up in every general-interest philosophy book. What struck me most is his argument that a nation can't hoard gold and silver indefinitely through a trade surplus, because as specie flows in, domestic prices rise, eroding the very competitiveness that produced the surplus in the first place, so the imbalance is supposed to correct itself automatically without any mercantilist meddling. In a separate essay, he's just as emphatic about the dangers of financing government spending through mounting public debt rather than taxation, essentially predicting that a nation which kept piling up debt without limit was setting itself up for ruin.

That got me wondering what Hume would make of the arrangement the United States is actually in today: decades of large, persistent trade deficits with a country like China, conducted entirely in a currency with no metallic backing whatsoever, where a huge share of the dollars sent abroad simply flow back in through foreign purchases of US government debt, with China's own central bank among the biggest holders. None of the self-correcting price-specie adjustment Hume described seems to be taking place, and yet the arrangement has persisted for decades rather than collapsing the way his reasoning might lead you to expect.

Working strictly from the actual mechanisms laid out in his essays, rather than from his general reputation as a champion of commerce or a critic of concentrated power, how do you think Hume would explain what's going on? Would he argue his self-correcting mechanism is still operating today in some disguised form, would he treat the dollar's special global status as a loophole he simply hadn't anticipated, or would he see the whole arrangement as exactly the kind of debt-fueled illusion of prosperity he warned about, just on a scale far beyond anything Britain faced in the eighteenth century? I'd like an actual reconstruction of his likely reasoning, not just a vague gesture toward "he was skeptical of government power."
