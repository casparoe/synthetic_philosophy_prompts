I have no formal training in philosophy, but as a software engineer I recently came across Nāgārjuna's tetralemma (catuṣkoṭi), the recurring pattern in Madhyamaka Buddhist philosophy whereby a given claim is examined under four possibilities: true, false, both true and false, or neither. This struck me as essentially the same structure as tools I already use professionally — Belnap's four-valued logic for reasoning over inconsistent database records, and the four-state (0, 1, X, Z) signal model that Verilog uses to represent unknown or floating values in a circuit. Since logicians and engineers arrived at an equivalent four-valued scheme many centuries later specifically to formalize contradiction and missing information, would it be fair to say that Nāgārjuna's doctrine of emptiness is essentially an early philosophical anticipation of paraconsistent, many-valued logic — that is, a proof that reality simply does not respect classical bivalent logic? I would like to understand exactly how far this comparison holds up.
