Three months ago I started a new job at a small AI-governance nonprofit whose founders came out of the old LessWrong rationalist scene, and during my first week someone brought up Roko's basilisk at lunch as a kind of infamous community legend — the thing critics cite to make our field look ridiculous. That offhand mention turned into a surprisingly technical and fairly heated argument between two of my colleagues, Tobias and Renata, and since I had previously only ever seen the basilisk treated as an internet joke, I had real trouble telling who was actually getting the better of it.

(1) Tobias opened by arguing that the basilisk isn't just a silly meme: if you accept the reasoning that's supposed to justify one-boxing on Newcomb's Problem — that a rational agent should choose as though it were fixing the output of an algorithm that may be correlated with events it has no causal access to — then a future AI's disposition to punish people who knew about it and didn't help is exactly analogous to Newcomb's predictor, and it's inconsistent to accept that reasoning in one case while dismissing it in the other just because the predictor here is speculative rather than stipulated. (2) Renata countered that Newcomb's Problem only has bite because the predictor is stipulated to be a near-perfect predictor specifically correlated with you; nothing establishes that any real future AI would run a simulation faithful enough to be correlated with you in particular, so the analogy just helps itself to the one premise doing all the work. (3) Tobias replied that even a small probability of that correlation, multiplied against astronomically bad stakes, has exactly the structure of an argument you don't get to wave away on a hunch — that's a Pascal's Mugging, and the accepted response to a mugging is to explain why the probability shrinks faster than the payoff grows, not to simply declare the whole class of arguments silly. (4) Renata's response was that there is a principled reason here, drawn from the very decision theory that gives the basilisk its credibility in the first place: an updateless or functional decision theorist doesn't ask "what should I do given this threat" but "what policy, if I were known in advance to follow it, serves me best" — and a policy of caving to acausal threats makes you exploitable by every possible threat of that form, so the same theoretical apparatus that supposedly generates the basilisk also implies that neither a well-designed AI nor a well-reasoning person should ever let it get off the ground. (5) Tobias's comeback was that this only shows sufficiently sophisticated agents can inoculate themselves by adopting the right policy — an option only available once you already know the argument — so it concedes that the dynamics are real rather than showing the original 2010 reasoning was confused from the start. (6) Renata's last word was that this flips the community's own "too dangerous to even discuss" reaction on its head: if correct reasoning is what protects you, then learning about the argument isn't the hazard, bad decision theory is, which undercuts the very reason the whole affair became notorious.

I'd like a more careful outside verdict than Tobias and Renata managed to reach by the time lunch ended.
