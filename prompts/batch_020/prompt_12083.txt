I'm a junior on my school's math team, and this whole thing started as a joke during our AP Statistics unit on probability. A few of us set up a small play-money prediction market, the kind of thing where people trade fake currency on real-world outcomes, to bet on things like which of our teammates will qualify for state or whether our team's average score this year will beat last year's. It's grown into something people take a little too seriously, and lately the most popular markets are on whether certain famous unsolved problems in number theory are actually true, especially Goldbach's Conjecture (that every even number greater than 2 can be written as the sum of two primes). People trade on it exactly like they trade on competition results: if you think it's 90 percent likely to be true, you buy shares at that price, and the price moves as people trade.

One member of our team, easily our most careful proof-writer, the kind of person who won't accept a step in a proof that isn't fully justified, refuses to trade on the math markets at all, even as a joke, and says the rest of us are confused. His point is that Goldbach's Conjecture isn't uncertain the way a competition result is: it's either a necessary consequence of the axioms of arithmetic or it isn't, and there's no die being rolled anywhere to decide it. So on his view, putting an 85 percent price on it is a category error, not fundamentally different from putting a probability on whether 2 + 2 equals 4; we're mistaking our own ignorance of the proof for the universe itself being undecided. The rest of us aren't convinced, because our confidence doesn't feel made up: the conjecture has been checked by computer for enormous numbers without a single exception, and number theorists have real heuristic arguments for why it should be true, so "how confident are you" seems like a perfectly good question with a real, evidence-sensitive answer here, just like it is for any other market, only with unusually strong evidence behind it.

We're about to raise the stakes from bragging rights to small amounts of real money on our next competition trip, so I'd like to actually work out who's right before we do, rather than just let whoever argues loudest win. Is my teammate correct that there's something confused about assigning a probability to a claim that is already necessarily true or false, even if we personally don't know which? Or is there a real, defensible sense in which our gradual, evidence-responsive confidence about an unproven conjecture is meaningful and rational, in a way that's genuinely different from simply being ignorant? I'd like to understand what's actually going on here, not just who wins the argument.
