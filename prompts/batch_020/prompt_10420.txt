I don't have any formal training in philosophy or physics, but over the past year I've been working through popular accounts of quantum entanglement, and that reading eventually pulled me sideways into Leibniz, of all people, after I came across a passing mention of his monadology. What struck me is that Leibniz denied outright that any created substance ever causally acts on another: monads are, in his famous phrase, "windowless," so that nothing really passes between them, and yet their internal states track each other perfectly across the whole universe because God arranged every monad's unfolding in advance -- what he called pre-established harmony. I haven't been able to stop wondering whether that scheme was, in effect, built to answer exactly the kind of question Bell's theorem raises, even though Leibniz died more than two centuries before Bell ever wrote a line of it.

What I'd like from you is an honest reconstruction of how Leibniz might have responded if the full argument had been put to him: the derivation of the Bell inequality (or its more experimentally practical CHSH version) from the combination of locality, the assumption that outcomes are fixed by some pre-existing hidden state ("realism"), and the independence of the experimenters' measurement choices from that hidden state -- together with the fact that real entangled particles have been shown experimentally to violate it. Would he treat the violation as vindicating his own lifelong rejection of causal influence between finite substances, essentially biting the bullet on the "measurement independence" assumption (what's sometimes called superdeterminism) and accepting that even the experimenters' choices were fixed in harmony from the first instant of creation? Or would he push back on the whole framing on the grounds that "locality," defined the way physicists define it, presupposes that space and light cones are more fundamental than he ever took them to be, given that in his correspondence with Samuel Clarke he treated space as nothing more than a relational ordering of phenomena rather than a real feature of the monads themselves?

Please don't just hand me a verdict -- walk through the reasoning as you go, and flag which parts of this comparison you think Leibniz's actual texts genuinely support and which parts you think are being stretched to make the analogy fit.
