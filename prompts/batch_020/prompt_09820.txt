So this semester I'm in a seminar on the ethics of aggregation, and we spent something like two weeks on Taurek's "Should the Numbers Count?" paper plus Parfit's reply to it, and now it's bleeding into real life in a way thats kind of annoying. I'm on the exec committee for this small giving circle on campus (basically EA-adjacent, though nobody officially calls it that) that pools whatever we fundraise each semester and sends it somewhere. This term we've got about $3000 and two options on the table: fully fund cataract surgery for four specific elderly patients our partner NGO already identified and sent us updates on, or put the same money toward a bednet program where the statistically expected number of lives saved is way higher but there's no faces attached to it. At the last meeting, one member who's also in my seminar brought up Taurek and argued we're not actually obligated to just pick whichever option "saves more," and half-joked that we should flip a coin instead. Now half the club thinks that's a genuinely important point and the other half thinks its just an excuse to be sentimental about the people we happen to have photos of.

Honestly I'm a little lost on how far his argument is even supposed to travel outside its original setup. Taurek's case (and Parfit's reply) is built around a forced either/or rescue where everyone involved is facing roughly the same kind of loss, but our situation is comparing a certain benefit to a few named people against a probabilistic benefit to a much bigger, unnamed group, which feels like a different shape of problem and not just a bigger number stuck on one side of the same problem. I also can't quite tell how much of Taurek's point is actually distinct from stuff we covered earlier in the course, like Rawlsian maximin or plain old skepticism about utilitarian math, versus something that only works because of his specific claim that there's no one who experiences "the loss of five" as such. Could you lay out the most important implications of Taurek's actual argument for a decision like ours -- not the 101 version where utilitarians say numbers count and deontologists say they don't, but what his reasoning would specifically commit us to (or not) if we tried to take it seriously as a club, including whether something like a lottery is even a coherent thing to import into our situation, or whether that move only makes sense inside his original kind of case.
