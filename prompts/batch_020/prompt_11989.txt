I've been reading about Avicenna's "flying man" thought experiment, where a person is created instantaneously, fully formed and rational, but suspended in a void, cut off from sight, sound, and even the feeling of his own limbs touching one another. Avicenna argues that this person would still be certain of his own existence, and uses this to argue that self-awareness is immediate and entirely independent of body-awareness — evidence, for him, that the soul is a substance distinct from the body.

What strikes me is that modern sensory-deprivation ("float") tanks are a rough real-world version of this exact scenario. Yet people who spend extended time in them sometimes report the opposite of what Avicenna predicted: not a sharper sense of "I exist," but a fading or dissolving sense of self that resembles what's now clinically called depersonalization-derealization — where someone knows intellectually that they still exist, but loses the immediate, felt certainty of selfhood that Avicenna's argument seems to assume will always surface once the senses are removed.

Since Avicenna never had to reckon with cases like this, how do you think he would respond? Would they pose a real problem for the internal logic of his argument, or is there a way of reading the flying man argument that survives them? Please base your answer on the actual structure of his reasoning, rather than just his general commitment to dualism.
