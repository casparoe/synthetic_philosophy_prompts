I'm a philosophy PhD student, and for my comprehensive exam I've been assigned to defend Leibniz's Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles against Max Black's two-spheres counterexample -- a debate I'd only ever seen gestured at in a single lecture during a survey course. It stopped feeling academic last week, when a friend's statistical mechanics seminar convinced me that the same argument-structure recurs in current disputes over whether identical quantum particles are genuinely indiscernible or merely appear so. Having now read the primary material properly, I find I can build a serious case for either side, and I no longer know what I actually believe. Write a dialogue between a philosopher defending Leibniz's principle and one defending Black's argument, staged as though both have been dropped into Black's own symmetric universe and are standing between the two spheres, each trying to convince the other whether they are looking at one object or two. They should engage each other's strongest specific moves -- whether spatial relations count as genuine properties, weak versus strong formulations of the principle, and whether "primitive thisness" is a legitimate defense of Leibniz or just a concession to Black -- rather than restating the setup.
