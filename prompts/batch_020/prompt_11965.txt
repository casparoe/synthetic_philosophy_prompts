The question, worth 20 points total, reads as follows.

(a) [8 points] Explain the mechanism behind the no-trade theorem: why is it that two risk-averse, expected-utility-maximizing traders, who share a common prior over the payoff-relevant state of the world and who start from an allocation that is already Pareto-optimal given that prior, can never agree to a trade motivated purely by their subsequent private information, once it becomes common knowledge between them that a trade is on offer?

(b) [6 points] This result sits awkwardly next to the enormous trading volumes observed in real asset markets. Give at least two distinct, standard ways of reconciling the theorem with that observation, and state which premise of the theorem each one denies.

(c) [6 points] The no-trade theorem is sometimes presented, in survey-level treatments, as though it supplies the game-theoretic foundation for the efficient markets hypothesis. Is that characterization accurate, or does it paper over a real tension between the two ideas? Defend your answer.

Here is the student's answer, in full.

(a) The no-trade theorem, proved by Aumann (1976), formalizes an old piece of horse sense: if the only reason your counterparty wants to trade with you is that she has different information, then her willingness to trade is itself evidence you should update on. Start from a Pareto-optimal allocation given the common prior, then let each side receive a private signal. If it becomes common knowledge that trader 1 wants to sell asset X to trader 2, trader 2 should reason: "She would not sell unless her signal made her think X is overpriced relative to what the prior-informed price would be, so I should mark down my own valuation." But trader 1 can anticipate that trader 2 will reason this way, so she updates too, and so on. Because the traders share a prior, and their rationality and the structure of the offer are common knowledge, this regress converges immediately: any trade that looked mutually beneficial before the offer was made no longer looks beneficial once the very fact of the offer is priced in, so no trade motivated purely by private information survives contact with common knowledge.

(b) Real markets obviously look nothing like this. Two standard escapes are worth distinguishing. First, traders might not share a common prior: if trader 2 believes trader 1's model of the world is simply different rather than better informed, the unraveling argument in (a) never gets started, since an offer to trade need not signal an informational edge rather than a divergent, no-more-"correct" prior. Second, much of real volume comes from participants who are not trading on information at all: traders rebalancing a portfolio, meeting a margin call, or hedging a risk unrelated to the asset's fundamentals. Because their trades are not motivated by an informational edge, the regress in (a) never starts for them, and their presence also gives genuinely informed traders enough cover to trade without immediately revealing themselves, which is the resolution pursued in market microstructure models such as Kyle (1985).

(c) I would defend that characterization. The no-trade theorem is what gives the efficient markets hypothesis its game-theoretic teeth. The EMH's weak claim is that one cannot earn above-normal returns trading on information already "in" the price; the no-trade theorem explains why that has to be so: if you tried to profit from private information, a rational counterparty who shares your prior would infer, from the mere fact of your offer, that you must know something unfavorable to her, and would refuse the trade, or reprice it, before you ever got to realize your edge. The two results are not just loosely related; they are the same insight at different levels of description, with the EMH describing the equilibrium outcome (prices reflect available information) and the no-trade theorem describing the mechanism that produces it.
