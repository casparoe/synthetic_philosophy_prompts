So I teach a graduate seminar on epistemic game theory, and a couple of weeks ago we spent a session on Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree result -- the one that says two Bayesians who start out with the same priors can't have common knowledge that their posteriors for some event disagree. That same week I ended up in our usual fight with my husband over whether the city wasted its money on the downtown bike lanes, an argument we've been having on and off for about three years now. At some point, half showing off, I told him that since we're both reasonably rational people who've hashed this out enough times to know exactly where the other one stands, the theorem basically proves one of us has to be less rational than we think, because two rational people can't just stably disagree forever once they know each other's positions. He wasn't having it -- said thats a cute party trick but it can't actually be diagnosing our marriage -- and I didn't have a great comeback beyond "well, that's what the math says."

Thinking about it more since then, I don't think I was being fair to the theorem, or to him. I have this nagging memory that it needs the priors to match up in the first place, and that "common knowledge" is doing a lot more technical work than just "we've argued about this a bunch and know each other's stance," but I can't reconstruct exactly why that matters or whether it actually gets either of us off the hook. Was I misusing the result on him, and if so, where exactly does the misuse creep in?
