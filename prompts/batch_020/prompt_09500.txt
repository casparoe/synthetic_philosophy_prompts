My old editor Frank is retiring after thirty-some years at the paper, and a bunch of us talked me into giving the toast at his going-away dinner next month, which is stressing me out for reasons that have nothing to do with public speaking. Frank built his entire reputation on being the guy who'd fire you for so much as smoothing over a quote or inventing some "she paused, looking out the window" detail that never actually happened -- he called it the cardinal sin and gave a version of the same lecture to every intern for decades, me included. Then last week, while helping a friend fact-check a chapter of the media-history book she's writing, I found out that Franks own breakout profile from 1988, the one that got him hired at the paper in the first place, has a quoted deathbed scene that, according to the family she interviewed, just didn't happen the way he wrote it. Nobody else on staff knows this yet, and I genuinely don't know what to do with that.

I cover science and ideas for a living, so naturally I've gone and read way too much philosophy about this instead of just writing the toast like a normal person. Apparently there's a whole literature on what philosophers call "standing to blame" -- the idea that being a hypocrite can cost you the standing to condemn something even when you're completely right that it's wrong, without that making the thing any less wrong or letting the people he fired off the hook either. A couple of my old newsroom friends think I'm way overanalyzing a five-minute speech, and they're probably right, but I definately can't write anything that isn't either total hagiography or some inside joke that only I, and maybe one old-timer or two, would even notice was loaded. I don't want to out him, and I don't want to be cute about it either. Could you actually write the toast for me -- something like four or five minutes, warm and genuinely funny in the places it should be funny, that works for a room split between newsroom lifers who half-know Frank's legend already and his actual family who just think he's a sweet old man who used to work at a newspaper, but that's still honest in the way I'd want it to be if Frank were somehow the only person in the room who could hear what I really meant by it?
