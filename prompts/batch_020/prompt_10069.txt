Over the last few months my thesis work has pivoted from lattice gauge theory to using random matrix theory (RMT) to model level-spacing statistics in a quantum-chaotic billiard system — the kind of system the Bohigas–Giannoni–Schmit conjecture is about — and while reading up on the history of RMT I kept running into the Montgomery–Odlyzko story: Hugh Montgomery's pair-correlation conjecture for the non-trivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function, which he described to Freeman Dyson over tea at Princeton, turned out to match exactly the pair-correlation formula for eigenvalues of the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble (GUE) — the very ensemble Wigner had introduced in the 1950s to fit nuclear energy-level spacings — and Andrew Odlyzko's later high-precision computations of zeta zeros only tightened the match. Since I now spend my days fitting GUE statistics to a system that has nothing to do with prime numbers, I finally read Wigner's actual 1960 essay instead of citing it secondhand, and I've drafted the notes below toward a short piece arguing that this particular case is a harder problem for the usual deflationary replies to Wigner than the examples he and everyone after him keeps recycling (complex numbers in quantum mechanics, non-Euclidean geometry and general relativity, group representations and the "eightfold way"). I'd like your comments before I flesh it out into something longer.

My notes so far:

1. The standard "selection effect" reply to Wigner — that mathematicians only remember the pieces of pure math that later turn out to be applicable, and quietly forget the graveyard of equally beautiful theorems that never found a use — works reasonably well against the textbook cases, because in each of those an already-existing piece of pure mathematics was later picked up and applied to a new empirical domain by someone actively looking for a formalism. The zeta/GUE case runs backwards: a statistical tool invented for a narrow, phenomenological purpose (fitting nuclear resonance data) turned out to encode facts about an object with no physical content at all, which number theorists had been studying for reasons entirely internal to number theory, and nobody was looking for a connection to physics when it turned up.
2. It's also not merely a qualitative resemblance between two bell-shaped curves; Odlyzko's numerics track the GUE prediction closely enough that coincidence is hard to sustain as an explanation.
3. Against my own case: Dyson and others have pointed to the Hilbert–Pólya idea (never actually published by either man, as far as I can tell, only reported second-hand) that the zeta zeros might literally be eigenvalues of some self-adjoint operator, together with the RMT "universality" results showing that the local eigenvalue statistics of an enormous range of random and deterministic operators converge on the same GUE/GOE limits regardless of the fine details of the underlying system. If that is the right diagnosis, the coincidence dissolves into "eigenvalues are eigenvalues," rather than remaining a case of pre-established harmony between two formalisms invented for unrelated reasons — though I suspect this just relocates the mystery (why is the universe so full of systems whose spectra fall into one of these few universality classes to begin with?) rather than dissolving it.

What I'd actually like from you is pushback rather than encouragement. Is point 1 a fair and accurate rendering of how the selection-effect objection is normally run, or have I straw-manned it to make my case look stronger than it is? Does the universality consideration in point 3 concede more to the deflationary side than I'm letting on, given that I'm the one who raised it and then tried to defuse it in the same breath? And is leaning on the fact that Wigner himself invented the GUE a substantive part of the argument, or just a biographical coincidence I should drop? I'd also welcome a flag on anything in the Montgomery–Dyson–Odlyzko history that I've gotten wrong, since I'm relying on secondhand popular accounts rather than the original papers.
