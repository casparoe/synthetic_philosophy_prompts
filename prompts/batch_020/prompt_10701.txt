I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the Overcoming Bias days, and in three weeks I'm giving the toast at my oldest friend's wedding. Some background that I think matters for the request: Priya, the bride, is marrying Daniel, a management consultant with a decision-theory hobby, who spent a solid month before they got engaged arguing that a wedding was a wasteful, purely symbolic expense layered on top of the marriage license — "legally we'd be exactly as married for a tenth of the cost" — until, by his own sheepish account, Priya's sister sat him down and walked him through the difference between mutual knowledge and common knowledge: that quietly signing paperwork gives the couple, and maybe two witnesses, the fact that they're married, but standing up in front of a hundred and fifty people, all watching the vows and watching one another watch, produces something categorically stronger — not just that everyone present knows, but that everyone knows that everyone knows, and knows that they know that, all the way up. That is exactly the structure epistemic game theorists reach for when they talk about common knowledge of rationality underwriting an equilibrium, and exactly what stays merely mutual, however many levels deep, in things like the coordinated-attack problem or the blue-eyed-islanders puzzle, until some public event forces it into the open. Daniel, apparently, found this persuasive; the wedding is now happening; and I have volunteered to give the toast that has to actually sell it.

What I'd like from you is the toast itself — spoken text, not notes, running about four or five minutes — built honestly around that distinction, for a room that's maybe a fifth people who will recognize precisely what I'm doing and wince if I get it hand-wavy, and four-fifths family and old friends who have never heard "common knowledge" used this way and must not feel lectured at for a second. It should actually explain, correctly, why standing in a room together accomplishes something a courthouse visit does not, rather than just gesturing at "it's more meaningful" — while remaining, first and always, a toast: warm, a little roasting of Daniel for his original spreadsheet-based objections, and ending on a toast line the whole room can raise a glass to, not just the handful who'd clock the phrase "partition structure."
