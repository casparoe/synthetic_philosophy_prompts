I've been reading LessWrong and its offshoots for the better part of a decade now, and there's a move I keep encountering -- in Bostrom's 2012 paper "The Superintelligent Will," in Superintelligence itself, and in countless forum comments since -- where the orthogonality thesis is given a respectable philosophical pedigree by appeal to Hume. The reasoning goes: since reason cannot cross the is-ought gap and is, in Hume's own words, "the slave of the passions," there is no rational constraint that would prevent arbitrarily high intelligence from being combined with more or less any final goal. It's a tidy story, and it circulates often enough in the rationalist blogosphere that I suspect most people who repeat it have never gone back to check how much of Hume's actual moral psychology it leaves on the table.

What gets left out, I think, is everything Hume builds on top of that deflationary claim: his distinction between calm and violent passions (and his warning that calm passions are easily mistaken for the deliverances of reason itself), his account of how custom and general rules discipline particular passions into stable, predictable dispositions, and above all his argument, in Book 3 of the Treatise, that what later commentators call the "circumstances of justice" -- moderate scarcity together with only limited human generosity -- make convergence on stable possession, promise-keeping, and the pursuit of power and resources close to unavoidable among finite, needy, social creatures. None of that reads like "any goal is as psychologically available as any other."

So here is what I'd like you to do: reconstruct, using only the resources of Hume's own system rather than the one-line slogan usually attached to his name, what he would actually say about the orthogonality thesis and about the instrumental convergence thesis (the Omohundro/Bostrom claim that agents with almost any final goal will tend to converge on subgoals like self-preservation, resource acquisition, and cognitive enhancement). Does the fuller Humean apparatus really license the strong reading of orthogonality that Bostrom's argument needs, or does it commit Hume, on his own terms, to something that looks a lot more like instrumental convergence in eighteenth-century dress -- the view that any sufficiently capable, finite, socially embedded mind will end up wanting recognizably similar things, as a matter of the "science of man" rather than logical necessity? I'm less interested in a verdict on who turns out to be right by our lights than in the strongest, most textually grounded reconstruction of Hume's own considered position, tensions and all.
