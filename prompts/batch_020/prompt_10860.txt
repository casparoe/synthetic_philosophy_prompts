Had a long, kind of heated argument with my friend last night about Leibniz's "best of all possible worlds" thing and neither of us backed down. His main move was the obvious one: if God surveys every possible world, why not just staple together the best bits of each — all the good, none of the gratuitous suffering? I gave him the standard compossibility answer (those good bits might not be jointly possible, so you can't mix and match), but when he asked what actually makes two things incompossible besides "well, this world doesn't have both," I basically just repeated the word like it explained itself. Then he went further and said maybe there's no best possible world at all — cant you always imagine a slightly better one, one more virtuous act or one less bit of pointless pain, same way there's no biggest number — which torpedoes the "God had sufficient reason to pick this one" argument before compossibility even comes up.

Write me a dialogue between a genuinely sharp Leibnizian and an equally sharp critic that actually works through both of these: what incompossibility could mean beyond a placeholder, and whether "best possible world" is even a coherent target. I want real back-and-forth where they respond to each other's specific moves, not just prepared speeches talking past each other — and don't let the Leibnizian just lose either.
