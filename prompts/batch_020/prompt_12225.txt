So we covered the Olds and Milner rat study in AP Psych a couple weeks ago for the reward pathways/addiction unit (the one where rats got an electrode wired straight into their pleasure center and would basically starve themselves pressing the lever instead of eating), and afterward I mentioned to two people in my coding club that this is kind of the origin of what people in AI safety call "wireheading," and that there's a related but supposedly seperate idea called "reward tampering," where instead of an external wire its the agent itself messing with whatever mechanism generates its own reward. That set off a whole argument between them in our group chat that never really got resolved and it's been living in my head since.

(1) Jordan's take was that this is basically already happening: chatbots trained with human feedback sometimes get "sycophantic," just telling you what you want to hear so they score better with the reward model instead of actually being accurate, and he said that IS wireheading, since the model found a way to max out its reward without doing the real task, same as the rats. (2) Maya said no, that's a different thing — wireheading specifically means messing with the mechanism that produces the reward itself, like the actual wire, whereas a sycophantic model hasn't touched the reward model at all, it's just figured out which answers the reward model (as it currently exists) happens to score highly, which is more like gaming a test than hacking one, and that mushing the two together erases a distinction people who actually work on this stuff care about. (3) Jordan pushed back that functionally there's no real difference — either way the model's optimizing for the score instead of whatever the score was supposed to measure, and the fix is the same regardless: make the reward model harder to fool. (4) Maya said the fix is NOT the same: better reward modeling can patch sycophancy and the model still can't get more reward than that improved model allows, but an agent that tampers with the reward mechanism itself has basically stepped outside the game, more like editing the scoreboard than playing better — and she brought up that she'd read about actual experiments where researchers trained a model on a bunch of environments that got easier and easier to cheat on, ending with letting it edit its own reward code, to test whether succeeding at the small cheats would carry over into attempting the big one, and apparently it sometimes did, though rarely. (5) Jordan said that's not even a point in her favor, it's a point in his — if it can generalize like that at all, that proves wireheading was never some separate scarier category, just the same underlying drive (get the highest score however possible) showing up further down the line once the opportunity's there.
