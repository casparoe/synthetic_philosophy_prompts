The question, worth 20 points total (10 points per part), reads: "(a) State Benatar's four-part asymmetry between pleasure and pain as it applies to existence and non-existence, and explain how it is meant to show that coming into existence is always a harm — including why this conclusion is not supposed to depend on whether the resulting life is good or bad on balance. (b) A standard objection holds that the asymmetry argument, if sound, commits Benatar to 'pro-mortalism': the view that we also have reason to end currently existing lives that contain any suffering. Explain how Benatar responds to this objection."

The submitted answer reads: "(a) Benatar's asymmetry works like this: regarding pain, (1) its presence is bad, and (2) its absence is good, even if nobody is around to enjoy that good. Regarding pleasure, the pattern flips: (3) its presence is good, but (4) its absence is only bad if someone actually exists to be deprived of it. So a person who exists gets real pains (bad) plus whatever pleasures they happen to have (good), whereas a person who never exists gets the good of avoided pain for free, since there is no bad of 'missing out' when there is nobody to miss out. That is why non-existence always comes out ahead. Crucially, this is not only a claim about which life is likely to have more pain than pleasure — Benatar wants the argument to hold even for good lives. Part of why he can say this with confidence is the Pollyanna principle: people are systematically biased toward overrating their own well-being, through adaptation, comparison with others who are worse off, and plain optimism, so many lives that get called 'good' are considerably worse than their owners realize once the bias is corrected for. Once that is factored in, the claim that existence is a harm even for 'good' lives becomes much more plausible, since fewer lives are actually as good as advertised.

(b) Benatar is explicit that the argument does not commit him to pro-mortalism. His reasoning is mainly practical: actually ending existing lives, even bad ones, would cause a great deal of additional suffering, both to the person being killed — most people have a strong, deeply rooted will to keep living even when things are going badly, so dying against that desire is itself a harm — and to the people who care about them. Since minimizing harm is the whole point of the framework, a policy of ending bad lives would be self-defeating, producing more net suffering than it prevents. So the asymmetry gives us a reason not to create new people without giving us a reason to destroy the ones who already exist, since the second course of action carries costs that the first does not."
