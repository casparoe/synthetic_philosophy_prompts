I'm a retired research chemist, and ever since my daughter (she's doing a philosophy PhD) left her old copy of Parfit's Reasons and Persons on my kitchen table over Christmas, I've been completely absorbed by his Hitchhiker case - the one where you're dying of thirst in the desert and a driver will only take you into town if he's confident you're the sort of person who'll actually hand over $1000 once you get there, even though by that point paying does you no earthly good anymore. As I understand it Parfit uses this to illustrate something people call "rational irrationality" - the idea that it can be rational, in some higher sense, to want to become the kind of person who'll act irrationally later on - though I'm honestly not sure if that's his own phrase or one that got attached to the case afterward. Digging around some more I found Gregory Kavka's toxin puzzle from the early 80s: an eccentric billionaire pays you a million dollars just for genuinely forming, by midnight tonight, the intention to drink a nasty but harmless toxin tomorrow afternoon (the chemist in me appreciated that he's careful to specify it's harmless) - and crucially you never actually have to go through with drinking the stuff for the money to be yours. My first instinct was that this is the same trick as the hitchhiker case wearing different clothes.

The more I sit with the two though, the less sure I am they're testing quite the same thing, and there's nobody round here to hash it out with (my old physics buddies just glaze over when I bring this stuff up). In the toxin case the reward seems to be locked in by the intention alone, so whatever you actually do the next afternoon looks beside the point, whereas the hitchiker case seems to fall apart as a scenario unless you genuinely cough up the cash once you're safe in town. Is that a real, structurally important difference, or am I just not seeing how they're equivalent underneath? I've got a vague memory, maybe from a footnote somewhere, of a distinction between a puzzle about "forming" an intention and one about "sticking to" it, which might map onto this, but I couldn't tell you anymore whose distinction that was or whether I've mangled it. Could you lay out precisely where these two cases actually agree and where they genuinely diverge, and then tell me which one you think is the harder nut for a straightforward causal decision theorist to crack, and why?
