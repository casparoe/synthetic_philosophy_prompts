I'm a software engineer, and about a year ago I left my job to co-found a startup with a friend. We're now finishing our investor pitch deck, and my co-founder insists that one slide has to state a specific figure for our odds of succeeding — something like "we estimate a 35% probability of reaching profitability within three years." I keep resisting giving a single number. It isn't false modesty: given how little solid information we actually have, any figure from roughly 15% up to 45% strikes me as about equally defensible, and picking one point in that range feels made up.

My co-founder argues this doesn't let me off the hook. He points out that whenever I make a call under uncertainty — how much runway to keep in reserve, whether to accept a modest early acquisition offer instead of holding out — I behave exactly as someone with one exact number in their head would behave, so I might as well commit to a figure honestly instead of pretending I haven't already picked one implicitly. Is he right that consistent decision-making under uncertainty secretly commits me to a single number whether I admit it or not, or can it actually be rational to act on a genuine range of odds without ever collapsing it down to one?
