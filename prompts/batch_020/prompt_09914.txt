I don't have any formal training in philosophy — I'm a self-taught reader who got into this after a book on effective altruism mentioned Henry Sidgwick as one of the intellectual ancestors of utilitarianism. Digging around on my own since then, I came across something that genuinely startled me: this famously careful, skeptical philosopher spent something like the last twenty years of his life co-founding, and twice presiding over, the Society for Psychical Research, seriously investigating mediums and other purported evidence of the paranormal. I've written up a short theory of my own about why a mind like his would do this, and I'd like your honest feedback before I take it any further.

Here's what I've got:

Sidgwick ends the Methods of Ethics on a genuinely unhappy note. He cannot find a way to reconcile rational egoism with the utilitarian demand that we weigh everyone's happiness equally — what he calls the dualism of practical reason — and he essentially admits that without some further guarantee that duty and self-interest ultimately coincide, the whole tidy structure of morality he has built threatens to collapse into something closer to chaos. The obvious guarantee would be the traditional religious one: a just God sorting everything out in a future life. But Sidgwick had already resigned his Trinity fellowship because he could no longer in conscience declare himself a member of the Church of England, so that route was closed to him on his own intellectual terms.

My theory is that psychical research was Sidgwick's attempt to get the same guarantee back, but through the front door of science instead of the back door of faith. If you could establish, using the same careful empirical methods you'd use in any other science, that human personality survives bodily death, you would have secular, rigorously evidenced grounds for believing in something like a moral order to the universe — enough, maybe, to patch the hole he'd found in his own ethics. On this reading, the ghost-hunting isn't some odd hobby sitting alongside his philosophy; it's the very same problem, pursued by other means, once the religious solution was no longer available to him.

What I can't tell, without access to the scholarly literature, is whether this is basically the accepted explanation among people who actually study Sidgwick, whether I'm flattening a story that's really much messier, or whether I've gotten some of the history wrong outright. I'd appreciate your comments on the accuracy of what I've laid out, anything important you think I'm missing, and whether the core idea strikes you as a serious interpretation or just a neat-sounding coincidence.
