Descartes' claim that animals are just elaborate machines with no inner life at all keeps coming up in things I read about animal cognition, usually as the go-to example of a great philosopher getting something badly wrong. Digging into it on my own, though, I found that in his correspondence—with Henry More, in particular—when pushed on how this licenses cruelty toward animals, he doesn't simply say they feel nothing; he is far more consistent about denying that animals think or reason than about denying that they have any sensation at all. That leaves real room between the flat "animals are unfeeling robots" summary and what Descartes actually committed to in writing. But I don't see how a softened reading, on which animals have some sort of unconscious sensation, fits with the rest of his system, since elsewhere he treats sensation itself as a mode of thought, something only a mind can have. So which is it: did Descartes really deny animals any feeling whatsoever, or only that they reason and use language, leaving the question of sensation genuinely open—and if it's the latter, is that a coherent position by his own lights, or just an evasion? Write an essay that works through the textual evidence for each reading and commits to one, rather than settling for "it's complicated," and then briefly say what your conclusion implies about how much Descartes himself is to blame for the cruelty his view is often said to have encouraged.
