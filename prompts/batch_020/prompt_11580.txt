I spent almost forty years running low-temperature experiments on glasses and spin systems before I retired, so Boltzmann's name has been stiched through my whole career — and now that Ive got time on my hands I've fallen into the philosophy-of-cosmology literature, which my son-in-law (a postdoc in gravitational physics who finds this whole midlife crisis of mine hilarious) keeps feeding with new papers. The thing currently keeping me up at night is the Boltzmann brain problem: if our universe really is headed toward an eternal, near-empty de Sitter phase, then given enough time, random quantum fluctuations in the vacuum will keep producing isolated, momentarily-existing "brains" with false memories of an orderly past, and there's no time limit on this — so on most of the standard ways cosmologists have tried to count observers, a "typical" observer in a universe like ours turns out to be one of these freak brains rather than someone who actually evolved. Having spent a career worrying about entropy and metastability for a living, this bothers me more than I expected, so I went and read up on the various proposed ways out. I've distilled what I found down to six moves, and since my philosophical judgment on this stuff is still pretty green, I'd like your help sorting them by how well they actually work rather than trusting my own gut:

1. Reductio move — if a given vacuum/measure combination predicts overwhelming Boltzmann-brain domination, just treat that as evidence against the model or measure itself; you never have to say anything about whether you personally are a brain.
2. Causal-patch fix — only compute probabilities within the region actually reachable from one observer's own worldline, rather than comparing volumes across the whole eternally-inflating spacetime; this suppresses (but doesn't always eliminate) brain domination, depending on how fast the local vacuum decays.
3. Ditch proper-time cutoffs — the plain proper-time measure has a "youngness" defect where it wildly overweights freshly-nucleated regions, which is really the same disease as the brain problem wearing a different coat, so just discard that whole family of measures instead of patching it.
4. Cognitive-instability veto — a purely epistemic move rather than a physics one: if you really were a Boltzmann brain, none of the reasoning that got you worrying about Boltzmann brains would be reliably hooked up to anything real, so the hypothesis undercuts its own evidence base and can be assigned ~zero credence no matter what any measure says.
5. Past Hypothesis — just stipulate a low-entropy boundary condition at the start of the universe rather than explaining our low entropy as a fluctuation in the first place; it doesn't block late-time brains once dark energy takes over, but it does block the temptation to explain our existence via a fluctuation at all.
6. Measure nihilism — accept that in a genuinely infinite multiverse there is no principled, unique fact about what fraction of observers are Boltzmann brains, so the "problem" is confused rather than solved.
