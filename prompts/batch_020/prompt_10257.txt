Three months ago I joined the grants committee of a small family foundation that distributes roughly three million dollars a year to global health and development charities, and I've found myself out of my depth in a way I didn't anticipate when I agreed to serve. At our last meeting we were deciding whether to renew our grant to a direct-delivery charity — it funds bed-net distribution in a district where the malaria burden and the cost per life saved are unusually well documented — or redirect part of that money to a newer applicant that runs policy advocacy aimed at reforming land-titling and small-business regulation in a handful of low-income countries, on the theory that the resulting economic growth would eventually dwarf the health charity's impact. One committee member, who had recently circulated an essay on "cluelessness" in consequentialist ethics, argued that we have no principled way to weigh the advocacy charity's effects at all: not merely that they are uncertain, but that our uncertainty about them doesn't wash out the way the ordinary unforeseen side-effects of the bed-net program arguably do, so the two grants aren't even comparable on consequentialist grounds. A second member called this special pleading for the status quo, pointing out that the bed-net program has its own vast, untraceable downstream effects — on population growth, local labor markets, decades-out political stability — that we simply aren't in the habit of looking for, so that "measurability" is doing all the work, and none of that work is principled. A third wants to split the money down the middle and move on.

I've been asked to draft the memo the committee will discuss before we vote next month, and I would rather not paper over the disagreement with a compromise figure. Could you write that memo for me? I'd like it to stake out an actual position on how much weight the cluelessness worry should carry in this particular choice — whether the bed-net program's downstream effects really do put it in the same boat as the advocacy charity, as the second member claims, or whether there's a principled way to treat the two cases differently after all — and then close with a concrete recommendation for the vote, including whether there is some option, short of an even split, that takes the worry seriously without simply paralyzing the committee.
