I'm a retired machinist with no formal background in philosophy, and a couple of years ago I picked up an old paperback of Leviathan mainly to find out where the "nasty, brutish, and short" line actually comes from. I ended up more interested in the man than in the political theory, and one thing that has stuck with me since is Hobbes's peculiar relationship with geometry. From what I've read, he came to it relatively late in life and fell for Euclid almost instantly, treating geometry ever after as the one clear model of what genuinely certain knowledge looks like. Yet this is the same man who spent a good part of his later years publicly insisting he had squared the circle and trisected the angle, was taken apart on the actual mathematics by John Wallis again and again over something like two decades, and, as far as I can tell, never once conceded he was simply wrong, even as his own proposed solutions kept quietly shifting. For someone who supposedly prized geometrical rigor above almost everything else, that looks like an odd thing to be so intransigent about, and plain stubbornness doesn't feel like a sufficient explanation to me.

What I would like help understanding is what geometry was really doing for Hobbes underneath all this -- not just as a science he happened to admire, but as the template he thought any genuinely certain science, including his account of civil society, had to follow. I've come across two ideas that seem related but that I can't quite connect on my own: first, that Hobbes wanted definitions to state how a thing is generated or constructed rather than merely what it is, so that a line, say, is whatever a point traces out in motion rather than Euclid's "length without breadth"; and second, that he thought we can have perfect certainty only about things we ourselves make or construct, an idea he then seems to lean on when arguing that civil philosophy can be just as certain as geometry, since the commonwealth is something we construct by covenant. Could you explain how these two ideas fit together within his broader theory of scientific knowledge, and then use that picture to help me judge whether his refusal to yield to Wallis makes more sense as a specific philosophical commitment being at stake, rather than as simple wounded pride? I would also value your honest sense of whether that is a fair reading of him, or whether it credits him with more coherence than his actual conduct in the dispute really earned.
