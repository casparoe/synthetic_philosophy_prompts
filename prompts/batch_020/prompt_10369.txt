My co-facilitator and I disagreed about how to grade one of the writeups over Slack, so I figured I'd get a third opinion before sending comments back, since grading short philosophical answers is always harder to do fairly than it looks.

Here's the question I gave them: "Goodhart's Law is usually stated as 'when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.' Explain why this folk formulation actually collapses together at least two mechanistically different phenomena, referencing the fourfold taxonomy. Then classify the following case, with justification: a hospital starts publicly ranking surgeons by 30-day patient mortality in order to reward its best surgeons and identify its worst. A year later, hospital-wide mortality has ticked down slightly, but several of the most skilled surgeons have quietly stopped accepting the riskiest, most complex cases, referring them elsewhere instead."

And here's the answer, reproduced exactly, typos included: "Goodhart's Law in its folk form conflates at least two things: (1) the purely statistical fact that any proxy which is only imperfectly correlated with a goal will, when selected on hard enough, pick up variance that has nothing to do with the goal -- this is regressional goodharting, basically regression to the mean under selection pressure -- and (2) the fact that once people know a number is being used to judge them, they start optimizing the number instead of whatever it was supposed to track. Goodhart himself later refined this in his own follow-up work distinguishing regressional, extremal, causal and adversarial variants, of wich adversarial is closest to his original 1975 point about monetary policy targets. For the hospital case: this is a clean instance of regressional goodharting. Mortality rate is a noisy proxy for surgical skill, since outcomes depend heavily on how sick the patient already was and not just on the surgeon, so ranking on raw mortality doesn't only select for skill, it also selects for who happened to get easier patients that year, and skilled surgeons who take on hard cases end up looking artificially worse by comparison. That statistical distortion is exactly why the ranking gives a misleading picture, even though nobody involved is doing anything strategic."
