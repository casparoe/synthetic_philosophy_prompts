Since retiring after forty years directing an experimental physics laboratory, I have devoted myself to reading political philosophy in earnest, and Hannah Arendt's essay "Truth and Politics" has become something of a fixation. She distinguishes "rational truths" — the truths of mathematics, logic, and philosophy — from "factual truths," the contingent record of what actually happened, a distinction that maps neatly onto the line I drew throughout my career between necessary and merely contingent knowledge. Since factual truths are merely contingent, resting on nothing sturdier than the testimony of witnesses, it would seem to follow that Arendt regards a politician's lie about facts as the comparatively minor offense, reserving her real alarm for the corruption of properly rational, necessary truths such as scientific reasoning itself. Have I understood her correctly, or am I missing something in her argument?
