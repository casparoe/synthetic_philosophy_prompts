The question set to the class was: "State the core argument for why a superrational agent is supposed to cooperate against another superrational agent in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, with no communication and no repeated play. Explain in what way, if any, this conclusion departs from the verdict of standard game-theoretic analysis of the same game." Here is the response I have been asked to grade:

"Hofstadter's superrationality was his proposal, first worked out in Gödel, Escher, Bach, for how two purely self-interested but perfectly rational agents could arrive at mutual cooperation in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma without communication or the chance to build a reputation. The key move is to notice that if both players are rational, know the other is rational, and know that the situation is entirely symmetric between them, then whatever line of reasoning leads one player to a given choice is exactly the line of reasoning available to the other; since the two cannot rationally arrive at different answers to what is, from each side, the very same problem, the only self-consistent outcomes are (Cooperate, Cooperate) and (Defect, Defect). An ordinary rational agent reasoning causally would defect regardless of what the other does, since defection strictly dominates cooperation payoff by payoff, but a superrational agent instead compares the two symmetric outcomes to one another and picks whichever of the two is jointly preferable, namely cooperation. Because both players run the same argument and reach the same conclusion, mutual cooperation actually turns out to be the Nash equilibrium of the game once superrationality is factored in: neither player could gain by unilaterally switching to defection, since a genuinely superrational opponent's choice is not free to vary independently of one's own, so there is no real 'unilateral' deviation available in the way there would be for two ordinary causal-decision-theory players. This is what makes superrationality a genuine rival to orthodox game theory rather than merely an application of it."
