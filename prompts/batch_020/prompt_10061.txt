Okay so this happened in the discussion section for my decision theory class (it's cross-listed with the AI safety seminar in the CS department, so we split time between classic axiomatic stuff and why any of it matters for future AI systems). We'd just gone through the money-pump argument: if you prefer A to B, B to C, but also C to A, someone can start you off holding C, sell you B for C plus a penny, sell you A for B plus a penny, then sell you C back for A plus a penny, and you're right back where you started but three pennies poorer -- forever, if they feel like looping you. The professor's big point was that this, plus the parallel Dutch book argument for why your credences have to obey the probability axioms, is supposed to show that any agent that can't be exploited like that has to be describable as maximizing expected utility for some utility function. That's the "coherence argument" our syllabus keeps hyping up.

Anyway two people in my section, Theo and Naomi, got into it after class and then kept going over group chat, and it's kinda been bugging me since. Theo's opening move: if your preferences can be turned into a money pump, holding them is irrational, full stop, that's basically what "irrational" means here. Naomi pushed back that the pump only works on a "naive" chooser who evaluates every offer on it's own; a "sophisticated" agent can see the whole loop coming from the start and just declines the first trade, so it never pays a cent while still technically ranking A over B over C over A. Theo's comeback was that if the agent's choice about that first trade depends on what happens several steps later, it isn't really choosing between C and B on the merits anymore -- it's following a policy over the whole sequence, which might be a perfectly coherent preference, just one level up (over plans, not outcomes). So sophistication doesn't rescue intransitive preferences over outcomes, it just relocates the coherence requirement, which he said basically concedes the professor's point rather than refuting it. Naomi's reply: fine, but if you're always allowed to climb one level up to find coherence, the money-pump argument stops being a general proof that intransitivity is irrational and becomes almost empty -- it only bites agents that can't or won't commit to a plan and stick to it, which is a fact about the agent's psychology, not pure logic. So the honest conclusion is "exploitable if you can't self-bind," not "intransitive preferences are irrational as such." Theo's last word was that maybe that's technically true, but basically no real agent -- and probably not most AI systems anyone builds anytime soon either -- can reliably self-bind like that, so for every agent anyone actually cares about, the original argument goes through pretty much untouched. Neither of them was gonna budge, they were both defintely dead set by the time the thread died at 1am.
