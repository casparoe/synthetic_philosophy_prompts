Since retiring from a career in physics I have spent much of my time working through the history of analytic philosophy, and Frege's invention of quantificational logic in the Begriffsschrift, together with his work on the foundations of arithmetic, strikes me as one of the great achievements in that history. So I was unsettled to learn that the diary he kept in 1924, discovered among his papers long after his death, shows him espousing a virulent antisemitism and a contempt for parliamentary democracy, and looking forward to an authoritarian strongman who would strip Jews of political rights. I gather that even Michael Dummett, who did more than anyone to secure Frege's twentieth-century reputation, admitted to being shaken when this material came to light.

What I would like from you is an essay on whether, and how, this diary bears on the assessment of Frege's philosophy. Please do not dwell on the platitude that a thinker's bad character cannot invalidate a valid argument; I take that for granted. The harder question, which I want properly weighed, is whether there is some deeper kinship between the political temperament on display in the diary and Frege's central philosophical commitments — his militant hostility to psychologism in logic, and his doctrine, defended in the 1918 essay "Der Gedanke" ("The Thought"), that thoughts inhabit an objective "third realm" answerable to nothing merely human or consensual. Is that an elective affinity with the anti-democratic, anti-egalitarian cast of mind on view in the diary, or is the coexistence of the two in one man philosophically idle — a mere accident of biography with no real bearing on how we should read his work? Weigh the strongest considerations on both sides, but end with a genuine verdict rather than a shrug.
