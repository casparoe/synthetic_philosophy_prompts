The question we set was: "Explain what it means to say that the continuum hypothesis is independent of the axioms of ZFC. Your answer should describe the respective contributions of Gödel and Cohen, explain why both of their results, rather than just one, were needed to establish independence, and close with a brief remark on what, if anything, this result shows about whether the continuum hypothesis has a definite truth value."

Here is the answer that was submitted:

"The continuum hypothesis (CH) states that there is no set whose cardinality lies strictly between that of the natural numbers and that of the real numbers — equivalently, that 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₁. Cantor conjectured this in the 1870s and spent years trying unsuccessfully to prove it; it later became the first of the twenty-three problems Hilbert posed in his famous 1900 address.

To say that CH is independent of ZFC is to say that the ZFC axioms are simply too weak to settle it either way: neither CH nor its negation can be derived from them, assuming ZFC itself is consistent. Establishing this took two separate results more than twenty years apart. In 1940, Gödel showed that CH cannot be disproved from ZFC. He did this by constructing an inner model called the constructible universe, L, built up in stages by taking only the definable subsets of each previous stage, and proving that L satisfies both ZFC and CH. Because L is a model of ZFC in which CH holds, this shows that ZFC + CH is consistent as long as ZFC is.

In 1963, Paul Cohen completed the picture by inventing the method of forcing, which let him extend a given model of ZFC by adjoining carefully chosen new sets to it. Using this technique, Cohen proved that CH is false, which is the other half of the independence result: Gödel had ruled out disproving CH, and Cohen showed that it fails as well, so neither it nor its negation is a theorem of ZFC.

Both halves were needed because a consistency proof in only one direction leaves the other direction open. Gödel's result alone would have left it possible that ZFC secretly implies CH after all; Cohen's method alone, without Gödel's L, would have left it possible that ZFC implies its negation instead. Only the combination pins down full independence in both directions.

What this shows, I think, is that the continuum hypothesis simply doesn't have an answer: it isn't that mathematicians haven't yet found the proof, but that there is nothing to find, since its truth value is left open by the only axioms we're entitled to assume. In that sense the situation resembles Euclid's parallel postulate: once you see that both it and its denial are consistent with the rest of Euclidean geometry, asking which one is 'really' true stops being a meaningful mathematical question."
