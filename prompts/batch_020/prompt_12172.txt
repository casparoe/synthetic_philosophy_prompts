In his early papers on radical interpretation, Davidson argues that to understand a speaker just is to be in a position to construct, and confirm on the basis of behavioral evidence, a Tarski-style recursive theory of truth for that speaker's language, with the principle of charity doing the work of fixing an otherwise underdetermined trade-off between attributed beliefs and attributed meanings. I've been working through this material for a graduate seminar, and there's a tension I can't quite resolve between that picture and the one Davidson offers in his later essay "A Nice Derangement of Epitaphs." There, discussing how hearers manage to cope with malapropisms and other improvised departures from standard usage, he distinguishes a speaker's or hearer's "prior theory" (roughly, what each brings to the exchange in advance) from their "passing theory" (what's actually needed to correctly interpret this utterance, here and now), and argues that only convergence of passing theories, not prior theories, is required for communication to succeed. He goes on to conclude from this that there is no such thing as a shared language in anything like the sense assumed by conventionalist accounts of linguistic competence.

Could you explain, in some detail, exactly what work the prior theory/passing theory distinction is doing in that argument, and why Davidson thinks it shows that mastery of a fixed, shared set of conventions is not necessary for successful interpretation? I would also like to understand whether this later view is genuinely in tension with the earlier picture of understanding as knowledge of a compositional truth theory, or whether there is a way of holding onto something like that picture -- perhaps by relativizing truth theories to particular occasions of utterance -- once one has granted that prior theories can diverge indefinitely between speaker and hearer.
