I've been chewing on a puzzle that came out of an ACX open-thread tangent a few months back, and I still haven't managed to resolve it to my own satisfaction. The conversation nominally started from Yudkowsky's old "A Human's Guide to Words" sequence, though it wandered pretty quickly into actual academic semantics/pragmatics territory that sequence never really touches. Someone raised the standard Gricean test for telling literal, truth-conditional meaning apart from mere conversational implicature: cancelability. If you can add an explicit disclaimer ("some of the boys came to the party — in fact, all of them did") without contradiction or weirdness, then the implied content wasn't part of what was literally said, just something pragmatically suggested by context. They then pointed out, correctly as far as I can tell, that the derogatory force of a slur fails this test completely: "she's a [slur], but I don't mean anything derogatory by that" doesn't read as a normal, successful cancellation the way the "some/all" case does. It reads as either a lie or a flat contradiction.

That seemed to leave exactly one place for the derogatory content to live: baked directly into the word's literal semantic meaning, the same way "bachelor" just semantically means "unmarried adult male." But if that diagnosis is right, I can't see how to avoid a strange conclusion. A sentence like "X is a [slur]," uttered truthfully about someone who really is a member of the relevant group, would simply be asserting something true, just in a low-register or contemptuous way — more like calling an informant a "snitch" than like making a false or unfair claim about someone. On that view, the entire moral case against slurs would have to reduce to a complaint about tone or social register, rather than to the speaker asserting anything false. I mentioned this to a friend who's read more philosophy of language than I have, and she brought up a "prohibitionist" view of slurs (Anderson and Lepore, I think, though I haven't read the paper directly) that treats them as simply taboo words rather than as having any special content at all — which only left me more confused about where exactly my reasoning goes wrong. So: does the cancelability data really push us toward treating slur-avoidance as basically an etiquette norm dressed up in moral language, or am I collapsing a distinction that a working philosopher of language wouldn't collapse?
