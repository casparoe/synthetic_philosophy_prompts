I spend a fair amount of time reading rationalist blogs and forums, where arguments about utilitarian aggregation keep running into the same wall: can welfare even be compared, let alone summed, across different people? That interest is part of why I agreed to TA a graduate seminar on social choice and welfare economics this term, and before I return one of the take-home answers, I'd like a careful, independent second opinion on how to grade it.

The question, as it appeared on the problem set, was:

"Using the debate over interpersonal utility comparisons as your throughline, explain (a) why Robbins's ordinalist critique made such comparisons look illegitimate to mid-twentieth-century economists; (b) how Arrow's impossibility theorem is standardly read as bearing on the prospects for a science of social welfare that dispenses with interpersonal comparisons; and (c) how Harsanyi's and Rawls's respective uses of a 'veil of ignorance' represent two different responses to the problem, and why they end up endorsing average utilitarianism and the difference principle, respectively."

Here is the student's answer in full:

"Before Robbins, welfare economists like Pigou were happy to say that a pound transferred from a rich man to a poor man raised total utility, on the assumption that the marginal utility of income diminishes and that a given amount of money buys roughly the same 'amount' of satisfaction for anyone. Robbins argued that this assumption is untestable: there is no instrument that measures utility across skulls, and any comparison of my satisfaction to yours smuggles in a value judgment rather than reporting an observation. Because economics was supposed to be a positive science modeled on physics, Robbins concluded that interpersonal comparisons should be excised, and economists retreated to purely ordinal rankings and the Pareto criterion, which never requires saying that A's gain outweighs B's loss.

Arrow's theorem shows why this retreat is costly. If all we are allowed to use is each individual's ordinal ranking of social states, with no way of comparing the intensity of one person's preference to another's, Arrow proved that no procedure for aggregating those rankings into a social ranking can simultaneously satisfy unrestricted domain, the Pareto principle, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship. Arrow's own theorem is usually taken to show that interpersonal comparisons of utility are impossible, which is why later work by Sen classified the different 'levels' of comparability (ordinal, cardinal unit, cardinal full) needed to escape the impossibility result and recover reasonable social welfare functions, including utilitarian and Rawlsian ones.

Harsanyi and Rawls both try to license a particular kind of interpersonal comparison by imagining a chooser behind a veil of ignorance who doesn't know which person in society she will turn out to be. Harsanyi's aggregation theorem asks such a chooser to maximize her expected utility over equally likely identities, and shows that the resulting social ranking must be a weighted sum of individual utilities, i.e., utilitarianism. Rawls uses the same device but reasons that, because the stakes behind the veil are so severe and irreversible, a rational chooser will run the same expected-utility calculation while giving overwhelming weight to the worst possible outcome, which yields the difference principle rather than average utilitarianism. So the disagreement between them is ultimately about how risk-averse it is rational to be when the gamble is over one's entire life prospects."

Please grade this answer on a 20-point scale, with the corresponding letter grade, and justify the grade point by point rather than with a general impression. I'd find it useful if you cast the point-by-point part as a short JSON rubric, one entry per sub-question (a), (b), and (c), each with points awarded out of points possible and a one- or two-sentence comment, followed by the overall score, the letter grade, and a brief closing note on whatever you take to be the most significant issue in the answer, if any.
