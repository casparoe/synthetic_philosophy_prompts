My grandfather died ten days ago at seventy-six. He was drafted in 1968, served two tours in Vietnam, and for the rest of his life gave the same answer whenever the war's justice came up at family dinners: that a soldier who fights within the rules of war is the moral equal of the man on the other end of his rifle, whatever the politicians back home got wrong, and that this was exactly why he could live with himself. He meant it sincerely enough that he told my mother, more than once, that he hoped whoever gave his eulogy would say as much, and his old Army buddies will be at the funeral expecting nothing less. I'm giving that eulogy. I'm also halfway through a seminar on just war theory this semester, and I've become genuinely persuaded by the revisionist case against the moral equality of combatants: that whether you're permitted to kill in a war depends on the justice of your side, and that the case for Vietnam having been unjust is fairly strong. I can't get up in that room and affirm his view as though I still held it, but I don't think he was a murderer either, and several cousins who grew up hating that war will be listening closely for exactly that implication. Could you write the eulogy, about five minutes long? It needs to actually comfort people who've never heard of Walzer or McMahan, without asserting anything about the ethics of killing in war that I don't believe.
