Aumann's agreement theorem shows that two Bayesian agents who share a common prior cannot "agree to disagree": if their posterior probabilities for some proposition are common knowledge (each knows it, knows that the other knows it, and so on), then those posteriors must coincide. Yet well-informed, thoughtful people routinely sustain sincere, persistent disagreements even after extensively exchanging evidence and arguments. Write an essay assessing what, if anything, this tension reveals about the rationality of real-world disagreement. Weigh the theorem's key assumptions — a shared prior, common knowledge of posteriors, and idealized Bayesian updating — against the standard defenses of disagreement, which typically deny that one or more of these assumptions hold outside idealized settings. Reach and defend a definite verdict on whether persistent disagreement between epistemic peers can ever be rational, rather than leaving the question unresolved.
