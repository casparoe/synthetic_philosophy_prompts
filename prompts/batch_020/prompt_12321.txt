I'm about a third of the way into a novel where one of my two POV characters is an aging philosophy professor -- a Davidson scholar, radical interpretation was literally her whole career -- who gets hired as an "ethics consultant" by a startup building a companion chatbot, and the book is basically about her slowly souring on the gig. I did a philosophy minor a million years ago so I've got the basic Davidson stuff down (principle of charity, solving for belief and meaning off the same evidence at once, all that), but the piece I actually want to build her big confrontation scene around is the later triangulation material -- the argument that you don't get genuine, determinate, "about the world" content unless you've got two creatures simultaneously responding to a shared stimulus AND to each other's responses to it. I think the essay where he really lays this out is called "Rational Animals" but don't quote me on that, its been a while.

Does it just flatly fail his condition (so: whatever the outputs look like, theres no genuine thought or meaning happening), or is there a plausible Davidsonian story where soaking up that much already-triangulated human output secondhand counts as some kind of deferred, distributed version of the real thing?
