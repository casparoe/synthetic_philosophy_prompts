Two friends of mine stayed behind after a philosophy-of-mind reading group last week and got into a long argument about illusionism about phenomenal consciousness that I haven't been able to stop turning over since.

(1) Mira opened by pressing what she took to be a fatal problem: if it merely seems to us that experience has a rich, intrinsic, ineffable quality, then there is something it is like to have that seeming - and that is just what phenomenal consciousness is. So illusionism, in the very vocabulary of "seeming" and "appearance" it relies on, presupposes exactly what it sets out to deny.

(2) Dan replied that this only works if "seeming" is already assumed to mean "phenomenally seeming." The introspective mechanisms illusionists posit, he said, generate representations with a certain content - roughly, that this state has an intrinsic, ineffable quality - without those representations themselves needing to be phenomenally felt, just as a visual system can represent motion in a static image without anything in it actually moving.

(3) Mira answered that the analogy fails: a motion illusion admits of a complete third-person, functional explanation with nothing left unexplained, whereas the first-person, felt-from-the-inside character of experience is exactly what needs explaining. Positing a further representational state, she said, just relocates the same question one level up: what makes that further state one that is felt from the inside, rather than just more unconscious information-processing?

(4) Dan responded that this simply reasserts, as though it were an argument, that any introspectively accessed state must be genuinely phenomenal rather than merely representing itself as having that character - which is exactly what's in dispute. Insisting on it isn't a refutation of illusionism, he said; it just restates the intuition illusionism was devised to explain away.
