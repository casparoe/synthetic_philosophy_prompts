For most of my career as a physicist working in statistical mechanics, I made free use of the standard apparatus of functional analysis -- Hahn-Banach extensions, compactness arguments for taking infinite-volume limits, the blithe assertion that every vector space has a basis -- without ever pausing to ask what, precisely, licensed the underlying existence claims. Since retiring I've joined a philosophy of mathematics reading group, and I was rather taken aback to discover how much of that apparatus quietly rests on Zorn's Lemma, and what a contentious history the Lemma (via its equivalents, the Axiom of Choice and Zermelo's Well-Ordering Theorem) actually has. I mean things like: the outcry from Borel, Baire, and Lebesgue when Zermelo first published his non-constructive well-ordering proof in 1904; the fact that Gödel and Cohen between them showed the matter can never be settled by proof within the usual axioms of set theory, only stipulated one way or the other; the old joke, sometimes attributed to the mathematician Jerry Bona, that the Axiom of Choice is obviously true, the Well-Ordering Theorem obviously false, and Zorn's Lemma anyone's guess, despite all three being logically equivalent; and, from the constructive side, results like Diaconescu's theorem, which shows that admitting choice even into a constructive framework drags the law of excluded middle back in with it.

I'd like your help thinking this through in essay form. Take up the question of whether Zorn's Lemma (equivalently, full Choice) should be accepted as a legitimate principle of mathematical reasoning, or whether the constructivist unease with it is ultimately well-founded, and weigh the strongest considerations on both sides. I'd rather you not lean mainly on the Banach-Tarski paradox, since that's the one example every popular treatment of this reaches for; I'm more interested in how you weigh things like the sheer indispensability of choice, or choice-equivalent results, across analysis and algebra against the fact that, over a century on, nobody has ever exhibited anything resembling an explicit well-ordering of the real numbers. Please don't leave the question hanging -- commit to and defend an actual verdict, even a conditional one (something of the form "accept it for pure mathematics, but not for mathematics meant to describe physical reality" would count, if that is genuinely where your reasoning leads).
