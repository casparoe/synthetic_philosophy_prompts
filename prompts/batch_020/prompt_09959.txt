Here's the question, copied straight off his worksheet:

"Two people are bargaining over a payoff. If they fail to reach agreement, both get 0, so the disagreement point is d = (0,0). The set of feasible payoff pairs (u1, u2) is bounded above by a Pareto frontier that runs in a straight line from (0,3) to (2,2), and then in a straight line from (2,2) to (4,0); everything on or below that boundary (with u1, u2 ≥ 0) is also feasible. Recall the Nash Bargaining solution maximizes (u1-d1)(u2-d2) over the feasible set, while the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution is the point on the frontier lying on the straight line from d to the "ideal point" a = (a1,a2), where a_i is the highest payoff player i could possibly get. (a) [8 pts] Find the Nash Bargaining solution. (b) [8 pts] Find the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution. (c) [4 pts] These two should NOT come out the same — explain, in terms of the axioms each solution concept gives up, why they diverge here."

And here's Danny's answer, word for word:

"(a) We want to maximize u1*u2. On the first piece, u2 = 3 - 0.5u1, so the product is 3u1 - 0.5u1^2. Its derivative is 3-u1, which is still positive at u1=2, so this piece is climbing the whole way to its right endpoint. On the second piece, u2 = 4-u1, so the product is 4u1-u1^2, with derivative 4-2u1, which hits zero exactly at u1=2 and goes negative right after, so this piece is falling from u1=2 onward. So the product is maxed right at the kink where the two pieces meet, and the Nash Bargaining solution is (2,2).

(b) The ideal point is a=(4,3): 4 is the most player 1 could ever get (when u2=0) and 3 is the most player 2 could ever get (when u1=0). The KS solution sits where the line from (0,0) to (4,3), which is u2=(3/4)u1, crosses the frontier. Plugging that into the first piece's equation: 3-0.5u1 = 0.75u1, giving 3 = 1.25u1, so u1=2.4 and u2=1.8. So the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution is (2.4, 1.8).

(c) Both solutions are Pareto efficient, symmetric, and invariant to rescaling, so those three axioms aren't where the disagreement comes from — it has to be the fourth one. Nash's solution is pinned down by Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives: shrink the feasible set but leave the old solution point available, and it has to stay the answer. This is essentially the identical condition that gives Arrow's impossibility theorem its bite over in voting theory. Kalai and Smorodinsky dropped IIA in favor of individual monotonicity instead, which says that if one player's opportunities expand while the other's stay fixed, that player should never end up worse off. Since the two segments of our frontier have different slopes, the two axioms end up pinning down two different points on it, exactly like we found."
