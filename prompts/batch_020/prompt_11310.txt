I've been reading Kierkegaard for a class and got kind of obsessed with the Corsair affair - the way he basically provoked a Danish satire paper into attacking him, and they retaliated by caricaturing him over and over as a hunchback with mismatched trouser legs, until random kids were yelling "Either-Or!" at him in the street and even his own tailor asked him to take his business elsewhere because the jokes about his pants were bad for the shop's reputation. What's interesting is that this whole humiliating episode seems to be what's behind his book Two Ages (the part everyone quotes as "The Present Age"), where he comes up with this theory of "the public" as a kind of phantom or abstraction - not a real community, just this faceless, unaccountable thing that the press conjures up - plus "leveling," where anyone who stands out gets dragged back down to average. He's even got this image of the public basically owning a dog it sics on anyone impressive, and the dog (the press) tears at the guy until the public gets bored and calls it off.

I keep thinking about how much this sounds like modern internet pile-ons and cancel culture, but I dont want to just lazily equate "the public" with "Twitter" myself and call it a day - I'd rather hear what you think he'd actually say, using his own categories instead of a generic "old philosopher hates technology" take. Would he look at a modern X pile-on or Reddit dogpile and say it's just the same mechanism sped up, or does something about today's version not fit his diagnosis - like, does it matter that a lot of modern shaming happens under people's real names instead of anonymously, or that algorithms are doing the amplifying instead of newspaper editors? And since his own response to leveling wasn't nostalgia for the old hierarchies but something about leveling actually clearing the ground for the "single individual" to relate to the eternal instead of the crowd, what would he actually tell someone going through a pile-on today - just keep walking the same streets and doing your work like he did, or is there more to his advice than grim endurance?
