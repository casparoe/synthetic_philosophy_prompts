In The Concept of Mind (1949), Gilbert Ryle argues that dispositional predicates such as "brittle" or "soluble" are not names for some hidden inner state, but law-like licenses for inferring what an object would do under specified conditions -- a purely conditional analysis that was part of his broader campaign against the Cartesian "ghost in the machine" and what he called the "para-mechanical hypothesis" of hidden inner causes. In the 1990s, long after Ryle's death in 1976, philosophers including C. B. Martin, Mark Johnston, David Lewis, and Alexander Bird developed two families of counterexamples to any such conditional analysis of dispositions. "Finkish" dispositions are switched on or off at the very instant the test condition occurs, as when a dead wire is connected to a device that makes it live the instant a conductor touches it, so that the disposition and the relevant conditional come apart. "Masked" (or "antidoted") dispositions are fully retained while something else in the setup blocks their normal manifestation, as with a fragile glass safely packed in styrofoam, or a poison neutralized by a simultaneously administered antidote.

Arguing strictly from Ryle's own commitments in The Concept of Mind, rather than from how later dispositional realists redeployed these cases, how do you think he would have responded to finks and masks? Could his framework defuse them as merely more elaborate law-like statements requiring nothing more than an implicit "normal conditions" clause of the kind ordinary usage already tolerates, or do they expose a point at which his anti-mechanistic account of powers covertly needed exactly the hidden, mechanism-involving picture he wanted to dissolve?
