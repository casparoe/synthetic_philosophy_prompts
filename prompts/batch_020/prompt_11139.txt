I am at work on a historical novel about Galileo's long quarrel with Rome, and I have spent the last week failing to write a scene that has become the hinge of the book's second part: an imagined private conversation between Galileo and Cardinal Robert Bellarmine, set in Rome during the winter of 1615–16, in the months after Bellarmine's letter to Paolo Antonio Foscarini and before his own admonition to Galileo the following February. I have read enough of the primary material and secondary literature (Finocchiaro's documentary collection, Blackwell's study of Bellarmine's biblical hermeneutics) to know that my early drafts were caricatures: Bellarmine kept coming out as a mouthpiece for reflexive dogmatism, and Galileo as a modern secularist who had already won the argument before opening his mouth. I would like a dialogue that resists both temptations and instead stages, as living speech, the genuinely difficult argument the two men were having on paper.

Please have Galileo argue substantially the position of his Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina: that Scripture and nature both proceed from God and so cannot truly conflict; that Scripture, being adapted to the capacities of ordinary people, routinely speaks according to appearances rather than literal physical fact, much as it lends God hands, feet, or anger; and that, following Augustine, once a claim about nature has been fixed by sense experience and necessary demonstration, it is the conflicting scriptural passage that must be reinterpreted, not the demonstration that must yield. He may, if it comes naturally, invoke the line he borrows approvingly from Cardinal Baronius, that the Holy Spirit's business is to teach how one goes to heaven, not how heaven goes. Please have Bellarmine argue substantially the position of his 1615 letter to Foscarini: that it is one thing to use heliocentrism as a computational device for saving the appearances, as eccentrics and epicycles do for Ptolemy, and quite another to assert it as physically true; that the Council of Trent forbids abandoning the unanimous reading of the Fathers on a scriptural passage without genuine necessity, and the Fathers are unanimous that the sun moves and the earth is still; and that absent a strict demonstration, which he does not grant Galileo yet has, prudence favors keeping the traditional reading rather than staking Scripture's credibility on an unproven hypothesis. Let them actually cross-examine one another: Bellarmine should press Galileo on whether the phases of Venus, the Medicean moons, and the tides he is so fond of amount to demonstration in the strict sense rather than merely one more hypothesis that saves the appearances, and Galileo should press Bellarmine on why an incidental, undefined agreement among the Fathers on a point of natural philosophy should carry the weight of a matter of faith at all, and on whether Bellarmine's own caution might itself imperil Scripture's credibility if the traditional reading is later overturned by better evidence.

Neither man should be given access to anything unavailable in 1615 — no stellar parallax, no Foucault pendulum, nothing beyond what Galileo had actually observed and published by then — and neither should simply fold. Both should be allowed their full intellectual force, so that a reader cannot tell in advance which one the author favors. I would be glad if the exchange closed not with a resolution but with the two of them trying to state, in their own terms, exactly where their disagreement stops being about astronomy and starts being about the nature and limits of scriptural authority. Something in the range of 900 to 1300 words, written as a script with speaker labels rather than narrated prose, would fit the chapter as I currently have it planned.
