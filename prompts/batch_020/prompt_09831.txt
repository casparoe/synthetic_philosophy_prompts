I spent almost 40 years as a physicist before I retired, and for the last year or so I've been reading a ton of philosophy for the first time, which is how I stumbled onto Max Black's old thought experiment: a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres, two miles apart, identical down to the last property, supposedly a counterexample to Leibniz's principle that no two distinct things can be exactly alike (the identity of indiscernibles). Every account I've read offers the same rescue: the spheres aren't really indiscernible, because each one stands in the relation "is two miles from the other," a relation neither bears to itself, so they're "weakly discernible" even though no intrinsic property tells them apart. It's the same move people make about two electrons stuck in one orbital -- except there the electrons at least differ in spin -- and from what I can tell the move gets a lot shakier once you look at bosons piled into a single shared state, where nothing seems to play that discerning role.

Please argue that this "weak discernibility" defense is a hollow victory for Leibniz's principle: that it saves the letter of the identity of indiscernibles while quietly abandoning whatever made the principle interesting in the first place, and that it's trouble with the boson case is a symptom of that, not some unrelated side issue. Don't just summarize the debate -- make the strongest case you actually can for that verdict.
