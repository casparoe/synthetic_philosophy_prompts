I go to a smallish rationalist-adjacent meetup, and last week's talk pitched futarchy -- Robin Hanson's old idea of replacing policy debate with conditional prediction markets: fix a welfare metric democratically, then run a market on "metric given policy A" against one on "metric given policy B" and go with whichever prices higher. The toy case everyone reaches for is a company deciding whether to fire its CEO by betting on whether the stock rises conditional on firing him vs. not. Two people in the audience argued about it over drinks for like twenty minutes afterward and I still can't shake it.

1. Marcus said the CEO case gives it away: nothing stops the CEO, or a board member who already wants him gone, from just buying up whichever side helps their case -- so "the market decided" quietly means "whoever had the most money to burn decided."
2. Elena said that objection already has an answer: if his price is wrong, that's free money on the table, so other traders pile in against him and drag it back toward the honest number. A manipulator is basically a fish subsidizing the smart money -- the harder he pushes, the more he personally loses, and the more accurate the market gets.
3. Marcus said that story only works if there's enough time and enough smart money to correct the price before the vote locks in. If the board's just deciding on a set date, not trading forever, the CEO can eat a seven-figure trading loss and still come out way ahead if keeping his job is worth ten times that -- losing money "in the market" doesn't matter if you were never optimizing for market profit.
4. Elena granted the deadline problem but called it an engineering fix, not a fatal one: subsidize liquidity and pay a market maker whose whole job is hunting down price moves that don't track new information. Make manipulation expensive enough and the arithmetic flips back.
5. Marcus said fine, forget manipulation -- theres a subtler problem baked into the design itself: whichever side isn't chosen never has its outcome realized, so those bets just get refunded. So the moment "fire him" starts looking like the favorite, nobody has much reason to keep researching or betting hard on "keep him," since a voided position is dead capital either way -- so it can quietly tip toward whichever side looked good early, with nobody manipulating anything.
6. Elena said that one's real too, but the fix is the same genre: subsidize both branches enough that correcting the "losing" side still pays. And every decision procedure has some version of this -- boards anchor on whoever built support first, voters flock to the apparent front-runner -- so naming the flaw doesn't tell you what to do instead, unless futarchy's version is actually worse than whatever he's implicitly defending in its place.

Neither of them budged and I honestly can't tell who's ahead.
