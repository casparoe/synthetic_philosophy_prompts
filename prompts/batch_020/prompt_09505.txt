Quick one -- I've been re-reading Ramsey's Epilogue essay (the piece he read to the Apostles in 1925, tacked onto the end of Foundations of Mathematics) where he refuses to feel humble before "the vastness of the heavens" and says his picture of the world is drawn in perspective, not to scale, with humans in the foreground and the stars shrunk down to "threepenny bits." He even jokes that the eventual heat death of the universe has a "present value at compound discount" of almost nothing, so its not worth losing sleep over. Funny thing is, its the same Ramsey who, a few years later in his economics, argued that discounting future generations' welfare just because they're further off in time is basically indefensible -- an argument longtermists now lean on to justify caring enormously about the "astronomical waste" of humanity not colonizing the stars.

So: if Ramsey had somehow lived to see Bostrom/Ord/MacAskill-style longtermism, do you think his own commitments would push him toward sympathy for it, or does the "perspective, not scale" picture of value in his Epilogue put him fundamentally at odds with the whole project? Don't just point out the tension -- tell me how you think he'd actually land, and why.
