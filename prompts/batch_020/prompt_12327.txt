Over the past couple of months I've been part of the team at my company drafting the internal "safety case" by which each generation of our AI system is meant to formally certify that deploying its successor is safe, with the current generation checking the successor's safety argument before signing off on the handoff. While working through the verification logic, we ran straight into what the AI-safety literature calls the Löbian obstacle: because our proof calculus is at least as strong as Peano arithmetic, Löb's theorem blocks it from ever adopting an unrestricted reflection schema ("if I can prove X, then X is true") about itself without collapsing into triviality — yet something like that schema is exactly what one agent needs in order to trust that a successor's proofs are sound. Since then I've been reading through the main proposals for getting around this obstacle, or reframing it so that it stops being a problem:

- Infinite descending hierarchies of proof systems via parametric/schematic polymorphism
- Probabilistic reflection principles in place of binary provability
- Logical induction
- Paraconsistent or relevant logics that block explosion
- Reframing self-trust at the level of policies via updateless/functional decision theory, rather than via proof
- Simply relying on a strictly weaker, "good enough" meta-theory as a one-off pragmatic patch

I'd like your assessment of how much genuine progress each one represents toward actually resolving the obstacle, as opposed to being a clever workaround that leaves the underlying tension intact.
