I'm working on a feature about the prehistory of the modern infographic, and one strand of it concerns Otto Neurath's Isotype system — the pictogram method he developed with the designer Gerd Arntz and his close collaborator, later his wife, Marie Reidemeister, at Vienna's Social and Economic Museum, intended to let ordinary readers grasp statistics on housing, wages, or mortality without depending on fluent literacy in any single language. While gathering background from a historian of design, I was told, almost as an aside, that Isotype is best understood as Neurath's fellow Vienna Circle member Ludwig Wittgenstein's picture theory of meaning carried out of the Tractatus and into visual practice: since Wittgenstein held that a proposition is a logical picture sharing its structure with the fact it depicts, and Neurath wanted a genuinely universal picture-based language, the Isotype charts are, on this reading, that very theory realized in ink and cardboard.

That story is tidy enough that I would like to build a section of the piece around it, but before committing it to print I want to check the premise: is it accurate to treat Isotype as essentially an applied version of Wittgenstein's picture theory, and is Wittgenstein correctly described as a fellow Vienna Circle member alongside Neurath? If the lineage is looser than I've been led to believe, I would welcome a clearer account of where Neurath's own notion of a "picture language" actually came from, and how it relates to, or diverges from, what Wittgenstein meant by a picture.
