After thirty-five years as a physical oceanographer, much of it spent deploying autonomous instruments whose firmware had an unnerving habit of doing exactly what it was told rather than what was meant, I retired four years ago and have since been filling in the philosophy and mathematical logic I never had time for during my career — most recently Gödel's and Löb's theorems, worked through with a retired mathematician friend over an embarrassing number of coffees. That unlikely hobby is apparently why our university's provost recruited me, along with two other emeritus scientists from outside computer science, onto the newly formed Autonomous Systems Oversight Committee: formal training and no departmental stake in the outcome were considered assets.

We are currently reviewing a proposal from a robotics lab for a general framework for self-modifying research agents, to be piloted on a fleet of long-duration ocean gliders that must revise their own navigation and collision-avoidance code mid-deployment, entirely out of contact with any human operator. The safety mechanism is "proof-gated" self-modification: an agent may install a revision to its own decision code only if it can produce a machine-checked proof, within a fixed formal system, that the revision will never violate a specified list of hard constraints (depth and pressure limits, a minimum stand-off from shipping lanes, no autonomous surfacing inside restricted zones). Reading the technical appendix, it struck me that the whole scheme quietly depends on an agent being able to trust, in full generality, that whatever a future version of itself proves safe really is safe — and that this is exactly the kind of general self-trust Löb's theorem rules out, since a system expressive enough to reason about its own proofs can only prove "if I can prove P, then P" for the P's it could already prove outright, never as a free-standing license to be cashed in later. I raised this at our last meeting expecting either a quick rebuttal or a quick concession and got neither. The lab insists it has sidestepped the obstacle because no version of the agent is ever asked to trust a successor reasoning with a system as strong as its own: each successor is confined to a fixed formal system strictly weaker than its predecessor's, so the certification is never truly self-referential in the sense Löb's theorem forbids. Two committee members remain unconvinced, partly on the grounds that an agent whose successors are permanently limited to ever-weaker proof systems hardly deserves to be called self-improving, and want the whole proposal sent back in favor of the newer, probabilistic treatments of this kind of self-referential uncertainty that I've seen called "logical induction," which drop the demand for proof-based certainty altogether.

Could you draft the short memo I am expected to circulate before next week's vote? It should reach one concrete recommendation — approve, reject, or approve subject to specific, checkable conditions and a date for re-review — laid out so that my colleagues, mostly roboticists and a bioethicist rather than logicians, can actually follow the reasoning. It should treat the lab's "weaker system for each successor" defense as a serious technical claim rather than dismissing or accepting it on the strength of the vocabulary alone, and, whichever way you come down, it should give the losing side's strongest argument a genuine hearing rather than a caricature of it.
