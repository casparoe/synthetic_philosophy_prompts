I have served for the past three years on my university's Academic Integrity Panel, a standing three-member faculty board that hears contested plagiarism referrals once the Dean of Students' office decides a case cannot be resolved informally. Under our code, a student is found "responsible" for a violation only when two elements both hold: (i) the submitted work substantially reproduces another's work or ideas without proper attribution, and (ii) the student knew, or reasonably should have known, that this was prohibited under the syllabus's collaboration policy. In our most recent case -- a lab report containing several paragraphs paraphrased from a classmate's report obtained through a shared drive -- I voted that both elements were satisfied. A colleague from the history department, who reads for exactly this kind of textual overlap professionally, agreed the reproduction was substantial, but thought the syllabus's collaboration policy was genuinely unclear as applied to lab write-ups, so she voted no on the knowledge element. A colleague from chemistry thought the overlap fell short of "substantial," given how much lab-report phrasing is dictated by a shared experimental setup, but held that the student would certainly have known had it been substantial, so he voted yes on knowledge. Voting directly on the bottom-line verdict, as our panel always has, we came out "not responsible," two votes to one.

It was only while drafting the file memo that I noticed something uncomfortable: a majority of us had voted "yes" on element (i) taken by itself, and a different majority had voted "yes" on element (ii) taken by itself -- so an issue-by-issue procedure would have delivered "responsible" from the very same three ballots that produced "not responsible" under our usual up-or-down vote. This is close enough to the discursive dilemma I cover in my formal epistemology seminar that I recognized the shape of it at once, but recognizing the shape hasn't told me what to do about it. The historian now wants the panel to adopt an issue-by-issue procedure as our standing rule for future split verdicts, on the grounds that a majority of us did, element by element, accept everything the code requires for responsibility. The chemist wants to keep voting on the bottom line, on the grounds that our "not responsible" finding is at least the real, all-things-considered verdict of two of the three of us, whereas "responsible" was only ever my own view of the case as a whole. I notice, uncomfortably, that the procedure the historian favors happens to vindicate my own vote, which makes me trust my own judgment here even less. I have been asked to bring a short written recommendation to the Faculty Senate subcommittee that oversees the conduct code before it meets next month. Could you draft that opinion for me? It should say what, if anything, ought to happen with this particular student, take a clear position on which voting procedure the panel should adopt going forward (or propose a third option, if you think both on offer are flawed), and engage honestly with the strongest thing to be said for whichever procedure you end up not recommending, since I will have to defend the memo to whichever of my colleagues ends up on the losing side.
