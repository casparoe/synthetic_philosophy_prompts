Over the past few weeks I have had to decide what to do about a difficult family situation: I recently discovered that my brother has been misappropriating money from our elderly mother's accounts, and I have felt torn between reporting him to the authorities and simply confronting him privately in order to keep the matter within the family. In trying to understand why the second option feels so compelling despite seeming to flout ordinary standards of fairness, I picked up an introduction to Confucian role ethics and came across the following exchange from the Analects (13.18, in James Legge's translation):

"The Duke of Sheh informed Confucius, saying, 'Among us here there are those who may be styled upright in their conduct. If their father have stolen a sheep, they will bear witness to the fact.' Confucius said, 'Among us, in our part of the country, those who are upright are different from this. The father conceals the misconduct of the son, and the son conceals the misconduct of the father. Uprightness is to be found in this.'"

My understanding is that this brief exchange has become something of a flashpoint in debates about whether Confucian ethics is best understood as a "role ethics," in which obligations attach to specific relationships rather than to persons as such, and that it has even provoked a genuine scholarly controversy over whether such a view amounts to an endorsement of nepotism. Could you help me work through it? Specifically, I would like to understand why Confucius describes concealment, rather than its opposite, as "uprightness" (zhi); how defenders of the role-ethics reading answer the charge that this passage licenses corruption; and whether anything in the text or its interpretive tradition offers a principled way to distinguish my situation, which involves ongoing harm to a vulnerable person, from the comparatively minor theft of a sheep.
