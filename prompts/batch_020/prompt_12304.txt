I'm giving one of the toasts at my friend Priya's wedding to her partner Daniel this weekend, and I want your help getting the framing exactly right. Priya wrote her dissertation on mereology before leaving academia for corporate law, and last night at the rehearsal dinner the two of us ended up in a two-hour argument about van Inwagen's Special Composition Question — specifically, whether "the two shall become one," which her officiant fully intends to say tomorrow, can be cashed out as anything more than a nice turn of phrase. Priya was defending mereological nihilism: there's no such thing as a couple, only two people arranged marriage-wise, and the ceremony changes nothing about what actually exists. I was pushing van Inwagen's own answer instead — that composition occurs only when some things' activity constitutes a life, so a marriage doesn't bring any new object into being either, but for a completely different reason than Priya's, and one I think is more interesting. Neither of us budged, and rather than paper over that tomorrow, I'd like the toast to be built on it.

The trouble is the room. About a third of the guests are people from Priya's old department, including at least one committed defender of unrestricted composition, who on her view would say the "couple" has existed, trivially, since the day the two of them met, and who will absolutely notice if the toast gets the theory wrong. The rest are family, law school friends, and Daniel's side of the aisle, essentially none of whom has ever heard the word "mereology" and all of whom just want a toast that makes them tear up a little.

Could you write me a five-minute toast that uses the Special Composition Question as its actual structure, rather than as a decorative name-drop, so that both the joke and the sentiment depend on getting the metaphysics right, while still working as a warm, generous speech for the guests who came for the cake and have no idea what any of this means?
