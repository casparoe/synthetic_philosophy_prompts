Since retiring from a career in experimental gravitational-wave physics, I have been spending my mornings working through the philosophical literature on space and time, and I have become stuck on the hole argument. Every historical account of how Einstein talked himself out of the indeterminism his own earlier reasoning seemed to force on him points to a single paragraph in his 1916 review article, "The Foundation of the General Theory of Relativity." The paragraph reads:

"That this requirement of general co-variance, which takes away from space and time the last remnant of physical objectivity, is a natural one, will be seen from the following reflexion. All our space-time verifications invariably amount to a determination of space-time coincidences. If, for example, events consisted merely in the motion of material points, then ultimately nothing would be observable but the meetings of two or more of these points. Moreover, the results of our measurings are nothing but verifications of such meetings of the material points of our measuring instruments with other material points, coincidences between the hands of a clock and points on the clock dial, and observed point-events happening at the same place at the same time. The introduction of a system of reference serves no other purpose than to facilitate the description of the totality of such coincidences."

I am entirely comfortable with the mathematics that generates the problem: given one solution of the field equations, a diffeomorphism that acts as the identity outside some region but not inside it produces a second, formally distinct solution, so that the matter distribution together with the field outside the hole fails to fix the field within it. What escapes me is how the paragraph above is supposed to dissolve that problem rather than simply change the subject. On its face it is an epistemological remark about what a physicist can measure, yet philosophers treat it as licensing a metaphysical verdict: that the two solutions related by the diffeomorphism are not, after all, distinct physical possibilities. Could you spell out the missing premises that carry Einstein from "only coincidences are observable" to "diffeomorphic models are physically equivalent," and tell me whether that is essentially the same move philosophers now label Leibniz equivalence, or whether his actual argument is doing something narrower, or more question-begging, that merely happens to land on the same verdict?
