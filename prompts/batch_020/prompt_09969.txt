For a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, write a dialogue between a sharp Bayesian, who holds that a fully rational agent's degrees of belief are always given by a single probability function, and an imprecise-credence theorist, who holds -- in the tradition of Levi and Walley -- that rational credence can legitimately be represented by a whole set of probability functions when the evidence itself is ambiguous rather than merely scant. Have the sharp Bayesian press a version of Adam Elga's sequential-bets argument: a pair of bets on some proposition that, taken one at a time, the imprecise theorist's own favored decision rule permits declining, yet which together guarantee a profit regardless of whether the proposition turns out true or false. The imprecise-credence theorist should offer the strongest available reply, then turn the tables by asking where the sharp Bayesian's single number is supposed to come from in cases with no symmetry to appeal to at all -- an Ellsberg-style urn with a wholly unknown mix of colors, say, or a novel risk that reasonable experts assess differently for structural rather than statistical reasons. Neither side should get the last word simply by restating its opening position; each move should answer the specific point just made.
