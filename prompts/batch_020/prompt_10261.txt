Me and a friend got into a whole thing last night about Nietzsche's Genealogy of Morals and I cant stop thinking about it. It's that bit in the First Essay, section 13, right after the birds-of-prey-and-lambs stuff, where he writes something like:

"For just as the popular mind separates the lightning from its flash and takes the latter for an action, for the operation of a subject called 'lightning,' so popular morality also separates strength from expressions of strength, as if there were a neutral substratum behind the strong man, which was free to express strength or not to do so. But there is no such substratum; there is no 'being' behind doing, effecting, becoming; the 'doer' is merely a fiction added to the deed -- the deed is everything."

My friend's take was that this is basically Nietzsche doing a Hume-style no-self/no-free-will move and just applying it to action generally -- no agent, only events happening. I pushed back, because a few sections earlier (and constantly through the rest of the essay) he talks about ressentiment as something the weak actively do: he says the slave revolt in morality begins when ressentiment "becomes creative and gives birth to values," and describes the man of ressentiment as compensating himself with an "imaginary revenge" -- which sure sounds like it needs a real doer choosing something, not just "the deed is everything." Is that an actual contradiction in the text, or is there a more careful position hiding here that lets him deny there's a metaphysical subject/agent while still treating ressentiment as a genuine kind of doing? Neither of us could work out whos right, so figured i'd just ask.
