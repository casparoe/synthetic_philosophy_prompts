I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and this term I'm taking a graduate seminar on metaontology that has spent the last few weeks on the Hirsch–Sider exchange over quantifier variance, using mereological composition as the running example. The background dispute is the Special Composition Question: universalists hold that for any two or more objects whatsoever, however scattered or gerrymandered, there exists some further object that is their fusion (so there is, strictly speaking, an object composed of my left shoe and the Eiffel Tower), while nihilists hold that composition never occurs, so that, strictly speaking, there are no tables, chairs, or shoes at all — only simples arranged table-wise, chair-wise, and shoe-wise. What I can't settle in my own head is the second-order question sitting on top of this: Hirsch argues that this kind of dispute is merely verbal, since each side's sentences can be charitably translated into the other's idiom so that both come out true, meaning neither quantifier meaning is more correct than the other; Sider resists this, holding that at most one candidate meaning for "exists" is joint-carving — that is, actually matches the structure of reality — so that which ontology is correct is a fully substantive question to be settled by ordinary theoretical considerations like parsimony or the argument from vagueness.

Could you write me a dialogue between a committed universalist and a committed nihilist who have been arguing about composition for years and finally stop to ask each other whether their disagreement has been substantive all along or merely verbal? I'd like the universalist to be the one who first raises the deflationary possibility, prompted by the thought that, since a fusion is arguably nothing over and above its parts, her claim that "there exists a further object" may just be a more liberal way of describing exactly the particles the nihilist already accepts — and have her press this into something like Hirsch's charity-based translation test. I'd like the nihilist to resist, partly on the grounds that if the dispute really is merely verbal, then nihilism no longer buys him any of the theoretical payoff (dissolving puzzles about coincident objects, avoiding causal overdetermination between a composite and its parts, and so on) that made anything so revisionary worth accepting in the first place, and have him reach for something like Sider's joint-carving realism to argue that the sparse quantifier, not the permissive one, is the one that carves at the joints. From there I want real engagement rather than two monologues: she should press him on what could possibly make one candidate meaning more joint-carving than another without already begging the question against her, and he should press her on whether her proposed translation actually preserves what each of them finds theoretically compelling about their own view, or just achieves superficial word-matching. Please keep both characters equally sharp and don't let either concede more than their view warrants — I would rather the dialogue end with the disagreement more precisely located than with either side winning the other over.
