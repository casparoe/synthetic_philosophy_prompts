Over the past few months I've been teaching myself some philosophy of language in my spare time, mostly by following one Stanford Encyclopedia article to the next after work, and I've gotten stuck on a puzzle I can't quite let go of. It started from a simple observation: "Desdemona loves Cassio" and "Cassio loves Desdemona" are built out of exactly the same three ingredients (Desdemona, Cassio, and the loving relation), yet they say completely different, even incompatible, things. That seems to show that a fact or a proposition can't just be its ingredients sitting side by side like objects on a shelf — something has to actually unify them, and unify them in one direction rather than the other. Chasing that observation led me to F.H. Bradley's old regress argument: if you try to explain how a relation manages to actually connect two terms by treating the relation as one more separate item that needs tying to the others, you immediately need a further relation to do the tying, and then another relation to tie that one on in turn, and so on without end. Bradley apparently used this to conclude that relations, and so the whole ordinary world of distinct, related things, can't be ultimately real.

What I find fascinating is that people are still fighting over how, or whether, the regress gets stopped, and the fight seems to split into two camps that are talking about genuinely different things rather than just wording the same idea differently. One camp, which I gather goes back to Frege, says the regress never gets off the ground because a relation or concept isn't a complete, freestanding item in the first place — it's inherently "unsaturated," carrying its own gap, so that slotting the right objects into that gap produces something unified automatically, with no extra glue and no extra relation required. The other camp, associated with names like Hanks and Soames that I've come across more recently, says this doesn't really solve anything, because an unsaturated concept plus some objects is still just a structure — no more inherently unified or truth-apt than a set or an ordered sequence — until some thinker or speaker actually does something with it, namely predicates one of the objects of the other. On this second view, unity isn't a standing feature of relations or of propositions considered as free-standing entities at all; it only shows up when a mind performs an act of judgment or assertion.

I'd like to see these two views actually argue with each other rather than just be described one after another. Could you write me a dialogue between a philosopher who defends the unsaturated-relation solution and a philosopher who defends the act-of-predication solution, in which they use the Desdemona/Cassio case (or something similarly concrete) to test each other's accounts and press directly on their weak points — for instance, whether "unsaturation" is really explaining the unity or just re-describing it, and whether an act of predication doesn't secretly need something unsaturated for the mind to be predicating with in the first place? They don't have to be the historical figures themselves; stand-ins are fine, so long as the arguments are as precise and demanding as the real debate rather than a simplified version of it. I'd like the two sides to be evenly matched, genuinely responding to whatever the other just said instead of restating their own position, and I'd rather the exchange end in a genuine standoff than have either side simply concede, since from what I can tell this is still very much an open dispute.
