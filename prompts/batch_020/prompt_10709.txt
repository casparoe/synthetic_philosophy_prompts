During the postmortem for a nasty production incident last week, my team ran into a probability puzzle that I haven't been able to let go of, and I'd like to actually understand what's going on before it comes up again. We classify incidents along two independent axes: which layer failed (Backend or Frontend) and what kind of failure it was (Config or Code), giving four categories total. Based on years of incident history, we treat these four categories as equally likely going in, 25% each, so Backend-vs-Frontend and Config-vs-Code are each an even 50/50 split a priori. Partway through the incident, the on-call engineer sent a Slack message just before losing signal on the subway: "if it's a backend issue, the odds are 3:1 that it's a config problem rather than a code bug" — and that was the last we heard from her. Notice that the message says nothing at all about frontend; it only pins down the internal ratio inside the backend branch.

For the write-up, we wanted to fold that one piece of testimony into our four-way distribution while otherwise staying as uncommitted as possible, since we had no basis for opinions beyond what she told us. One engineer wrote a short script that solves for the distribution over the four categories that satisfies the new 3:1 backend-internal ratio while being as close as possible, in KL-divergence terms, to our original uniform prior. Its output surprised all of us: the total probability assigned to "frontend" also drifted, from an even 50% up to roughly 53%, purely as a byproduct of the optimization, even though the only new information concerned the backend split. A second engineer insists this is a bug in our reasoning rather than a real result: in his view, the sensible update leaves backend-vs-frontend locked at exactly 50/50 and only redistributes probability inside backend according to the 3:1 ratio, changing nothing else. When I pushed him on why that particular axis, rather than some other way of carving up the same four categories, deserves to be the one held fixed, he couldn't really justify it.

So I'm stuck between two updates that each claim to be the neutral, assumption-free one, and I genuinely can't tell whether my colleague's fix is the obviously correct patch for a flawed method, or whether the script's counterintuitive output is actually the more defensible answer and our sense that "this evidence is irrelevant to frontend" is simply wrong. Is there a principled basis for saying which side has it right, or is there some third way of updating on this kind of conditional evidence that avoids the tension altogether? I'd like a real answer I can defend to my team the next time this comes up, not just a shrug that reasonable people disagree.
