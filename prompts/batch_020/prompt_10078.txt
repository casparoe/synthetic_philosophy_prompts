Over dinner with some friends from a rationalist meetup a while back, we got stuck on something that felt bigger than the specific debates it kept coming up in. On LessWrong and Overcoming Bias threads about anthropic reasoning — the Doomsday Argument, the Sleeping Beauty problem, Bostrom's Self-Sampling Assumption versus the Self-Indication Assumption — the sticking point always seemed to trace back to an older, more general puzzle: the reference class problem. Whenever you want to say "I should reason as though I'm a random draw from population X," the choice of X is almost never forced by the facts, yet the probability you end up assigning, and the decision that follows from it, depends entirely on that choice. Even Reichenbach's old fix of using the narrowest class for which you have reliable statistics just pushes the question back a step, since "narrowest" is itself relative to how finely you're willing to individuate people. The same structure shows up far from exotic thought experiments too — in an oncologist quoting a patient's five-year survival odds, an actuary pricing a policy, or a juror weighing a DNA match against "the population of possible matches" — and it bothers me that there doesn't seem to be a principled algorithm for picking the "right" class once you actually try to specify one.

I'd like you to write a short story, maybe 1000–1800 words, that makes a reader feel this problem rather than have it explained to them, in the way Greg Egan's Permutation City makes the weirdness of duplicated minds felt long before any character starts lecturing about it. Set it wherever seems most alive to you: a hospital ward, an actuarial office, a courtroom, a research station full of copies of an uploaded mind, a fertility clinic running probability-of-success numbers, or something else entirely. Build the plot around a protagonist whose fate — a diagnosis, a verdict, a decision about who gets a scarce resource — turns on being treated as a representative member of some population, and give at least two other characters good-faith, individually reasonable grounds for slicing that population differently, arriving at conclusions that don't agree with each other. Please don't have any character actually say the phrase "reference class problem" or turn into a mouthpiece for a philosophy lecture, and don't resolve the story by vindicating one class over the others — the unresolved plurality is the point.
