So this happened at the pub after our department's decision theory reading group a few weeks back, and I still haven't decided who came out ahead. Two of my PhD students, Leo and Naomi, got into a whole thing about the Pasadena game -- the Nover and Hájek gamble where you flip a fair coin until it lands heads, and if the first head turns up on toss n you win 2^n/n dollars when n is odd but have to pay 2^n/n dollars when n is even. I wasn't taking notes so this is reconstructed from memory plus a few texts afterward from people who were there, but it went roughly like this.

(1) Leo said the fair price to play is ln(2), about 69 cents, because thats what you get when you add the payoffs up in the order the flips actually happen: 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2.
(2) Naomi said that's an illusion -- the exact same payoffs, same probabilities, same coin, can be added up in a different order (say, two negative terms for every positive one) to get exactly half that, ln(2)/2, or literally any number you like, or no settled value at all, because the series only converges conditionally. So there's no fact about "the" expected value here; Leo's just partial to one ordering among infinitely many.
(3) Leo answered that temporal order isn't one arbitrary ordering among equals -- it's the order in which the bet's uncertainty is actually resolved in the world. Nobody actually reshuffles coin flips.
(4) Naomi replied that this can't be doing the work he wants: the gamble is completely specified by its conditional payoffs (if the first head lands on toss k, you get such-and-such), and rewriting that list of conditionals in a different order changes nothing about the bet itself. So if summing "in temporal order" and summing "in some other order" give different answers from the very same specification, that's a problem with treating the sum as well-defined, not a reason to trust the temporal listing over any other one.
(5) Leo tried once more: maybe you don't need a strict expectation to fix a price. If you played the game a huge number of times and charged ln(2) per play, your average winnings per play would converge to ln(2) with probability as close to 1 as you like. So ln(2) is still the operationally right price, even granting Naomi's point about rearrangement.
(6) Naomi granted that the long-run idea captures something real, but said it doesn't rescue the theory generally, since you can build close variants of the same game where even that long-run average never settles down either. And seperately, take a version of the game where every single payoff is a dollar higher, in every possible outcome -- that game is obviously better than the original, full stop, yet nobody can say its expected value is higher, because on the usual definition neither game has one. That's a stranger failure than "which number is correct," she said.
