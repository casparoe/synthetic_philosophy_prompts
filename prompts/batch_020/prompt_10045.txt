So a couple weekends ago I went down a total rabbit hole, and I blame a philosophy podcast I had on in the background while fixing a flaky test suite. They were doing the classic "statue and the clay" puzzle from material constitution debates, using Gibbard's actual names for it — Lumpl for the lump of clay, Goliath for the statue you squash it into. Lumpl and Goliath sit there made of the exact same matter at the exact same time, but Lumpl could've survived being flattened into a pancake and Goliath couldn't, so by Leibniz's law (if x=y they share literally every property, including modal ones) they can't be the same object — which apparently means you've got two distinct things stacked in the same spot, which as a programmer felt uncannily like the reference-equality-vs-structural-equality distinction, except way more annoying because nobody agrees on the answer.

Anyway I got curious enough to actually read up on the real historical Leibniz instead of just "the law named after him," and it turns out he was pretty down on ordinary objects even counting as "real" unified things at all. Stuff like heaps, armies, and (I'm assuming) statues are just "aggregates" that only count as one thing because some mind groups them that way — real, per se unity only belongs to monads or to organisms with one dominant soul running the whole show, and he's got this whole bit about how natural "machines" (living bodies) stay machine-like all the way down to infinity, while a man-made machine like a gear tooth, or presumably a statue, stops being machine-like once you zoom in past a certain point. None of this was written with Gibbard's case in mind, obviously, it's a few centuries too early, but it made me wonder if Leibniz would just refuse to play the "one object or two?" game that basically every modern answer (the coincident-objects view, Baker's constitution thing, four-dimensionalism, the nihilist "it's really just atoms arranged statue-wise" line) takes for granted.

Could you actually work through, using his real views and not a monads-101 summary, what Leibniz would say if you handed him the Lumpl/Goliath case specifically? Like — does his system commit him to two coincident things, one thing, or (given the aggregates-aren't-real-substances stuff) neither the statue nor the lump counting as a genuine unity to begin with? And if it's that last one, how's that supposed to square with him also insisting bodies are still "well-founded" phenomena rather than made-up fictions — founded in what, if neither one is a true substance? Bonus points if you can say which modern position his view ends up resembling most, and exactly where that resemblance breaks down. Sorry if some of this is garbled, I've got zero formal philosophy training, I just can't let it go.
