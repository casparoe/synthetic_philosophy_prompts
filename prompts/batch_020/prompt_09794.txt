I teach formal epistemology and decision theory, and for the past two years I've also served, somewhat incongruously, as the faculty senate's representative on my university's Emergency Preparedness Committee. We have an actual case bearing down on us: a hurricane is projected to make its closest approach to our coastal campus in roughly 60 hours, and the committee must decide by tomorrow morning whether to order a mandatory evacuation of the residence halls. The National Hurricane Center's official wind-speed probability product currently gives our specific location a 28% chance of sustained hurricane-force winds before the storm passes. Our longtime severe-weather consultant, who has an unusually good track record on storms taking this particular track, believes the real risk is considerably higher, based on a specific interaction between forward speed and wind shear that he thinks this model run underweights -- though he can't give us a number of his own, only his confidence that 28% is too low.

The committee has split into two camps that have more or less stopped listening to each other. One camp says we rely on the Hurricane Center's model precisely so that individual hunches, however well-credentialed, don't drive a decision this consequential, and that letting our consultant's dissent move us off the official figure is exactly the mistake the literature on clinical versus actuarial judgment has been warning about since Paul Meehl's original studies in the 1950s. The other camp says refusing, as a matter of policy, to ever let an articulable expert judgment shift our number isn't really deference to objective chance at all, since the actual disagreement is over what the current chance is, not over whether we should act on it once we know it. A third member, more or less accurately, keeps insisting that the whole dispute really turns on whether our consultant's evidence counts as "admissible" in David Lewis's sense for purposes of the Principal Principle, and that nothing gets settled until someone says what admissibility requires here -- but she hasn't managed to finish that thought, and neither, honestly, have I.

Could you draft the short memo I'll send the committee tonight? It needs to end in a specific decision procedure with a real deadline attached to it -- not just "gather more information" -- and it should take the actuarial camp's worry as seriously as it deserves, rather than resolving the dispute by simply declaring the more philosophically fashionable side the winner.
