Then, using the nineteenth-century ether theory of light as your example, explain how a realist might try to resist the conclusion in that specific case, and assess whether the response fully succeeds."

Here's the answer, copied exactly as they wrote it: "Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction is an argument against scientific realism, specifically against the no-miracles argument, which claims the best explanation for why our best theories make such successful, often novel predictions is that they are approximately true and their central terms refer to real things. Laudan undercuts this by pointing to the history of science: he lists many theories that were empirically successful in their day but that we now regard as false, with central terms that refer to nothing at all - phlogiston, caloric, the crystalline spheres, the humours, and more. If success this common turned up among theories we now reject, success can't be reliable evidence of truth, and the abductive step in the no-miracles argument is undermined. It's a 'meta'-induction because it's an induction over the track record of the field's first-order inductive inferences, not a first-order induction itself. A good test case is the ether theory of light: Fresnel's wave theory, built on the idea that light is a vibration in a mechanical ether, generated novel and successful predictions, like the bright spot at the centre of a small disc's shadow. We now think there is no ether, so realism looks refuted in this very case. Worrall's structural realism resists this by saying the ether episode actually teaches us to give up belief in unobservable entities behind our theories entirely, treating something like 'there is an ether' as a useful fiction and only ever committing to the observable predictions the mathematics generates; restricting realism this way means there is nothing hidden left for the pessimistic induction to be wrong about. Because Fresnel's mathematical equations carried over essentially unchanged into Maxwell's electromagnetic theory afterward, this shows the strategy fully works: whatever part of a successful theory survives into its successor was always the part we were entitled to believe, so the ether case, far from being a problem, actually shows how realism can be completely rescued from Laudan's argument."
