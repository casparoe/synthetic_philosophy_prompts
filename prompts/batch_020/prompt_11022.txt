Twenty-three years into my marriage, my husband and I separated four months after our daughter left for college. There was no affair and no single precipitating quarrel; it was closer to a quiet, almost administrative recognition, arrived at separately and then together, that whatever had been holding us to each other had been completed rather than broken. I'm currently taking a continuing-education course on the philosophy of love, sex, and marriage, and we were recently assigned Russell's *Marriage and Morals*, a book I'd skimmed in college without really absorbing. Rereading it now, his argument that the true institutional rationale of marriage is the rearing of children — that romantic passion is what gets a marriage started, but children, not love, are what give society, and often the couple itself, a reason to enforce constancy through the staleness that decades together can produce — landed uncomfortably close to home.

I've since drafted some notes toward a short essay for the course, and I'd like your comments before I write it up properly. My central move is to take Russell's functionalist claim at face value and push it slightly further than he actually goes: if children are what make a marriage's stability worth enforcing, a marriage should become considerably more fragile once the children are grown and gone, regardless of whether the couple has cultivated any of the civilized, non-possessive companionship he also recommends as a substitute for dead romance. On this reading, what happened to us wasn't a failure of love, or of Russell's ideal of dissolving jealousy through generosity of spirit — there was no third party for either of us to be jealous of — but simply the institution doing exactly what, on his account, it was always going to do once its actual job was finished. I note in the draft, somewhat uneasily, that this is a rather convenient story for me to tell, since it lets me treat the ending as structurally inevitable rather than ask harder questions about what, if anything, we built together that wasn't about parenting.

What I'd most value from you is, first, whether I'm representing Russell accurately here or flattening a more careful and qualified argument into something more deterministic than he intended; second, whether it's even a legitimate philosophical move to test a claim about marriage as a social institution against a single marriage, or whether that's a category error dressed up as introspection; and third, whether there's a sharper objection to the whole framework — his or my extension of it — that I'm too close to the material to see clearly. I'd rather have the argument taken apart rigorously than be reassured.
