I'm a PhD student in ancient philosophy, and a few of us in the department run a small recorded interview series in which we invite scholars to talk about corners of the history of philosophy too out of the way to make it onto an undergraduate syllabus. I've spent this term TAing a course that stops at the Republic and the Symposium, so preparing for next month's guest has been my first real chance to sit closely with the material she works on: the long, strange defense of regulated wine-drinking in Books I and II of Plato's Laws. As I understand her published position, the Athenian Stranger's case for supervised symposia — run by a sober symposiarch, and justified on the analogy that pleasure can test and train sophrosyne the way danger tests and trains andreia — is not the throwaway concession to Athenian custom it is often read as, against the objections of his interlocutors Megillus and Clinias, but a genuinely load-bearing piece of the dialogue's moral psychology and its theory of civic education.

Could you draft seven to nine interview questions that do not just let her restate that thesis in slightly different words each time? I would like at least one question on how this position squares with the far stricter line the Republic takes on wine for the guardians; one on the apparent regress involved in needing an already self-controlled, incorruptible supervisor to run the very exercise that is supposed to produce self-controlled citizens; one on whether the ethnographic comparisons the Stranger draws — Spartan and Cretan abstention, against Carthage's narrower rule for soldiers on campaign, against the freer customs he attributes to Persia and other non-Greek peoples — are doing real argumentative work or are mostly rhetorical throat-clearing before the actual argument starts; and one on the odder material later in Book II, where wine is supposed to loosen the self-consciousness of older citizens enough to make them willing performers in choral education. For each, please add a brief follow-up question that anticipates the answer she is most likely to give and presses on it, rather than simply moving on to the next topic.
