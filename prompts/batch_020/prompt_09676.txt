I've been working through some of David Lewis's less-discussed papers on ethics and philosophy of religion, and one of them has stuck with me: a short, comparatively obscure piece called "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" (1997), in which he brings his usual analytic rigor to bear on a doctrine that has long been central to Protestant, and especially Reformed, accounts of the atonement — namely, that Christ was punished in the place of sinners, and that this vicarious punishment satisfies the demands of justice on their behalf. Lewis's way into the topic is a comparison with debt. A debt is straightforwardly transferable: if I owe you money, anyone at all can pay it off for me, and once it's paid my obligation is discharged, full stop, regardless of whose money it was. Punishment looks different. If I've committed a crime, it's far from obvious that a willing volunteer could simply serve my sentence in my place and thereby leave me no longer liable to be punished; our ordinary retributive intuitions seem to tie punishment to the particular wrongdoer in a way that debt isn't tied to the particular debtor.

I'd like an essay that takes this debt/punishment asymmetry as its organizing thread and reaches a real verdict on the question posed in Lewis's title. Does the asymmetry show that penal substitution, taken literally, is conceptually incoherent — a kind of category mistake dressed up as theology — or can the doctrine be reformulated so that it survives the challenge, for instance by adopting some particular theory of punishment (retributive or otherwise) or by weakening what "substitution" has to mean? Don't just lay out considerations on both sides and decline to choose: commit to an answer, even a conditional one (e.g., "coherent only given assumption X, incoherent otherwise"), and be explicit about which premises concerning desert and the nature of punishment are doing the real work in getting you there.
