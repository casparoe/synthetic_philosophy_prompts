The practice question reads: "The philosopher J. R. Lucas, and later the physicist Roger Penrose, each argued that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show the human mind cannot be equivalent to any computing machine or formal system — that is, that mechanism is false. (a) Reconstruct the core logical structure of this argument. (b) Present one substantial objection that has been raised against it in the literature. (c) State, with reasons, whether you find the objection convincing." The rubric awards 20 points total, split evenly across four criteria: (1) accuracy of the explanation of Gödel's theorem and the "Gödel sentence"; (2) faithfulness of the reconstruction of the Lucas/Penrose argument, including correct attribution of claims to the right thinkers; (3) accuracy and depth of the objection presented; (4) quality of reasoning in the student's own verdict on that objection.

Here is my classmate's answer, in full:

"Mechanism is the thesis that a human mind is nothing more than a very complicated machine — formally, that there is some Turing machine, or equivalently some formal axiomatic system F, whose outputs match everything a human mathematician would eventually assert. Gödel's first incompleteness theorem tells us that for any consistent formal system F powerful enough to express elementary arithmetic, there is a sentence G(F), built by a self-referential trick so that it essentially says 'I am not provable in F,' such that neither G(F) nor its negation can be proved inside F, yet G(F) is true.

Lucas's move, later taken up and refined by Penrose, is to point out that although F cannot prove G(F), a human being reasoning about F from the outside can see that G(F) must be true, precisely because F is consistent. Since this works no matter which particular F a mechanist offers as a candidate description of the human mind, the human always has access to a truth the proposed machine cannot reach, so no single machine can be an adequate model of the human mind. This point is strengthened by Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, which shows that a consistent F can never prove its own consistency, whereas a human being can informally satisfy themselves that their own reasoning is consistent. Indeed, this is exactly the conclusion Gödel himself drew in his 1951 Gibbs lecture, where he stated outright that the human mind's mathematical powers exceed those of any Turing machine. Penrose later tried to strengthen the case further in The Emperor's New Mind and Shadows of the Mind, arguing that if mathematical insight really is non-algorithmic, its physical basis in the brain must involve some non-computable process, which he speculatively located in quantum effects in microtubules.

The most serious objection, due to Hilary Putnam, is that the argument proves less than it appears to. Even granting that for every specific F offered, the human can produce a truth F misses, this only shows the human isn't that particular F; it does nothing to show the human isn't some other, unspecified formal system. Since we do not know our own 'program,' we are never actually in a position to exhibit, for the system we truly are, the one sentence it cannot prove — the argument only ever defeats machines we have already been told to reject.

I find Putnam's objection convincing: Lucas and Penrose show that the mind is not equivalent to any formal system a mechanist has actually specified, which is a genuine result, but that is a much weaker claim than 'the mind is not any formal system whatsoever,' and only the weaker claim is actually established."
