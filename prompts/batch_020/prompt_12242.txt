I'm a retired chemist, and since I hung up my lab coat a few years back I've finally had time to read all the philosophy I always meant to get around to. Right now I'm slowly working through Hobbes's Leviathan, and I got stuck on a bit in Chapter 4 that I can't quite unpack on my own. Here's the passage:

"Seeing then that truth consisteth in the right ordering of names in our affirmations, a man that seeketh precise truth had need to remember what every name he uses stands for, and to place it accordingly; or else he will find himself entangled in words, as a bird in lime-twigs; the more he struggles, the more belimed. And therefore in geometry, which is the only science that it hath pleased God hitherto to bestow on mankind, men begin at settling the significations of their words; which settling of significations, they call definitions, and place them in the beginning of their reckoning."

What's tripping me up is the "only science" claim. Coming from chemistry, where basically everything I ever learned got nailed down by fighting with recalcitrant data rather than by starting from definitions, this idea that geometry alone gets to count as a real science, and gets to count as one because it starts from settled words, feels almost backwards to me. I vaguely remember reading somewhere (in a commentary, not Hobbes directly, so take it with a grain of salt) that he has this whole other idea kicking around his work, that geometry is uniquely certain because we ourselves construct the figures we reason about, we're the "makers" so to speak, whereas with natural bodies we only see the effects and have to guess backwards at causes we didn't build. If that's right, I don't fully see how it connects to what's actually said here about definitions and "settling significations." And to make it weirder, I later found out this is the same Hobbes who spent decades insisting, against all correction, that he'd squared the circle, which as a chemist who's been embarassed by bad data before I find almost sympathetic, except the guy never once admitted the error. Could you help me actually work through what Hobbes is claiming in this passage, why definitions are doing so much load-bearing work for him, and whether his own track record as a stubbornly bad geometer points to a real weakness in the view itself, or whether that's just a fun irony with no philosophical bite to it?
