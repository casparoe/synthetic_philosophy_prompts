In the graduate seminar I'm taking on mathematical logic and self-reference, we've spent the last two weeks moving from Gödel's incompleteness theorems to Löb's theorem and then, rather unexpectedly, to its afterlife in contemporary AI safety research. Our instructor assigned the Yudkowsky–Herreshoff paper on tiling agents, whose central worry is roughly this: a proof-based agent that wants to construct, or simply vouch for, a successor running on its own proof system would need something like a general reflection principle along the lines of "whatever this system proves is true." Löb's theorem shows that any consistent theory strong enough to prove that principle for an arbitrary sentence P can already prove P outright, so a fully general reflection schema collapses into inconsistency (this is essentially Gödel's second incompleteness theorem in disguise, recovered by letting P be a falsehood). The upshot is the so-called Löbian obstacle: such agents seem condemned to an infinite regress of successors, each trusting only a strictly weaker proof system than its own, never able to hand full trust to "a system exactly as strong as me."

A few of us in the seminar ended up debating, after class, whether any of this amounts to a kind of posthumous vindication of Gödel's own philosophical instincts. In his 1951 Gibbs lecture, Gödel argued that the incompleteness theorems force a disjunction: either the mathematical powers of the human mind outstrip those of any single fixed formal system, so that minds are not machines, or else there exist mathematical propositions that are absolutely undecidable by any mind or machine whatsoever. By most reconstructions of his private views, he leaned toward the first horn, even while remaining publicly guarded about asserting it outright.

What I'd like your help thinking through is how Gödel, given that cast of mind, would most plausibly have reacted had someone explained the Löbian obstacle to him specifically as a problem for building AI systems that can safely trust and improve their own reasoning. Would he have read it as fresh technical support for his anti-mechanist disjunct — evidence that no fixed formal system can ever underwrite its own reliability the way a mind with direct insight into mathematical truth supposedly can — or would his well-documented caution about drawing sweeping philosophical morals from technical results have made him resist that reading? Separately, do you think he would have treated the regress of ever-weaker successor systems as a genuine dead end for self-improving AI, or merely as the kind of limitation that rules out any single static system without closing off cleverer, growing hierarchies of theories — the sort of move familiar from Turing's ordinal-logic hierarchies, which emerged from a milieu Gödel knew well? I'd like an answer that actually engages with the technical structure of the Löbian obstacle and with Gödel's specific, documented views, rather than a generic summary of either side.
