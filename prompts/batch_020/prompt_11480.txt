I'm a cognitive science PhD student and somehow got talked into being the grad rep on my university's exam accommodations appeals committee this year. We've got a case that's been stuck in committee limbo for weeks and I have to draft the recommendation memo before we reconvene next Tuesday. Here's the situation: an undergrad in one of the intro cognition courses has a documented brain injury that lets her learn things fine in the moment but makes it really hard for her to reliably hold onto new information for more than a few days. Disability services already approved the standard accommodations (extra time, a quiet room), but now she's requesting something the policy doesn't cover: permission to use, during closed-book exams, a running notes file she's kept and added to for three years, searchable in plain language, which she says she now relies on more than her own memory at this point.

Two of the faculty on the committee want to deny it outright, arguing a closed-book exam exists to test what's actually in the student's head and this just smuggles in an open-notes exam under a different name. But the committee chair, who almost went into philosophy of mind before switching to comp neuro, keeps bringing up some old thought experiment about a man with Alzheimer's who uses a notebook in place of his own memory, and argues that if you take it seriously, her file might not be so different from her own recall. I dont know what to make of that, honestly, it feels like its tracking something real but also like it could be used to justify basically any device in an exam room. Could you draft the actual memo I'd bring to the committee, one that lands on a specific recommendation (approve, deny, or approve with conditions), gives the two skeptical faculty reasons they'd actually have to engage with rather than just restating the disability policy, and is upfront about whether it matters that she searches the file with something AI-ish rather than just scrolling or ctrl-F-ing through it?
