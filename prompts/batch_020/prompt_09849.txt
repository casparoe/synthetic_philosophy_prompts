I'm prepping next week's session for my seminar on vagueness and I keep getting stuck on how to present the epistemicism/supervaluationism fight without it flattening into "one side says there's a secret line, the other side says truth comes in degrees" (my students always reach for the degree-theory gloss no matter how many times I tell them Keefe's view isn't that). What I want instead is a dialogue, something like 700-900 words, between a hard-nosed Williamson-style epistemicist and a Fine/Keefe-style supervaluationist, arguing it out at a whiteboard over a concrete Sorites series — say a strip of 10,000 paint chips running from a patch that's clearly red at one end to one that's clearly orange at the other, and the question of exactly where "red" stops applying.

I don't want two position papers stapled together, I want them actually responding to each other's moves. Have the epistemicist press the supervaluationist on the fact that, on her own semantics, "there is a sharp boundary somewhere in the strip" comes out supertrue even though no particular chip is determinately the boundary, and ask why that's supposed to be less weird than a brute, unknowable cutoff secured by margin-for-error constraints on knowledge. Then have the supervaluationist push back by asking what, on the epistemicist's picture, could possibly fix a precise semantic fact that no amount of investigation could ever uncover even in principle — and maybe suggest that sounds less like meaning and more like magic. If you can work in a short skirmish about higher-order vagueness too, i.e. whether "definately red" is itself a vague predicate on either view, that'd be great, since that's usually where both sides start visibly straining. Don't let either of them land a knockout blow by the end — I want my students arguing about who's right, not reading off a verdict.
