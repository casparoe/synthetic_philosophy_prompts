I've spent a lot of my spare time working through Leibniz on my own, and two passages in particular keep nagging at me: his correspondence with Arnauld, and the scene near the end of the Theodicy where the goddess Pallas guides a visitor through a palace containing a room for every possible world, each populated by its own version of Sextus Tarquinius living out a different life. The two seem to pull in opposite directions. In the Arnauld letters, Leibniz seems to say that a version of Adam who never sinned simply wouldn't be our Adam at all, since sinning is written into Adam's complete concept — which sounds like a flat denial that one and the same individual could ever exist in more than one possible world. But the palace scene reads more like each room contains a separate Sextus, merely resembling the others rather than being strictly identical to any of them, which sounds much closer to what's now called counterpart theory. Given that Leibniz also denied that unrealized possible worlds are anything more than ideas in God's understanding — unlike Lewis, who treated them as just as concrete and real as our own — where do you think his considered view actually lands: nearer to Lewis's counterpart theory, nearer to Kripke-style transworld identity, or does his metaphysics resist being sorted into either camp?
