I cover game theory and decision theory for a science publication, and while reporting a piece on rational cooperation I keep finding two ideas treated as near-synonyms despite coming from very different traditions: Douglas Hofstadter's superrationality, and the more formal game-theoretic notion of program equilibrium, which Moshe Tennenholtz formalized by having players submit programs, rather than actions, that can read one another's source code. Before I flatten the two together in print, I'd like a rigorous comparison. What does program equilibrium deliver, by way of an actual equilibrium argument, that superrationality could only assert on the strength of a symmetry intuition among ideal reasoners? And does building cooperation out of literal, inspectable programs import new difficulties — self-reference, non-termination — that Hofstadter's purely informal version never had to face? On balance, has program equilibrium vindicated superrationality, or quietly exposed an assumption it was relying on all along? Justify your verdict.
