While reading about the more practical and pedagogical side of the Vienna Circle's output — work that tends to receive far less attention than the movement's epistemology of protocol sentences and verification — I kept returning to Otto Neurath's ISOTYPE project: the pictogram-based "picture language" he developed first as the Vienna Method of Picture Statistics and later, after emigrating, as the International System of Typographic Picture Education. Neurath held that ordinary verbal language, especially in the social sciences, trades in loosely bounded "Ballungen," or clusters of meaning, that create a false impression of precision, and he offered his standardized pictograms — always repeated at a constant size to indicate quantity rather than scaled up or down — as a comparatively unambiguous "helping language" for conveying statistical and social facts across barriers of literacy and native tongue.

What strikes me is that Ludwig Wittgenstein — whose Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus the Circle had famously pored over line by line, and whose residual mysticism Neurath was known to find exasperating — had, in a rather different key, built an entire theory of meaning around the very same word: on the Tractatus's picture theory, a proposition manages to depict a possible state of affairs at all only because it shares a "logical form" with that state of affairs, its elements standing for objects and being combined just as those objects would have to be combined for the proposition to be true. I'd like you to compare these two uses of "picture." Are Neurath and Wittgenstein really drawing on the same underlying idea of depiction, or is the shared vocabulary a coincidence masking two essentially unrelated projects — one a practical proposal about pedagogy and visual design, the other a metaphysical thesis about how any representation whatsoever latches onto reality? Does Neurath's insistence that his picture language is merely a supplementary aid, incapable of replacing full verbal or logical argument, sit comfortably with the Tractatus's distinction between what can be said and what can only be shown, or does it implicitly resist that distinction? Please defend a clear verdict on how deep the resemblance between the two really runs, rather than simply cataloguing similarities and differences.
