My husband and I went through in vitro fertilization last year, and we now have a nine-month-old daughter, Priya, along with one more embryo from the same cycle — the clinic's paperwork calls it embryo two — still sitting in frozen storage. We have to decide within the next few months whether to try for a sibling with it, donate it, or let the clinic dispose of it, and working through that decision produced a surprisingly heated argument at dinner with an old friend from graduate school who now teaches metaphysics: he insisted that, whichever way we decide, embryo two could never, under any circumstances, have been Priya, because — as he put it, citing Kripke's discussion in Naming and Necessity — a person's particular biological origin is essential to who they are, so that anyone developing from different genetic material simply is not, and could not have been, that person, however similar the resulting life turned out to be.

That struck me as more or less conclusive until I went reading around online, including the Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on David Lewis, and ran into his counterpart theory, which seems to deny the whole picture my friend was relying on: on Lewis's view, no individual exists in more than one possible world, so "Priya could have developed from embryo two" isn't made true by Priya herself having a different origin somewhere out there, but by there being a suitably similar counterpart of hers who does — and which similarities are the relevant ones is supposed to depend on context rather than being fixed once and for all. I can't work out whether that's a real disagreement with my friend's Kripkean line or just the same verdict dressed up in different vocabulary. I also vaguely remember that Kripke's own most famous complaint about counterpart theory involves a politician named Humphrey who could have won an election, and that the complaint there is about whether Humphrey should care what happens to some similar-but-distinct counterpart, which sounds like a different worry from the question of whether embryo two's occupant would even count as Priya — though I'm not fully confident those two questions come apart as cleanly as that suggests.

Before I reopen this with my friend, I'd like a firmer grip on the issue than I currently have. Could you construct a specific, carefully worked-out example — built around our actual embryo situation if it turns out to be sharp enough to do the job, or a cleaned-up variant of it if the real case is too messy — in which counterpart theory and Kripkean essentialism about origins genuinely deliver different verdicts, rather than merely different explanations for the same verdict? I'm especially interested in whether the context-sensitivity of Lewis's counterpart relation could make the answer to "could this child have developed from the other embryo" actually flip depending on what's contextually salient (genetic origin versus the future the child goes on to have, say), in a way that Kripke's necessity of origin forecloses from the outset. If you can work it in, I'd also value your view on whether the Humphrey-style objection is doing any real work against counterpart theory in a case like ours, or whether it's a separate complaint that just tends to get raised in the same breath.
