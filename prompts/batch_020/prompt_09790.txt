My aunt has worked as an ICU nurse for over a decade, and at a family dinner recently she and my cousin, who had just watched a documentary about organ transplants, got into a long disagreement about what it actually means for someone to be dead. Neither of them budged, and I've turned it over since without settling it myself, so I'd like a more careful outside opinion on who actually had the better of each exchange.

(1) My cousin's opening complaint was about organ donation "after circulatory death": doctors wait only a couple of minutes, five at most, after someone's heart stops before declaring them dead and moving to retrieve organs. He said that's nowhere near long enough to know the heart could never have been restarted with CPR or a defibrillator, so calling this "irreversible cessation" — the actual wording the law uses — is a convenient fiction that lets transplant timelines work rather than a real description of the body.

(2) My aunt answered that "irreversible" has never meant, in ordinary bedside practice, "impossible to reverse by any intervention whatsoever" — if it did, no death could ever be declared quickly, since CPR can in principle be tried on almost any heart that has just stopped. What clinicians actually confirm, and what she thinks the law is best read as asking for, is permanent cessation: once there's a valid decision not to attempt resuscitation and the heart hasn't restarted on its own, the stoppage is permanent, and permanence is treated as good enough evidence of irreversibility.

(3) My cousin replied that this just relabels "irreversible" as "irreversible, given that nobody intends to reverse it," which quietly turns a claim about the body into a claim about paperwork. Two people whose hearts stop for the same two minutes could come out differently, one dead and one not, purely depending on whether a do-not-resuscitate form was already on file, and whether someone is dead shouldn't hinge on that.

(4) My aunt pointed out that the brain-based half of that same law already works this way, and my cousin wasn't objecting to that half. Someone declared dead by neurological criteria can be kept on a ventilator with a beating heart, and she'd read that some such bodies have been sustained this way for years, even growing and going through puberty. Nobody takes this to mean brain death isn't real death; everyone just says a machine is propping up other organs after the thing that mattered was permanently gone. So there's already an accepted gap between "some tissue is still running with artificial help" and "the person is still alive" — and he can't invoke that gap against the circulatory standard while resting on it for the neurological one.

(5) My cousin said the two cases aren't as parallel as she needed them to be. A correctly done neurological determination is supposed to certify that a specific organ has been destroyed, a fact about tissue that doesn't depend on anyone's decisions, whereas a stopped heart is a process that has, on the face of it, merely paused — exactly the kind of thing that can often be restarted. Treating that pause as "irreversible" before anyone has tried to restart it is a weaker, far more decision-dependent move than calling destroyed brain tissue irreversibly nonfunctional. And if anything, he added, the very survival cases she'd cited suggest the "destroyed organ" story is shakier than doctors like to admit, since those bodies kept on doing things a brain was supposedly needed for.

Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say, for each, whether the point being made actually holds up, rather than just calling an overall winner? I'd also like to know if there's something both of them are missing, and to hear your own take on the two bigger questions underneath this: whether "permanent" is really an adequate stand-in for "irreversible" in the way my aunt claims, and whether those long-survival cases end up being a problem for the neurological standard too, and not only for the circulatory one.
