I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I went down a research rabbit hole after watching Oppenheimer and got curious about the strongest historical objections to the decision to actually use the bomb on cities, rather than the generic tit-for-tat about whether it was worth it that I kept running into online. That's how I came across G.E.M. Anscombe's pamphlet "Mr. Truman's Degree," written to protest Oxford's 1956 decision to award Harry Truman an honorary degree. What surprised me is that her argument isn't the pacifist point I expected going in: she says explicitly that she has no quarrel with war as such, or with killing enemy combatants. Her claim is narrower and harder for me to dismiss: "choosing to kill the innocent as a means to your ends is always murder," full stop -- and this verdict, she insists, doesn't even depend on whether the bombings saved more lives overall than a land invasion would have cost. I mentioned this to a friend who did a philosophy minor, and she said the obvious countermove is the doctrine of double effect: argue that the civilian deaths were a foreseen but unintended side effect of hitting legitimate military and industrial targets, the way collateral damage is usually defended, rather than a means to anything. But she also thought that move is unusually hard to make stick for these particular bombings, for reasons I couldn't fully follow.

I'd like you to help me see the disagreement clearly by writing a dialogue between two philosophers sharing a cab home after a just-war-theory workshop, one defending Anscombe's verdict and the other trying to rescue the bombings using double effect rather than a bare lives-saved calculation, which Anscombe would just wave away as beside the point. Please give the double-effect defender the strongest, most honest version of that case you can, rather than a caricature who's obviously just excusing atrocity, and have the Anscombean press specifically on whether the killing of civilians functioned as the means of shocking Japan's government into surrender or merely as a foreseen side effect of destroying military targets, since I gather that's where the real disagreement actually lives. I'd like them to genuinely respond to each other's points rather than trade prepared speeches, and I don't need a tidy resolution at the end -- an honest impasse, or at least a sharper picture of exactly where they part ways, would suit me better than a false handshake. Something in the neighborhood of 700-900 words would be ideal.
