"Lewis's target is what he calls the 'debt of punishment' picture. He points out that this is a bad metaphor: with an actual debt, all that matters is that the creditor doesn't end up out of pocket, so it makes no difference who pays it. But when we say a criminal 'owes' a debt of punishment there is no creditor who stands to lose something if the debt isn't paid -- what's wanted is that the wrongdoer himself suffer. So on the usual retributive, reformative, or deterrent stories about why we punish, having some innocent stand-in suffer instead does nothing: nobody benefits, and the guilty party still hasn't been dealt with. This is why Lewis thinks a court would just laugh off a burglar's friend offering to serve his prison term.

Lewis argues that Christians who accept a penal substitution theory of the Atonement -- that Christ was punished so we don't have to be -- are being inconsistent, since they don't carry the principle over into ordinary criminal law: nobody thinks a murderer's devoted friend should be allowed to take the electric chair in his place. But Lewis's real target is broader than the Christians: he thinks all of us, gods or no gods, show the same inconsistency, since while we reject substitution for prison and death sentences, we're perfectly comfortable letting a friend pay someone else's fine. He considers whether this is just because it's impossible to stop people quietly gifting each other money to cover fines, but decides that doesn't let us off the hook, since if we were really opposed to substitution on principle, that impracticality would show up as a standing complaint against fines as a form of punishment -- and it doesn't.

Lewis brings in Swinburne's penal substitution theory to handle the obvious size mismatch between a few hours on a cross and an eternity of damnation: on this view Christ doesn't pay the whole debt, just enough to count as the penance a sinner would owe anyway as part of genuinely repenting, and the rest is simply forgiven once the sinner repents. Having laid all this out, Lewis's conclusion is fairly deflationary: penal substitution turns out to be a piece of confused, leftover medieval legal thinking, and the theological doctrine built on it doesn't hold up any better than the burglar's-friend case did."
