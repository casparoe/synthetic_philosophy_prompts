I am in the middle of writing a campus novel and have reached a scene I keep putting off, because I would rather get it right than fake it. My two leads, Elena and Marcus, were inseparable as graduate students in mathematics and have spent the two decades since drifting into a warm but entirely genuine rivalry over the foundations of their subject. Elena works in classical analysis and has no patience for what she considers unnecessary self-denial: to her, mathematical objects are simply there, discovered rather than built, and a proof by cases that never tells you which case actually holds is still a proof. Marcus wrote his doctorate under a constructivist and has spent his career since building analysis on a diet that avoids the law of excluded middle wherever possible; he thinks a proof of existence has to be capable, at least in principle, of producing the thing in question, and that showing the alternative is contradictory is a different and lesser achievement. The two of them end up the last people at a colloquium dinner, snowed in, and — not for the first time — pick the argument back up, starting from the old case-split proof that there exist irrational numbers a and b with a^b rational (the one that runs through √2^√2).

I audited a philosophy of mathematics seminar while researching this book, so I can tell when an argument has been smoothed over for effect, and I would rather you not do that. Could you write their exchange as a proper back-and-forth — each of them getting several turns, actually answering what the other just said, with neither playing the reasonable one who is obviously right? Elena needs a real comeback when Marcus objects that the classic proof never tells you which pair (a, b) actually works, and Marcus needs a real comeback when Elena points out that we now know, via the Gelfond–Schneider theorem, exactly which case holds. If it fits naturally, I would also like the exchange to settle something I have never been sure I understand correctly myself: whether a constructivist actually thinks the law of excluded middle is false, or holds some stranger and more specific position than that. Plain dialogue is fine — the scene doesn't need to resolve, since these two will have this argument again next winter — but each of them should land at least one point the other cannot simply shrug off.
