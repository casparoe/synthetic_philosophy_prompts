Afterward two coworkers, Leah and Omar, got into it about whether the idea actually holds up, and it's been bugging me since because I genuinely can't tell who's winning.

Leah's opening point (1): the whole trick is that the judge doesn't need to verify or even generate a hard argument themselves, they just need to recognize one clearly-pointed-out flaw, and since the honest debater can always point at exactly where a liar's argument breaks, lying should be a loosing strategy once you're at equilibrium. Omar's reply (2): that only works if flaws are always cheap to locate, and the "obfuscated arguments" problem shows you can build a big, individually-plausible-looking argument where the actual mistake is smeared across a ton of correlated sub-claims instead of sitting at one node, so the honest side can only wave at "something in here is wrong" without ever handing the judge the one crisp rebuttal they need -- meanwhile the liar just keeps growing the tree faster than anyone can audit it. Leah's comeback (3): that's exactly what cross-examination and forced decomposition are for, you're not allowed to leave a claim un-decomposed, everything eventually has to bottom out in something small enough to check directly, it's basically the same move that lets interactive proof systems verify stuff a lone poly-time checker never could on its own. Omar's last word (4): fine in theory, but that assumes the decomposition step itself can't be gamed, and in the actual experiments where people ran debates in front of real human judges, the judges got talked into the wrong side by confident, complicated-sounding arguments often enough that "the structure guarantees truth wins" looks more like a hope than a demonstrated result -- so at best debate beats not-having-debate, which is a much weaker claim than "solves scalable oversight."
