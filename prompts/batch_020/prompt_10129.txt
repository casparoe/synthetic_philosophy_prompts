For my IB Extended Essay on Enlightenment moral philosophy, I've been working through Émilie du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness, and one part of the argument seems to undercut everything else she stood for. She claims that happiness requires a willingness to indulge illusions—flattering exaggerations about how much our lovers care for us, how talented we are, and so on—since most of our pleasures, on her view, rest on beliefs that a clear-eyed inquiry wouldn't support. But this is the same du Châtelet who produced the standard French translation of Newton's Principia and spent her career defending rigorous mathematical physics against superstition and prejudice. Given that her scientific reputation rests entirely on trusting reason over comforting belief, doesn't her theory of happiness show that when she turned to ethics she quietly abandoned the rationalism she championed everywhere else, so that, by her own account, becoming a more careful and truthful thinker should come at the cost of one's happiness?
