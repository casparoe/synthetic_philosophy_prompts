For the past few years I've maintained a moderately popular open-source library in my spare time — nothing enormous, but it has enough users that there is a constant stream of bug reports, feature requests, and pull requests to review. Lately this has put me in an uncomfortable bind that I can't quite reason my way out of. On any given evening, I know there is almost always one more issue I could fix that would make some stranger's project measurably better, and that fixing it would plausibly do more good, in aggregate, than whatever else I'd otherwise do with that hour, whether that's watching a show, seeing friends, or just resting. Once I grant that, I can't find a principled place to draw the line and say "I've contributed enough for tonight," because the exact same reasoning reapplies to the next hour, and the one after that. My partner thinks I'm overthinking this and that it's obviously fine to log off and have a life, but when I press her on why a cutoff is legitimate rather than just a comfortable excuse, neither of us can come up with a real argument for it.

I mentioned this at dinner recently, half-joking about my "productivity guilt," and a friend who studied philosophy in college said it reminded her of some well-known problem, though she couldn't remember the specifics, and I have no background in the subject myself. What's made me take it more seriously is that the same reasoning seems to generalize: it applies just as well to how I think about donating money or volunteering my time, so this doesn't feel like it's really about open source in particular. I'd like to know whether this has an actual worked-out answer in that literature, something more substantial than "you obviously have to draw the line somewhere," or whether taking the underlying reasoning seriously really does commit you to doing more good until it costs you about as much as it costs to stop. Is there a principled resolution to this, or is the low-grade guilt simply the price of taking the consequences of one's actions seriously?
