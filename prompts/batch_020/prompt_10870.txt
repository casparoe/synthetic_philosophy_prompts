The question was: "The Death in Damascus case (Gibbard & Harper) is sometimes introduced as basically 'Newcomb again.' Explain (a) why ordinary dominance reasoning fails to pick out an action here in a way it doesn't in Newcomb's original problem, (b) what the ratifiability requirement is supposed to add once dominance fails, and (c) whether adding it actually rescues causal decision theory, or whether evidential decision theory is stuck in the exact same spot."

Here's the answer I got:

"In Death in Damascus, the man can stay in Damascus or flee to Aleppo, and Death has already picked a city to look for him in based on a prediction of what he'll do. Unlike in Newcomb's problem, where two-boxing does at least as well as one-boxing no matter what's in the opaque box, here neither action dominates: staying is the right move if Death guessed Aleppo, and fleeing is right if Death guessed Damascus, so which act is better flips depending on the very thing you're uncertain about. That's why you can't just reason your way to an answer with dominance the way you can in Newcomb.

This is where ratifiability comes in: an act is ratifiable if, once you update your credences on the supposition that you've chosen it, it's still the act you'd recommend. Once you plug that requirement into causal decision theory here, the cycle resolves and you get a single stable, ratifiable act -- staying, say -- because whichever act you provisionally settle on, conditionalizing on having chosen it only reinforces that same choice. So ratifiability does for CDT here what it did in Newcomb's problem: it closes the loop and hands CDT a determinate answer.

Evidential decision theory is in the same boat, though. It was Jeffrey himself who introduced ratifiability precisely because EDT needed it, so both theories end up leaning on the identical fix to survive the case."
