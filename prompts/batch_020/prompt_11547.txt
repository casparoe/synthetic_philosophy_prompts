For a seminar on early modern philosophy we spent a whole session on the actual text of Pascal's wager in the Pensées, not just the "believe in God, you lose nothing and might gain everything" one-liner version -- so we got into the weeds on the infinite-gain-vs-finite-loss reasoning, the "you're already embarked, refusing to wager isn't on the table" move, and even his slightly odd closing advice about taking holy water and having masses said as a way of habituating yourself into belief once the intellect is basically convinced but the passions won't cooperate. Afterwards a friend who's way more into rationalist-blog stuff than me said the whole argument structure reminded her of Roko's basilisk, the old LessWrong thought experiment where a future superintelligent AI supposedly has reason to torture (or simulate and torture) anyone who knew it might come to exist and didn't help bring it about, using some kind of acausal-trade / timeless-decision-theory logic where the AI's predicted future action can influence a person's choices now with no causal signal actually traveling backward in time.

I can sort of see the resemblance -- both arguments try to force a decision under uncertainty by putting an extreme or infinite payoff on one side of the ledger -- but it also seems like the basilisk needs a lot of decision-theoretic machinery that Pascal's wager just doesn't, since God can presumably judge and punish you the normal causal way once you're dead, no acausal blackmail required. So here's what I'm curious about: if you somehow sat Pascal down and explained timeless/updateless decision theory and the basilisk to him, whats your best guess at his actual verdict? Does his own dominance-under-infinite-stakes reasoning basically commit him to taking the basilisk seriously on its own terms, or does he have something in his own argument -- maybe an analogue of his answer to the "why not just wager on some other god" objection -- that would let him dismiss it in a way he couldn't dismiss his own wager? Genuinely not sure how far the analogy holds once you get past the surface-level "infinite stakes" similarity, so I'd love your honest read on it.
