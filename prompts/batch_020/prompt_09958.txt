Michael Walzer's defense of the moral equality of combatants holds that soldiers on either side of a war are equally permitted to kill enemy combatants and equally entitled to combatant privileges such as prisoner-of-war status if captured, since responsibility for a war's justice belongs to political leaders rather than to the soldiers who are ordered to fight it. Jeff McMahan's revisionist critique denies this: on his responsibility-based account of liability to defensive harm, an unjust combatant who poses a threat in the service of an unjust cause is typically liable to be killed, whereas a just combatant who neither initiates the conflict nor exceeds the bounds of legitimate defense is not liable at all — so the two sides are not morally symmetric, whatever the laws of war say about how they should be treated. I keep running into this disagreement in a seminar on the ethics of war, and I'd like to see the strongest versions of each side argued out against each other directly, rather than summarized in parallel the way they usually appear in survey articles.

Please write a dialogue between a traditionalist who defends the moral equality of combatants and a revisionist who denies it, framed around a single case: a conscripted soldier who privately doubts the justice of the war he has been sent to fight, but has no realistic way to investigate the matter or refuse service, kills an enemy soldier defending his own country against the invasion. Have the traditionalist press the strongest version of the epistemic argument — that ordinary combatants are in no position to reliably determine which side's cause is just, and that holding them to that standard is itself unfair — together with some reciprocity- or convention-based rationale for preserving equal combatant status even if the underlying morality is asymmetric. Have the revisionist grant the epistemic point but insist on the distinction between an excuse and a justification, explaining why non-culpable ignorance might make the conscript less blameworthy without making him any less liable to defensive force. Each side should engage the other's actual objections rather than simply restate its own position, and the dialogue need not resolve neatly — an honest standoff would be a fair reflection of where the debate actually stands.
