For the panpsychism unit in my philosophy of mind seminar, I have become stuck on what the literature calls the combination problem. William James raised an early version of this worry back in 1890, arguing that no matter how many individual "feelings" one piles together, their sum is never itself a further feeling: each stays sealed off from the others, so a hundred small experiences do not thereby add up to one larger experience that has them all as parts. Contemporary panpsychists such as Philip Goff have sharpened this into what he calls the "subject-summing problem": if even fundamental particles have some rudimentary form of experience, what could possibly make a vast number of these micro-subjects combine into a single, unified macro-subject, such as a human mind, rather than simply leaving all of those micro-subjects sitting side by side, each having its own tiny experience, with none of them adding up to a single experience that is mine? There is also the related puzzle of why, if such combination happens at all, it seems to happen only for some aggregates (the neurons inside a skull) and not for others (you and the chair you are sitting on). Rather than writing another explanatory paragraph about this for my notes, I would like to see the puzzle dramatized. Could you write a short story, under roughly 1000 words, that puts a character in a situation forcing them to confront the subject-summing problem directly, ideally one that leaves the reader with the same genuine uncertainty about what has happened to consciousness that the philosophical puzzle itself produces, rather than resolving it neatly in either direction? Please avoid leaning on the standard "hive mind" or "robot wakes up" tropes.
