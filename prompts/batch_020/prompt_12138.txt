The instructor's great about letting us non-credit "senior auditors" participate properly, which this week meant she handed a few of us actual student papers to grade (anonymized) as practice, just so we get a feel for it. My assigned one was on Einstein's hole argument and what its supposed to show about whether spacetime points are real, individual things in their own right (what the philosophers call substantivalism). Thing is, I know the physics of diffeomorphisms and field equations cold, but the philosophical dialectic is still new to me, and I definately want to get the grading right rather than just wing it, so before I scrawl a mark on this kid's paper I wanted an independent, more expert read.

Here's the question as given: "Explain how the hole argument is supposed to threaten determinism in general relativity for someone who accepts manifold substantivalism. Be sure to say what a diffeomorphism is doing in the argument, and describe at least one way a substantivalist might try to resist the argument's conclusion."

And here's the student's answer, in full: "Substantivalists think spacetime points exist as individuals in their own right, independently of whatever fields (like the metric) happen to be defined on them. Take any solution of Einstein's equations, a manifold M with metric g and matter fields T, and pick an open 'hole' region H where T is zero. Now construct a diffeomorphism d of M that's the identity everywhere outside H but smoothly different from the identity inside H, and drag the metric along with it to get a new field g* = d*g. Because the field equations are generally covariant, g* solves them just as well as g does, and since d is the identity outside H, g and g* agree on everything outside the hole and only disagree inside it. For the substantivalist, since g and g* assign different metric values to the very same individual points inside H, (M,g,T) and (M,g*,T) count as two distinct physical possibilities - yet they're built from identical data everywhere outside the hole. So the same complete history outside H fails to fix a unique metric inside it, and determinism breaks down, seemingly everywhere, since you can carve out a hole in any empty region of any model. Earman and Norton's own way of blocking this is to say g and g* don't actually represent different possibilities at all - they're just two descriptions of one and the same physical situation, a policy called Leibniz equivalence. But that's just the relationalist idea that only the relations encoded in the fields matter, not the bare identity of the points underneath, so Earman and Norton's verdict is basically that the hole argument refutes substantivalism outright and we should all be relationalists about spacetime instead."
