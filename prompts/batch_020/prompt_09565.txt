I've been reading about experimental philosophy for an independent project, and I've become especially interested in Joshua Knobe's well-known "chairman" case, in which a company chairman is told that a new policy will increase profits but also harm the environment as a side effect. He says he doesn't care about the environment either way and approves the policy purely for the profit, and when people are asked whether he intentionally harmed the environment, almost everyone says yes. But when the story is changed so that the side effect is helping the environment instead, with the chairman voicing exactly the same indifference, most people say he did not intentionally help it. Later studies even found the same asymmetry for other words describing mental states, such as "decided" or "was in favor of," not just "intentionally."

I would like to know what David Hume would have predicted about this result, had he somehow been presented with it, given his sentimentalist theory of moral judgment. Would that theory lead him to expect precisely this kind of asymmetry, or would he actually expect the two versions of the case to be judged the same way, since the chairman's underlying attitude of indifference is identical either way? And is there some specific piece of Humean psychology, beyond the general idea that reason is subordinate to the passions, that he could appeal to in order to explain the asymmetry if his theory didn't predict it automatically?
