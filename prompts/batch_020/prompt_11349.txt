I spent thirty-five years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist before retiring, and for the last couple of years I have been filling my retirement with philosophy, mostly self-taught and, I confess, mostly via AI-safety writing rather than any proper syllabus. The idea that has hooked me hardest is "embedded agency," as set out in Scott Garrabrant and Abram Demski's sequence for MIRI: the observation that the standard picture of a rational agent — a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer sitting outside its environment, interacting with it through a clean sensory channel and a clean motor channel — quietly assumes a Cartesian boundary that no physically realized agent actually has. Once the agent is just one more process embedded in the very universe it is trying to model, one runs headlong into problems like the grain-of-truth difficulty that arises once agents must model other agents who are modeling them back, the so-called "5-and-10" problem for proof-based agents, and Löb's theorem standing in the way of an agent ever fully trusting a smarter or future version of itself. I have read enough to be unsettled by this, but not enough to know whether it names a genuinely new difficulty for the theory of rational agency or is just old-fashioned bounded rationality wearing a modal-logic costume.

What would help me more than another exposition is a dialogue that stages the disagreement properly, since I find I can steelman only one side of it at a time before the other quietly turns into a strawman in my head. One character should hold, and defend under real pressure, the view that the idealized "outside-view" agent of classical decision theory remains the right standard of rationality even for embedded agents: that the grain-of-truth failure, the 5-and-10 problem, and the Löbian obstacle to self-trust are all soluble in principle — whether by reflective oracles, logical induction, or some successor formalism not yet invented — and that none of this discredits the idealization itself. The other should hold that these are not patchable engineering glitches but symptoms of the idealization being structurally the wrong picture of what an agent is, so that bolting further machinery onto the same Cartesian scaffolding only relocates the problem rather than resolving it. I would like each of them to engage with the other's specific examples rather than retreat to restating their own framework, so that if one raises Löb's theorem, the other has to say something substantive back instead of changing the subject to logical induction — and I would rather the exchange end with the disagreement sharpened than with either side gracefully conceding.
