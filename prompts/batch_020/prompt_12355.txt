I'm outlining a novel where one character, a lapsed-Catholic defense lawyer, keeps quoting Augustine at his sister to justify why he wont lie to protect their dad in a nasty custody thing. So I went and dug up the actual passage he'd be half-remembering, from Augustine's short treatise "On Lying," the part in section 25 where he counts up his whole taxonomy of different kinds of lies. Now I'm the one who's stuck on it, lol. Here it is:

"For first to be eschewed is that capital lie and far to be fled from, which is done in doctrine of religion; to which lie a man ought by no consideration to be induced. The second, that he should hurt some man unjustly: which is such that it profits no man and hurts some man. The third, which so profits one as to hurt another, but not in corporal defilement. The fourth, that which is done through only lust of lying and deceiving, which is an unmixed lie. The fifth, what is done with desire of pleasing by agreeableness in talk. All these being utterly eschewed and rejected, there follows a sixth sort which at once hurts nobody and helps somebody; as when, if a person's money is to be unjustly taken from him, one who knows where the money is, should say that he does not know, by whomsoever the question be put. The seventh, which hurts none and profits some: except if a judge interrogate: as when, not wishing to betray a man who is sought for to be put to death, one should lie; not only a just and innocent, but also a culprit; because it belongs to Christian discipline neither to despair of any man's amendment, nor to bar the way of repentance against any. Of which two sorts, which are wont to be attended with great controversy, we have sufficiently treated, and have shown what was our judgment; that by taking the consequences, which are honorably and bravely borne, these kinds also should be eschewed by brave and faithful and truthful men and women. The eighth sort of lie is that which hurts no man, and does good in the preserving somebody from corporal defilement, at least that defilement which we have mentioned above."

Ok so he's ranking these 8 kinds worst-to-least-bad, fine, I can follow that much. But numbers 6 and 7 are literally described as hurting nobody and helping somebody, and then in almost the same breath he says even those "should be eschewed" by good people?? Is he saying those two are more like forgivable-but-not-admirable, or is he saying they're still genuine sins and a truly virtuous person should just let the bad thing happen rather than tell even a harmless lie? I need to know which one my guy would sincerely believe so he doesn't come across as contradicting himself mid-scene. Can you walk me through what's actually going on in Augustine's logic here?
