In discussions of the Ellsberg paradox, it's usually enough to note that most people, offered a fifty-fifty urn and an urn of unknown composition, will pay a premium to bet on the known urn no matter which color they are betting on -- a pattern that cannot be reconciled with any single additive probability assignment to red and black in the unknown urn. Less often discussed is Howard Raiffa's 1961 reply to Ellsberg, which pointed out that a subject facing the unknown urn could simply flip a fair coin beforehand to decide whether to bet on red or on black. Since this randomized strategy wins with probability exactly one-half no matter how the unknown urn actually turns out to be composed, its payoff distribution is objectively identical to a bet on the known urn. Raiffa took this to show that the discomfort behind ambiguity aversion is not fundamental, since it can be hedged away by an act of randomization that does nothing to resolve the underlying ambiguity. Proponents of ambiguity-sensitive theories such as Gilboa and Schmeidler's maxmin expected utility have replied that a rational ambiguity-averse agent should indeed strictly prefer the coin-flip strategy to either pure bet on the unknown urn, and that this is not a concession but exactly the pattern their theory predicts: Schmeidler's own uncertainty-aversion axiom, which says that a mixture of two acts one is indifferent between should be weakly preferred to either of them on its own, is built for precisely this kind of case.

Write a dialogue between two decision theorists of equal sophistication -- a committed Bayesian in Raiffa's mold, and a defender of maxmin expected utility in Gilboa and Schmeidler's mold -- who happen to be standing in front of an actual pair of Ellsberg urns and start working through this. Have the Bayesian open with the randomization argument, pressing the claim that the maxmin theorist's own preferences collapse back into ordinary probabilistic reasoning as soon as hedging is put on the table. Have the maxmin theorist resist that diagnosis on its own terms, rather than simply restating the general case for ambiguity aversion. Then push the exchange past the hedging point alone: have the Bayesian raise a dynamic-consistency challenge along the lines of "once the coin has landed, but before the ball is drawn, wouldn't you, reconsidering at that very moment, want to unwind the whole arrangement, since you are now just holding one of the two pure bets you found unattractive to begin with?" -- and have the maxmin theorist give the best reply available to their view. The dialogue can run as long as it needs to in order to do all of this properly, but each turn should respond to the specific point just made rather than to a generic version of the other side's position, and neither character should concede more than their actual view requires.
