Ok this is a bit of a rabbit hole but here goes. Everyone in the rationalist-sphere has basically internalized the Aumann's-agreement-theorem story that *common knowledge* (I know P, you know P, I know you know P, you know that I know that you know P... all the way up) is what actually licenses coordination and honest disagreement-resolution, way more than each of us just happening to know a lot about the other's beliefs. But then theres Rubinstein's old "email game" paper and it kind of wrecks that story. Roughly: two players in a coordination game where matching on the risky move pays off big but mismatching is a disaster, one player learns the state and their computers auto-exchange confirmation emails, except each email has some tiny chance of getting lost, so nobody's ever fully sure the other side got the last one. Rubinstein's result is that no matter how many hundreds of confirmations go through, the only equilibrium is exactly as if zero emails were sent — full common knowledge unlocks coordination, but any amount of merely "almost" common knowledge, however close, unlocks none of it. I vaguely recall there's a follow-up move (something like "common p-belief"?) meant to patch this, but I don't remember if it actually restores continuity or just relocates the cliff.

So, essay time: is the strict, infinite-tower notion of common knowledge actually doing real explanatory/normative work in how rational agents coordinate and settle disagreements, such that the email game is correctly telling us real-world coordination is way more fragile than it looks — or is the whole apparatus basically a mathematical artifact of Rubinstein's specific payoff structure, with no real bearing on how bounded, noisy agents obviously do manage to coordinate constantly? Give me an actual answer, not "it's complicated," and defend it.
