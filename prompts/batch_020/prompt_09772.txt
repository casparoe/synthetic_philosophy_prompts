So I had my yearly physical a few weeks back and my cholesterol came back kind of borderline, and my doctor ran one of those "10-year risk of heart attack or stroke" calculators on me and got 9%, which put me juuust over her cutoff for recommending a statin. Being the anxious person I am (ask my partner, he's sick of hearing about this by now), I went home and plugged the same numbers into a different, supposedly more refined calculator I found online, one that also factors in family history and an inflammation marker, and it spat out 4% — a totally different risk category. When I brought it up at my follow-up, my doctor just kind of shrugged and said something like "these tools are built off population averages, they can't tell you exactly what's going to happen to you," which, ok sure, but then I genuinely don't know what either number is supposed to mean for me personally.

The more I chew on it, the weirder it gets. Any calculator has to lump me into some group of "people relevantly like me" and use how that group fared as a stand-in for my odds, but there's no natural stopping point for defining that group: people my age; people my age and cholesterol; people my age, cholesterol, zip code, and diet; and so on. Every time you add or drop a factor, the number moves, sometimes a lot, like mine did. I assumed narrower and more specific must automatically be better, until a friend who works in public health pointed out that once you slice the population that thin, you often don't have enough data left for the resulting number to mean much of anything either, which honestly just made me more confused instead of less. It feels like there should be one true fact about how risky my particular arteries are, but every way of estimating it depends entirely on which bucket of "similar people" someone chose to compare me against, and noone can tell me which bucket is actually the right one, just that some feel more refined than others.

Is there an actual principled way to pick the correct comparison group when you're trying to get odds for one specific person, or is any percentage a doctor hands you basically an educated guess wearing a lab coat? I keep going back and forth between feeling like I'm missing something obvious and suspecting that the whole idea of putting one clean number on "my" personal risk might be kind of incoherent from the start. Would love an actual answer instead of just quietly panicking about which number to trust.
