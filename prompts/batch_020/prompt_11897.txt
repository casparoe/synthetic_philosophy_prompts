I don't have any background in philosophy, but ever since it happened, I've been turning over a disagreement that broke out between two friends of mine. Afterward, I went down a bit of a rabbit hole reading about theories of knowledge on my own, since it seems to touch on a real debate that people who study this for a living actually have. The background is this: our friend Theo was in the final stretch of buying a house and needed a signed form couriered to the title company before closing. The courier's own website simply said "standard delivery: three business days," and Theo had four business days to spare, so he skipped the forty-dollar rush option and told us, "I know this will get there in time." It did arrive on time and the sale went through without a hitch, but when Theo told the story afterward, two other friends of mine, Naomi and Ben, ended up arguing about it for the better part of an hour, and I still haven't been able to work out who was right.

Naomi's opening claim was that Theo hadn't actually known the form would arrive in time, however things turned out: with a twenty-thousand-dollar deposit on the line, "packages like this usually arrive in three days" isn't good enough to count as knowledge, even though the exact same evidence would obviously amount to knowledge if he'd said it about a paperback he'd ordered for fun. Ben pushed back that this confuses how bad it would be to be wrong with how likely he was to be wrong: Theo's actual evidence about the courier was identical either way, and if raising the cost of error were enough to erase knowledge, we'd have to say surgeons never know an operation is safe, since a rare death is always possible. Naomi answered that none of us are that literal-minded in practice -- we're all more reluctant to say "I know the parachute will open" than "I know the vending machine will work," even holding the odds fixed, which suggests the bar for knowing really does rise with what's at stake. Ben's closing move was to ask Naomi whether she'd judge Theo's claim the same way if he were telling us this story in real time, before anyone knew how it would turn out, as she does now, hearing it comfortably after the fact with nothing left riding on it for any of us -- because if her verdict would shift between those two tellings, then what's doing the work is our situation as the audience, not Theo's situation as the one who made the call. Naomi admitted she wasn't sure, and neither am I.

Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who has the stronger argument at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'm particularly stuck on Ben's closing question -- it feels like a fair challenge, but I can't tell whether it actually undermines Naomi's position, or whether there's a good answer to it that neither of them found. And if there's a more basic distinction the two of them are talking past each other on, I'd like to understand what it is.
