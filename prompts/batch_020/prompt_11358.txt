In a seminar on early modern rationalism, I've been working through Leibniz's Discourse on Metaphysics, his correspondence with Arnauld, and parts of the Theodicy, and I keep getting stuck on a tension that seems to sit at the very center of his moral philosophy rather than being a peripheral puzzle about his metaphysics.

Leibniz holds that the complete concept of any individual substance contains, from eternity, every predicate that will ever truly be said of it, so that a full grasp of the concept "Julius Caesar" would already reveal that he crosses the Rubicon. He also holds that God, being supremely wise, good, and powerful, is bound by the principle of sufficient reason to actualize the best of all possible worlds and could have had no sufficient reason to choose otherwise. Yet he insists, against the obvious threat this poses to genuine freedom, that human beings remain morally responsible: that we truly deserve praise, blame, reward, and punishment for actions we could have done otherwise than perform. To hold this together, Leibniz distinguishes absolute or metaphysical necessity from mere moral or hypothetical necessity, maintains that reasons and motives "incline without necessitating" the will, and argues that contingent truths, unlike necessary ones, can only be resolved into identities through an infinite analysis that no finite mind, only God's, could complete. Critics going back to Bertrand Russell's early logical study of Leibniz have argued that these distinctions are little more than ad hoc terminological patches over a system whose own logical and theological commitments make it thoroughly necessitarian, leaving the moral apparatus of sin, merit, and just deserts built on top of it without any real foundation. Others have argued that Leibniz's distinctions are principled and sufficient to secure a coherent, if unusual, form of freedom and responsibility.

Write an essay that assesses whether Leibniz's reconciliation of his necessitarian-leaning metaphysics with his ethics of freedom, responsibility, and justice actually succeeds. Engage with the strongest form of the objection that his system collapses into necessitarianism as well as the best available defenses of his distinctions, and close with a clearly stated verdict: for example, that the reconciliation works on Leibniz's own terms, that it fails and his ethics would need a different (say, straightforwardly compatibilist) grounding to survive, or some other precisely formulated position, rather than leaving the question open.
