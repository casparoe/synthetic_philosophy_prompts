Ok, this is going to sound like a strangely specific problem, but here goes. I'm a philosophy PhD student working on early modern philosophy of mind, and my current dissertation chapter is on Descartes' bête-machine doctrine -- the Discourse V material about language and the "universal instrument" of reason as the two tests for a real mind, plus the follow-up letters to the Marquess of Newcastle (1646) and to Henry More (1649) where he gets more specific about animal passions and sensation. My family dog Juno died this past weekend after almost fifteen years, and I've been asked to say a few words at a little memorial we're having in the backyard this Saturday, mostly for my mom and my younger sister, who are both wrecked.

Here's my actual problem, which I recognize is a very grad-student problem to have. I've spent three years telling anyone who'll listen, including at every family dinner, that Descartes' position is more careful than the "animals are just robots that feel nothing" version people give him credit for -- he's explicit with More that he isn't denying animals "life" or even "sensation insofar as it depends on a bodily organ," only "thought" in the technical sense that's tied to having an immaterial soul. Which almost sounds generous, except that on basically every serious reading (Cottingham's famous 1978 paper not withstanding) that still adds up to there being no subject there, nothing it was like to be Juno, not even when she was whimpering at the end. Nobody wants a lecture on seventeenth-century animal automatism at a dog's memorial, and I don't blame them. But I also can't get up and say the standard comforting things -- she knew how loved she was, she's at peace now -- without it being obvious to my sister, at least, who has had to hear about this dissertation way too many times, that I'm quietly contradicting my own chapter just to make everyone feel better.

Could you actually write the eulogy for me? Something like 350-450 words, meant to be read aloud to a backyard full of non-philosophers, that's genuinely warm and doesn't sound anything like a seminar, but also doesn't require me to assert anything about Juno's inner life that a careful Cartesian -- which, fine, is basically what I am -- would have to reject. Bonus points if nobody notices that's the constraint you were writing under.
