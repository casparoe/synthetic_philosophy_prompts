I've been mulling over a disagreement that keeps resurfacing whenever calibration training comes up at my local rationalist meetup: when someone reports a credence like "somewhere between 0.3 and 0.7, and I refuse to be more precise than that," is this a legitimate description of a rational doxastic state, or just a refusal to do the Bayesian's job properly? Rereading Adam Elga's "Subjective Probabilities Should Be Sharp" pushed me further into the literature: Elga runs a variant of the diachronic Dutch book argument to claim that any agent who can be walked through a suitable sequence of decisions toward a guaranteed loss must, on pain of irrationality, resolve their attitudes into a single sharp probability function. On the other side there is the dilation literature descending from Seidenfeld and Wasserman, which shows that conditioning a credal set on new evidence can make it systematically less precise rather than more -- usually cited as proof that sharpening throws away something real about the agent's evidential situation.

I'd like a dialogue between two people stuck at exactly this impasse. One is a committed "sharpener" who holds, in Elga's spirit, that rational credence must ultimately be represented by a single probability function, however murky or reluctant his introspective report of it might be, and who treats invocations of "genuine," Knightian, or irreducible uncertainty as a confusion between facts about the evidence and facts about the attitude he takes toward it. The other represents her uncertainty as a set of probability functions and is prepared to defend some decision rule for acting on that set -- whether Levi's E-admissibility, Walley's maximality, or Gamma-maximin -- against the charge that any such rule either collapses into a disguised sharp probability or licenses a choice that looks dominated once you step outside the set. Please have them work through a specific case together, such as updating on some piece of genuinely ambiguous evidence, rather than trading slogans: the sharpener should have to answer the dilation objection with something better than "so much the worse for dilation," and the imprecise-credence advocate should have to answer the sequential-decision argument with something better than "so much the worse for sequential decisions." Neither side should simply concede, and I'd like the exchange to leave open whether what's really at stake is a question about rationality itself or a prior, unresolved question about what credences are even supposed to be for.
