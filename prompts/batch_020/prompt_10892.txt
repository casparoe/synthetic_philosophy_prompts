Over the last year or so I've fallen into the habit of reading about decision theory in my spare time, mostly through blog posts and old papers I track down online, even though I never studied philosophy or mathematics formally. I started with Newcomb's problem, and that eventually led me to a stranger relative of it that I gather Allan Gibbard and William Harper introduced back in 1978, usually called "Death in Damascus." As I understand the setup, a man is told by Death, who is guided by an appointment book fixed in advance and never wrong, that Death is coming for him tomorrow; the man can either stay in Damascus or flee overnight to Aleppo, and he dies if and only if he ends up in whichever city the book names. What strikes me as even more unsettling than Newcomb's problem is that causal decision theory apparently can't settle on a stable recommendation here: whichever city the man firmly commits to seems to become exactly the wrong one the moment he commits to it, since that commitment is exactly what a sufficiently reliable predictor would already have written down. The fix I keep running into is to have the man flip a coin instead of choosing a city outright, on the grounds that a fifty-fifty mixed strategy is the only option that counts as "ratifiable" — stable even once you factor in what choosing it would reveal about where Death already is.

This supposed fix has been nagging at me. It seems to quietly assume that while Death's book is accurate enough to make any settled, deliberate choice self-undermining, it somehow has nothing to say about the outcome of a coin flip, as though chance were the one thing this otherwise infallible predictor cannot get a grip on. I even tracked down the abstract of a paper with a title something like "Dicing with Death," which suggested to me that I'm not the first person to find this suspicious, though I couldn't get past the paywall to see how the argument actually went. If Death's book is genuinely fixed in advance and correct about everything else, I don't see a principled reason why it wouldn't also correctly record which way the coin will land, in which case flipping it seems to buy the man nothing at all. I'd like you to make the strongest case you can for the position that appealing to ratifiable mixed strategies does not really dissolve the instability in Death in Damascus, but merely reproduces it one level up — so that causal decision theorists have been too quick to treat the coin flip as a genuine solution rather than as a restatement of the very problem it was supposed to solve.
