But then we got to the "undermining" problem, which my professor kept calling Lewis's "big bad bug," and I don't think I've actually internalized why it's a real problem for the Principle, as opposed to just being a fancy way of saying "new evidence can change your credences," which is true of literally every theory of probability and doesn't seem like a "bug" at all.

The way I understood it from the reading: on a best-system Humean view, the chance of some event at t is fixed by the pattern of ALL the actual events in history (including stuff that occurs after t), so there can be possible continuations of history so atypical relative to a given chance distribution that a different best system would fit them better and assign different chances all along — i.e. outcomes can "undermine" the very chances that were supposedly in force when they occured. Apparently this makes info about the future inadmissible in a way that's supposed to threaten the Principal Principle itself rather than just being routine conditionalizing, and Lewis's patch was some "New Principle" move that conditions on the whole chance-theory of the world instead of just "the chance at t," to dodge the issue. Honestly I can parrot that back but I don't have a real gut feeling for why undermining is special/threatening rather than mundane, or what (if anything) we actually lose by switching to the New Principle — does it just relabel the problem, does the Humean pay some real cost here that a non-Humean about chance wouldn't?
