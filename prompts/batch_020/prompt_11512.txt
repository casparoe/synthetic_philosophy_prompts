My aunt is retiring this spring after something like 35 years as a clinical psychologist, most of it spent working with dissociative identity disorder patients (what used to just get called multiple personality disorder), and I got roped into giving the toast at her retirement dinner. Because I'm the nephew who did a philosophy MA, everyone's now expecting me to say something "smart" about her work, no pressure. Here's the actual complication: for the back half of her career she's talked, very deliberately, in Ian Hacking's terms about DID being a "looping" or "interactive" kind rather than a plain natural kind -- her view is that the diagnosis, the media attention in the 80s and 90s, and the therapy techniques of that era (recovered-memory work especially) genuinely helped shape the symptom profile that clinicians then wrote back into the diagnostic criteria, which then shaped how the next cohort of patients presented, and so on round the loop. She thinks real damage got done by that cycle. But she is emphatically NOT someone who thinks the disorder is fake or that her patients were performing for an audience -- she'll tell you their fragmentation and distress was completely real, and that getting the looping story right was, for her, about taking that suffering more seriously, not less.

The problem for me is the room: half the guests are old colleagues and a couple of cog-sci/philosophy people who will know exactly what "looping effect" means and will notice immediately if I flatten it into something dumb like "labels create the illness." The other half are family and, weirdly, two or three of her actual long-term patients who asked to come, and if the toast sounds even slightly like its saying their diagnosis was made up or that she caused their symptoms by treating them, that's a disaster, not a joke. Can you write me a warm, only lightly funny, roughly 5-minute toast that actually puts the looping effects idea to real use in describing what her career was about, gets the mechanism right rather than just dropping the buzzword, and still lands as pure affection and respect -- for both halves of the room at once?
