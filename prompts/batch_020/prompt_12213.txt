Over dinner last week, a friend of mine who works on interpretability at an AI lab and I engaged in a long, ultimately unresolved argument about whether it is ever literally correct, rather than merely a convenient shorthand, to say that a large language model believes something. His position was that once one can point to the actual mechanism behind an output — the relevant circuit, attention pattern, or feature direction — the vocabulary of belief and desire becomes decorative, a story superseded in principle by the lower-level account. I pushed back with Dennett's intentional stance, and in particular his "real patterns" argument that a pattern can be real, and an intentional ascription correct in his sense, even when a design-level or physical-level story is also fully available — much as we still treat "gliders" as real, higher-level objects in Conway's Game of Life despite knowing the complete micro-level update rule, and much as neuroscience does not seem to threaten the reality of ordinary human beliefs even where a complete mechanistic story is available in principle. Where we got stuck was on how far this actually helps: neither of us could agree on how Dennett's own hesitations about thin, "as-if" cases like thermostats or simple game-playing programs should bear on systems as large as current language models, and I additionally worried that Davidson's holism about the mental — the idea that a genuine believer's attitudes must hang together under a constitutive presumption of rationality — poses an independent problem, given how readily such models contradict themselves across contexts.

I would like a more rigorous treatment than we managed at the table. Write an essay addressing whether the availability of a complete mechanistic (design-stance or physical-stance) account of a system should, by Dennett's own lights, undermine the legitimacy of intentional-stance belief-ascriptions to that system, or whether the two levels of description are simply compatible; then apply your answer specifically to present-day large language models, taking the Davidsonian holism worry seriously as a possible independent obstacle. Weigh the strongest considerations on each side, but do not leave the matter open — end with a definite, well-justified verdict on whether, and in what sense, it is correct to say that today's language models have beliefs.
