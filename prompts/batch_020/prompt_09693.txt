This term I am teaching a graduate seminar on modal metaphysics, and last week we worked through the standard opening exchange between David Lewis's counterpart theory and the rival view, associated with Kripke's remarks on rigid designation and with Plantinga's serious actualist essentialism, according to which ordinary individuals are literally, numerically identical across possible worlds. That exchange — Kripke's "Humphrey objection" and Lewis's reply via the problem of accidental intrinsics — is well-trodden enough that the class reconstructed it from the readings without much difficulty. For their next assignment I want to set a subtler and less frequently discussed difficulty, one that turns on a structural disanalogy between the two views: strict identity is provably one-to-one (if a is identical to b and a is identical to c, then b just is c), whereas a Lewisian counterpart relation, being a matter of comparative similarity along multiply weighted respects, is not — a single individual can have several equally good, even incompatible, counterparts in one and the same other world, depending on which respects of resemblance happen to be salient. Lewis himself is untroubled by this "inconstancy," treating it as an accurate reflection of how flexibly ordinary modal language already works; critics take it to show that counterpart theory cannot underwrite robust, non-relative facts about what a particular individual could or could not have been.

Before I set this for the class, I would like you to do some of the legwork with me. Please invent an original example — not the lectern, not the table that might have been made of ice, not Humphrey and his election — involving a single ordinary object or person, together with two distinct and independently well-motivated counterpart relations. Specify explicitly which respects of similarity each relation privileges, and why an ordinarily competent speaker might reasonably favor one over the other in different conversational settings. Then show that, relative to the first relation, some de re modal claim about your protagonist comes out true, while relative to the second, its negation comes out true. Once the example is on the table, tell me how much philosophical weight it can bear: does it vindicate the complaint that counterpart theory quietly converts an objective question about a particular individual into a question about which similarity metric a conversation happens to be tracking, or is Lewis right that ordinary de re modal talk was never as determinate as the objection assumes? And, briefly, does the transworld identity theorist genuinely escape the analogous worry, or do they merely relocate it to an equally contestable question of which properties are essential enough to fix an individual's identity across worlds in the first place?
