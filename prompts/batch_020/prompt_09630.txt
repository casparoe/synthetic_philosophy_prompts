My older sister is finishing a philosophy postdoc built around a fairly hardline version of moral error theory: in something close to Mackie's original sense, she holds that ordinary moral claims purport to describe objective, categorically binding facts, that no such facts exist (dinner guests get the argument from queerness whether they ask for it or not), and that almost every ordinary positive moral sentence — "he deserves better," "you were right to forgive her," "they're perfect for each other" — is, strictly speaking, false. She has also published a fairly unforgiving take on moral fictionalism, arguing that carrying on talking as though such claims were true while privately believing otherwise just relocates the dishonesty rather than removing it. None of this has been dented by the fact that she is marrying a committed moral realist, who treats whether the two of them are right for each other as no less a matter of objective fact than anything else in the world; if anything, several years of arguing metaethics with him over breakfast have only sharpened her on the point. She has asked me to give the toast and has told me, half seriously, that she will notice if I say anything from the head table she would have to privately disagree with.

The room will be split roughly evenly between her philosophy department, who will be listening for exactly this problem, and family and childhood friends, who have never heard of error theory and just want a toast that sounds like a toast. I need one that actually solves the constraint rather than dodging it: no bare, unhedged lines like "you deserve this" or "you two were made for each other," since those are precisely the sentences her view rules out; but also no winking fictionalizing and no blanket hedges like "in a sense" or "for what it's worth," since she has explicitly ruled that move out too, and it would read as evasive to the non-philosophers regardless. Please write this toast: about three minutes long, warm and genuinely funny rather than merely careful, with one line placed for the people in the room who know what the argument from queerness is, and a closing toast line she could raise her glass to without wincing.
