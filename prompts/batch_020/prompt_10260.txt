I've been down a rabbit hole on the Axiom of Choice this week after a coworker made an offhand joke during a code review that `set.pop()` in Python only works because of "the axiom of choice" (pretty sure this was a troll, but it got me curious anyway). So I went and read up on it properly, and the thing that's tripping me up is that AC, Zorn's Lemma, and the Well-Ordering Theorem (WOT) are all supposed to be logically equivalent given the rest of ZF. I can sort of accept Zorn's Lemma -- "every chain has an upper bound so there's a maximal element" sounds like a resonable, if non-obvious, structural fact. But WOT says the real numbers can be well-ordered, meaning every subset of R has a least element under this order, and that seems to directly contradict something I already know about the reals: they're dense, so between any two reals there's always a third one, which means no real number has an "immediate successor." But isn't having a successor for basically every element the whole point of a well-order -- doesn't the stuff above any given element also need a least element, which would be its successor? So density seems like it should rule out well-ordering R completely.

Given that, either WOT is just false, or I'm misunderstanding what it's actually claiming -- and if its false, I dont see how it can be "logically equivalent" to Zorn's Lemma, which seems totally fine to me. Is "equivalent" doing some narrower technical job here than the everyday meaning of the word, or is the Well-Ordering Theorem actually the shaky, more controversial claim of the three and people just gloss over that part when they teach this stuff?
