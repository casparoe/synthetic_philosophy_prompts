A close friend from law school is being sworn in as a state trial court judge next month, and she has asked me to give three or four minutes of remarks at the investiture, wedged on the program between a sitting appellate judge and her own mother. Half the room will be bench and bar; the other half will be family and old friends who have never read a page of philosophy. I keep returning to Pascal's fragment on justice and force from the Pensées -- his line that "justice without force is powerless; force without justice is tyrannical," and the colder point he makes in the same breath, that people must be told to obey laws simply because they are laws and not because they are just, in exactly the way we obey a superior not because he is right, but because he is a superior. I've spent enough time on rationalist blogs to be constitutionally suspicious of any story that comforts mainly because it comforts, but this is a swearing-in, not a seminar, and I don't want to stand up on the best professional day of her life and tell her, in effect, that her new authority is just force with good manners. At the same time, I don't think Pascal's point is actually incompatible with real admiration for her -- I suspect it's closer to the opposite. Could you write these remarks? They should be genuinely warm and specific to her, legible to the family half of the room, and honest to what Pascal is actually arguing for the lawyers who will recognize the reference -- not softened into a platitude about "justice prevailing."
