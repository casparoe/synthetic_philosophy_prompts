heres the student's answer in full, verbatim:

"Kavka's toxin puzzle asks you to imagine an eccentric billionaire who will pay you a million dollars tomorrow morning if, by midnight tonight, you form a genuine intention to drink a toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin will make you painfully ill for a day but won't kill you or do any lasting damage, and crucially you're paid just for intending to drink it -- verified by a reliable predictor -- not for actually drinking it. The puzzle is that once midnight has passed and the money is already yours, you have no reason at all to go through with drinking the toxin, since by then it would just be pointless suffering. So a purely instrumentally rational agent, reasoning things through at that later time, wouldn't drink it, which seems to mean you can't really form the intention in the first place, since you'd know in advance that you won't follow through. Kavka thinks this shows that rationality can pull against itself: it's rational to want to be the kind of person who intends to drink it, but somehow irrational, or even impossible, to actually intend it.

This is essentially Newcomb's Problem again with an extra step: in both cases a reliable predictor rewards you for having the right disposition rather than for anything you causally do, and in both cases the reward has already been secured (or not) before the 'real' choice arrives. Gauthier's proposed way out is resolute choice: an agent who resolves at the outset to carry out whatever plan is best when evaluated as a whole, and who then simply doesn't revisit that evaluation later, can sincerely intend to drink the toxin and so collect the money. But if resolute choice is what licenses forming the intention in the toxin case, then by the same reasoning it must be resolute choice, rather than causal decision theory's two-boxing, that is the truly rational response to Newcomb's Problem as well."
