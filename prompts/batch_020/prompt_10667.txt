I'm a PhD student in political philosophy working on early modern theories of political obligation, and I want to press Hobbes's account of covenants against a case his text never anticipated. In Leviathan, Hobbes holds that a covenant where neither party performs immediately is void in the state of nature "upon any reasonable suspicion" that the other will renege — unless a common power exists with the right and force to compel performance, which is why "covenants, without the sword, are but words." Suppose Hobbes were shown a smart contract: an agreement encoded on a blockchain that executes automatically and irreversibly once its conditions are met, with no sovereign or court needed to compel anyone. Taken at face value, his own test seems satisfied without any sword, since reasonable suspicion has been removed by the code itself rather than deterred by fear of punishment. Would Hobbes accept that consequence, or insist that reasonable suspicion hasn't really been eliminated — and if so, where, on his own terms, would he locate the residual sword: in whoever controls the network, in whoever resolves disputes when the code's output diverges from the parties' intentions, or elsewhere? Answer from Hobbes's own premises about trust, power, and the state of nature, not by summarizing the contemporary "code is law" debate.
