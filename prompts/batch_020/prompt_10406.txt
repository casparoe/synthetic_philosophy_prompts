Last week a friend and I argued for a good hour about whether the Church-Turing thesis has actually been proved, and neither of us really won. I took the Kleene line: the thesis can only ever be heuristically supported, since it identifies a precise formal notion (recursiveness, or Turing computability) with an irreducibly informal one (effective calculability), and no formal derivation can bridge that gap. My friend countered with the move made by Gandy and, later, Sieg: replace Turing's own 1936 talk of a human "computer" with explicit axioms of boundedness (on what can be observed at each step and on the number of internal states available) and locality (how one step determines the next), and Turing computability drops out as an honest theorem, so the thesis has already been proved and Kleene's verdict is simply dated. She added that Gödel, who had been unpersuaded by Church's own proposal, found Turing's analysis completely convincing once he saw it, which she took as evidence that something proof-like, not merely heuristic, was going on. I could not counter that combination on the spot. Please make the strongest possible case for her side. In particular, address head-on the objection that the boundedness and locality axioms do not constrain "effective calculability" independently at all, but merely restate Turing computability in other words — making the derivation circular.
