Hey -- could use a sounding board for something I'm prepping for my seminar on practical reason. We're doing a unit on hard choices and incommensurable values, and I want my students to actually get Ruth Chang's argument for "parity" as a fourth basic value relation on a gut level, not just memorize the SEP summary and move on. Here's the setup as I understand it: the "trichotomy thesis" says that whenever two options are compared with respect to some value, exactly one of three relations has to hold -- better, worse, or equally good. Chang's small-improvement argument (which apparently goes back to Raz and others before her) is supposed to break this: take her example of Mozart and Michelangelo's creativity -- neither seems better than the other, but they don't seem equally good either, because if they really were equally good, sweetening Mozart's creativity just a hair should make improved-Mozart come out ahead of Michelangelo, and intuitively it doesn't. So far I can follow it fine.

Where I keep loosing my grip, and where I suspect my students will too, is the next move: why does that failure argue for Chang's weird sui generis "parity" relation rather than just for Raz's own conclusion that the two are incomparable, full stop -- or for the more deflationary line that "equally good" was always a vague, rough predicate that can absorb small improvements without flipping over into "better"? Ive got a colleague down the hall in the law school who thinks the whole "parity" apparatus is just relabeling our uncertainty and adds nothing. Can you walk me through, fairly intuitively and step by step (not just a string of definitions), (1) how exactly the small-improvement argument is supposed to sink the trichotomy thesis, and (2) what explanatory work "parity" is actually doing that mere vagueness, roughness, or plain incomparability can't already do just as well? And if you think my colleague has a real point buried in there, don't be shy about saying so.
