In my seminar on the ethics of war, we've spent the past two weeks on Elizabeth Anscombe's 1956 pamphlet "Mr. Truman's Degree," written to protest Oxford's decision to award Harry Truman an honorary doctorate. Anscombe's central claim is that Truman is guilty of murder for ordering the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki: he intentionally brought about the deaths of tens of thousands of innocent civilians as the very means of forcing Japan's surrender, and on her view no appeal to good consequences, however large, can license the intentional killing of the innocent. This rests on the doctrine of double effect, which she develops further in her essay "War and Murder," and in particular on its distinction between intending a death and merely foreseeing it as a side effect of an otherwise legitimate act of war.

Write an essay evaluating whether Anscombe's argument succeeds against the standard consequentialist defense of the bombings -- that they minimized total lives lost by making a land invasion of Japan unnecessary. Engage seriously with at least one objection to her position: for instance, that the bombings could be redescribed as attacks on military and industrial targets with civilian deaths merely foreseen rather than intended, which would satisfy rather than violate her own double-effect criterion, or that the intending/foreseeing distinction cannot bear this much moral weight once such redescriptions are permitted. Don't just present both sides -- reach and defend a definite verdict on whether Truman's decision counts as murder in Anscombe's sense.
