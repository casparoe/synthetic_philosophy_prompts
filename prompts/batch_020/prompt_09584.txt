As a software engineer, I spend my working days thinking in terms of true, false, and the occasional null, so Nāgārjuna's catuṣkoṭi (the "tetralemma") struck me as a direct challenge to that framework when I first came across it. In the chapters of the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā dealing with the Tathāgata and with nirvāṇa (22 and 25, if I have the numbering right), Nāgārjuna refuses to say that an awakened being, after death, exists, does not exist, both exists and does not exist, or neither exists nor does not exist. Refusing the first two horns already seems to give up on excluded middle; adding the third and fourth on top of that looks like it courts outright contradiction.

I gather serious people disagree about how much weight to put on this. One camp — I recently read a paper I believe was by Jay Garfield and Graham Priest — takes the contradiction at face value and concludes that Nāgārjuna's reasoning, pushed to its limit, requires something like a paraconsistent logic; call this the dialetheist reading. Another camp thinks the appearance of contradiction is really an artifact of running together the conventional and the ultimate truths, and of treating a negation that merely withholds a claim as though it asserted some positive alternative in its place; call this the two-truths reading.

Could you write a dialogue between a philosopher who defends the dialetheist reading and one who defends the two-truths reading, arguing specifically over the nirvāṇa case? Please make them equally sharp, have them engage with each other's strongest objections rather than talk past one another, and end with the two of them locating exactly where their disagreement still stands, even if neither budges.
