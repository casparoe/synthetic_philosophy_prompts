So I went down a rabbit hole a while back reading about "accuracy-first epistemology" — this idea that stuff like probabilism (the rule that your credences should obey the probability axioms) can be derived just from wanting beliefs that are close to the truth, no betting/Dutch book arguments needed. The classic move, it's Jim Joyce's, is that any credence function that breaks the probability axioms is "accuracy-dominated": there's always some probabilistically coherent alternative that's guaranteed to be at least as accurate as it in every possible world, and strictly more accurate in some.

Except that proof only goes through once you restrict attention to measures of inaccuracy that count as "legitimate," which basically cashes out to strictly proper scoring rules (the Brier score, stuff like that). And thats what's been bugging me — who gets to decide which scoring rules count as genuine measures of "closeness to the truth," on grounds that don't already assume probabilism is correct? Feels like strict propriety is suspiciously exactly the condition you'd need to rig in advance to make the coherent credences win, so the whole "non-pragmatic vindication" might just be smuggling the question-begging part one level up, into the choice of scoring rule.

Can you build me the strongest version of this circularity objection to Joyce-style accuracy-dominance arguments for probabilism? I don't want a wishy-washy both-sides answer, i want the best possible case that the argument is secretly question-begging.
