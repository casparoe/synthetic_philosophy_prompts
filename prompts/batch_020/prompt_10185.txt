I'm deep into a novel that follows one family through the outbreak of a revolution, and a craft problem I keep circling back to turns out to have a real philosophical pedigree. When I explain why the uprising breaks out when it does, rather than simply narrating that it did, am I offering a shorthand version of a covering-law explanation—the sort Hempel thought all genuine historical explanation must ultimately rest on, general laws and all, even when the historian never states them outright—or is something else doing the explanatory work, something narrativists like Dray and Danto insist can't be cashed out that way? I understand that Hempel had a term for accounts like mine, ones that gesture at laws without stating them—an "explanation sketch"—meant to preserve the covering-law model even in the messiest singular cases. What I'd like is a sharper sense of what's actually at stake between the two camps: is there any real test for whether a historical explanation is elliptical nomological reasoning in disguise, or is the narrativist claim that no law is hiding there a genuinely different, non-reducible kind of claim about what explanation in history consists in?
