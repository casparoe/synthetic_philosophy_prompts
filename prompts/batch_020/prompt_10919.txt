For my school's philosophy club, we have spent the last few meetings working through paradoxes in epistemology, and this week our faculty advisor distributed a short passage on what she called "the dogmatism paradox," which she attributed to an old lecture of Saul Kripke's that Gilbert Harman later wrote up. As I worked through the argument, it seemed to run as follows: if I genuinely know that some claim P is true, then P really is true, which means that any future evidence that appears to count against P would have to be misleading, since genuine evidence cannot point away from something true. But if I already know in advance that any such evidence would be misleading, it looks as though the rational response is to disregard it the moment it appears, rather than let it unsettle something I already know.

I have actually started applying this reasoning in debate rounds: since I already "know" that my side's position is correct before a round even starts, I have told my partner that we are being logical rather than stubborn when we dismiss the other team's rebuttal evidence without truly engaging with it. A few people in the club pushed back, insisting that something must be wrong with the argument precisely because it is called a paradox rather than a proof, but none of them could identify the flawed step, since each individual move still strikes me as valid. Could you help me locate exactly where this reasoning goes wrong, and tell me whether the dogmatism paradox actually licenses the way I have been treating counter-evidence in debate, or whether I am missing something important about what the paradox is supposed to show?
