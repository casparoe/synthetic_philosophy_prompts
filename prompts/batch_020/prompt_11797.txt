I've seen the give-now-versus-give-later debate rehearsed many times on the EA Forum and various rationalist blogs, but it usually elides the real technical crux: whether the pure rate of time preference in the Ramsey discounting formula can legitimately be positive at all. Write a dialogue between an economist who defends a positive rate of pure time preference in social discounting and a philosopher in the Sidgwick-Ramsey-Parfit tradition who considers any positive rate unprincipled favoritism toward the present. Have the philosopher open with Ramsey's own charge that such discounting is mere "weakness of the imagination," and have the economist resist conceding by offering real rejoinders rather than naked self-interest: that undiscounted utility sums over an unbounded future may fail to converge without some positive rate, and that market interest rates carry genuine normative information. The philosopher should press back with Parfit's analogy between temporal and spatial bias, point out that Cowen and Parfit mounted a related joint critique of the social discount rate, and note that the rates actually used in models like Nordhaus's vastly exceed whatever infinitesimal rate mere mathematical convergence would require. Have them genuinely engage each other's strongest points as equals, in order to settle whether the Stern-versus-Nordhaus dispute over climate policy is really an ethical disagreement about pure time preference, or an empirical disagreement about growth and utility curvature wearing an ethical disguise.
