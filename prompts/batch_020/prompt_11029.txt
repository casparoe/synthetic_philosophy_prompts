Two weeks ago I inherited a slot in my department's decision-theory reading group, and I now have to lead the session on Brian Hedden's paper "Time-Slice Rationality," which none of us had read before. Until then, I had only ever seen money-pump and Dutch-book arguments put to a single, uncontroversial use: showing that an agent with intransitive preferences, or one who refuses to update credences by conditionalization, can be exploited by a clever bookie for a sure loss across a sequence of trades or bets, and that this exploitability is precisely what makes such an agent irrational. Hedden's paper turns the very same cases against that reading. His thesis is that every genuine requirement of rationality is synchronic — it binds a person at a time, full stop — and that there is no further, freestanding requirement binding a person across time. On that view, vulnerability to money pumps and diachronic Dutch books cannot be taken at face value as evidence of some distinctively diachronic failing: either the pattern of exploitation fails to indict any single time-slice's choices or credences (each of which can be perfectly in order relative to that time-slice's own attitudes), or, if some time-slice really is at fault, the fault has to be relocated into an ordinary synchronic failure — say, not following through on a resolution one currently endorses — rather than left as a bare failure of cross-time consistency.

I ran this by my advisor, who is largely won over by Hedden, and separately by a friend in the metaphysics group who works on personal identity and finds the whole maneuver a bit too slick. She thinks resolute choice, and caring about one's own past commitments, are just diachronic requirements wearing a synchronic disguise, and that the view only looks plausible because it quietly helps itself to exactly the resources — foresight, planning, loyalty to past selves — that a temporally extended agent has and a mere time-slice does not. Neither of them was willing to spend real time on the other side's strongest moves, and I would rather not lead the discussion having only pressure-tested one of them.

Could you write a fairly substantial dialogue between a time-slice theorist and a diachronic theorist working through this face to face, rather than past one another? I would like the diachronic theorist to press the hardest cases — an agent who can foresee, at the very first choice node, that the whole sequence of individually tempting trades ends in certain loss, and the sequential decision problems that seem to make some form of resoluteness necessary — and the time-slice theorist to answer each with a real, non-question-begging reply rather than a restatement of the general thesis. Please do not let either side concede more than their own arguments actually force, and if the exchange is genuinely at an impasse in the literature, let the dialogue end there rather than manufacturing a winner.
