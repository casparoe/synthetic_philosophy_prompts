I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and while researching a piece on labs that outlast their founders, I sat in on a reunion dinner for alumni of a well-regarded genomics lab, where two former postdocs, Sonia and Marcus, spent an hour arguing about why the lab had kept producing major papers for nearly two decades after every founding scientist had left.

Sonia argued that the whole run can be explained one person at a time: each cohort learned specific habits -- a particular way of stress-testing results before submission, a fixed ratio of senior to junior mentorship -- from the cohort before it, and tracing every chain of who-trained-whom leaves nothing left over to explain. Marcus objected that this misses a selection effect: the lab quietly pushed out people who didn't fit a risk-tolerant, collaborative style and kept those who did, and that filtering is a property of the system, not of any one mentor's choices. As evidence, he pointed to a sister lab that had copied the same explicit rules -- with several of the same senior scientists rotating through -- and never came close to matching the run.

Sonia replied that "selection pressure" is still shorthand for individual PIs' hiring judgment and individual students' choices to stay or leave, so the failed transplant is explicable the same way, one different person's judgment at a time; complexity isn't irreducibility. She added that a pattern needing its own explanation doesn't make it some further thing with its own causal powers -- flocks of birds produce stable large-scale shapes from simple individual rules, with no "flock culture" required. Marcus countered that this move would save her account from any counterexample whatsoever, and that it's circular besides: the hiring judgment she treats as independent was itself formed by years of immersion in the lab's existing norms, so it can't explain those norms without presupposing them. As for the birds, he said the analogy breaks down exactly here, since a bird's rules don't change depending on the flock's history, while a PI's hiring judgment demonstrably does.

They turned to me to settle it, and I honestly couldn't. Could you go through the exchange move by move, saying who has the better of the argument at each step rather than declaring an overall winner, and flag anything important that neither of them raises?
