I've been sitting in on a graduate seminar on metaontology, and we recently spent two sessions on the question of whether disputes in analytic ontology are substantive or "merely verbal," using the debate over unrestricted mereological composition as the test case. On one side, universalists hold that for any two or more objects whatsoever, however scattered or heterogeneous, there exists a further object that is their sum (so that, say, the fusion of my left shoe and the Eiffel Tower is itself an object); nihilists and restricted compositionalists deny this, at least for suitably gerrymandered cases. Eli Hirsch has argued, via his "quantifier variance" thesis, that this dispute is merely verbal: there is no metaphysically privileged meaning of "there exists," so the universalist and the nihilist are simply using existential quantification with different but equally legitimate meanings, and once this is made explicit there is no further fact about the world they disagree on. Ted Sider has resisted this, arguing in "Ontological Realism" and later in Writing the Book of the World that reality has an objective, joint-carving structure, and that there is a best candidate meaning for the quantifier -- what he calls "ontologese" -- such that at most one of universalism and nihilism, stated in that privileged idiom, is true.

Could you explain, in more depth than the sketch above, how Hirsch's quantifier variance thesis is actually supposed to defuse the composition dispute, and how Sider's appeal to joint-carving/ontologese is supposed to block that deflationary move? I'd also like your own assessment of who has the better of this exchange: does Sider succeed in re-establishing that the dispute is substantive, or is "ontologese" just a stipulation that relocates the problem rather than resolving it? And, applying something like David Chalmers's proposal that a dispute is (broadly) verbal just when it would dissolve into agreement once each side's key terms are suitably disambiguated, would the composition dispute itself -- or, for that matter, the Hirsch/Sider dispute about the composition dispute -- come out as verbal by that standard?
