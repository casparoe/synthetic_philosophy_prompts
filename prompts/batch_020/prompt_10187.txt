A friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about the two-envelope paradox, and we never quite agreed on where the "you should always switch" reasoning actually breaks down. She maintained that the fix is basically Bernoulli's: once you replace the money in the envelopes with utility, the tempting calculation that switching raises your expected payoff by twenty-five percent evaporates, in much the same way that a concave utility function tames the infinite expectation of the St. Petersburg lottery. I maintained that this misdiagnoses the problem, and that the real fault sits further upstream, in a probability distribution over the envelopes' contents that the naive argument quietly assumes but that cannot actually exist. Neither of us budged, so I'd like your help settling the matter a different way. Suppose Daniel Bernoulli, fresh from writing his 1738 paper on the St. Petersburg problem, were handed the classic, bounded two-envelope setup, with no hidden infinities lurking anywhere in it. Working only with the mathematical and probabilistic concepts he actually had on hand in the 1730s, how do you think he would have diagnosed what goes wrong with the switching argument — and would the very utility-curvature move that rescued him in the St. Petersburg case have rescued him here too, or would it have missed the point entirely?
