I spent my working life as a condensed-matter physicist, and only since retiring have I begun reading philosophy in any systematic way. At the moment I am making my way through Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, and I've been stuck for some time on a passage in Book IV, Chapter III, Section 6, where, discussing the limits of what we can know about matter and thought, Locke writes:

"We have the ideas of matter and thinking, but possibly shall never be able to know whether any mere material being thinks or no; it being impossible for us, by the contemplation of our own ideas, without revelation, to discover whether Omnipotency has not given to some systems of matter, fitly disposed, a power to perceive and think, or else joined and fixed to matter, so disposed, a thinking immaterial substance: it being, in respect of our notions, not much more remote from our comprehension to conceive that God can, if he pleases, superadd to matter a faculty of thinking, than that he should superadd to it another substance with a faculty of thinking; since we know not wherein thinking consists, nor to what sort of substances the Almighty has been pleased to give that power."

My first instinct, shaped no doubt by decades spent thinking about collective phenomena such as superconductivity, was to read Locke's talk of God "superadding" a faculty of thinking to suitably disposed matter as some kind of anticipation of emergentism, though I suspect that projects a modern picture onto a passage with a rather different, more theological logic of its own. What I'd value help with is this: is Locke here making only the modest epistemic point that we cannot know a priori whether matter thinks, or the stronger claim that our very inability to conceive how matter could think has no bearing at all on whether it really can? And, either way, what does the passage imply for arguments like Descartes' in the Sixth Meditation, which move directly from what is clearly and distinctly conceivable to conclusions about the real distinctness — and so the real possibility of separate existence — of mind and body?
