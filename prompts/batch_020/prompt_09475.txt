I'm partway through a historical novel set in London and Hanover in 1715 and 1716, and one thread follows a fictional courier drawn into the real dispute between Newton and Leibniz -- a dispute that, as it happens, never actually took place face to face. Leibniz wrote from Hanover to Caroline, then Princess of Wales, who forwarded his letters to Samuel Clarke, Newton's chosen defender, and Clarke's replies traveled back by the same circuitous route. For a pivotal scene, I want to take the novelist's liberty of having the two men confront one another directly, with no Clarke standing between them, and I'd like your help drafting that confrontation.

What matters most to me is that the exchange stay on the actual theological ground the correspondence occupied, rather than flattening into a generic "science versus religion" shape. I want Leibniz to press his real objection: that Newton's God, who apparently has to keep adjusting the clockwork of the solar system, and who somehow "perceives" bodies through space as though space were a sense organ, ends up sounding like either an imperfect craftsman or an oddly embodied one. And I want Newton to answer back in his own voice, not simply restate Clarke's published replies: that Leibniz's world, made so complete and self-sufficient at the very first instant of creation that it never needs adjusting, is one in which God has quietly forfeited any real freedom to choose or act -- so that Leibniz's "providence" amounts to providence in name only. If it isn't too much of a stretch, I would also like Newton's stubbornness on this particular point to occasionally connect back to his private anti-Trinitarianism -- his conviction that a simpler, purer original Christianity had been corrupted by later theologians -- since his insistence on a God who remains actively and continuously sovereign over creation seems to me, a non-historian, to rhyme with his horror at anything, including a co-equal Son, that might quietly dilute the Father's unique supremacy. Tell me if I'm forcing a connection that isn't really there.

Please give both men their full intellectual due: Leibniz shouldn't come across as merely irreligious, and Newton shouldn't be caricatured as a pious tinkerer out of his depth. I want real back-and-forth, with each man responding to what the other has just said rather than delivering a prepared monologue, and I don't need the scene to resolve neatly. Historically, Leibniz died only a few months after sending his fifth letter, and Clarke's final reply went unanswered -- an ending that leaves genuine, unresolved cost on both sides would, if anything, feel truer to how the actual dispute broke off.
