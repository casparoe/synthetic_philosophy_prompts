Hannah Arendt's actual argument about lying in politics is considerably sharper than the potted "she predicted post-truth politics" takeaway she's usually reduced to in journalism. In "Truth and Politics," she argues that factual truth, unlike rational or scientific truth, is uniquely fragile in political life because it is contingent, could always have been otherwise, and survives only through fallible testimony and record-keeping. In "Lying in Politics," her essay occasioned by the Pentagon Papers, she distinguishes the ordinary liar, who still tacitly respects the boundary between truth and falsehood even while crossing it, from the more modern manager of "images," who treats that boundary itself as just another variable to be managed. And in The Origins of Totalitarianism, she argues that propaganda's most dangerous achievement is not making people believe particular falsehoods, but eroding their very capacity to distinguish fact from fiction at all.

Arendt died in 1975, long before generative AI could fabricate photorealistic video and audio of events that never happened, and before algorithmically personalized feeds ensured that people arguing about politics often aren't even looking at the same purported facts to begin with. Working from her actual categories rather than a generic "she'd be horrified" reaction, how do you think Arendt would assess this situation? Would she treat it as basically continuous with the propaganda and image-management she already diagnosed, or as a genuinely new kind of threat to political life -- and does her account of how factual truth gets established in the first place suggest any real safeguard against its disappearance?
