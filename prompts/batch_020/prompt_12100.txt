I am several drafts into a novel that intercuts two timelines. One follows Cicero through the last two years of his life, composing the philosophical dialogues he turned to in the uncertain months after Caesar's assassination, before he threw himself back into politics against Antony and the proscriptions caught up with him. The other follows a present-day biogerontologist on the verge of a therapy that would arrest, and eventually reverse, human cellular senescence. The two strands never meet directly, but I want them to argue with one another, and the hinge connecting them is old age itself.

The Roman chapters draw closely on the actual case Cicero makes in the Cato Maior de Senectute, so the modern strand needs a proper foil for my biogerontologist: a bioethicist whose objections are recognizably Ciceronian rather than a vague, secondhand appeal to "accepting mortality gracefully." A classicist friend who read an early draft warned me that most contemporary invocations of Cicero on aging stop at exactly that kind of bromide, and I would like to avoid the same mistake in the finished book.

What I need, then, is the position reconstructed properly, rather than inferred from Cicero's general reputation for equanimity. Cato's speech answers four standard complaints against old age in turn — that it withdraws us from active life, weakens the body, empties it of pleasure, and stands too near death — leans on exempla like Fabius Maximus and Sophocles to argue that old age is a culmination rather than a deficiency, and closes by treating an ending as part of what makes the preceding life cohere, rather than as a misfortune to be staved off indefinitely. Given that structure, what would Cicero's considered verdict be on a therapy that simply grants, in full, the very things he keeps insisting the old should stop wanting? Would he take it as belatedly delivering the goods that old age was supposed to compensate for lacking, or does his consolation depend, in some deeper way, on decline and death remaining fixed features of a life, such that engineering them away would unravel the argument rather than complete it? I am less interested in a tidy verdict than in seeing exactly where that reconstructed position strains.
