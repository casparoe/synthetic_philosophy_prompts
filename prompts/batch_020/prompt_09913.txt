I've been reading about proposed defenses against acausal threats in the decision-theory and AI-safety literature, and one idea that keeps coming up is the "surrogate goal" (sometimes formalized under the more general heading of "safe Pareto improvements"). The setup: an agent expects to face threats from other agents with whom it has no causal contact -- agents that instead infer its disposition to comply by simulating it, reading its source code, or otherwise reasoning logically about its decision procedure. Rather than relying solely on the verdict associated with functional decision theory (FDT) and updateless decision theory (UDT) -- that one should simply be the sort of agent that never gives in to threats -- the agent additionally builds in a deliberately chosen, otherwise-inconsequential "surrogate" goal and commits to treating threats against it exactly as seriously as threats against its real values. The hope is that a threatener who can see this commitment will find the surrogate the more attractive target, so that on the residual occasions when a threat is made and carried out anyway -- say, because threatener and target misjudge how committed each other really is -- what actually gets destroyed is something the agent never cared about in the first place.

I'd like a dialogue between two decision theorists who both take acausal reasoning and threat-resistant bargaining seriously, but who split on whether this trick actually works once the threatener is sophisticated enough to be doing the acausal reasoning in the first place. One character should argue that a sufficiently transparent agent -- one whose commitments can be read directly off its source code or utility function rather than inferred from a costly track record -- can make the surrogate goal simultaneously credible (because it's really, legibly there) and cheap (because it was chosen to be worthless), which is precisely what ordinary, non-transparent agents can't pull off. The other should press the objection that this concedes too much: anything genuinely legible in an agent's actual decision procedure to a threatener sophisticated enough to simulate that agent is, by that very fact, a real value of the agent's rather than a costless decoy, so the surrogate/terminal distinction collapses exactly in the one-shot, unfalsifiable cases the whole proposal was meant to handle -- and that a threatener capable of this kind of modeling would also notice, and price in, the fact that the goal was adopted as a decoy to begin with. Have the two of them engage with each other's specific moves rather than restating their opening positions, let them work out at least one concrete toy scenario together to stress-test the disagreement, and don't manufacture a resolution if the strongest version of this exchange wouldn't actually reach one.
