I spent my career in behavioral ecology, where I absorbed the standard lesson that cooperation between self-interested parties generally requires repeated encounters and reputation — the lesson of Axelrod's tournaments, in which tit-for-tat prevails only because the same players meet again and again. Now that I am retired and reading more widely, a puzzle from a friend who works in software has unsettled that lesson. He described two firms arranging a single, one-off deal in a country whose courts neither trusts, where ordinarily neither side wants to hand over its goods or payment first, since the other could then simply keep it and give nothing back, so that no deal can be made at all. His proposed fix is that each firm write its side of the bargain as a short program, fully visible to the other side in advance, that releases its own payment or goods only once specified conditions are met, rather than simply promising in plain language to behave. He insists that letting each firm inspect the other's decision-making program before anything is committed changes the strategic situation, even though nothing about the one-off, unenforceable nature of the deal has changed. I am inclined to doubt this: surely a firm could always show one piece of code while quietly running another, so reading a program in advance seems no more binding than hearing a promise. Is my friend onto something real about what mutual visibility of a decision procedure can accomplish that an exchange of promises cannot, or is my skepticism justified? And if he does have a point, I would like to know exactly what feature of the setup is doing the work.
