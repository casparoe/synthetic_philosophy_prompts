QUESTION (20 pts): "Explain the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem as it arises for buck-passing/fitting-attitude accounts of value. Illustrate with a concrete example, and briefly discuss whether distinguishing object-given reasons from state-given reasons actually solves the problem."

STUDENT ANSWER: "Fitting-attitude theories analyze evaluative properties in terms of the reactions it's fitting to have towards their bearers: for X to be good just is for X to be such that a pro-attitude (desire, admiration, choice) toward X would be fitting. Moore's buck-passing analysis puts this most sharply: what makes something good is not some further natural property, but the fact that its other properties give us reason to favor it -- goodness itself 'passes the buck' to those other, reason-giving properties.

The wrong-kind-of-reasons (WKR) problem, raised by Rabinowicz and Rønnow-Rasmussen, is that there are reasons to have a pro-attitude that have nothing to do with whether the object is actually good. Suppose a demon says he'll torture you forever unless you come to desire a saucer of mud. You now have an excellent reason to desire the mud -- but the mud hasn't thereby become good. So there must be a distinction between 'right kind' reasons, grounded in the object's own value-relevant features, and 'wrong kind' reasons, grounded merely in the causal benefits of having the attitude itself.

Honestly I think this just collapses into Moore's open-question argument: for any proposed reason-giving property, we can always sensibly ask 'yes, there's reason to desire it, but is it really good?' So goodness can't be reductively defined via reasons at all, wrong-kind or otherwise, and restricting to object-given reasons doesn't really help, since we could run the same open question about which reasons count as object-given in the first place."
