I am teaching a graduate seminar this term on Quine's philosophy of language, and we have just worked through the chapter on radical translation in *Word and Object* alongside the earlier essay "Two Dogmas of Empiricism." A disagreement broke out in our last session that I would like some outside perspective on before we meet again. Several students maintain that the indeterminacy of translation thesis — illustrated by the gavagai case, where rival translation manuals for a native speaker's utterances can fit all possible behavioral evidence equally well without agreeing on what any given sentence means — is really nothing more than a special instance of the underdetermination of theory by evidence that Quine had already defended through confirmation holism and what is now called the Duhem-Quine thesis. On this reading, once you grant that no finite body of evidence ever fixes a unique theory, indeterminacy of translation simply falls out as soon as that general epistemological point is applied to the domain of meaning and reference. Other students, leaning on the objections Quine faced from critics such as Chomsky and Putnam, insist that this runs together two genuinely different claims, and that Quine himself resisted treating indeterminacy of translation as just another case of ordinary underdetermination.

I would like your help settling this for the class. Please compare Quine's indeterminacy of translation with the underdetermination of theory by evidence, and spell out as precisely as you can in which respects, if any, indeterminacy of translation is a stronger or otherwise distinct thesis rather than a mere application of ordinary underdetermination to the domain of language. In particular, please address the "no fact of the matter" language Quine reserves for translation: is an analogous claim available, or even coherent, for underdetermined physical theories, and if not, what exactly keeps the two cases apart? I would like an answer detailed enough that I could turn it directly into a handout, since a couple of my sharper students are sure to press on any point where the distinction is merely asserted rather than actually demonstrated.
