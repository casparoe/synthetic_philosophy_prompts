I'm a PhD student in political theory drafting a chapter on early modern arguments for democracy, and I'd welcome comments on the section below before I bring it to my advisor.

Spinoza is usually credited with being the first major philosopher to argue for democracy on principle rather than merely tolerate it as the least bad option. The argument, as I reconstruct it, falls out of his general theory of natural right: since a thing's right extends exactly as far as its power, the right of a political body is nothing more than the combined power of the multitude that composes it. Monarchy and narrow aristocracy work by concentrating this power in a few hands, which for Spinoza is inherently precarious, since a ruler's own passions are then insufficiently checked by any countervailing force, and subjects who obey chiefly out of fear or induced superstition are the least securely bound of all. Democracy, by contrast, leaves power more or less where it already lies, which is presumably why Spinoza calls it the most "natural" regime and treats it as the standard against which the rationality of other constitutions is measured.

The trouble starts when he actually tries to specify democratic government rather than praise it from a distance. The Tractatus Politicus works through the constitutions in turn, and monarchy and aristocracy both receive extensive, increasingly granular institutional treatment (councils, terms of office, safeguards against standing armies and hereditary factions), while democracy is saved for last and gets by far the least. When Spinoza finally reaches it, in chapter eleven, the first question he addresses is who is entitled to a voice in the sovereign council, and he begins excluding categories of people, most notably women, on the grounds that no society has ever been observed in which women rule men, from which he infers that women lack the capacity for rule by nature rather than by custom or historical circumstance. The chapter, and the treatise, breaks off only a few paragraphs later, unfinished at his death.

My (tentative) claim is that this is not simply an unfortunate biographical accident that can be set aside to preserve an otherwise egalitarian theory. Because Spinoza's whole argument identifies right with actual power, an inference from observed subordination to natural incapacity is not a prejudice smuggled in from outside his system; it is that system's own method for reading natural capacities off historical outcomes, applied to a case whose result we happen to find intolerable. If that's right, the unfinished state of chapter eleven marks the exact point at which the naturalistic argument that makes the case for democracy in general starts to look considerably less comfortable once it is run on the actual, unequal distribution of power among persons.

I'd value pushback specifically on: whether the "right as power" reconstruction in the first paragraph is a fair summary of Spinoza's actual argument; whether the tension I'm describing in the third paragraph is real or dissolves under scrutiny; and whether this point is already established in the secondary literature in a form I should be citing rather than presenting as my own observation.
