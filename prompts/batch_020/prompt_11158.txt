I have been asked to toast two former graduate students of mine at their wedding, one of whom wrote her dissertation on Sartre and will know at once if I misrepresent him. I want to build the toast around his claim that no past resolution, including a vow, can bind one's future freedom: his own illustration is the gambler who sincerely swears off the tables and then, standing before them again, feels the anguish of discovering that yesterday's resolve has no grip on today's choice. The comfortable thing to say at a wedding — that the vow now settles the matter, that the couple need never choose each other again — is exactly the sort of bad faith Sartre spent his career diagnosing, and I don't want to say it. But neither do I want the guests who have never read a page of existentialism to leave the reception thinking I have just told the couple their vows mean nothing. Write the toast — a few minutes long, not a lecture — so that it satisfies the philosophers in the room without losing everyone else, and leaves the marriage, and not just the metaphysics, feeling like the main event.
