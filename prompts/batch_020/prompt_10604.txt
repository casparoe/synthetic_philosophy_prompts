So somehow I, a novelist with zero science background, ended up as the "non-scientist community member" on my university's animal research oversight committee -- they needed someone off the street with no research ties, and I was the only one at a dinner party willing to admit it. There's a protocol up for review that's been bugging me: about 300 mice get nerve-constriction surgery to induce chronic neuropathic pain, then get tested for roughly 10 weeks to validate a new non-opioid painkiller, with analgesia kept minimal during testing because otherwise it wrecks the model -- so it's flagged as significant, largely unrelieved pain. The PI's harm-benefit writeup is straightforwardly utilitarian: weigh the suffering against the speculative-but-huge benefit to future chronic pain patients, and the numbers come out favorable. What's nagging me is something I half remember from a Nozick footnote in college -- the idea that we basically run utilitarian aggregation for animals (a big enough benefit can always outweigh the suffering) while insisting on Kantian, rights-based limits for humans (you can't do this to a person no matter the payoff, consent aside). Can you draft the memo I'd actually bring to the full commitee -- an actual recommendation (approve / reject / approve-with-conditions), not a "here's both sides" essay -- that takes the strongest version of the PI's case seriously, but also really grapples with whether pure aggregation is how we should be treating the animals, and whether some side-constraint ought to hold regardless of the projected benefit?
