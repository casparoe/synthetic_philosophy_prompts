Three weeks ago I was cast as Alceste in a small company's revival of Molière's *The Misanthrope*, and at our first table read the dramaturg mentioned, almost in passing, that Rousseau singled out this very play as proof that the theatre corrupts rather than cultivates virtue. I went home and read the relevant pages of his *Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre*, and the argument hasn't left me since. Rousseau's charge is that the play makes Alceste's blunt honesty look ridiculous while making Philinte's complaisant sociability look like wisdom, and that this is symptomatic of what theatre does more generally: it lets an audience enjoy pity and moral approval from a safe distance, none of which ever has to translate into actual conduct. What unsettles me more, given my own part in this, is his charge against actors specifically -- that making a living out of performing other people's characters is a kind of habitual self-loss that a spectator society rewards rather than punishes.

I know Diderot, once Rousseau's closest friend before their famous falling-out, would have disputed nearly every part of this, both through his "paradox of the actor" and through his hopes for a morally instructive theatre. Since I can't stage the argument with either man in person, could you write a dialogue between Rousseau and Diderot working through their disagreement in real depth? Center it on the Misanthrope case, on whether theatrical pity spurs civic virtue or only simulates it, and on whether acting amounts to self-loss or self-mastery. Give both men their strongest material and have them genuinely engage each other's objections rather than talk past one another -- I'd like to come away still undecided, since that's rather where I stand as we head into tech week.
