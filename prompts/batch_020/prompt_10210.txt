My grandmother died twelve days ago after thirty-two years as a philosophy professor at a small liberal-arts college, where she spent much of her career defending perdurantism against the endurantism that most people simply assume without realizing it even has a name. Her actual position, as best I can put it, was this: a person is not wholly present at every moment of her life, the way endurantism has it, but is instead extended through time the way a body is extended through space, made up of 'temporal parts' the way a body is made up of spatial ones, so that the real object is the whole four-dimensional 'worm' running from birth to death, and any single moment we experience is only a cross-section of it. She'd done her doctorate under a student of David Lewis's and mentioned it at every possible opportunity. She was also notorious at other people's funerals for visibly wincing at 'she's in a better place' or 'he's watching over us,' while getting just as irritated at 'gone forever' or 'we lost him' — phrases she said smuggled in exactly the endurantist assumption she had spent her career arguing against, namely that a finished life has simply stopped being there, rather than having merely reached the far edge of a whole whose earlier parts are, on her view, exactly as real now as they were when they were present.

Among the instructions she left for her own service was a half-serious line that whoever spoke should feel free to say she had 'stopped getting longer' but never that she had 'stopped existing.' That job has fallen to me. I'm her oldest grandchild, a senior this year, and the only one of the cousins who ever actually sat still for her arguments at Thanksgiving — the closest thing my school offers to philosophy is an elective called 'Big Questions,' which I only ended up taking because of her — so the family has asked me to give the eulogy this Saturday. Most of the room will be relatives and old friends, including a very devout great-uncle who will not enjoy any of this if it's handled clumsily; a handful of her former colleagues and graduate students are also flying in, and they will notice immediately if I flatten her actual position into generic 'she lives on in our hearts' sentiment.

Could you write the eulogy for me? It should run about four minutes read aloud, needs to genuinely comfort people who are simply grieving and not thinking about metaphysics at all, has to stay honest to what she would have accepted as true on her own terms rather than drifting into either the religious language she rejected or a flatly bleak 'nothing survives' that she would have found just as sloppy, and ideally it should include at least one moment that rewards the people in the room who already know what a temporal part is, without turning the rest of the speech into a seminar.
