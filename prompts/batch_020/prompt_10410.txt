I'm reporting a longread on the private Isaac Newton — the one who spent more hours on scripture, alchemy, and biblical chronology than on optics or gravitation, most of it in manuscripts that only became easy to consult once the Newton Project started transcribing and digitizing them. I've drafted an opening section laying out the argument I want to build the rest of the piece around, and before I go further I'd like a sanity check from someone willing to push back on both the history and the reasoning, not just tell me it reads well. Here's the section:

Newton kept his Cambridge fellowship only thanks to a legal workaround. Fellows of Trinity College were expected, within a few years of taking their degree, to be ordained into the Church of England — which meant swearing to the Thirty-Nine Articles and their Trinitarian formula. In 1675, a royal dispensation quietly exempted the Lucasian professorship from that requirement. It's just as well: Newton's private papers show that he had come to regard the doctrine of the Trinity itself as a corruption, smuggled into Christianity in the fourth century, of what had originally been a purely unitarian faith. He confided this to almost no one. When he did try to make the case in writing — an essay sent to John Locke arguing that two of the Bible's clearest Trinitarian prooftexts, 1 John 5:7 and 1 Timothy 3:16, were later scribal insertions — he lost his nerve and pulled it before it could appear under his name.

It's tempting to file all this under "eccentric private hobby," safely quarantined from the physics. But it doesn't sit alone: the same private Newton spent decades re-dating the kingdoms of the ancient world against biblical chronology, and reconstructing the exact floor plan of Solomon's Temple down to its measurements. The natural conclusion — the one I want to build the piece around — is that theology, chronology, and physics were not three hobbies but one project wearing three coats. Newton seems to have believed that a single, pure body of knowledge, both religious and natural-philosophical, had been available to humanity in deep antiquity, and had suffered two parallel corruptions since: idolatrous, Trinity-worshipping priestcraft on the religious side, Aristotelian scholasticism on the natural-philosophical side. Read this way, Newton's insistence — in the General Scholium and in the Opticks alike — that the order of the solar system reflects the continuing "dominion" of an intelligent, active God, one who might need to step in now and then to keep the planets on their courses, isn't a pious afterthought tacked onto the physics. It's the same restoration project, seen from its other side. (Leibniz, notably, thought this made Newton's God an incompetent watchmaker, forever fixing his own machine.)

Two things I'd like your take on. First, accuracy: is there anything in that draft I've overstated, oversimplified, or gotten flatly wrong? Second, the argument itself: does "one project wearing three coats" actually hold up as the best explanation for why Newton pursued theology, chronology, and physics with equal seriousness and secrecy, or is it a tidier story than the evidence supports — one that historians, or I, might be imposing after the fact on a mind that could just as easily have kept its rooms separate? I'd rather hear the objections now than after this runs.
