Question: "Explain Fodor's Language of Thought (LOT) hypothesis. Be sure to explain what the productivity argument and the systematicity argument are each meant to establish, and explain how LOT bears on Fodor's argument about whether primitive concepts can be learned."

Student's answer: "Fodor's Language of Thought (LOT) hypothesis holds that thinking is carried out in an inner mental code, 'Mentalese,' that -- like a natural language -- has a combinatorial syntax and a compositional semantics: complex thoughts are built out of simpler constituents by fixed rules, and what a complex thought means depends on the meanings of its parts and how they're arranged.

The productivity argument says we can entertain indefinitely many distinct thoughts despite having only finitely many concepts and combination rules, which is best explained if thought is combinatorial, similar to how a finite grammar lets us produce infinitely many sentences. The systematicity argument is really the same point dressed up differently: it notes that anyone who can think 'the dog bit the man' can also think 'the man bit the dog,' and draws the same conclusion the productivity argument does, that thought must have the unbounded, recursively generated structure needed to explain why it's productive in the first place.

LOT also backs up Fodor's notorious argument about concept learning: since testing a hypothesis about what a new word means already requires being able to represent that meaning, you can't acquire a primitive concept like RED or DOG through hypothesis-testing without already having it. So Fodor concludes that primitive concepts are innate parts of Mentalese, already in place before a kid starts learning to talk -- and it's this shared innate stock of concepts, gradually shaped and organized by whichever native language a given child ends up acquiring, that lets completely different languages like Mandarin and English each get picked up as a first language."
