I'm a software engineer on my company's model deployment review board -- the committee that has to sign off before any new model ships to production. Our standing policy requires every model to clear a full safety and capability evaluation suite (jailbreak resistance, bio- and cyber-uplift screening, deceptive-behavior probes, and the like) before release. The applied ML team is now asking for a fast-track exemption for "derivative" models -- quantized, distilled, or otherwise compressed versions of a base model we've already approved -- arguing that since these produce statistically indistinguishable outputs on every benchmark and sampled prompt we throw at them, rerunning the full suite is just redundant cost and delay. A couple of us are uneasy about this: an advisor with a philosophy background told me this is basically the old "multiple realizability" problem from philosophy of mind, and that matching behavior on everything we've tested so far doesn't guarantee the compressed model implements the same underlying function -- it could diverge precisely on the untested edge cases the evals were designed to surface. Please draft the board's decision memo for our meeting next week: recommend a concrete policy on when, if ever, a derivative model may skip the full evaluation suite, spell out criteria the ML team could actually satisfy, and take seriously the real cost of the alternative -- that mandatory full re-evaluation on every compression or hardware port will slow down deployments, including ones that would make the model cheaper or safer to run.
