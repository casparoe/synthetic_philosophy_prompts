I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm currently reporting a longer piece on the intellectual history of tyrannicide — how political thinkers, from antiquity through the July 20, 1944 plot against Hitler to present-day arguments over the targeted killing of dictators, have tried to draw a line between justified resistance and plain murder. For the medieval chapter, I sat in on a workshop on scholastic political theory, and during the discussion after a talk on resistance to unjust rulers, two of the scholars present, Owen and Ana, fell into a disagreement about Aquinas specifically that the moderator never quite managed to close down. I took rough notes and would like help sorting out who had the better of it, move by move, because I honestly can't referee this one myself.

(1) Owen argued that Aquinas is considerably more permissive of tyrannicide than his reputation as a defender of settled authority suggests. He cited Aquinas's early commentary on Peter Lombard's Sentences, where Aquinas holds that someone who seizes power through outright usurpation, with no legitimate title at all, was never a genuine authority to begin with, so using force against him — up to and including killing him — isn't the sin of sedition, since sedition presupposes disturbing an order that was legitimate in the first place; he read this as Aquinas licensing private individuals to kill such a person. (2) Ana didn't dispute the reading but said it was being stretched past its target: that passage is confined to a ruler illegitimate from the outset, whereas the harder and far more common case — a king crowned by right who later turns tyrannical — is treated separately, and much more cautiously, in Aquinas's unfinished treatise on kingship, written for a king of Cyprus, where he explicitly warns against letting private citizens judge for themselves when a ruler has become tyrannical enough to kill, on the grounds that this standard would just as easily let the ambitious murder decent rulers under the same pretext; correction of a legitimate ruler gone bad, he says there, belongs to whatever public body installed him in the first place, and failing that, to prayer, not the dagger. (3) Owen answered that this concedes too little: in the Summa's own discussion of sedition, Aquinas is explicitly considering a government that is tyrannical in how it's exercised, and he says there that such a regime, because it serves the ruler's private good rather than the common good, is itself what sows discord among the people — so it's the tyrant, not those who move against him, who is guilty of sedition. Since sedition is precisely the charge that would ordinarily make forcible resistance wrong, he argued, lifting it clears the main obstacle to violent removal even in the legitimate-ruler-gone-bad case, not only in outright usurpation. (4) Ana granted that this passage does soften Aquinas's view of the harder case, but denied that it collapses her distinction: showing that resistance isn't the sin of sedition tells you who bears that particular guilt, not that any private person is thereby authorized to act as executioner. She pointed out that in the very treatise where Aquinas discusses the legitimate-ruler-gone-bad case at length, he still channels correction through public authority or prayer rather than private violence, reserving unauthorized lethal self-help for the narrower usurpation case; Owen, she said, was quietly sliding from "resisting him isn't sinful" to "any private citizen may kill him," a considerably stronger claim she didn't think Aquinas makes once a ruler's original title was legitimate.
