My younger brother Dan is getting married next month, and as his best man I've been asked to give the toast. Dan is a philosopher of mind who has spent his entire career on what he calls "the exclusion problem": if every physical event already has a sufficient physical cause in the brain, what causal work, if any, is left for a belief or a desire to do? He's a committed non-reductive physicalist, which as far as I understand it means he wants to say the mental is nothing over and above the physical without also conceding that it's just inert decoration riding on top of it, and I've watched him spend fifteen years and two books defending that combination against colleagues who think you eventually have to give one half up. His fiancée, Priya, is a cognitive neuroscientist, and their entire courtship seems to have doubled as a running argument about whether her imaging data is quietly making his position untenable; her standing joke at dinner parties is that she's going to find the exact neuron that "really" proposed to him. Roughly half the wedding will be philosophy-of-mind and neuroscience people from their overlapping departments, who will notice immediately if I get the argument wrong or reach for an easy "it's all just chemicals" line that neither of them actually believes; the other half is family who have never heard the word "supervenience" and just want to laugh and tear up a little.

I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training of my own — everything above is secondhand from years of half-following Dan's dinner-table explanations — so I'd like your help writing the toast itself, about four minutes long. The exclusion problem needs to do real work in it rather than sit there as a name-drop: ideally there's a joke or image that only actually lands if it's honest about why the problem is hard, instead of one that quietly resolves it in a way either of them would privately wince at. If the toast can end somewhere that uses their marriage as a small, affectionate instance of the very thing Dan has built his career on defending — that a pattern can be doing real causal work and not merely riding along on top of the biology underneath it — without turning into a defense of his dissertation, so much the better. But the main requirement is that it reads as a brother's love for his brother, not a philosophy lecture that happens to rhyme with a wedding.
