I'm auditing a game theory course this semester (partly an excuse -- I've read enough decision-theory posts on LessWrong/ACX over the years that I wanted the actual formalism under all the "dominated strategy" talk), and two people in my problem set group, Marcus and Yuki, got into an argument over one step of an iterated-dominance question that never really got settled. It's been stuck in my head since.

Here's the setup they were arguing about. Player 1 picks a row, Player 2 picks a column, and Player 1's payoffs (Player 2's own payoffs don't matter for this bit) are:

      L     R
T     3     0
M     0     3
B     1     1

Marcus said B drops out in the first round of elimination. Yuki pushed back: dominated by what, exactly -- T isn't better than B against both columns (it craters to 0 under R), and neither is M (craters to 0 under L). Marcus's answer was that a 50/50 mix of T and M pays 1.5 no matter what column does, which beats B's guaranteed 1 either way, so B is strictly dominated by that mix even though no single pure strategy dominates it. Yuki agreed the arithmetic works out but said it feels like a sleight of hand -- player 1 never actually commits to flipping a coin, so how can a strategy lose to a randomization nobody's running? Marcus's comeback was that this isn't really about coins at all: B pays worse than the T/M mix no matter what belief you have about column, so a rational player would never touch B, period. That part I could sort of follow. Then Yuki raised something that stumped both of them: if mixed strategies can knock out stuff pure strategies can't, doesn't that make the whole elimination process order-dependent? Like, what if some column strategy only becomes dominated once B is gone, but some other row strategy only becomes dominated once a different column strategy dies first -- couldn't you end up with different survivors depending what you check first? Marcus said no, order never matters here, its a theorem. Yuki wasn't convinced, because their TA had docked someone points the week before for eliminating "in the wrong order" on a different problem, so either the TA screwed up or Marcus's "order never matters, full stop" claim is overstated. Marcus guessed the TA's problem involved ties rather than strict inequalities, which he said is a totally different animal, but Yuki thought that was a bit of a dodge since none of our actual problem sets ever bother saying "strict" or "weak."

I can tell there's a real, clean answer buried in here somewhere but I don't know enough to referee it myself. Could you go through this move by move -- the dominated-by-a-mix claim, the "nobody's flipping that coin" objection, the order-independence claim, and the TA disagreement -- and say who's right about each one specifically, not just who wins overall? A rough score from -2 (clearly wrong) to +2 (clearly right) for each of the four moves would be a nice bonus before you get into the explanation.
