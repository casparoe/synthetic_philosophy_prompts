Here's the question, verbatim off the exam:

"Explain the Frege-Geach problem for moral expressivism. Your answer should (a) state the problem precisely, using an example, and (b) explain why this poses a distinctive challenge for expressivism specifically, rather than for other metaethical positions."

And here's the student's answer, also verbatim:

"Expressivism is the metaethical view that moral utterances like 'Stealing is wrong' don't describe facts about the world but instead express the speaker's attitudes — on the classic emotivist version, saying 'Stealing is wrong' is a bit like saying 'Stealing, boo!' It's expressing disapproval, not asserting a proposition that could be true or false.

The Frege-Geach problem, first raised by Frege against Ayer's emotivism, points out that moral sentences don't only occur asserted on their own, like 'Stealing is wrong' — they also occur unasserted, embedded inside more complex sentences, like the antecedent of a conditional: 'If stealing is wrong, then getting your roommate to steal for you is wrong.' Nobody is expressing disapproval of stealing just by uttering that conditional. But now consider the argument: (1) If stealing is wrong, then getting your roommate to steal for you is wrong. (2) Stealing is wrong. (3) Therefore, getting your roommate to steal for you is wrong. This is valid by modus ponens, and it's only valid because 'stealing is wrong' means the same thing in premise (1), where it's embedded and unasserted, as it does in premise (2), where it's asserted on its own. This is basically the same lesson as Hume's point that you can't validly derive an 'ought' from an 'is' — moral language just doesn't slot into ordinary logical inference the way factual language does, which is exactly what makes it distinct from purely descriptive claims.

This is a special problem for expressivism specifically because if the meaning of 'stealing is wrong' just is the expressing of an attitude of disapproval, it's unclear what attitude is being expressed when the sentence is sitting unasserted in the antecedent of a conditional — there's no attitude being expressed there at all. So it looks like the sentence has to mean something different in the two contexts, which would make the argument equivocal rather than valid. A descriptivist view doesn't have this problem, because 'stealing is wrong' just states a proposition, and ordinary logic for conditionals works on propositions regardless of whether they're asserted or embedded, so the meaning trivially stays constant."
