I have an undergraduate and a master's degree in mathematics from this department, though I left before the PhD to write novels instead, and my most recent book has a set theorist named Ruth as its protagonist -- which is presumably why the chair, who taught me real analysis fifteen years ago, asked me to give this year's departmental commencement address rather than inviting back the more obvious choice, a distinguished alumnus who now runs a hedge fund. It's a small ceremony, held the evening before the university-wide commencement, with maybe eighty graduating majors, their families, and the faculty who spent the last four years making them prove things. I'm told these addresses usually run about seven minutes, and that nobody has ever complained one was too short.

I want to build the address around Zorn's Lemma, which is also, not coincidentally, the theorem Ruth is explaining to her dying father in the novel's best chapter, the one she says she finds consoling rather than merely true: that a nonempty partially ordered set in which every chain has an upper bound is guaranteed to contain a maximal element, even though nothing tells you in advance which one, how to construct it, or whether it's the only one. What I don't want is the easy version of this speech, the one that quietly slides Zorn's Lemma into the folksy infinite-socks version of the Axiom of Choice that half the room will already dimly remember from a discrete math class; that's a different theorem doing different work, and the mathematicians present, graduating seniors and their professors both, will notice the substitution immediately. What I need handled honestly, and made to land as something other than a corrected footnote, is that a maximal element is not a maximum -- that there can be several, incomparable with one another, none dominated by any other and none the single best of all available lives -- and that the lemma's promise to these eighty people is only ever that such a life is reachable from where they now stand, never that it is unique, or optimal, or visible in advance.

Could you draft the address? It has to be correct enough to survive the front two rows, warm enough that the parents in the back don't feel lectured at, and it has to end, I don't see a way around this, sounding like hope rather than like a hedge.
