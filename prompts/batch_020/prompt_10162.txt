So in my early modern phil seminar there's this side debate that broke out after class about Leibniz's "best of all possible worlds" thing, and I can't stop turning it over. Two people in my discussion group, Ben and Nadia, argued about it for like twenty minutes and I honestly couldn't tell who came out ahead, so I want an outside read.

Ben opened with: God could've made a world exactly like ours minus, say, the Lisbon earthquake, everything else held fixed. Nothing contradictory about an earthquake-free 1755, so that world is possible, and clearly better, so ours can't be the best possible world and the whole framework is dead on arrival.

Nadia said that's the standard move against Leibniz, but he's actually got an answer: not all possibles are compossible. Every substance's complete concept already includes its relations to literally everything else in its world, so "our world minus the earthquake" isn't a small edit, its a completely different bundle of substances, and theres no guarantee a better bundle is even sitting nearby in possibility-space.

Ben's comeback did two things at once. First, that this sounds unfalsifiable -- any time he names a removable evil, Nadia can just say "ah, but that changes everything, who knows if it's for the better." Second, what actually makes two possibles non-compossible if not straightforward logical contradiction? There's nothing contradictory about a slightly calmer 1755 taken by itself, so if there's no real criterion for compossibility beyond "well, they just don't fit," Leibniz has just swapped one brute fact (this is the best world) for another (these are the ones that fit) and explained nothing.

Nadia's reply was that sure, it's a costly commitment, but Leibniz didn't invent it just to dodge the problem of evil -- compossibility is doing independent work in his system (it's also supposed to explain why there's only one actual world instead of literally everything possible being actual), so it isn't special pleading, even if the exact criterion for it stays contested.

Can you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of it at each stage, instead of just picking an overall winner? Also tell me what you think both of them are still missing.
