This semester I am taking a seminar on philosophy of cognitive science that has spent the last few weeks on Karl Friston's free energy principle and its extension into active inference, including the work that introduces "epistemic value" and formalizes policy selection through expected free energy. A disagreement I had with two classmates after our last session has been nagging at me since. As I understand the central claim, any biological system that persists over time must act so as to minimize variational free energy, a quantity that serves as a proxy for the surprise, or negative log-evidence, of its sensory states given its internal generative model. Read straightforwardly, this seems to predict that a well-adapted organism should always gravitate toward the most predictable, least stimulating state available to it, since that is what would minimize surprise most reliably; yet organisms plainly explore, play, and seek out novelty as a matter of course.

Our professor's response to this worry was to invoke "expected free energy" and its epistemic value component, which are meant to show that seeking out uncertainty is itself free-energy-minimizing once an agent is evaluating policies over a time horizon rather than a single instant. My classmates took this to be exactly the kind of principled unification for which Friston is well known; I argued that it looks suspiciously like defining a new quantity for the sole purpose of reclassifying an apparent counterexample, namely curiosity and exploration, as confirming evidence, so that no observed behavior could ever really threaten the framework. After all, if minimizing free energy predicts boredom-seeking and minimizing expected free energy predicts novelty-seeking, the theory appears able to accommodate essentially any behavior after the fact simply by choosing which quantity, generative model, or set of preferences to plug in.

I would be grateful if you could help settle this for us. Specifically, is epistemic value mathematically entailed once one commits to expected free energy as the objective for action selection, or is it best understood as an independent assumption introduced after the fact to handle a class of behaviors that the original, perception-focused version of the theory could not explain? I would also value your view on how this bears on the "dark room problem" that keeps coming up in our readings, since it seems to be the same underlying issue restated at the level of an organism's whole lifestyle rather than a single policy choice.
