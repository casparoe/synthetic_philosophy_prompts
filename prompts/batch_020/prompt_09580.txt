A friend of mine from my PhD cohort -- we TAed intro ethics together before she jumped ship for law school in year three -- is being sworn in as a superior court judge next month, and she's asked me to give the remarks at her investiture. The complication is that she has been quietly obsessed with Pascal since grad school, and the fragment she keeps circling back to, usually a glass of wine into dinner, is the one on justice and force: that justice without force is powerless, force without justice is tyrannical, and since nobody could manage to make what's just strong, people settled for making what's strong count as just instead. She told me point-blank that if I get up there and do the standard toast about blind, impartial justice floating serenely above power and custom, she'll know I didn't mean a word of it, and so will half the room.

I teach the Pensées most years in my seventeenth-century seminar, so I know the fragment fine on paper -- I've just never had to turn it into something you say out loud, to a room that's maybe half judges and litigators who'll clock the reference before I finish the sentence, and half aunts, uncles, and a couple of very bored kids who've never heard of the guy. What I actually need is the remarks themselves, about five minutes' worth. They have to take Pascal's point seriously rather then sanding it down -- including the genuinely unsettling bit, where he seems to think obeying whatever authority has actually managed to establish itself is close to the whole of practical justice we get, not because its the real thing but because the alternative, everybody privately relitigating which force deserves the name "just," is worse. And it still has to land as a warm, specific tribute to her, not a Pascal lecture with her name stapled onto the end. I've drafted this twice myself already and keep sliding into one of two bad versions -- either Pascal gets flattened into plain might-makes-right cynicism, which she'd correct me on before I sat down, or I lose my nerve halfway through and drift back into scales-and-blindfold greeting-card stuff, which she'd hate even more. Can you write the version that does neither?
