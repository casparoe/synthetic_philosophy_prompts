I trained as a physicist and spent nearly forty years doing condensed-matter work before retiring, and only in the last couple of years have I started filling in the considerable gaps in my education by reading philosophy and its more formal neighbors. A colleague from my old department, now retired himself and drifting into economics, got me started on social choice theory, and this month I finally sat down and worked through the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem properly: the result that for any voting rule over three or more alternatives that is not a dictatorship, and that lets every alternative win under some possible set of ballots, there is always some voter who, given some configuration of everyone else's preferences, could secure a better outcome by not voting their true preference. Coming from a discipline where a clean impossibility proof — no perpetual motion machine, no signaling faster than light — is treated as about the most informative thing you can be told about a system, I was struck that this one, despite being just as clean and just as general, doesn't seem to carry anything like the same currency outside specialist circles, given how much of public life runs on aggregating preferences.

Reading around the theorem's history took me back to Condorcet, who by every account is the true origin of this whole line of thought: the same man who discovered, nearly two centuries earlier, that majority rule can cycle with no consistent winner at all, and who nonetheless spent his career, and in the end his life, defending the conviction that reason and the calculus of probabilities could still be trusted to govern collective decisions on something like a scientific footing. He was, of course, still writing in that spirit — an optimistic sketch of the endless progress of the human mind — while hiding from the Terror that killed him.

What I would like from you is your best reconstruction of how a mind like his would have reacted to being shown the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem: whether he would have read it as the rigorous completion of the worry his own paradox had already raised, or whether his optimism gives us reason to think he would instead have gone hunting for an escape route, along the lines his own proposed remedy for the paradox suggests — ranking the pairwise majorities by how decisive they are and building up a consistent order from the strongest down, overriding only whichever majority is weakest wherever a cycle forces the issue. I am less interested in a restatement of either result than in whether Condorcet's actual mathematical instincts and his political commitments point toward one of those reactions rather than the other, or whether, honestly, they would have pulled him in both directions at once.
