I'm a junior, and this year I've been serving as one of the student members on my high school's Honor Council, a five-person panel (three students, two faculty advisors) that hears academic-integrity cases and decides whether a student is "responsible" for a violation. Under our bylaws, a finding of responsible requires the panel to vote separately, by majority, on two distinct questions: first, whether the student's conduct actually meets the handbook's definition of a violation (for instance, submitting AI-generated text as one's own original work), and second, whether the student knew, or reasonably should have known, that the conduct was prohibited given the assignment's instructions. Only if both questions receive a majority "yes" is the student found responsible.

Last month we heard a case where this procedure produced a result several of us found hard to accept. Two of the five panelists thought both questions should be answered yes; one thought only the first should be; one thought only the second should be; and one thought both should be no. That meant three of us voted yes on the violation question and, separately, three of us voted yes on the notice question, so under our bylaws the student was found responsible — even though, if you had instead simply asked each panelist to give their own overall verdict, three of the five would have said "not responsible," since only the two who answered yes to both questions actually believed that themselves. When I described this to my AP Government teacher, she told me I had essentially rediscovered something philosophers and legal theorists already have names for — the "discursive dilemma," or "doctrinal paradox" in the older law-review literature on multi-judge courts — and mentioned offhand that there's a whole judgment-aggregation literature on it associated with Christian List and Philip Pettit, though we didn't have time to get into it before the bell rang.

Could you walk me through the most important implications of this for how a small panel like ours should actually operate? I'm especially curious whether we should keep voting question-by-question (what I gather is called a "premise-based" procedure) or switch to having each member just cast one overall verdict (a "conclusion-based" procedure), and whether either option is more vulnerable to being manipulated by whoever gets to decide which questions we vote on and in what order. I'd also like to understand what this paradox suggests, more philosophically, about whether the Council's official ruling in a case like this can really be said to reflect the judgment of any actual person who sat on the panel, and what follows from that for how fair the process is to the student on the receiving end.
