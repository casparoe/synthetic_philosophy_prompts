Here's the question they were given:

"Explain the difference between the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) and the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA). Which one does the classic Doomsday Argument rely on, and which one does the Presumptuous Philosopher case count against? Briefly say what a defender of the assumption under attack could say back."

And here's one student's answer:

"SSA says that when reasoning about who you are, you should treat yourself as a random draw from the class of all observers who actually exist -- past, present, and future -- within some appropriate reference class, like 'all humans.' SIA instead says you should treat yourself as randomly drawn from the class of all possible observers, and because your own existence is more likely on hypotheses where more observers get to exist, SIA automatically boosts the probability of 'bigger' hypotheses just from the fact that you're here at all.

The Doomsday Argument uses SSA: since you're probably not unusually early or late among all the humans who will ever be born, finding yourself fairly early in the count is mild evidence that the total number of humans who will ever live isn't astronomically large, which nudges the odds toward humanity ending sooner rather than later.

The Presumptuous Philosopher case is the standard strike against SSA. Two cosmological theories predict the same observations except that one says the universe contains far more observers than the other; physicists plan an experiment to tell them apart, but the presumptuous philosopher says the experiment is pointless, since SSA already tells us to favor the many-observer theory overwhelmingly just from the number of observers it implies.

A defender of SSA could say that being 'presumptuous' isn't the same as being wrong, and that the Doomsday Argument shows the reference-class reasoning behind SSA has bite in other, less embarrassing cases too, so the theory shouldn't be thrown out just because one application feels weird."
