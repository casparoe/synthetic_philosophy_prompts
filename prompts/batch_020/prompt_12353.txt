I'm a PhD student working in mathematical logic, currently splitting my attention between proof theory and the formal side of AI safety, and for the past few weeks I've been chewing on the so-called Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents. The problem traces back to Löb's theorem: for a consistent formal system T that can express its own provability, T can prove the schema "if T proves P, then P" only for those P it can already prove outright; it cannot get this reflection principle as a free bonus for statements it hasn't already settled. That sounds like a curiosity confined to proof theory until you try to design an agent that wants to certify, in advance, that a successor built on the same axioms — say, after a self-rewrite or a resource expansion — will be trustworthy for actions neither of them has yet considered. The obvious certificate, something like "the successor only acts on P once it has proved P, and whatever it proves is true," is precisely the unrestricted reflection schema Löb's theorem forbids. So the parent is caught: either it settles the object-level question itself, which makes deferring to the successor pointless, or it hands over trust on some weaker basis that isn't backed by the kind of proof it demanded of itself. Every fix I've come across — hierarchies of successively weaker successor systems, probabilistic rather than logical reflection — buys only a finite amount of slack before the same wall reappears one level up.

What keeps pulling me back to this, beyond its appeal as a pure logic puzzle, is how faithfully it seems to formalize something we all do constantly with much less rigor and much more anxiety: mentors vouching for students, one generation of an institution certifying the next, a person mid-transformation trying to decide how much to trust who they will have become on the other side. A friend in my reading group joked that Löb's theorem is really just a proof that you cannot write your successor a blank check, however carefully you raised them. I would like to see that joke taken seriously.

Could you write me a short story, on the order of 1000–1800 words, built around this specific bind rather than around a generic tale of AI misalignment or rebellion? I don't especially mind whether the agents involved are literal artificial minds, a lineage of formally-trained clerics, or something else entirely — a research team about to hand off control of an aging verification system, and an order of monastic logicians each required to certify the next before stepping down, would both work equally well — but I would like the plot to actually turn on the structure of the obstacle, the impossibility of an unrestricted, in-advance certificate of a successor's trustworthiness, rather than have it sit in the background as set dressing, and ideally without any character simply reciting the theorem at the reader. I would also rather you not resolve the bind too neatly: part of what makes it interesting to me is that none of the known workarounds are fully satisfying, and a story willing to sit with that would serve me better than one that discovers a clean escape.
