A friend and I spent a long dinner arguing about the "emergent abilities" debate in machine learning: the claim that large language models suddenly acquire skills like multi-step arithmetic or reliable in-context learning once they cross some scale threshold, rather than improving at them gradually. We couldn't agree how seriously to take the word "emergent" here, especially since some researchers now argue these apparent jumps are largely artifacts of using discontinuous, pass/fail metrics, and that the same underlying performance looks smooth and predictable once you switch to a continuous one. It struck me afterward that John Stuart Mill had already worked out the sharp version of this question in A System of Logic, where he distinguished "homopathic" effects, in which the joint result of several causes is simply the sum of what each would have produced alone, from "heteropathic" effects, such as a chemical reaction, whose product cannot be deduced from the separate properties of its ingredients no matter how thoroughly those are understood. Mill was confident that chemistry harbored genuine heteropathic laws of this kind, while being comparatively open to psychology someday reducing to physiology. Arguing only from the distinctions Mill himself drew: would he treat a capability jump of the sort at issue as a heteropathic effect, or does his own account of homopathic, additive causation already hand the skeptics their diagnosis -- that what looks like a qualitative jump is really just a smooth process viewed through a measure too coarse to show it? And of his two paradigm cases, is whatever is happening inside these models closer to his chemistry or to his psychology?
