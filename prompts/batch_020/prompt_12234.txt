A friend of mine, who works on intergenerational choice theory in an economics department and is considerably more comfortable with the relevant formalism than I am, and I spent much of a long train journey arguing about whether utilitarian aggregation can survive contact with an infinite universe. The trigger was an offhand remark about cosmology: on many current models space is infinite, so if value and disvalue are distributed through it the way our best guesses suggest, any two candidate worlds we might want to compare will each contain infinitely much good and infinitely much bad, and the ordinary utilitarian move of simply adding everything up stops working — the sums fail to converge, and even where a limiting value can be coaxed out of them, it turns out to depend on seemingly arbitrary choices, such as the order in which people, times, or regions get enumerated. We spent a couple of hours going back and forth about which of the standard proposed fixes for this, in what's sometimes called "infinite ethics," is actually least objectionable, and neither of us came away satisfied: whichever fix I favoured at a given moment, she would point out that it smuggled in exactly the kind of arbitrary, non-impartial stipulation — a discount rate, a privileged sequence of regions, a choice of limiting procedure — that it was supposed to avoid.

I would like a more careful adjudication than the two of us managed to reach on our own.
