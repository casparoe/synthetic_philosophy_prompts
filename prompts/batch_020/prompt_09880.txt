the question we gave was: "Explain what the Common Prior Assumption is and what work it's doing in Aumann's Agreement Theorem. Your answer should cover (a) what the theorem actually proves, (b) the specific role the CPA plays as a premise in that proof, and (c) whether the theorem shows that two honest, rational people can never end up with different beliefs about the same question. Illustrate with a simple example."

and here's their answer, word for word:

"Aumann's Agreement Theorem (1976) says that if two Bayesian agents start out with the same prior probability distribution over the possible states of the world -- this shared starting point is the Common Prior Assumption -- and they each update on their own private information, then if their resulting posteriors for some event ever become common knowledge (both know it, both know the other knows it, and so on), those posteriors have to be equal. The intuitive idea is that a difference of opinion is really evidence: if I know your honest opinion and you know mine, and we're both rational, any gap between us is itself data we should update on, closing the gap until nothing's left to explain except different information. This is often summed up as 'rational agents cannot agree to disagree.'

The CPA is doing a lot of the work here. Without it, two Bayesians could learn each other's posterior and just keep disagreeing forever, since the whole convergence argument assumes any remaining difference has to come from different signals rather than different starting points. Really the CPA is just an application of Bayesian rationality itself -- since two rational agents given the same evidence should reach the same conclusions, requiring them to start from the same prior is simply what rationality demands of them.

For example, say two traders each get a private signal about whether a coin is fair or biased towards heads, and they both start with a 50/50 prior. If they announce their posteriors to each other and this becomes common knowledge, they have to end up assigning the coin the same probability of landing heads. This is also the logic behind Aumann's 'no-trade theorem': if it's common knowledge that a proposed trade looks good to both sides, no trade based purely on private info can actually happen, because your counterparty's willingness to trade is itself evidence you should update on.

Does this mean two honest, rational people can never really disagree? Not exactly -- the theorem gets over-read this way a lot, but really it just shows that persistent, common-knowledge disagreement between people who share a prior means something's broken, like one side not being fully honest about their posterior, or common knowledge never actually being reached. Real people basically never have true common knowledge of each other's exact posteriors, so it's more of an idealized benchmark than a promise that arguments will resolve."
