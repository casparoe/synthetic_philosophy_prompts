"Quick recap: OpenAI's Eliciting Latent Knowledge report imagines a SmartVault protecting a diamond, watched by a predictor network that predicts future camera frames given a proposed sequence of actions. We want a 'reporter' that reads the predictor's internal state and answers questions like 'is the diamond still in the vault' so a human overseer doesn't just have to trust the raw feed. The catch is a good enough burglar can tamper with the camera so the feed still looks normal after the diamond's gone, and since we can only train the reporter on human labels, the humans get fooled in exactly the tampering cases too. So a reporter that learns to predict 'what would a human say looking at this feed' (the human simulator) matches the training labels exactly as well as one that reads off what the predictor actually represents (the direct translator) -- gradient descent has no signal favoring the second one, which is obviously the one we actually want.

Does penalizing inconsistency over time fix it? Not really -- the report's own counterexample is that a human simulator can be made just as internally consistent as a direct translator, since its simulating a coherent human mind rather than spitting out noise, so a consistency check can't separate them. Since none of the fixes the report tries (consistency, cross-examination, etc) survive their own counterexamples, the report's conclusion is basically that ELK is proven impossible to solve with a purely behavioral training signal, which is a pretty rough result if you cared about interpretability-based alignment."
