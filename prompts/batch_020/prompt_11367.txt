My grandfather taught Islamic philosophy and theology for almost forty years, and when he passed away this spring I inherited several boxes of his books, among them a heavily annotated copy of Michael Marmura's translation of al-Ghazālī's Tahāfut al-Falāsifa. I've been working through it slowly this summer, before the rest of his library goes to his former students, partly as a way of spending a bit more time with what occupied him for so long. I've stalled, though, on the Seventeenth Discussion, where he lays out the argument against necessary causal connection that anyone who has taken a class on medieval philosophy seems to know in outline, but that I'm realizing I don't actually follow in its details. I'm retyping the relevant stretch below; my grandfather amended a couple of words in pencil, so this isn't quite anyone's published translation verbatim, but it's close to it:

"On our view, there is no necessary bond between what people are in the habit of calling a cause and what they are in the habit of calling its effect. Take any two distinct things, such that the one simply is not the other, and such that neither the affirming of the one requires the affirming of the other, nor the denying of the one requires the denying of the other: it cannot then be necessary, merely from the existence of the first, that the second should exist, nor necessary, merely from the nonexistence of the first, that the second should fail to exist. So it is with thirst being quenched and the drinking of water, satiety and the eating of food, the burning of cotton and its contact with fire, daylight and the sun's having risen, a man's death and the severing of his head, healing and the swallowing of medicine, the purging of the bowels and the taking of a purgative - and so through everything observed to run in tandem in medicine, in astronomy, and in the crafts. What ties each such pair together is nothing but God's prior decree to produce the two side by side; it is not that the things are bound to one another in themselves in a way that could never be undone. It lies within His power to produce satiety with no eating at all, to bring about death with no severing of the head, to let life continue after the head has been severed, and so on for every such pair. The philosophers deny that any of this is so much as possible, and insist that it could not be otherwise."

Two things about this are giving me trouble, and I would rather work through them properly than get back something along the lines of "Ghazali denied that causes necessitate their effects." First, the abstract principle in the second sentence looks like it's meant to carry the whole argument: any two non-identical things, lacking mutual entailment between them, cannot necessitate one another. But that principle looks far too strong, since it would seem to rule out plenty of connections nobody wants to give up - a triangle's having two equal sides given that it has two equal angles, for instance, where the properties in question are distinct yet necessarily linked. Is he tacitly restricting the argument to some narrower class of "things" - created substances and their accidents, say - in a way that would exempt logical or mathematical necessities, and if so, on what grounds? Second, the examples he actually reaches for - thirst and drinking, purgatives and purging - are all empirical, contingent-sounding pairings, which makes me wonder whether the real engine of the argument is this "no entailment between distinct things" principle at all, or a quieter empiricist point about observation only ever delivering constant conjunction, merely dressed in the language of logical entailment. Do those read to you as one argument or two, and if two, which one is actually doing the work?
