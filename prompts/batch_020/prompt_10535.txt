Mira is my granddaughter, and she is one of seven students graduating this June from the small, ungraded school she has attended since she was twelve — the kind of place with no grades, no class rank, and no fixed timetable, where the students mostly choose their own projects and the adults are really just people who happen to know things and are willing to be asked. I never went past high school myself and spent my working life running a hardware store, but watching Mira go from a shut-down, miserable ten-year-old at her previous school to someone who will now cheerfully explain soil chemistry to anyone who stands still long enough made me curious enough to start reading about education on my own time, the way other people take up woodworking or genealogy once they have the hours to spare. A teacher at the school eventually put a battered copy of Alfred North Whitehead's "The Aims of Education" in my hands, and something in it clicked for me: his idea that learning has a natural rhythm rather than a straight-line progression — a stage he called "romance," when a subject first seizes you before you can even say what you are looking at; then a stage of "precision," when you drill down into rules, technique, and exact detail; and only after that a stage of "generalization," when the technique sinks below the surface and you are left with a wide, free command of the whole thing. He argued that ordinary schooling goes wrong constantly by forcing precision on children before they have ever been permitted their romance, which leaves them stocked with what he called "inert ideas" — things they technically know but have never once used for anything.

The school has asked me, rather than one of the actual teachers, to give the short address at the graduation, and I suspect it is precisely because I never went to college. The room will be an odd mixture: the seven graduates themselves, who will switch off the moment anything sounds like a lecture; the founder and the two teachers, who could recite Whitehead's three stages in their sleep and will notice at once if I reduce his ideas to a cliché; and a good number of aunts, uncles, and family friends who thought the whole school was a reckless experiment and are still privately wondering whether the children have actually learned anything, given that none of them can point to a single grade or transcript to prove it. I would like you to write this speech for me — something that runs to roughly five minutes read aloud. I want Whitehead's rhythm of romance, precision, and generalization to do real work in it rather than sit there as a nice quotation: it should answer the doubtful relatives on Whitehead's own terms, by making the case that what looked to them like an absence of rigor was really rigor arriving on its proper schedule, while also giving the graduates themselves something true and specific about the threshold they are standing on — all without it ever sounding like I am handing them a lecture on the philosophy of education.
