A running joke with a friend in quantitative finance has stopped feeling like a joke now that I have real money riding on the answer. After a chunk of my equity vested last quarter, we sometimes bet small amounts for fun — for instance, a coin flip where I win $25 on heads and lose $20 on tails. I always decline, even though it favors me on average, simply because losing the $20 would bother me more than winning $25 would please me. He says the standard economic explanation for that feeling is that money has diminishing value to me at the margin, and he worked out that if this is genuinely my whole reason, and it holds at every wealth level near mine, then extrapolating the way these arguments usually go, I should also refuse a bet where I could lose a few hundred dollars for a real shot at a life-changing sum. Since he's confident I would take a good enough version of that second bet, he concludes that "diminishing value of money" can't be my real reason, and that my reluctance on the coin flip is just an inconsistent, irrational quirk rather than a defensible attitude toward risk. I don't feel irrational, and declining still seems sensible to me, but I can't locate the flaw in his reasoning. Now that I'm deciding how to actually invest this money rather than just settle friendly bets, I'd like to know: is he right that my reluctance can only be chalked up to bias, or is there a coherent way to defend it?
