I'm several drafts into a novel narrated from close within the mind of Émilie du Châtelet during her final years at Cirey and Lunéville, and I'd rather her philosophical interior feel earned than borrowed from the usual "brilliant woman of the Enlightenment" summary. To that end I've been working slowly through her one openly personal piece of writing, the *Discours sur le bonheur*, checking my own rough French against the sense as I go, and I've stalled on a passage that sits right at the center of what I want the novel to dramatize: a mathematician who spent her life translating Newton's *Principia* ends up, in her most private text, arguing against the very faculty she elsewhere trusted without reservation.

Here is the passage, first as she wrote it and then in my own working translation:

"Il faut, pour être heureux, s'être défait des préjugés, être vertueux, se bien porter, avoir des goûts et des passions, être susceptible d'illusions, car nous devons la plupart de nos plaisirs à l'illusion, et malheureux est celui qui la perd. Loin donc de chercher à la faire disparaître par le flambeau de la raison, tâchons d'épaissir le vernis qu'elle met sur la plupart des objets ; il leur est encore plus nécessaire que ne le sont à nos corps les soins et la parure."

"To be happy, one must have rid oneself of prejudices, be virtuous, be in good health, have tastes and passions, and be susceptible to illusions — for we owe most of our pleasures to illusion, and unhappy is the one who loses it. Far, then, from trying to dispel it with the torch of reason, let us try instead to thicken the varnish that illusion lays over most objects; it is even more necessary to them than grooming and adornment are to our bodies."

What I can't work out is how much weight "illusion" is meant to carry as a term of art here, and I'd value a close reading rather than one that smooths the difficulty over. In the same sentence she lists having "rid oneself of prejudices" as a separate condition for happiness, so préjugé and illusion are apparently not just two names for the same danger — is there a principled line she is drawing between the false belief we must clear away and the one we must instead cultivate and thicken, or is this a seam in the argument rather than a real distinction? Relatedly, is the "varnish" meant fairly literally, as something like sustained self-deception about how things actually stand, or is it closer to an aesthetic or attentional disposition — nearer the willing suspension of disbelief one grants a play than a false factual belief? And is there any principled way to square this defense of illusion with her rationalism elsewhere, or should I simply take the collision at face value as the point she is making? I would rather have the honest complexity than a tidy resolution, since the tension itself is what I am hoping to give my narrator.
