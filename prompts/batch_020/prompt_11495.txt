So this happened to a friend of mine last year, and I can't stop turning it over in my head as a possible case study for my rational choice seminar -- even though on the surface it doesn't look like philosophy at all. My friend Dana and her brother jointly inherited their late mom's lake cottage. Neither of them wanted to just sell it and split the proceeds -- too much sentimental stuff wrapped up in who got to keep it -- so instead of working it out between themselves, they each hired a lawyer to fight for a bigger share in the court partition proceeding. Every time one lawyer filed a motion, the other side's lawyer fired back with a counter-motion, and the legal bills ticked up another few thousand each round. Neither would back down, because, as Dana put it to me, quitting now would mean everything she'd already spent was for nothing AND her brother would basically win. Eighteen months and something north of $70k combined in legal fees later -- on a cottage worth maybe $180k -- the place finally got sold off, with almost nothing left for either of them once the lawyers were paid. Dana told me that if she could redo it, she'd have walked away in month two and just eaten the loss.

I mentioned all this to a colleague over coffee and she just shrugged and said "yeah, sunk cost fallacy, textbook case" and went back to her grading. That answer's been nagging at me since, though, because it doesn't quite capture what actually trapped them -- it wasn't just that each of them was anchored on money already spent, it was that they were reacting to eachother the whole way through. Is there a more specific structure at work here, something to do with this being a genuine contest between two parties rather than one person facing a private decision? And here's the part I really can't resolve: was there ever a point in those eighteen months where escalating was actually the individually rational move for Dana, fully anticipating how things would probably end -- or were she and her brother doomed the moment they each lawyered up, no matter how clearly either of them reasoned it through? I'd love your take, ideally with enough analytical bite that I can actually use it with my students, rather than landing on "they should've communicated better."
