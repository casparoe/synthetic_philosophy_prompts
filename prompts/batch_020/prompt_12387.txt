I've spent years reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum, and a reading group I'm part of has recently been working through the "coherence arguments" case for expected utility maximization: the move, laid out most cleanly in Yudkowsky's "Coherent decisions imply consistent utilities," from "an agent that violates axiom X can be money-pumped" to "sufficiently advanced agents will end up satisfying axiom X" — which is supposed to be part of the basic case for expecting advanced AI systems to end up as goal-directed expected-utility-maximizers. The transitivity case has always struck me as close to airtight: if an agent strictly prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A, you can walk it around that cycle charging a small fee at every step, and there's no policy short of simply not having had those preferences in the first place that lets it escape.

Completeness is the axiom I've never been able to convince myself deserves the same treatment, even though people often write as if an exactly parallel pump is available for it. When I tried to construct one myself — imagine an agent with a preferential gap between A and B and between A− and B, but a strict preference for A over A−, then walk it from A down to B and down again to A− — I noticed that the agent can dodge the whole thing for free just by adopting the policy "never choose, at a later node, an option I strictly disprefer to something I already turned down earlier." That policy never requires the agent to act against any preference it actually has, since by hypothesis it has no strict preference over the gappy pairs at all, and yet it seems to defang the pump completely, with nothing analogous available on the transitivity side. I gather this is close to the point Elliott Thornley presses in a post titled "There are no coherence theorems," where he runs the same kind of objection against both Yudkowsky's framing and a more careful, deliberately "forcing" construction due to the philosopher Johan Gustafsson — though I've never worked out for myself whether the escape route survives contact with Gustafsson's tougher version, or only the easier one.

What I'd like help with is the mechanism, not a verdict on who wins. Concretely: is the real disanalogy between the two money pumps just the forcing/non-forcing distinction — the transitivity pump never lets the agent decline a trade it doesn't strictly prefer, while the naive completeness pump does — or is something deeper about the difference between indifference and a mere preferential gap (the fact that gaps, unlike indifference, aren't sensitive to small sweetenings) doing the real work of letting a "don't reverse yourself" policy get off the ground at all? And does that same kind of policy, suitably strengthened, actually survive Gustafsson's forcing money pump, or is that the point at which the disanalogy with transitivity finally collapses? I'd rather come away understanding exactly where the load-bearing step is than just be told which side currently has the better of the argument.
