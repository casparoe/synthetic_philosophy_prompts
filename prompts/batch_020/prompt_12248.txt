"Street's dilemma starts from the fact that natural selection has had a huge influence on the content of our evaluative attitudes -- we tend to value things like our own survival, our children's welfare, and cooperating with people who cooperate with us, all because ancestors with those attitudes tended to out-reproduce ancestors without them. She then presses realists, who think evaluative facts hold independently of anyone's attitudes, to explain how these selective pressures relate to the independent truths.

There are two horns. On the first, there's no relation between the selective pressures and the independent evaluative truths, in which case it would be a wild coincidence if selection just happened to instill mostly true evaluative attitudes in us, so realists are stuck with a sweeping skepticism about our evaluative judgments. On the second horn, there is some relation, and Street thinks the only credible way to cash this out is with an "adaptive link account" on which selection favored attitudes because of the fitness-enhancing behavior they produced, not because the attitudes were true. She argues this account is more scientifically respectable than positing that selection was somehow tracking independent evaluative facts, since it explains the same patterns more economically.

Since realism can't plausibly fill in the relation between selection and truth, Street concludes that there are no independent evaluative truths at all, i.e. she ends up endorsing an error theory like Mackie's, on which moral claims are systematically false. This is supposed to be the "safe" conclusion, since it doesn't require positing any spooky non-natural properties for selection to have somehow tracked.

Enoch's response is to bite the bullet and defend the tracking account directly: he says that of course selection would favor creatures that could detect that survival is good, so it's not really a coincidence after all that our attitudes track the truth. I don't think this works, because it's still mysterious how a physical process like selection could be sensitive to non-natural evaluative facts, so Street's challenge survives."
