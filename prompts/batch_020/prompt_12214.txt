I'm a PhD student in computer science working on algorithmic fairness, and this term I'm TAing an interdisciplinary seminar, cross-listed with philosophy, that spends a couple of sessions on the impossibility results that grew out of the COMPAS/ProPublica dispute: the Kleinberg-Mullainathan-Raghavan and Chouldechova results showing that once base rates differ across groups, no imperfect classifier can simultaneously achieve calibration within groups, equal false positive rates, and equal false negative rates. That's a large part of why Northpointe and ProPublica were able to both be technically right while talking past each other. Every year, once the class has absorbed the impossibility, someone asks the obvious follow-up: fine, but if you actually had to ship a pretrial risk tool, which single criterion would you commit to? I've never been satisfied with my own answer on the spot, and most treatments of this material stop at contrasting statistical parity with equalized odds, without ever putting the fuller menu of criteria head-to-head.

I'd like your help thinking this through more carefully. Consider these eight criteria, all of which appear somewhere in the literature spawned by this dispute: statistical parity, conditional statistical parity, equalized odds, equal opportunity, predictive parity/calibration within groups, treatment equality, counterfactual fairness, and individual fairness. Now suppose a jurisdiction has accepted the impossibility result and wants to name exactly one of these as the formal compliance standard for a pretrial risk-assessment tool, treating violations of the rest as unavoidable trade-offs rather than independently actionable failures.
