I am serving as a teaching assistant for a graduate seminar on patristic and medieval theories of lying and deception, and I would like an independent read on one response to the take-home midterm before grades are finalized.

The question: "Augustine is often described as an absolutist about lying. Drawing on De Mendacio and Contra Mendacium: (a) state Augustine's definition of a lie, and explain why, on this definition, a false assertion need not be a lie while a true assertion can be one; (b) explain Augustine's position on 'officious' lies — lies told for someone else's benefit — and how it bears on his absolutism; (c) explain how Augustine's dispute with Jerome over the interpretation of Galatians 2:11–14 illustrates what is at stake in his position." (Students were told to aim for roughly 250 words.)

The student's answer: "Augustine defines a lie not as the mere assertion of something false, but as a false statement made with the intention to deceive (the voluntas fallendi, or 'will to deceive'). This is why someone who sincerely asserts a falsehood they believe to be true is not lying, while someone who says something literally true in order to mislead their audience is lying: what matters is the speaker's intent, not the objective truth-value of the sentence uttered.

Augustine is a rigorist about officious lies — lies told to benefit someone else — and denies that a good motive can excuse them, holding that the sin remains even in the hardest cases, such as a lie that would save an innocent life. Refining this position elsewhere, however, Augustine classifies lies into three kinds — officious, jocose, and malicious — of which only the malicious lie, told purely to injure, is a mortal sin; officious and jocose lies remain sins, but only venial ones.

This same rigorism drives Augustine's quarrel with Jerome over Galatians 2. Jerome had proposed, following earlier commentators, that Paul's public rebuke of Peter at Antioch was a staged piece of pedagogy rather than a real dispute. Augustine sided with this reading, arguing that such a well-intentioned fiction was no different in kind from the officious lies he elsewhere treats as merely venial."
