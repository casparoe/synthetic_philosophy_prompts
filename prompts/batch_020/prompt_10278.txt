I'm writing a historical novel with a subplot centered on Charles Dodgson — Lewis Carroll — drawing on his real years as a mathematics don and member of the Governing Body at Christ Church. Between 1873 and 1876 he wrote a series of pamphlets on committee elections, coined the term "cycle" for the situation where preferences among three or more candidates loop with no clear winner, and grappled with the danger of voters misrepresenting their preferences to secure a better outcome. For one scene, I want to imagine him living just long enough to be shown the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem: the result, proved decades after his death, that any such voting procedure must either be a dictatorship, where one voter's preference always prevails, or remain open to exactly the kind of strategic misrepresentation he had already worried about. Reasoning from what's actually known of his mathematical temperament and his own unfinished struggle to design a scheme immune to such tricks, how do you think he would have responded to learning that his project was, in a precise sense, impossible from the outset — resignation, denial, some idiosyncratic workaround, or a paradox-lover's quiet satisfaction? I'd like an answer specific and character-consistent enough to adapt into a page of his internal monologue, not a generic gloss on the theorem.
