My gogo died last Sunday and I've somehow ended up being the one to give the eulogy at the funeral in the Eastern Cape next weekend, which is stressing me out more than I expected. Part of it is that I only ever saw her every year or two growing up (we live in London), and now I have to stand up and talk about her like I really knew her. She was Xhosa, and everyone keeps saying the eulogy should be built around ubuntu, but I've realized I only ever knew that as the Desmond Tutu poster version — "I am because we are" — until my cousin, who's doing a philosophy PhD, mentioned offhand that there's a real argument about what "umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu" (or however it's actually spelled, I've seen a couple of versions) is even supposed to mean. Apparently a guy called Menkiti thought personhood is something you genuinely earn through your relations over time, to the point that a newborn, or a really isolated and selfish adult, isn't fully a person yet, while another philosopher, Gyekye, pushed back hard because that seems to leave no room for any individual self to exist before community in the first place, and then a third guy, Metz, who's more recent, apparently reframes the whole thing as a claim about moral character rather than metaphysics. Now I can't just write the nice version anymore — I keep getting stuck on which of these she'd have actually meant, if any.

The other problem is that the funeral is split right down the middle: half the room is family from the village who'll take the ancestor stuff completely seriously (including, I think, some real uneasyness about how you're even supposed to talk about her now, since technically she just became one), and the other half is my dad's side and my own friends flying in from London, who mostly think ubuntu is a Lion King reference and will also, out of pure habit, expect the standard eulogy about what a strong, self-made, independent woman she was — which is almost the opposite of what this philosophy is actually claiming. She ran the local stokvel for something like thirty years and pretty much co-raised half the kids on her street, so there's good material, I just don't know how to use it right. Could you actually help me write the eulogy? I don't need a philosophy lecture, I need something that takes the relational-personhood idea seriously enough that it's doing real work in the speech — maybe even the idea that a eulogy is itself one of the things that keeps a person a person — while still being about five minutes long and making a room full of non-philosophers cry for the right reasons instead of just sitting there confused.
