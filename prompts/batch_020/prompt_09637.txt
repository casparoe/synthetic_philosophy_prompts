Every summer, our neighborhood association runs a charity raffle, and this year ticket holders faced two separate, unrelated choices at two different booths.

At the first booth, I could redeem my ticket for a guaranteed $1,000 gift card, or drop it into a drum where 90 of 100 tickets pay that same $1,000, 9 pay a $5,000 travel voucher, and 1 pays nothing. I took the guaranteed card, like almost everyone ahead of me in line.

At the second booth, the choice was between a drum where 10 of 100 tickets pay $1,000 and the rest pay nothing, or one where 9 of 100 tickets pay $5,000 and the rest pay nothing. There I picked the $5,000 drum, and so did most people around me.

A friend who's an actuary told me afterward that no consistent way of weighing money against risk can justify both choices — that picking the guaranteed card over the first drum while picking the $5,000 drum over the second isn't just unusual, it's a contradiction, whatever my personal appetite for risk happens to be. I've checked his reasoning and can't find the flaw, yet each choice still feels obviously right on its own. Was I really being irrational, and if so, does it actually matter — or is my friend wrong to assume that "rational" choices about risk have to add up the way his math demands?
