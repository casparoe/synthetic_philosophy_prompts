In my current course on collective choice and bargaining theory, we recently moved from Nash's classic bargaining solution to the comparatively less-discussed Kalai–Smorodinsky (KS) solution, which drops Nash's independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives axiom in favor of a monotonicity condition defined relative to each bargainer's "ideal point" (the best outcome each party could get if the other were held to their disagreement value). I followed the axiomatic derivation well enough to reproduce it on a problem set, but I am struggling to see what the choice between these two solution concepts actually amounts to once you leave the blackboard and sit down at an actual negotiating table.

This isn't a purely academic puzzle for me at the moment. A close friend and I are planning to co-found a small tutoring company after graduation, and we will soon need to agree on how to split future profits and decision-making authority. Our positions are asymmetric in ways that seem relevant to this material: I already have a solid fallback job offer if the partnership falls apart, while she does not, and we each privately estimate our own best-case payoff from the venture rather differently.

Could you lay out the most important implications of the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution for how two parties in a situation like ours should think about reaching an agreement? I would especially like you to address: (a) what role our respective fallback positions and self-estimated "best-case" outcomes should play in what counts as a fair split under KS reasoning, as against what Nash's solution would have us focus on instead; (b) a concrete type of case in which the two solutions would recommend meaningfully different splits, so I can see the disagreement in action rather than just in the axioms; and (c) whether the fairness intuition behind KS actually survives contact with the fact that, unlike in the clean theoretical model, neither of us can simply read our "ideal point" off an objectively given feasible set — it has to be estimated, and each of us has some incentive to shade that estimate in our own favor. On balance, do you think this makes KS-style reasoning less trustworthy as a practical guide than it first appears, relative to Nash's approach?
