So I've been down a rabbit hole this week for a piece I'm working on about the deep history of monetary theory, and it turns out George Berkeley -- yes, the "to be is to be perceived" guy -- spent a chunk of the 1730s writing this strange little pamphlet called The Querist, basically a long list of leading questions about Ireland's economy. The gist, if Im remembering it right, is that he didn't think money was wealth at all, just a "ticket" or counter for moving real wealth around, and he wanted Ireland to set up a national bank issuing paper currency backed by the industry and credit of the people rather than by gold or silver sitting in a vault. Which is basically fighting words to Locke, who decades earlier had gone to war (rhetorically) defending the idea that a coin's value is anchored in the actual silver it contains, and that any government messing with that link was courting disaster.

They never argued face to face, obviously, but I can't stop imagining what that arguement would actually sound like, especially with everyone rehashing some version of it right now over crypto and central bank digital currencies. So: could you write me a dialogue between Locke and Berkeley hashing this out directly, like they've somehow been sat down in a room together? I don't want either of them to be a pushover -- Locke should really press Berkeley on what stops the state (or a bank) from just printing tickets forever once you sever money from anything scarce, and Berkeley needs an actual comeback rather than just repeating himself, and the same the other way around when Berkeley presses Locke on why silver gets to be the one thing that's "really" valuable. A page or so of genuine back-and-forth, please, not a lecture with two names slapped on it.
