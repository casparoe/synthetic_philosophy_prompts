I've been half-lurking on effective altruism forums for the past year (I know, I know) and theres this objection that keeps coming up called "cluelessness" whenever people argue about whether, say, funding malaria nets is actually better in expectation than funding some long-shot AI safety org. The gist seems to be that we're not just uncertain about the far-future ripple effects of our actions, we're fundamentally clueless about them, so any confident consequentialist "this action has the best expected outcome" calculation is kind of hollow underneath. Feels a bit like debugging a system where you can never actually see the full call stack, you just have to hope nothing downstream blows up.

Anyway I got curious where this idea actually comes from, and somebody in a comment thread said it basically traces back to G.E. Moore, of all people, in a 1903 book called Principia Ethica. I dug up the passage they meant, from a chapter called "Ethics in Relation to Conduct," and honestly its rough going — dense, semicolon-heavy, turn-of-the-century academic English (he spells "showing" as "shewing" at one point, which threw me for a second). Here's the bit I copied out, skipping a paragraph in the middle where he numbers a couple more sub-points that seemed less central:

"But it is obvious that our causal knowledge alone is far too incomplete for us ever to assure ourselves of this result, that an action is our duty: we can never be sure that any action will produce the greatest value possible. Ethics, therefore, is quite unable to give us a list of duties: but there still remains a humbler task which may be possible for Practical Ethics. Although we cannot hope to discover which, in a given situation, is the best of all possible alternative actions, there may be some possibility of shewing which among the alternatives, likely to occur to any one, will produce the greatest sum of good. This second task is certainly all that Ethics can ever have accomplished: and it is certainly all that it has ever collected materials for proving; since no one has ever attempted to exhaust the possible alternative actions in any particular case.

[...]

(3) Even this latter task is immensely difficult, and no adequate proof that the total results of one action are superior to those of another, has ever been given. For (a) we can only calculate actual results within a comparatively near future. We must, therefore, assume that no results of the same action in the infinite future beyond, will reverse the balance — an assumption which perhaps can be, but certainly has not been, justified…"

Can someone help me actually unpack what Moore is arguing here in plain English? Specifically: is this literally the same worry as the "cluelessness" thing from the forums, or is it a related-but-distinct problem that just looks similar on the surface? And that last bit is what really gets me — he basically admits the assumption that far-future effects won't reverse the balance "has not been justified," and then just moves on to the next section as if that's a settled matter. Does he ever grapple with what that admission does to his whole theory of duty, or is there some move I'm missing that lets him get away with it? Would love a read from someone who actually knows this stuff, since I clearly don't have the philosophy chops to place it myself.
