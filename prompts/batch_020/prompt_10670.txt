Since retiring after a long career in experimental physics -- much of it spent on experiments built around the hunt for particle interactions so rare that a null result, patiently accumulated over years, counts as real evidence in its own right -- I have taken up auditing courses in the philosophy department at my local university, partly out of curiosity and partly to keep my mind occupied. This term I have become friendly with the instructor of an introductory seminar in philosophy of religion, who is short-handed on graders and asked whether I might look over a few short-answer exam scripts on the problem of divine hiddenness. I found the unit unexpectedly congenial: the underlying logical shape of the argument -- what we ought to expect to observe if a hypothesis were true, and what we are entitled to conclude from its stubborn absence -- felt oddly familiar from my own working life. That said, I am still enough of a newcomer to philosophy that I do not trust my own eye for where the real subtleties lie, and I would rather not shortchange a student whose answer is broadly capable but may contain a slip that is easy for a non-specialist like me to miss.

The question set to the students was: "Explain J. L. Schellenberg's argument from divine hiddenness. Be sure to (a) lay out its key premises and explain the role played by the notion of 'nonresistant nonbelief'; (b) explain how the argument differs from the traditional argument from evil; and (c) present and assess one major objection to the argument." One student's script reads, in full:

"According to Schellenberg, God, if he exists, is by definition perfectly loving, and a perfectly loving being would want to be in a personal, reciprocal relationship with every human being capable of one. Since you cannot knowingly enter into a relationship with someone whose existence you don't even believe in, a perfectly loving God would see to it that everyone capable of relationship who was not resisting it actually believed. Schellenberg calls the failure to believe under these circumstances 'nonresistant nonbelief': nonbelief that isn't the nonbeliever's own fault, as in the case of a sincere seeker who has honestly searched and simply never found sufficient evidence. Since nonresistant nonbelief plainly occurs, no perfectly loving God exists, and therefore, since any God worth the name would have to be perfectly loving, God does not exist.

"This is a different argument from the traditional argument from evil, even though both are evidential rather than logical arguments: neither claims theism is self-contradictory, only that some feature of the world counts as evidence against it. The argument from evil points to the amount and distribution of suffering as the problematic feature; Schellenberg's argument instead points to the distribution of belief itself. The thought that hiddenness might count against God's goodness is not entirely new: Pascal, writing in the Pensées, already treated God's hiddenness as itself evidence against a perfectly good God standing behind it, though it was left to Schellenberg to turn the observation into a fully explicit argument.

"The strongest reply is what is usually called the skeptical theist response, defended in different forms by Hick and Swinburne: unmistakable, forced knowledge of God's existence would leave human beings no real choice about whether to enter into relationship with him, so a degree of hiddenness is required precisely in order to make a free, and therefore genuine, response possible. On this view, the very feature Schellenberg treats as damning evidence -- that belief takes effort and is not simply given to us -- is what makes an authentic relationship with God possible at all, since love that is coerced is not really love. I find this a genuinely strong reply, since it explains hiddenness as a cost worth paying for the sake of freedom rather than as a brute, unexplained gap."
