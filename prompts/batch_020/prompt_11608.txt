After a forty-year career as an analytical chemist, I retired three years ago and have spent much of the time since auditing philosophy of language courses at the local university — partly for the fun of it, partly out of a nagging suspicion that philosophers borrow examples from my old discipline without quite respecting how untidy the underlying chemistry actually is. Through the audit I ended up on the organizing committee for the department's public philosophy-of-science series, and next month I am interviewing a philosopher who works in the Kripke–Putnam tradition on names and natural kinds. She defends the claim that terms like "water," "gold," and "tiger" are rigid designators, so that theoretical identity statements such as "water is H2O" or "heat is the motion of molecules" turn out, on her view, to be necessary truths that we nonetheless discover only a posteriori. I have read Naming and Necessity and enough of the secondary literature to recognize the standard moves, and I would rather not sit through forty minutes of her simply restating the headline thesis for a lay audience.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that press on the places where I, as a chemist, find the picture shakiest, with a short follow-up after each keyed to the answer she is most likely to give? I would like at least one question on the jade case — two chemically unrelated minerals that were called by a single name for centuries before anyone could tell them apart — and what that does to the idea that a natural-kind term always tracks one hidden essence; one on isotopic variants such as heavy water, and whether the rigidity thesis has the resources to say whether D2O counts as "water" or not; one on the Twin Earth thought experiment, pressed on whether it actually shows that meaning is not "in the head" or merely relocates the question of what counts as the same substance; one on whether the same story is meant to extend to species names, given that biological species are populations with fuzzy boundaries rather than kinds with a single defining microstructure; and one on the two-dimensionalist rescue of the necessary a posteriori via primary and secondary intensions, and whether she would regard that apparatus as vindicating Kripke's picture or quietly replacing it with something weaker. Please close with a question asking her to specify the one empirical discovery that would actually lead her to abandon rigid designation for natural-kind terms specifically, rather than merely complicating its details.
