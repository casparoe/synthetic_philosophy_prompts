Over the past few weeks I have been TAing an introductory computability theory course while also sitting in on a philosophy of science seminar on the side, and the two have started to collide in an uncomfortable way. In section, I tell undergraduates, without much hesitation, that the Church-Turing thesis simply identifies the informal notion of an "effectively calculable" function with the formal notion of a Turing-machine-computable one. But in the seminar we have spent the last two meetings on the hypercomputation literature — infinite-time Turing machines that keep computing through transfinite ordinal stages, and the stranger case of Malament-Hogarth spacetimes, in which a device could run through an unending calculation along one worldline while a second observer, traveling along a suitably chosen path, receives news of the outcome after only a finite lapse of their own proper time — and most of the room treats it as obvious that physically building any such device would simply refute the Church-Turing thesis. That struck me as too quick. My own reading of Turing's 1936 paper is that he was analyzing what an idealized human clerk, following fixed mechanical rules, could accomplish, not what any physically constructible apparatus whatsoever could accomplish, and I recall that philosophers of computation such as Jack Copeland have pressed a similar distinction in arguing that hypercomputation is at least conceptually coherent. I have not settled the matter in my own mind, and I am expected to defend some position at next week's meeting.

Could you construct the strongest available argument for the position that the physical realizability of a hypercomputer — whether an infinite-time Turing machine, a Malament-Hogarth device, or anything else returning correct answers to problems no Turing machine can decide — would not, properly speaking, refute the Church-Turing thesis itself, but only a distinct and considerably bolder thesis about the computational powers of physical machines in general, one that neither Church nor Turing ever asserted? I would especially like you to engage with what seems to me the most damaging objection to this line of defense: that it saves the Church-Turing thesis only by trivializing it, since a thesis quietly narrowed to claims about idealized clerks with pencil and paper would no longer tell us anything about the actual limits of computation in our universe — so that the hypercomputation debate would simply be relocated rather than resolved.
