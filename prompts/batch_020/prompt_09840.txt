I moderated a panel on AI assistants recently, and afterward two of the panelists ended up arguing at the bar about whether chatbots are actually "agents," while I sat there nursing a beer, mostly eavesdropping. Honestly the best part of the night, and I still cant tell who won.

Theo (works on interpretability) opened with: chatbots aren't agents, they're simulators. The network's whole job is predicting the next token, so the "helpful assistant" persona is just a character it's simulating, same as it'd simulate a villain if you prompted it that way -- calling ChatGPT an agent is like calling a flight simulator a pilot.

Sana (works on RLHF at one of the labs) said fine, that's true of the base model, but finetuning on human preferences optimizes the whole system, end to end, to get reward across huge numbers of contexts -- so whatever comes out isn't simulating an agent anymore, it just is one. The mask became the face.

Theo shot back that you can jailbreak that same model into a totally different persona with the right prompt, which is exactly what you'd expect if "helpful assistant" is just the character training makes likely by default, not evidence of one persistent goal-driven thing underneath.

Sana said jailbreaking shows the agent's brittle, not that there's no agent -- plenty of things we call agents without hesitation, people included, can be talked out of their normal goals under weird enough conditions, and if brittleness disqualifies you from agency, most humans fail that test too.

Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who's actually right at each step, not just declare an overall winner? And is there a distinction neither of them reached for that would actually settle this, instead of them just trading intuitions back and forth?
