I'm writing a novel whose narrator resolves every January to quit gambling, holds firm for weeks, then empties his savings the moment a poker night comes up -- all while telling us, in the same breath, exactly how this will end. Over dinner I described him to two friends, Claire (finishing a philosophy PhD) and Ben (a behavioral economist), hoping they'd help me get him right, and they ended up arguing with each other instead.

Claire said this is a clean case of akrasia: the narrator judges, all things considered, that he should abstain, yet acts otherwise -- a gap philosophers since Socrates have called either impossible or deeply puzzling. Ben said the diagnosis is outdated: hyperbolic discounting shows there's no gap at all, just two perfectly consistent preference orderings held from two different temporal standpoints, and dressing that up as "weakness" is a moralized redescription of ordinary time-inconsistency. Claire replied that this leaves out something real: at the poker table, the narrator doesn't merely hold preferences he's indifferent between -- he still judges, in his own voice, that he shouldn't be doing this, and a model built entirely out of switched preferences has no room for that ongoing self-condemnation. Ben answered that the self-condemnation is just one more time-slice's preference, no less partial than the craving it condemns: the sober self disapproves of the excited one exactly as the excited self will later dismiss the sober one's caution, and calling either verdict the one that "really" speaks for the narrator is the move akrasia-talk uses to smuggle in a unified self the theory hasn't earned.

Neither would concede anything. Could you go through the exchange move by move, say who has the better of it at each stage rather than just declaring an overall winner, and note anything important that both of them are overlooking?
