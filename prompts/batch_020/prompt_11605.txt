I serve on my university's Academic Integrity Board, which adjudicates cases of alleged plagiarism and exam misconduct referred by the Dean of Students' office. Under our current bylaws, each case is heard by a panel of three faculty members drawn from a standing pool; all three read the same investigative file prepared by the Dean's office before voting, and a finding of responsibility requires unanimity, with a single dissenting vote resulting in dismissal of the charge and no avenue for appeal. After two panels reached opposite conclusions on strikingly similar cases last spring, a reform working group proposed replacing this system with seven-member panels decided by a 5-of-7 majority, arguing that larger panels are simply less likely to get the facts wrong and that requiring unanimity lets one idiosyncratic or biased panelist override the considered judgment of the other two. Several long-serving members are resisting the change, worried that abandoning unanimity will increase the number of findings against students who did nothing wrong. In defending their proposal, the reformers have leaned on the general idea that a large enough group of voters, each more likely right than wrong and judging independently of one another, will reach the correct verdict with near certainty as the group grows — while a skeptic on the committee has pointed out that our panelists do not decide independently in that sense, since they read a common file and deliberate together before balloting, and that the pool from which panelists are drawn already mixes tenured faculty with untenured and adjunct members whose experience with these cases differs considerably.

The Board votes on the proposal at its meeting in three weeks, and I have been asked to circulate a short written opinion beforehand. Please draft this memo. It should take a clear, specific position on panel size and voting rule — or propose some alternative the working group has not considered — and it should explain, on the merits, whether the reformers' appeal to the accuracy of large independent majorities actually supports their proposal once the skeptic's complications are taken into account. It should also engage directly with the worry that moving away from strict unanimity will increase wrongful findings, rather than dismissing that worry as a misunderstanding of the statistics. The recommendation should be concrete enough for the Board to vote on directly, including, if relevant, any procedural changes — such as how and when panelists register their views relative to deliberation — needed to make the underlying reasoning actually apply to our process.
