I have been teaching a graduate seminar on medieval Islamic philosophy this term, and this week we worked through the Seventeenth Discussion of al-Ghazali's Tahafut al-Falasifa together with Ibn Rushd's point-by-point rebuttal in the Tahafut al-Tahafut. My students were immediately struck by al-Ghazali's example of the fire and the cotton: he denies that there is any necessary connection between the two, arguing that the burning we observe is merely a habitual conjunction that God has instituted and could, at will, withhold, so that the true agent behind every event in nature is God rather than the fire itself. A few students were initially inclined to file this away as a theological curiosity, until we turned to Ibn Rushd's response, which presses hard on the thought that abandoning necessary causal connections in nature would make natural science, and demonstrative knowledge more generally, impossible.

For next week's discussion section, I would rather hand the students a dialogue than another secondary-literature summary. Could you write one, of roughly 700 to 900 words, staged as a direct, face-to-face exchange between al-Ghazali and Ibn Rushd, in which each philosopher responds to the other's actual arguments rather than simply restating his own position? I would like the exchange to move past the cotton-and-fire example itself and into the harder issues underneath it: whether occasionalism can really accommodate the stable regularities that everyday prediction and empirical inquiry both depend on, whether Ibn Rushd's necessitarian account of natural causation leaves enough room for the sort of divine power over nature, including miracles, that he could not simply disavow as a believing Muslim, and whether al-Ghazali's position, whatever his own intentions, ends up committing him to a broader skepticism about inductive inference. Please make both figures equally sharp philosophically, and have each one concede at least one genuine difficulty in his own view over the course of the exchange, rather than allowing them simply to talk past each other.
