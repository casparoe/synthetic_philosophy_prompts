I'm taking a graduate seminar on metaphysics this term, and this week's readings pair the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence with Max Black's 1952 paper "The Identity of Indiscernibles," in which Black imagines a universe containing nothing but two qualitatively identical iron spheres. I'm having trouble reconstructing the argument in the following passage from Leibniz's Fourth Paper to Clarke (in the original 1717 Clarke translation), which my professor says is the passage Black's thought experiment is really meant to target:

"3. 'Tis a thing indifferent to place three bodies, equal and perfectly alike, in any order whatsoever; and consequently they will never be placed in any order by Him who does nothing without wisdom. But then, He being the Author of things, no such things will be produced by Him at all; and consequently there are no such things in nature.

4. There is no such thing as two individuals indiscernible from each other. An ingenious gentleman of my acquaintance, discoursing with me in the presence of Her Electoral Highness the Princess Sophia, in the garden of Herrenhausen, thought he could find two leaves perfectly alike. The princess defied him to do it, and he ran all over the garden a long time to look for some, but it was to no purpose. Two drops of water, or milk, viewed with a microscope, will appear distinguishable from each other. ...

6. To suppose two things indiscernible is to suppose the same thing under two names. And therefore to suppose that the universe could have had at first another position of time and place than that which it actually had, and yet that all the parts of the universe should have had the same situation among themselves as that which they actually had, is an impossible fiction."

Two things about this are confusing me. First, section 3 already imagines "three bodies, equal and perfectly alike" — a scenario that looks structurally just like Black's spheres — yet Leibniz treats the mere conceivability of such bodies as grounds for concluding that none are ever actually produced, whereas Black treats an analogous scenario as grounds for concluding that the identity of indiscernibles is false. Are these really parallel thought experiments that simply reach opposite conclusions, or is something different going on in each? Second, could you walk me through exactly how the inference in section 6 — from "indiscernible" to "the same thing under two names" — is supposed to break down once we grant Black's spheres as a coherent possibility, given that section 6 looks like precisely the inference a "Leibniz-shift" argument needs?
