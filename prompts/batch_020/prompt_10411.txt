I'm in the final stretch of a PhD in cognitive science, where my dissertation concerns how people form confidence in predictions under uncertainty — which is probably why a recent dispute with my younger brother has been nagging at me far more than it reasonably should. Before this year's continental football championship, we each separately put money on the same long-shot team to win the whole tournament. I spent a couple of evenings working through squad depth charts, fixture congestion, and underlying performance statistics before settling on them; my brother backed the same team because, in his words, he'd had a good dream about their goalkeeper the night before, and by his own admission this kind of dream-based pick has hit for him about as often as chance alone would predict, across many years of similar bets. Improbably, the team won the tournament, and we each collected identical payouts from our separate wagers.

Ever since, my brother has insisted that his pick was "just as good" as mine, since we both ended up right about the same team and walked away with the same amount of money. When I objected that his reasoning doesn't deserve the same credit as mine, he made a fair point in response: my analysis could easily have failed too, since plenty of well-researched predictions do, so the only thing that actually distinguishes our two cases is that, this particular time, we both happened to be correct. He's willing to grant that my method might outperform his over many repeated bets, but insists that this is a separate question from whether his prediction on this one occasion was worth any less than mine, given that the matter is already settled, and settled the same way for both of us. I raised this in my department's decision-science reading group, expecting someone to make quick work of it, but people mostly laughed and told me to let it go. I can't shake the sense that they were too hasty: something does seem to distinguish the two predictions, but every version of that "something" I can think of collapses back into calling my brother lucky — which he reasonably notes doesn't disqualify his case without equally disqualifying mine, since my own correct call was, in some sense, lucky too. Is there a real, defensible difference in the value of what he and I each ended up with, or is my brother right that once an outcome is settled, the process behind someone's prediction stops mattering?
