I sit on the five-person hiring committee for a small nonprofit, and we recently had to choose among three finalists for an executive director position. To keep the process fair, we used a simple ranking system: each member privately ordered all three candidates from most to least preferred, and points were assigned by rank -- two for a first choice, one for a second, none for a third -- with the highest total winning. Under this system, one finalist, whom I'll call X, ended up narrowly ahead of a second, Y, while the third, Z, trailed far behind and was never seriously in contention. Before we could extend an offer, however, Z withdrew to take a position elsewhere, and our chair, purely as a formality, recalculated the totals with Z struck from every ballot. The result caught all of us off guard: with only X and Y left to compare, Y came out ahead of X, even though, as far as I can tell, not one committee member had changed their personal view of X versus Y.

This bothers me more than I expected it to. Losing a candidate with no realistic chance of winning shouldn't, on the face of it, have had any bearing on how the remaining two compared to each other -- and yet it evidently decided the outcome between them. A couple of committee members now suspect the process was somehow rigged or careless; others just want to treat whichever total we saw first as the "real" result and move on. None of us has any background in decision theory or political science, so it's possible we're missing something obvious, but I don't think anyone made an error, and I can't find anything wrong with the arithmetic. Is there a way of ranking or voting that would have avoided this particular reversal, or is this the sort of thing that any method for turning individual preferences into a single group decision is bound to run into sooner or later?
