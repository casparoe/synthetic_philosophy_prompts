I've been preparing a session for my graduate seminar on the philosophy of punishment around David Lewis's short 1997 paper "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" Yes, the same Lewis best known for modal realism and counterfactuals, here spending just a few dry, careful pages on a puzzle that starts in the criminal law and ends in the theology of the Atonement. Before I bring the material to class, I want to pressure-test an exercise built around its unresolved final move. Lewis's setup is simple: a guilty offender has been justly sentenced, and an innocent friend devoted to him volunteers to undergo the sentence in his place; would that give us any grounds for letting the actual offender go free? For prison terms and death sentences, Lewis takes it as obvious that we would refuse the swap outright, no matter how devoted the friend. But he then observes that most of us feel none of that same resistance when a friend discreetly covers someone else's substantial fine, even though a serious fine is supposed to function as genuine punishment rather than mere compensation to a victim. Lewis heads off the easiest way of dissolving this tension - the thought that we merely put up with fine-swapping because there is no practical way to stop it - by arguing that if this were really our attitude, the sheer ease of circumventing punishment-by-fine ought to count as a standing black mark against fines as a mode of punishment, and in practice nobody treats it that way. He leaves the puzzle open, remarking only that the Christians who explain the Atonement as penal substitution while still rejecting substitution for burglars and murderers, and the rest of us who reject it for burglars and murderers while still tolerating it for fines, are guilty of exactly the same pattern of inconsistency - which he offers as "some evidence that somehow they might both be right."

For the exercise, please write a dialogue between two philosophers who both accept Lewis's diagnosis - they agree that our ordinary beliefs about punishment really are inconsistent in just the way he describes - but who want to resolve that inconsistency in opposite directions. The first, call her the Restrictionist, holds that the fine case is the real outlier: a sufficiently severe fine is punishment in every sense that matters, so it is our tolerance of substitute payment that ought to give way, leaving the standard worry about the incoherence of penal substitution - theological or otherwise - fully intact. The second, call him the Expansionist, holds that Lewis has already ruled out the convenient impracticality story for the fines case, so our undiminished comfort with fine-swapping is real evidence that deserved punishment is more transferable than the usual view admits; that shifts the burden of proof onto the no-substitution intuition for prison and death sentences, and correspondingly leaves more room than we might think for something like the traditional doctrine of the Atonement to be defensible after all. Have the two of them press each other with concrete cases beyond fines and imprisonment - community service, corporal punishment, professional or reputational sanctions, forfeiture, and the like - with each one answering the specific point the other has just made rather than simply restating their own position. Neither philosopher should get the better of the exchange by default or come across as less rigorous than the other, and you need not have them reach agreement by the end.
