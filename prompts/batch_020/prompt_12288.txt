The example that stuck with me is the hypothetical where someone's hiding from people trying to hurt them, and the people looking for them ask you where they went — Augustine apparently says you're not allowed to straight up lie and say "they went that way," you can only stay silent or dodge the question, even if telling the lie would save a life. He also supposedly ranks different lies from less-bad to more-bad depending on the liar's motive, but the wild part is that even the "nicest" kind of lie (one that helps someone and hurts literally nobody) still counts as a real sin to him, just a smaller one.

Me and my friend Priya got into it at lunch about whether that logic even holds up now that so much of our day to day "lying" is directed at machines instead of people — like typing in a fake birthdate to get past an app's age filter, feeding a customer service chatbot a made-up sob story to get a refund, or using a totally fake AI-generated pic on a dating app. Augustine's whole reasoning is supposedly not really about wether the other party gets hurt, its about how the intent to deceive corrupts your own relationship to truth (and to God). So in his actual framework, would lying to a bot or algorithm that doesn't have a soul or beliefs of its own be just as sinful as lying to a real person, since the "damage" is to you either way? Or is there something in his real reasoning that would make him treat it totally differently?
