So I've been chasing a longer feature on the "is string theory even science" fight that keeps flaring up in physics, and it's left me stuck on a philosophy-of-science question I can't quite shake (I'm not a physicist myself, so bear with me if I get some of the details slightly wrong). A few years back a couple of well-known physicists wrote a piece, in Nature I think, essentially accusing string theorists and multiverse cosmologists of quietly moving the goalposts on what counts as science, since their favorite theories can't be tested by any experiment we currently have or maybe ever will. A philosopher of physics named Richard Dawid wrote back, more or less, that strict falsifiability was never really the standard working physicists used anyway, and that there are legitimate "non-empirical" reasons to trust a theory -- like the fact that nobody's found a workable alternative despite decades of trying, or that the theory keeps explaining things it wasn't built to explain in the first place.

For a sidebar to the piece I'm hoping you can write me a dialogue, maybe 700-900 words, between two people at a conference dinner after a panel just like that one: a physicist who's convinced her field is quietly abandoning it's own standards and drifting toward something closer to metaphysics, and a philosopher who thinks "falsifiability as the sole criterion" was always more of a slogan than something scientists actually lived by. I don't want either one to be a pushover -- they should each know the moves the other side is likely to make (the Duhem-Quine problem, the difference between "unfalsifiable in principle" and "untestable with current technology," the "no alternatives" argument, whatever else fits) and actually respond to what the other just said rather than restating their opening position. Honestly it's fine, maybe even better, if nobody "wins" by the end -- I mainly want it to be clear exactly where the disagreement bites.
