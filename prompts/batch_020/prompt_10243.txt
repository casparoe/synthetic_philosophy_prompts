For my metaethics seminar this term, we have spent several weeks on Sharon Street's evolutionary debunking argument against moral realism, which contends that because natural selection shaped our evaluative attitudes for reasons having nothing to do with tracking independent moral truths, realists cannot plausibly explain why those attitudes should nonetheless track the truth. The reply I find most forceful is the "companions in guilt" move associated with writers such as Justin Clarke-Doane: if evolutionary origin alone were sufficient to debunk a class of beliefs, then our basic arithmetical and logical beliefs would be debunked as well, since they too were shaped by selection pressures indifferent to abstract truth — yet almost no one is willing to accept mathematical skepticism on this basis. Write an essay assessing whether this parity with mathematics actually defeats Street's argument, or whether there is a principled disanalogy — for instance, appeals to indispensability, the modal status of the relevant truths, or differences in the causal etiology of mathematical versus moral belief — that allows moral realism to be debunked without mathematical realism suffering the same fate. Weigh the strongest considerations on both sides, and reach a definite verdict rather than leaving the matter open.
