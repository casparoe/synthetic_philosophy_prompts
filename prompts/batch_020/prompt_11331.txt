I never studied philosophy or anything like that, but I've been stuck on this idea for a few weeks now and finally sat down and wrote it out properly. Wanted some outside eyes on it before I decide whether it's worth doing anything more with. Quick bit of backstory: a while back I found out I only got praised for a project at work because my manager wanted to make a coworker she didn't like look bad in front of everyone, not because the project was actually any good (it might've been good anyway, honestly no idea). Knowing that basically ruined the compliment for me even though nothing about the work itself changed. Been chewing on why that bugged me so much ever since. Here's what I've got so far:

We normally think someone is admirable because they've actually got some admirable quality - guts, talent, whatever - and that's why it makes sense to admire them (I guess the fancy word for "makes sense" here is "fitting," found that out googling around). But you can obviously have a strong reason to admire someone that has nothing to do with whether they're admirable at all. Say your rich uncle tells you, and clearly means it, that he's cutting you out of the will unless you start telling him how much you admire him. Now you've definately got a genuinely powerful reason to admire the guy. Nobody thinks that makes him one bit more admirable though. So there's two totally different kinds of reason floating around for the exact same attitude - ones actually about what makes the person admirable in the first place, and ones that are just about what admiring gets you.

Here's where I got stuck. My first idea was: the "real" reasons are about the person themselves, the "fake" ones are about outside stuff that happens if you have the attitude. Except that breaks right away - if I admire some stranger because she jumped into a river to pull a kid out, that's about an effect she caused out in the world, not some quality sitting inside her, and that's still obviously a real reason to admire her. So "about them" vs "about outside consequences" can't be the actual dividing line, or at least not in the simple way I first thought of it.

Second idea: the real reasons are just whichever ones track whether the person actually IS admirable. Which I realized about two seconds after writing it down is completely circular, since "admirable" was the whole thing I was trying to explain using this "fitting reasons" idea in the first place. Kind of hit a wall there. Looked it up and apparently there's an actual name for this, the "wrong kind of reasons problem," but every writeup I found lost me in jargon within a paragraph, so I honestly don't know if real philosophers think its solvable or not.

Anyway - could you go through this and give me some honest comments? Mainly: does the river-rescue example actually sink my first idea, or am I confusing myself somewhere? Is the circularity in my second idea a dead end, or is there some way out of it that isn't just the same circle wearing a costume? And is this whole puzzle actually a real, hard thing, or is it a well-worn problem with a standard answer I just haven't stumbled on yet - if so, don't be gentle about it, I'd rather know.
