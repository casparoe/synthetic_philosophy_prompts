Our philosophy department is co-hosting a study day with the opera house next month, timed to their new production of Don Giovanni, and I've somehow ended up on the hook for the ten minutes of welcome before the first panel starts. Half the room will be company people and subscribers who came because they love Mozart and are a little suspicious that a philosopher is about to explain their own art form back to them; the other half will be my colleagues and grad students, who know Bernard Williams for moral luck and internal reasons and have mostly never opened the opera essays. I did my dissertation on Williams ethics years ago and always found the opera stuff a bit embarrassing to deal with in seminar, like a hobby that got a book deal, but going back to it for this I think I was wrong, and I want the welcome to actually make that case rather than just mention in passing that "the philosopher Bernard Williams also loved opera" before moving on to logistics.

Here's the thread I want it built around: Williams was on the board of ENO, I think -- don't quote me -- and had genuinely unfashionable opinions about performing opera in english translation rather than the original language, on the grounds that dramatic immediacy matters more than reverence for the text as sung; and he's got that essay, I want to say it's called "Don Giovanni as an Idea," where he deals with the fact that the music makes an audience half in love with an unrepentant seducer in a way no tidy verdict survives contact with. I think both of those come from the same worry that runs through his whole career against what he called "the morality system" -- the idea that ethics is basically a matter of rules and clean verdicts, which leaves out most of what actually matters to particular people in particular situations. Opera, for him, was somewhere you could feel that gap rather than just be told about it. Can you draft the welcome using that thread, something that would actually run about ten minutes read out loud? It needs real jokes in it, it can't just be a potted biography of Williams, and it has to survive two different audiences rolling their eyes for two entirely different reasons.
