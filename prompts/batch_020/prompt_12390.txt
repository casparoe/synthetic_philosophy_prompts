Quick one from the philosophy-of-biology rabbit hole I've fallen into while reporting a story on vestigial organs (the appendix, wisdom teeth, that whole "leftover of evolution" genre of article). A biologist source pointed me toward this etiological vs. causal-role split in how philosophers define "biological function" — apparently one camp, going back to a paper by Larry Wright and refined by people like Ruth Millikan and Karen Neander, says a trait's function is whatever it was naturally selected to do, while the other camp, following Robert Cummins, says the function is just whatever the trait currently contributes to some capacity of the organism, no evolutionary history required.

Here's what I cant shake: arent these just two ways of describing the exact same fact? If a trait is currently helping the organism survive and reproduce, that's presumably also why it got selected for in the first place, and if it was selected for doing X, it's presumably still doing X now — otherwise natural selection would've weeded it out by now, right? So "etiological vs causal role" seems to me like it should be a difference in emphasis or vocabulary, not an actual disagreement that could ever cash out in two different answers to "what is this trait's function." Is that basically right, and if so why do philosophers of biology write entire papers treating it like a live, substantive debate rather than a terminological one? And if I'm wrong, is there an actual case (maybe even one involving my vestigial organs!) where the two accounts would hand you genuinely different verdicts about a trait's function?
