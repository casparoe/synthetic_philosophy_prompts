So this happened at dinner with some friends from my old epistemology reading group, and I keep thinking about it. Someone told a story about her friend Nadia, who had a connecting flight to catch for her sister's wedding -- miss it and theres no other way to make the wedding, no backup flights that day. That morning Nadia had checked the departure time once online and told her husband "I know it's 7:40." On a normal Tuesday nobody would of blinked at that, but given what was riding on it, one friend, Sam, said something like "eh, I'm not sure she really knew that" -- and that somehow turned into a whole argument with another friend, Theo, that basically became contextualism vs. pragmatic encroachment. Roughly how it went:

(1) Theo said it's simple: "know" is context-sensitive, like "flat" or "tall." Once enough is at stake, the bar the sentence "Nadia knows the flight's at 7:40" has to clear goes up, so the very same sentence can flip from true to false. Nadia hasn't changed; the conversational standard has.

(2) Sam said that's not her view: Nadia's own evidence stopped being enough once Nadia's actual stakes got that high, period -- it's a fact about Nadia's situation, not about whoever's talking about her or from where.

(3) Theo pointed out that people at the table, with zero stakes of their own, felt the same pull to hesitate the second they heard how much was riding on it. He said that's better explained by his view: once the possibility of error becomes vivid in a conversation, the standard rises for everyone in it, whether or not they personally have anything on the line.

(4) Sam said that doesn't show anyone's standards moved -- thinking hard about someone else's situation just makes you track what they actually need, the same way realizing a Great Dane needs more food than a chihuahua doesn't require your own hunger to change. Then she gave a sharper case: two of Nadia's coworkers, both fully aware of the stakes, discuss the trip later -- the anxious one says "I don't think she really knew that," the relaxed one says "of course she knew, she checked." On Theo's view they're both right, since each only answers to her own conversational standard, but Nadia's situation never changed between those two sentences -- so whether Nadia knew comes down to which coworker's talking, which seems backwards.

(5) Theo bit the bullet: said that's exactly right, and it's no weirder than "nearby" varying with the speaker's location -- nobody's bothered by that for other words.

(6) Sam said the analogy fails: everybody already knows "nearby" is speaker-relative, which is why nobody hears a real dispute when two people in different cities both call something "nearby." The coworkers, though, each think they're disagreeing about Nadia, not talking past each other about separate things -- and it seems off to say they're both just correct and there was never a real disagreement there at all.

Can you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of it at each point -- not just crown an overall winner -- and flag anything you think both of them are still missing?
