In my department's Frege reading group last week, two colleagues, Mark and Renata, fell into a dispute after the session that neither of them could settle, and that I would rather put to you than adjudicate myself.

The occasion was Frege's 1924 political diary, not discovered and published until decades after his death, in which he voices antisemitic views, contempt for parliamentary democracy, and support for stripping Jews of political rights. (1) Mark's opening position was that none of this bears on Frege's philosophy in any interesting sense: the cogency of the Begriffsschrift, the sense/reference distinction, and the definition of number in the Grundlagen stand or fall on purely logical grounds, so treating the diary as relevant to assessing them is a version of the genetic fallacy -- judging a view by facts about the person who held it rather than by its merits. (2) Renata agreed that the diary cannot touch the validity of any of Frege's arguments, but denied that this settles the matter. She pointed out that Frege's signature commitments -- his ferocious anti-psychologism, his insistence on a mind-independent 'third realm' of objective thought, his refusal to let logical laws be hostage to any thinker's or community's perspective -- express the very same intolerance of plurality and relativization on display in the diary's hostility to political compromise, and that historians of philosophy routinely draw on this sort of biographical material to understand why a system took the shape it did, without thereby claiming that it changes whether the system is correct. (3) Mark replied that this kind of 'temperament' story is suspiciously easy to tell about nearly anyone in hindsight, and unfalsifiable besides: other prominent opponents of psychologism in logic, such as Husserl and Russell, fought exactly the same battle without landing anywhere near Frege's politics, which should make us doubt there is a real connection to be found even in Frege's individual case. (4) Renata answered that the absence of a general law linking anti-psychologism to authoritarianism shows nothing about this particular thinker; intellectual biography is not in the business of general laws, and we have Frege's own words tying his contempt for compromise in politics to the same demand for a single determinate order in logic, so excluding that evidence merely because it is biographical would make the history of philosophy strangely incurious about its own subject matter.
