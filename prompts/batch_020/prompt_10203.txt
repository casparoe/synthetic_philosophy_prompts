A friend of mine, who is finishing a doctorate on Frege and has developed something of a fixation on Michael Dummett's less-discussed writings, spent the better part of an evening arguing with me that Dummett's slim 2001 book, *On Immigration and Refugees*, gives us a ready-made yardstick for judging Britain's current asylum politics. I had only ever known Dummett as the architect of semantic anti-realism and the Gödel–Dummett logic, so it was news to me that he spent years driving to ports and airports to intervene on behalf of detained immigrants, co-founded the Joint Council for the Welfare of Immigrants with his wife Ann in 1967, and was knighted "for services to philosophy and to racial justice." The book itself splits neatly in two: a set of general principles about immigration and asylum, followed by a history of the postwar Acts by which Britain narrowed its own definition of nationality — the same legislative ratchet that would later strand long-settled Commonwealth citizens in the Windrush scandal.

What my friend kept coming back to, though, was a single move in the "principles" half. Responding to Margaret Thatcher's 1978 remark about Britons fearing being "swamped," Dummett does not simply dismiss the worry but reformulates it, granting that a nation could in principle have a limited "right not to be submerged" by immigration, while insisting that right is of extremely narrow application and that Britain, in his lifetime, never came close to triggering it. My friend's claim was that this yardstick transfers cleanly onto the present: that Dummett would have judged Suella Braverman's 2022 description of Channel crossings as an "invasion," the scheme to remove asylum seekers to Rwanda that the Supreme Court eventually struck down over the risk of refoulement, and the net migration figures that peaked at over 700,000 a year after Brexit, using exactly those two tests — realistic prospect of submersion, and compliance with the Geneva Convention. I objected that most of that post-Brexit surge was work and study visas and one-off humanitarian routes for Ukrainians and Hong Kongers, that the boats driving the "invasion" rhetoric were a small fraction of the total, and that a framework built to diagnose a specific mid-century racism against Commonwealth migrants might simply not yield a determinate verdict on this messier situation. We got nowhere.

I'd like you to settle it: reconstructing Dummett's actual commitments as best you can, rather than a generic sense that he was "pro-immigration," how do you think he would have assessed the Rwanda scheme, the "invasion" framing of small-boat crossings, and the composition of the post-Brexit migration figures, judged by his own tests rather than a simple pro- or anti-immigration label? Would any part of that picture count, on his own admittedly narrow standard, as approaching genuine submersion, and how would you expect him to reconcile his central historical thesis — that British restrictionism has been driven overwhelmingly by racism — with a period in which the government loosened controls on record numbers of predominantly non-white work and study migrants while directing its fiercest rhetoric at a much smaller number of asylum seekers arriving by boat? Please flag, as you go, which parts of your answer follow fairly directly from commitments Dummett actually made in the book, and which parts are more speculative extrapolation on your part.
