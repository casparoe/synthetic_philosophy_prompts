I run a small interview podcast on longtermist and existential-risk topics on the side of my day job as a software engineer, so I have no formal philosophy training -- most of what I know about decision theory I've picked up by working through papers and forum posts on my own rather than in a classroom. Next episode's guest is a decision theorist at a longtermist research institute who defends evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL), an idea that traces back to Caspar Oesterheld's 2017 work on multiverse-wide superrationality: roughly, if the universe or multiverse is large enough, we should give weight to the interests of causally disconnected agents whose decision procedures correlate with ours, because acting cooperatively toward them is evidential -- not causal -- reason to believe they're doing the same for us. Every writeup I've found eventually gets pushed on the stranger implications -- infinite-universe arithmetic, or the question of who exactly these "cooperators" are supposed to be -- and the standard move seems to be some version of "that's a hard problem for anyone reasoning about a large or infinite world, not a special problem for ECL," which conveniently lets the point get set aside rather than answered.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that don't let that move do so much work? I'd like at least one question pressing on why the argument needs evidential rather than causal or functional decision theory, given that even sympathetic commentators have noted that functional decision theory doesn't obviously deliver the same cooperative verdict once the parties are merely similar rather than exact copies of one another; one on how the guest actually delimits the reference class of "similar decision procedure, different values" cooperators without quietly assuming the conclusion, especially given the puzzle of what to do when acausal influence runs in a loop (you influence someone who influences someone who influences you back); one on whether, once the large-world framing is stripped away, the practical upshot just reduces to a cooperative heuristic she'd already endorse on ordinary grounds of moral uncertainty; and one on the real-world version of the toy cooperate/defect model, where it's genuinely unclear what counts as the "default" action that two mutually distant agents are supposed to be deviating from. For each question, add a short follow-up that anticipates the "not a special problem for ECL" deflection (or whatever dodge seems likeliest) and pushes past it, and close the list with a question asking what specific empirical or theoretical development would make her substantially lower her credence in ECL being action-relevant.
