I'm taking an advanced seminar on decision theory this term, and a few weeks ago we covered Gibbard and Harper's "Death in Damascus" case: a man encounters Death in Damascus, who tells him he is coming for him the next day. Death works from an appointment book filled in weeks in advance on the basis of highly reliable predictions of where the man will actually be, and the man dies exactly when he turns out to be in the city the book names. His options are to stay in Damascus or flee to Aleppo, but since resolving to do either is itself excellent evidence of what the book already says, whichever option he settles on seems, at the very moment he settles on it, to become the losing one. We have a short response paper due on the case next week, and a disagreement I overheard after class left me more unsure of the "right" answer than the reading did, so I was hoping you could help me work through it.

The disagreement was between two other people in the seminar, Nadia and Elliot, who kept arguing over coffee afterward. (1) Nadia said the resolution is that the man should flip a coin: since neither "stay" nor "flee" is stable under updating, the standard move is to require rational choices to be self-ratifying, meaning they still look best once you know you've made them, and a fifty-fifty randomization is the only option that clears this bar, since learning that you're about to flip a coin gives you no reason to think Death has guessed either city in particular. (2) Elliot pushed back with a variant in the spirit of the counterexamples Andy Egan has used against causal decision theory: same predictor set-up, but now there are two boxes, and a highly reliable predictor fills them based on what it expects you to do -- $100 in box A and $1,400 in box B if you're predicted to take A; $800 in A and $700 in B if you're predicted to take B; and both boxes empty if you're predicted to randomize. He pointed out that here too, neither pure box is self-ratifying, since whichever one you commit to, the other box would have paid more -- yet the one self-ratifying option, the coin flip, now guarantees you exactly nothing, which is worse than either pure choice gets you. A rule that has you walk away with zero for free, purely because zero happens to be "stable," must be mistaken somewhere, so self-ratifiability can't be what justifies the coin flip in Damascus either. (3) Nadia replied that Elliot's predictor isn't doing the same job Death does: it isn't just predicting where you'll end up, it's built to specifically zero out both boxes the moment it detects you're randomizing -- an extra, punitive rule with nothing to do with ordinary predictive accuracy. Death has no such rule; he simply goes to whichever city he predicts you'll be in, and if the man's coin happens to land on the other one, the man survives, exactly as he would have if he'd simply guessed instead of flipping a coin. Take away that extra rule, she said, and nothing dominates the coin flip in the first place, so self-ratifiability is still giving perfectly good advice in the case it was actually meant for. (4) Elliot answered that this concedes his real point rather than answering it: if flipping the coin is only justified once you've separately established that nothing dominates it, then "nothing dominates it" is the actual reason to flip the coin, and self-ratifiability isn't adding anything of its own -- it's just riding along in cases where that independent reason happens to agree with it, and getting exposed as useless in cases where the two come apart. A justification that only works when it isn't needed, he said, isn't really a justification.

Could you go through moves (1) through (4) and assess each on its own merits, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I would also be grateful if you could point out any consideration you think both Nadia and Elliot are overlooking about how self-ratifiability is actually supposed to function in predictor cases like this one.
