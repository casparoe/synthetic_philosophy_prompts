I spent thirty-six years running a low-temperature physics lab, and since retiring I've been working my way through a self-directed reading list in philosophy that has lately settled on bounded rationality: Herbert Simon on satisficing, some of Gigerenzer's work on heuristics, and an old paper by Michael Slote defending satisficing as sometimes the rational choice even when a better option is known to exist. This reading has collided rather directly with my duties on the board of a small private foundation that funds about fifteen early-career fellowships a year across the natural sciences, from an applicant pool that has grown to more than 300.

Our long-standing process ranks every finalist through three full rounds of comparative review -- something like 500 volunteer reviewer-hours a year, and up to four months of waiting for applicants -- on the premise that we are picking out the fifteen best proposals rather than fifteen good ones. A subcommittee now wants to replace the fine-grained ranking above a quality threshold with a two-stage rule: reviewers would sort every application only into "fund-worthy" or "not," against our existing rubric, and the fifteen awards would then be drawn by lottery from whoever clears that bar. Their case leans heavily on an internal exercise in which two panels independently re-ranked the same finalist pool and produced orderings that correlated at only about 0.3 with each other -- which they take to show that the later rounds buy us the appearance of fine discrimination rather than the reality of it, so that continuing to rank is the more irrational policy once reviewer time and applicant stress are counted as real costs. Other trustees are unmoved: they think a lottery, thresholded or not, abandons the foundation's founding promise to reward the most deserving work, treats finalists who are not in fact equally good as though they were, and will look to donors and passed-over applicants like we have simply given up on distinguishing excellent from merely good.

I've been asked to write the discussion memo for our March board meeting; could you draft it for me? I'd like a clear recommendation -- adopt the lottery mechanism, some modified version of it, or keep the status quo -- spelled out concretely enough that the subcommittee could act on it, including whether it should run first as a time-limited pilot and what result would justify making it permanent, together with reasoning that treats the desert-based objection as a genuine cost to be weighed rather than a misunderstanding to be corrected.
