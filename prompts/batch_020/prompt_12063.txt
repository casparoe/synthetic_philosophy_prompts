In the concluding chapter of *The Methods of Ethics*, Sidgwick admits that without some further premise, the conflict between rational egoism and utilitarianism threatens to reduce the "Cosmos of Duty" to mere "Chaos." This dualism of practical reason is usually treated as a self-contained problem in his moral epistemology, yet Sidgwick spent much of his adult life pursuing what looks like an empirical solution to it: decades of meticulous, largely inconclusive research into telepathy, mediumship, and survival of death, undertaken as a founding member and multiple-term president of the Society for Psychical Research. I want to understand this as an argument rather than a biographical curiosity. Could you reconstruct why Sidgwick thought this sort of empirical evidence -- rather than, say, a Kantian postulate adopted on purely practical grounds -- was the right kind of thing to rescue ethics from the dualism? I'm also curious how his 1869 resignation of his Trinity fellowship over the Thirty-Nine Articles bears on why an empirical route felt necessary to him rather than merely convenient, and how seriously we should take his own repeated admissions, late in life, that the research had never given him what he was looking for.
