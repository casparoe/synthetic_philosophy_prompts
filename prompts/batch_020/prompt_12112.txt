So this argument happened at dinner a couple weeks ago and I still can't stop turning it over. I'm doing a PhD in philosophy, mostly working in formal epistemology, and two friends of mine from grad school — Mara, who's in biostatistics, and Theo, who's in my own program but works on decision theory — ended up really going at it about something that started completely innocuously. Mara had just gotten her results from one of those standard 10-year cardiovascular risk calculators (you plug in age, sex, cholesterol, blood pressure, smoking status, and it spits out a percentage), and she was mildly annoyed at her 9%, because she runs marathons and has no family history of heart disease, so the number felt like it wasn't really "about her" at all.

(1) Theo ran with that feeling: he said the number is close to meaningless as a claim about Mara's individual risk, because she's simultaneously a member of indefinitely many reference classes at once — women her age, women her age who run marathons, women with her exact total-cholesterol-to-HDL ratio, women born the same year in the same country — and each class would spit out a different frequency, with nothing to privilege any one of them as "the" correct class. This, he said, is a real thing called the reference class problem, and its not just a technicality: it means a sentence like "the probability that Mara personally develops heart disease is 9%" doesn't actually pick out a fact, there's only ever "probability relative to whichever class you happened to pick." (2) Mara pushed back hard: sure, infinitely many classes exist in principle, but that doesn't make the choice arbitrary, because you use the narrowest class you actually have solid data for, and you only add a covariate like marathon-running once you've checked, statistically, that it carries genuine independent predictive power (cross-validation, held-out performance, the usual toolkit). As you fold in more of these validated covariates the estimate just keeps getting sharper and converges on something well-defined — a bread-and-butter statistical modelling problem, she said, not a metaphysical crisis. (3) Theo's comeback was that "narrowest validated class" quietly smuggles in what's actually in dispute: two equally narrow, equally well-validated partitions (marathon runners vs. people with her exact biomarker profile, say) can flatly disagree about her risk, and deciding which covariates even count as "relevant" already presupposes an answer to the causal question that's supposedly still open, so her cross-validation move doesn't dissolve the reference class problem, it just relocates it one step up. (4) Mara's closing volley was that this proves way too much: by that logic no prediction about any particular case would ever be justified, since you can always gerrymander some rival partition after the fact, and yet we obviously do adjudicate between rival models all the time using out-of-sample performance — Theo, she said, was quietly sliding from "we don't yet have a complete causal model of heart disease" to "there is no fact of the matter about Mara's risk," and those are very different claims.
