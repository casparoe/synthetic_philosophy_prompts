For the last several years I've covered formal epistemology and game theory for a science magazine. One of the researchers I've profiled most often — an epistemic game theorist who works on common knowledge of rationality, including the backward-induction result that seems to force rational players to grab the pot on the very first move of a centipede game rather than let it grow — is getting married next month, and has asked me, rather than any of her colleagues, to give the toast. Roughly half the room will be fellow game theorists and logicians who will catch a reference to that paradox instantly; the other half will be relatives who have never heard the phrase "epistemic logic" in their lives. I'd like the toast to lean on the distinction between merely mutual knowledge — something everyone happens to know — and true common knowledge — something everyone knows, and knows that everyone knows, without end — as more than a clever aside. It should explain, seriously, why standing up and saying this out loud today does real work, even though most of us in the room privately concluded these two were getting married years ago. And it should account for the one true story about the couple that this very distinction gives me good reason to leave out of the speech entirely. Please write the toast: a few minutes long, with at least one line that rewards the theorists in the room without losing anyone else.
