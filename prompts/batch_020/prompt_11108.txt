Over the past year, a forecasting partner and I have made a habit of fully disclosing our reasoning to each other before comparing final probability estimates, expecting this to bring our numbers into line. Instead, we keep arriving at meaningfully different figures — most recently a stubborn fifteen-point gap on whether a certain ballot measure will pass — even though neither of us doubts the other's intelligence or good faith. A friend versed in formal epistemology pointed out that this is exactly what Aumann's agreement theorem says cannot happen between two Bayesians who share a prior and have common knowledge of each other's posteriors, which has left me wondering whether our persistent disagreement secretly indicts one of us, or whether the theorem's idealizations simply do not describe people like us. Write a dialogue between two forecasters in an analogous position, each having just stated a firm, mutually known posterior on a shared question, in which one argues that their continued disagreement can only reflect some failure of honesty or rationality on somebody's part, while the other defends the possibility of lasting, fully rational disagreement between people who each recognize the other's competence. Have both characters move past mere restatement of the theorem and engage directly with the common-prior assumption, the difference between mutual and common knowledge, and the Geanakoplos–Polemarchakis result that repeated, honest exchange of posteriors ought to force convergence in finitely many rounds, with each side getting its strongest possible case.
