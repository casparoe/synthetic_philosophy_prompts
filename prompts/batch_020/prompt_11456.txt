So I'm teaching Political Liberalism in my seminar this term and we just finshed the public reason unit - background culture vs. the public political forum, the proviso, duty of civility, the whole apparatus. One of my students asked something in section that I fumbled and it's been bugging me since, so I want to actually work it through properly instead of hand-waving next week.

You know the Kennedy v. Bremerton case from 2022? Joe Kennedy was an assistant football coach at a public high school in Washington who used to kneel and pray at midfield right after games, first alone and then with players gradually joining him. The district asked him to stop doing it so visibly, worried about Establishment Clause exposure and about kids feeling pressured to join their coach's prayer, he refused, got suspended, and it eventually went to the Supreme Court, which ruled 6-3 in his favor on free exercise and free speech grounds. Gorsuch's opinion for the majority basically retired the old Lemon test along the way in favor of a "historical practices and understandings" standard. Sotomayor's dissent leaned hard on the coercion angle - the power imbalance between a coach and the kids whose playing time he controls.

Rawls obviously never touched this, he died in 2002 and it's not really the sort of case he wrote about anyway. What I actually want is more reconstructive, and I think its a genuinely hard case for his own machinery, not just "would Rawls have approved of the outcome": is a coach praying at a school event even the kind of act public reason governs at all, given that Rawls mostly has in mind legislators, judges, and citizens debating constitutional essentials - or does it just fall through the cracks of his framework, since it's an individual's personal conduct rather than an argument being offered in the public political forum? And if there's a defensible way to stretch the framework to cover it anyway, I want an actual verdict out of you, not just gestures at "toleration" - something closer in spirit to that (in)famous footnote in "The Idea of Public Reason Revisited" where he actually stakes out a position on abortion by weighing specific political values against each other instead of staying safely abstract. So: what are the relevant values in tension on Rawls's view here - Kennedy's own liberty of conscience, the school's neutrality obligations, whatever's really driving the coercion worry - and which way does a "reasonable balance" of them come out? Does the coach's asymmetric power over the students change the analysis in a way that, say, a lone parent praying in the stands wouldn't?
