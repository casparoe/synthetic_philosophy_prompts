I am assembling a collection of short fiction in which each story is built around a genuine logical or epistemic puzzle rather than a vague gesture at one, and I would like a draft for the next entry. Write a short story, no more than 1,000 words, about a nonfiction author finishing the preface to a work of narrative history on the night before it goes to print: she has fact-checked every sentence and privately believes each individual claim in the manuscript, yet she is equally confident that, somewhere among the hundreds of claims she is making, at least one will turn out to be wrong — and she states as much, plainly, in the preface. Dramatize this as a genuine epistemic dilemma rather than a gesture of authorial false modesty, and have her notice, unprompted, that her situation resembles holding a hundred tickets in a fair lottery: confident that any single ticket will lose, yet certain that one of them will win — while also registering, however briefly, a reason the two cases might not be exactly parallel. The puzzle itself should never be named outright; a philosophically literate reader ought to be able to identify it purely from how the story handles her reasoning. The story need not resolve the tension — most philosophical treatments do not — but it should leave the reader with a precise sense of exactly where the difficulty lies.
