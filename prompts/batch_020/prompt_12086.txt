I just gave a take-home midterm and I'm working through the stack now; before I commit to a grade on one answer I'd like an independent read, because the student writes confidently and it's late enough in the marking that I don't fully trust myself to catch a slippery error hiding under good prose.

Here's the question as I set it: "In 'Of Interest,' Hume denies that the rate of interest is governed by the quantity of money in a country. Explain his argument, and say what he thinks does govern the rate instead. Then explain how the mechanism from 'Of the Balance of Trade' undermines the mercantilist policy of hoarding specie. Finally: in 'Of Money,' Hume also says that an increasing money supply can stimulate industry, at least for a while. Is this consistent with his claim that money is merely the 'oil' of trade and contributes nothing on its own to a nation's real wealth, or is there a tension between the two claims? Defend your answer."

And here is the student's answer in full:

"Hume's target in 'Of Interest' is the common view -- he has John Law in mind, among others -- that a nation can bring down its interest rates just by increasing the quantity of money in circulation. On Hume's account, that's basically right: when coin is abundant, there is more of it available to be lent out, so lenders compete with one another for borrowers and interest falls, the same way abundance of any good drives down its price. He does note the awkward case of Spain, which after a century of importing New World silver still had some of the highest interest rates in Europe rather than the lowest, but he treats this as a temporary distortion caused by Spain's lack of manufacturing and commerce rather than as evidence against the basic mechanism. What really sets the rate, then, is how much coin is chasing how much lending demand at a given moment; profit and frugality only matter insofar as they affect that ratio.

He extends a similar picture in 'Of the Balance of Trade' to attack the mercantilist policy of hoarding gold and silver. If one nation somehow accumulated a disproportionate share of Europe's money, its prices would rise relative to its neighbours', its exports would become less competitive and its imports more attractive, and specie would drain back out until the balance was restored -- money distributes itself across trading nations the way water finds a common level across connected channels, so no country can hang on to more than its share of trade and population warrant. That makes the whole mercantilist project of running permanent trade surpluses to accumulate treasure incoherent: the accumulation triggers the very price movements that undo it.

As for the supposed tension, I don't think there really is one. When Hume calls money 'none of the wheels of trade' and only the 'oil' that lubricates them, he means money by itself isn't wealth -- only labour and commodities are -- so the sheer size of a nation's money stock shouldn't matter to how rich it really is. But he also says a growing money supply stimulates industry while prices are still catching up to it, and I take him to be saying that a country really can make itself richer by expanding its money supply, since more coin in circulation before prices rise gives producers more to work with and more incentive to produce. So the 'oil' image and the stimulus argument point at the same thing from two angles rather than pulling against each other: money doesn't matter directly, but managed well, growing the money supply is still a reliable way to grow the economy."
