Ok so this semester I'm in a seminar on practical reason and we spent like three weeks on Ruth Chang's stuff on hard choices and "parity," and I'm now kind of obsessed. The basic idea, if I'm getting it right, is that when a choice feels genuinely gut-wrenching (her go-to example, annoyingly, is choosing between being a corporate lawyer and being a philosopher, which is uncomfortably close to my actual life rn) it's not because we're too ignorant to tell which option is better, and it's not that the two are secretly, precisely equal either. She thinks the usual "trichotomy thesis" -- that any two things are either better, worse, or equally good -- misses a fourth relation, being "on a par," and the reason it can't just be reduced to vagueness (which is what Broome argued in that '97 volume she edited) is the small-improvement argument: if two options were really just tied, making one of them a little bit better should tip the scale, and with genuinely hard choices it doesn't. My professor thinks this is basically the most important idea in value theory in the last 50 years, and honestly I'm coming around to it too, probably because I'm sitting here right now deciding between a law school offer and a funded PhD spot and everyone keeps asking which one is "better" like there has to be an answer.

Here's my actual request though: I got picked to give the short student speech at our philosophy department's graduation dinner in a few weeks -- maybe 25 majors, our professors, and a bunch of parents and siblings who's never heard the word "incommensurable" in their life. I want to build the whole speech around parity, using it to say something to my cohort about the choices we're all facing that's actually earned by the concept rather than just generic "follow your heart" commencement mush -- and I want the small-improvement argument gestured at somewhere, lightly, as the reason we're allowed to stop looking for the "right" answer. Can you write me that speech? About 4-5 minutes out loud, needs to land just as well for the confused parents as for the philosophy nerds, and the parity idea has to actually be doing structural work in what I tell everyone to do next, not just get name-dropped in paragraph two and forgotten.
