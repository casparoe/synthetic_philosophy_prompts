I've been reporting a piece on companies that have imported "superforecasting" methods into ordinary risk management, and during a site visit to one firm's internal forecasting unit, I watched an argument break out between two of its forecasters that I still haven't been able to resolve in my own head. Sara and Theo had each been handed the same supplier dossier and asked, independently, for the probability that a critical overseas component shipment would miss its contractual deadline; Sara came back with 74 percent, Theo with 22 percent. When they compared notes afterward, neither could point to an actual mistake in the other's reasoning — no misread figure, no arithmetic slip, no piece of the dossier one of them had seen that the other had missed — and the conversation shifted from the shipment itself, whose fate won't be known for months, to a more abstract argument about what, if anything, they owed each other in the way of revising their estimates.

Sara went first: since neither of them could find a flaw in the other's reasoning, and both had the relevant experience to read this kind of report competently, she didn't see how either of them was entitled to just keep their own number, and thought they should both move toward something closer to the middle. Theo pushed back: he had gone back over his own reasoning line by line after hearing her figure, and it still struck him as sound; if that didn't count for anything just because someone else disagreed, he didn't know what would, since checking whether your reasoning still seems right is the only access you ever get to whether you've reasoned well. Sara replied that this couldn't be the end of it, because someone who has reasoned badly feels exactly as confident as someone who has reasoned well, so "it still seems right to me" isn't the kind of thing that can settle who's correct — and, she added, Theo evidently agreed at some level, since he'd only bothered re-checking his work because she disagreed with him in the first place. Theo conceded that much, but said the situation wasn't as symmetric as she made it sound: he had a five-year, Brier-scored track record on exactly this category of supplier forecast, while Sara had joined the unit eight months earlier, and a documented track record struck him as a different order of fact from a mere feeling of confidence — one that shouldn't count for less just because it came up only after the two of them had already disagreed.

Neither of them budged, and the meeting moved on before anyone got to respond to Theo's last point. Could you go through the exchange move by move — Sara's opening argument, Theo's appeal to his own reasoning, Sara's reply about symmetric confidence, and Theo's appeal to his track record — and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also be curious whether there's a consideration neither of them raised that should actually bear on how much either one revises their estimate.
