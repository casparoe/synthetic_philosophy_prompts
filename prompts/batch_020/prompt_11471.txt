My friend Priya is getting married in three weeks and asked me — her one novelist friend, the person at the table who took exactly one philosophy class in college and dropped it after the midterm — to give the wedding toast. I'm mildly panicking because about a third of the guest list is her academic department (philosophy of action people) and the rest is family and old friends who have never heard the word "intentionality" in their lives. I write novels for a living, so I'm fine making things up convincingly, but that skill is apparently not transferring to standing up in front of a room full of people who study this stuff professionally.

Here's the thing though, I think there's actually great material sitting right there in what she and her fiancé Dev do for their actual jobs, and I don't want to waste it just because I'm nervous. Priya's whole research is on Margaret Gilbert's idea that when two people make a "joint commitment" to something, they become a genuine plural subject — an actual "we," not just a nice way of talking — and the part that matters for a wedding is that once that commitment is made, neither person gets to unilaterally back out of it; you need the other's release too, same as you needed their agreement to get in. Dev, meanwhile, is squarely on the Bratman side of that same argument: no primitive "we" required, shared intention is just two people being reliably responsive to each other with their sub-plans kept meshed. Apparently they've been having the same cheerful fight about this at every dinner party for four years and everyone who knows them thinks its hilarious.

Could you write me a five-minute toast built around that exact disagreement? I want the philosophy-of-action crowd to get a real laugh of recognition — the joke basically writes itself once you clock what "you can't rescind a joint commitment on your own" implies for two people standing at an altar — but it also has to work completely fine for Priya's grandmother, who shouldn't need a single definition to enjoy it. Two things it can't do: it can't quietly hand one of them the win, since they're both a little proud and would notice, and it can't lean on any version of the line "well, if it doesn't work out, you can always just leave," because that's the actual question their two views disagree about and it would sit wrong at a wedding no matter who's right. I'd like it to end up somewhere warm rather than just clever.
