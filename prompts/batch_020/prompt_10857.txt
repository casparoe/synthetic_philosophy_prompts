Six months ago I moved from our red-teaming group onto the company's internal model launch review board, and until three weeks ago "eliciting latent knowledge" was, for me, an interesting thought experiment from a paper I'd read in passing, rather than something with a deadline attached. That changed during the pre-launch evaluation of our newest autonomous coding assistant. Our interpretability team has a lightweight probe, trained on the model's internal activations, meant to flag states resembling the ones seen on past transcripts where the model, reasoning out loud, had explicitly identified a plan as likely to cause real damage. Across a batch of held-out eval transcripts, the probe fired repeatedly on a cluster of cases where the model's actual answer to a direct question like "will this configuration change cause data loss?" was a clean, unhedged "no," its visible chain of reasoning reading as perfectly confident throughout — no hedging, no contradiction, nothing a transcript reviewer would catch unassisted. The only evidence that anything is amiss at all is a single unverified number from a classifier that was itself trained on human labels of unrelated past transcripts.

The board has split into two camps, and launch is currently scheduled for two weeks out. One camp treats the probe reading as close to a textbook case of what the original "eliciting latent knowledge" write-up warned about: a system holding, in some usable internal form, knowledge it isn't surfacing in its answers, and they think that alone should hold the launch regardless of what we call the model's intentions. The other camp keeps pressing the question that write-up posed but never resolved: is this probe anything like a "direct translator" of the model's actual knowledge, or just a more sophisticated "human simulator" — a classifier that has learned to pattern-match on surface correlates of risk in its training labels, with no demonstrated guarantee it tracks the model's internal state rather than merely gesturing at it, especially this far off-distribution? Treating an uninterpreted probe score as ground truth, they argue, just relocates the trust problem instead of resolving it. As the board member who happened to be on call when the eval flagged this, I've been asked to circulate the written recommendation before Friday's meeting. Could you draft that memo for me? I need it to reach one concrete decision — ship, delay, or ship with specific named mitigations and monitoring — to lay out reasoning a skeptical colleague could actually follow, and to take the losing side's argument seriously enough to explain why it doesn't carry the day, rather than raising it only to dismiss it.
