I'm working on a piece about why people—climate skeptics, anti-vaxxers, hardcore partisans, pick your example—never seem to budge no matter how much solid evidence you put in front of them, and a philosopher friend of mine brought up this thing called the "dogmatism paradox" over drinks last week, which apparently traces back to Saul Kripke and got its name from Gilbert Harman. As best I can tell the argument goes: if you actually know some claim h is true, then you also know that any evidence which might turn up against h later has to be misleading, since h is true and evidence against a truth can't really be pointing at the truth. And if you already know in advance that a piece of evidence would be misleading, you're entitled to just disregard it before you've even seen it. So there's supposedly an honest-to-god logical argument showing that once you really know something, the fully rational move is to preemptively write off whatever contrary evidence might show up down the road.

That's definately a great hook for the piece—"philosophers have shown that digging in your heels is rational, as long as you happen to be right"—but I don't want to put that in print if I'm garbling the actual philosophical upshot. So: is it fair to say that genuine knowers are actually justified in writing off future counter-evidence in advance, meaning the people we usually call "dogmatic" are just doing what rationality demands of them, provided their beliefs happen to be true? That's the line I want to run with, so tell me if I'm misreading it.
