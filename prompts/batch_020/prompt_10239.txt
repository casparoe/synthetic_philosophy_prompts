The summer before junior year, I wanted to attend a coding bootcamp that would have looked great on my college applications, but I was about $650 short of being able to afford a laptop good enough to actually do the coursework. My older cousin, who has always been the "responsible one" in our family, offered to lend me the money interest-free, on the condition that I pay her back in $50 installments once I started earning my own money. She was upfront that she doesn't do this for just anyone: she'd turned down a couple of our other cousins who asked for similar loans in the past because she had a feeling they'd never actually follow through, and she only agreed to help me because, based on how I've behaved over the years, she was confident that I actually follow through on what I say. There was no written agreement and nothing legal behind it — the entire arrangement rested on her judgment of my character.

The bootcamp went well, and a few months later I got a part-time job at a computer repair shop, so the $50 payments are now easy for me to afford. But something has been bothering me, and I've been arguing about it with my friends ever since: my cousin lives three states away, we see each other maybe once a year, and there is genuinely nothing she could do if I just stopped paying her. In the coldest possible terms, the laptop is already mine and the bootcamp already happened, so no future payment changes anything I've already received, which seems to mean that from here on, paying her back is a pure cost to me with no upside.

What I can't work out is what, if anything, is wrong with that reasoning. It can't be right that nobody should ever repay an "unenforceable" favor once its benefit has already been received — and this reasoning definitely wouldn't have gotten me the loan in the first place, since my cousin only helped me because she was confident I wouldn't think this way. But I can't articulate why the reasoning fails, or how I should really think about what I owe her now that her end of the deal is already done. I'd genuinely like to understand what's going on here, rather than just being told that paying her back is "the right thing to do."
