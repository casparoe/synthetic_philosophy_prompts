I'm on the "commitments review board" at the startup I work for (don't ask why we have a whole committee for this, long story). We make an app where people put real money down to keep promises to themselves — quit smoking, run more, whatever — and if a tracker says you failed, your stake gets sent to a charity automatically, no manual override. That's basically the whole pitch: you can't talk your way out of it later.

Last month a syncing bug caused about 140 users to get flagged as failing a commitment they'd actually kept — we've got the raw data now proving it, the tracker just didn't report in time. We're already refunding that batch, thats not the issue. What we can't agree on is the policy going forward. Half the board wants a real appeals process, since what people actually promised was "I'll run the miles," not "the api will say I ran the miles," so enforcing the api's version over what someone actually did just punishes a bug. The other half think that's a slippery slope that guts the whole product: the reason people pay us is that there's no wiggle room and no arguing your way out afterward, and the second we let humans make judgement calls about who "really" meant it, the thing stops actually binding anyone, even the people the tracker got right about.

Can you draft the short memo I'd bring to Thursdays board meeting? It should land on one actual policy (build an appeals process, hold the line completely, something narrower in between) with a rough timeline for rolling it out, not just lay out both sides, and it needs to take the "no exceptions" position seriously — I think it's onto something real about what makes a commitment binding in the first place, I just can't articulate what. I'm an engineer not a philosopher so don't worry about anything going over my head, just reason it out plainly.
