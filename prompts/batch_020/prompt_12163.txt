I teach a philosophy of biology seminar, and this semester we spent a couple of weeks on Gould and Lewontin's old spandrels paper alongside some of the real empirical literature that's grown up around specific candidate cases. It produced a genuinely great argument between two of my students that I'm still turning over. Jonah and Sasha stayed behind after everyone else cleared out and got into it over whether the human chin -- one of the running examples from lecture -- is a spandrel or an adaptation, and it snowballed into a bigger fight about whether "spandrel" is even a real, falsifiable category, or just adaptationism's just-so-story problem wearing a nicer coat.

Jonah went first: the chin is a derived trait unique to us -- not even Neanderthals had one, despite pretty similar jaws -- and when something that specific and consistent shows up right alongside the rest of the modern human package, treating it as functionless is the reckless move, not the cautious one. He leaned on the old mechanical account, that the chin braces the jaw against the "wishboning" stress of chewing and talking. Sasha shot back that the strain studies dont cooperate -- they've never reliably shown the chin bearing any real load -- and that recent comparative work across apes and fossil hominins finds most of what makes up "the chin" tracking other traits that are under direct selection instead, especially our shrunken front teeth and retracted faces, which is exactly the spandrel signature: a by-product thrown off by selection acting somewhere else on the skull entirely. Jonah's comeback was that "a by-product of selection elsewhere" doesn't explain why the by-product is also somewhat sexually dimorphic, citing a study that found men's and women's chins differing in shape -- which he took as evidence it's doing double duty as a signal of robustness, a strange thing for pure architectural leftover to be doing. Sasha had two responses: first, that a trait present in both sexes is a weak candidate for a sexual signal to begin with, unlike something sex-limited like a beard; and second, that even if it were later co-opted for signalling, that's just exaptation, which Gould himself made central to the idea -- a spandrel can originate as a pure by-product and only later come under weak selection for some new use without that history being any less spandrel-y. Her real complaint, though, was methodological: Jonah's whole approach -- "it's consistent and derived, so it must be doing something" -- is precisely the move Gould and Lewontin were attacking, treating adaptation as the default and then hunting for a plausible story, whereas the spandrel account actually predicts something specific and checkable (that chin traits should covary with the unrelated traits under direct selection, not with mechanical stress), and that's the prediction that's actually held up in the recent literature. Jonah's parting shot was that this is a bit rich coming from a Gould partisan, because the founding example of a "spandrel" -- the actual spandrels of San Marco -- turned out not to be structurally necessary either: architectural historians later showed that a dome over that kind of base didn't have to produce spandrels at all, there were other solutions available, so even the original case smuggled in exactly the kind of unfalsifiable "it had to be this way" story they were accusing adaptationists of. Maybe, he said, "spandrel" is just a just-so story with better branding.

Neither of them budged, and I copped out by telling them I'd have to think about it, which I'd now like to actually do properly. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's got the better of it at each stage -- not just an overall winner -- including whether Jonah's San Marco jab actually lands against Sasha's version of the argument or is more of a red herring? Take the empirical claims each of them makes at face value; what I'm actually interested in is whether the inferences being drawn hold up. And then tell me what, if anything, you think they're both missing about how you'd actually go about telling a spandrel from an adaptation in a case like this.
