In his 1979 Tanner Lecture "Equality of What?", Sen argues that neither welfare-egalitarianism nor Rawlsian primary-goods egalitarianism is an adequate metric of equality, since both ignore interpersonal differences in how people convert goods into valuable functionings, and he proposes capability equality as an alternative. Dworkin's "equality of resources" (1981) is often read as an attempt to defend a resourcist view against worries of exactly this kind.
(a) Explain Sen's "conversion factors" objection to resourcist metrics of equality, and explain how Dworkin's hypothetical insurance-market device is meant to answer at least part of it.
(b) Consider a person whose sincerely-held religious convictions require dietary ingredients that are only available via costly importation. Explain how Dworkin's theory of equality of resources would treat this case, and explain why this treatment has been a target of criticism.

And here's the answer we've been given to mark (this is exactly as written, I'm not paraphrasing):

(a) Sen's objection is that resources — including Rawlsian primary goods like income, liberties, and the social bases of self-respect — are only valuable as means to living a good life, and people differ in how efficiently they convert a given bundle of resources into valuable functionings. He calls the things that affect this efficiency "conversion factors": things like disability, metabolism, climate, or gender norms. Two people given the identical income could end up with very different real freedoms — a wheelchair user needs to spend more just to reach the mobility a non-disabled person gets for free, for instance. Dworkin tries to build a version of this sensitivity into his resourcist framework via the hypothetical insurance market: behind a veil of ignorance about which handicaps they'll personally end up with (but knowing the statistical odds), people decide how much coverage they'd buy against various handicaps, and the resulting average premiums become the basis for real-world compensation to those who do turn out handicapped, funded by something functioning like a tax on everyone else. So the insurance device is Dworkin's way of admitting that brute-luck differences in circumstances matter, without simply collapsing into equalizing welfare or capability directly.

(b) On Dworkin's theory, the religious dietary case would be handled the same way as a handicap: since the believer didn't choose their religious convictions any more than a person chooses to be born with a disability, the hypothetical insurance scheme would treat this as exactly the kind of costly, involuntary circumstance a prudent chooser behind the veil would want to insure against, so they'd be compensated for the extra cost of importing the ingredients. Sen's objection to this kind of solution is that resource compensation is the wrong thing to be checking in the first place — what should matter is whether the person actually ends up capable of practicing their faith adequately, and a cash transfer doesn't guarantee that outcome (e.g. if the ingredients are simply unobtainable at any price in that country). Cohen's objection is more internal to Dworkin's own framework: Cohen thinks Dworkin is wrong to make identification with a taste, rather than voluntary choice over it, the test for whether someone is responsible for it, since this means someone with a deeply held, totally unchosen conviction still isn't compensated just because they embrace rather than resent it, which looks arbitrary given Dworkin's own stated aim of neutralizing brute luck.

(c) Cohen's "equality of access to advantage" is basically Sen's capability insight combined with Dworkin's responsibility-tracking: like Sen, Cohen thinks the right currency has to be broader than raw resources and has to look at what people can actually achieve, not just what they hold, and like Dworkin, Cohen wants to filter out disadvantages that are the upshot of a person's own voluntary choices so we're not just compensating people for anything they happen to want. So access to advantage basically equals capability, weighted for choice. I think this succeeds as an improvement on Dworkin at least, since swapping in a straightforward choice test for the identification test handles the religious-diet case from (b) more sensibly.
