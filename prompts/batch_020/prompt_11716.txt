So a few people in my philosophy-of-mind reading group spent an entire session a couple weeks back on Turing's 1950 paper ("Computing Machinery and Intelligence"), and it turned into a genuine argument over a part almost nobody talks about. Everybody remembers the imitation game, but near the very end, after he's worked through objections like the theological one and Lady Lovelace's, Turing seriously entertains extrasensory perception (he lumps telepathy in with clairvoyance, precognition and psycho-kinesis) as a threat to his own test. His worry: if telepathy is real, the interrogator could ask the human contestant to guess cards being looked at by someone in another room; the human comes in above chance, the machine doesn't, and now the human gets "unmasked" for a reason that has nothing to do with thinking. His proposed fix is to put both contestants in a telepathy-proof room, the same way you'd soundproof a room against noise -- and, almost in passing, he says (more or less) that the statistical evidence for telepathy specifically is overwhelming, which is a wild thing to run into in the middle of an otherwise hard-headed paper.

Two people in the group, Owen and Mina, kept arguing about it over text after everyone else dropped off, and I can't decide who's actually winning. Owen's opening shot was that needing to invent shielding against telepathy on the fly shows the imitation game was never a principled test of thinking, just a test of "nothing turns up that a human interrogator would think looks like a machine," which will always need one more patch for the next weird channel someone dreams up. Mina said that's true of literally any real experiment -- you block confounds as you find them, the same way an RCT gets blinded and placebo-controlled, and nobody thinks that makes RCTs unprincipled. Owen came back that the analogy fails because with an RCT you know the mechanism you're blocking and can check the blocking worked, whereas Turing admits nobody knows how telepathy would even operate, so "telepathy proof room" isn't a control, it's just a label for a fix nobody could ever confirm actually works. Mina answered that Turing never claims anyone could build or verify such a room right now -- the whole passage is explicitly conditional, granting the objection its strongest form just to show it doesn't force him to abandon the method even in the worst case, and that demanding the fix be verifiable today proves too much, since we make conditional claims like that about all sorts of poorly understood effects. Owen then switched tracks and said the deeper problem is Turing's own apparent sincerity about the "overwhelming" evidence -- if he's that wrong about a question he clearly looked into, it should knock down our confidence in his instincts everywhere else in the paper. Mina thought that was a cheap shot: the imitation game doesn't stand or fall on Turing's parapsychological opinions, and anyway several serious statisticians at the time treated the Rhine card-guessing data as a real puzzle worth explaining rather then an obvious joke, so slapping "credulous" on him is importing hindsight that nobody had in 1950.

Could you go through this move by move and say who's actually right at each step, not just who wins overall? I'm especially curious whether Owen's "unprincipled patch" complaint and his "unverifiable shielding" complaint are really the same objection twice, or genuinely separate problems, and whether there's some angle on this section that neither of them spotted.
