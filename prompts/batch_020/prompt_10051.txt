Explain this view in your own words. And here's the student's answer (transcribed exactly, typos and all): "Berkeley's central claim in The Querist is that money is not identical with wealth -- real wealth is the industry and labor of a nation's people, and money, whether coin or paper, is merely an instrument or 'counter' for reckoning and transfering claims on that wealth. This is why he thinks Ireland doesn't need more gold and silver in circulation; what it needs is more industriousness, and a bank issuing credit can stimulate that industry even without any new specie coming into the country. This view is basically a direct application of his immaterialism: since Berkeley denies that matter exists independently of being perceived, it follows that gold and silver can't have the kind of intrinsic value that Locke assigns them, so money has to be understood as a mind-dependent sign rather than a material commodity. Locke, by contrast, held that money's value is closely tied to the intrinsic, natural properties of gold and silver as a scarce, durable commodity, which is the metallist assumption Berkeley is reacting against. Because Berkeley thinks money is only a sign of a nation's productive power and not a store of precious metal, he proposes a national bank whose notes would be backed by gold reserves held in trust, so that the paper could always be redeemed for 'real' money on demand, giving the public confidence in the new currency."
