(b) Explain how his position compares to Hume's later skepticism about causation. (c) Does the text commit al-Ghazali to full occasionalism, or does it leave the question open?"

And here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"Al-Ghazali's target is the philosophers' (chiefly Ibn Sina's) claim that a cause necessitates its effect by virtue of its essence, so that fire touching cotton must produce burning. He points out that we only ever observe the two events conjoined, never a necessary connection between them, and that it's conceivable God could create burning without the fire touching the cotton, or withhold burning despite the fire's presence -- which is exactly what he says happened when Abraham was thrown into the fire and it did not burn him. So the regularity we see is just God's habitual way of acting, not a power residing in fire itself.

This is essentially the same insight Malebranche would develop a few centuries later against Cartesian interactionism: both deny that finite things have genuine causal efficacy and locate all real agency in God, who produces the effect on the occasion of the apparent cause. Hume differs in motivation and conclusion -- his argument is empiricist, not theological (he's asking what impression our idea of necessary connection could come from, not defending the possibility of miracles), and he ends up denying necessary connection full stop, reducing causation to constant conjunction plus a habit of expecting one thing after another. Al-Ghazali keeps real necessitation in the picture; he just relocates it from secondary causes to God's will.

As for whether al-Ghazali is a full occasionalist, the text settles this: since fire is shown to have no power to necessitate burning, he concludes that fire -- and every other natural thing -- has no causal efficacy whatsoever, and that God alone is the real cause of every event, directly and without intermediary. The philosophers' 'natures' turn out to be causally idle."
