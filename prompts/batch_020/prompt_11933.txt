I'm a novelist. My most recent book was a work of historical fiction set in 1760s Glasgow and Edinburgh in which Adam Smith makes a couple of cameo appearances, and that book is apparently why I've been co-opted onto my university's Heritage and Naming Review Panel as the resident "expert" whenever his name comes up. It has come up this term. A student petition, backed by a number of humanities and social-science staff, wants the university to rename the Adam Smith Lecture Theatre on the grounds that his economic theory supplied the intellectual scaffolding for Britain's colonial expansion, and that continuing to honour him amounts to institutional whitewashing. The petition quotes Smith's own verdict on the colonial project — that it had "hitherto been, not an empire, but the project of an empire; not a gold mine, but the project of a gold mine" — as evidence that even he saw the whole enterprise as a costly failure. A rebuttal circulating among the panel's economists quotes his outrage at the "savage injustice of the Europeans" toward the native peoples of the Americas and the East Indies as evidence that he was, if anything, an early critic of imperial conduct rather than its apologist. Both quotations, as it happens, come from the same chapter of Book IV — "Of Colonies" — and I don't trust either camp to have read much past the sentence it needed.

Our terms of reference require the panel to settle on exactly one of three recommendations at our meeting in a fortnight: retain the name unchanged, retain it alongside a permanent contextualising plaque (for which we would need to sketch out the actual wording), or rename the theatre outright. Could you draft the short memo I will circulate to the panel beforehand? I need it to engage properly with what Smith actually argued in that chapter — about the monopoly of colonial trade, the costs that empire imposed on Britain itself, his proposal for a taxed-and-represented colonial union, and his treatment of the peoples on the receiving end of European expansion — rather than settling the matter by gesturing vaguely at "historical context" or "the standards of his time." Whichever recommendation you reach, state the strongest version of the losing argument in terms its proponents would recognize as their own, not a straw version of it; several of my colleagues have well-honed instincts for spotting the latter. And whatever you recommend, give it a concrete next step and a date — wording for the plaque, a timeline for the renaming, or a fixed point at which the panel should revisit the question if we retain the name outright.
