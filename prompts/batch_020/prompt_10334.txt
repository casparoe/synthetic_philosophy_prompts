I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I got into Popper through the usual "falsifiability" discourse in tech circles, and that eventually led me to his notion of verisimilitude: the idea that a theory can be strictly false and still count as "closer to the truth" than some other false theory, which is what lets him claim that science progresses even though our best theories keep getting refuted.

As best I can reconstruct it, Popper tried to formalize this in the early 1960s with a fairly simple set-theoretic idea: split a theory's logical consequences into its true consequences ("truth content") and false consequences ("falsity content"), then say one theory has more verisimilitude than another when its truth content includes the other's while its falsity content adds nothing new (roughly). I later read that Tichý and Miller independently showed, around 1974, that this definition quietly collapses: under Popper's own definition, no false theory can ever count as more verisimilar than another false theory -- the only way to improve on a false theory, in his technical sense, is to replace it with one that is completely true. Since virtually every historically interesting case of scientific progress (Newton improving on Aristotle, say) involves one false theory succeeding another, this seems to undermine the entire point of the concept.

I can accept that this is the received verdict, but I don't have a good gut-level feel for why the definition fails this way -- what exactly about combining "at least as much truth content" with "no more falsity content" forces the collapse once any falsity is allowed in at all? I'd also be curious whether the later similarity-based repairs (Niiniluoto's, Oddie's) genuinely solve this problem or just relocate it elsewhere. A full formal derivation isn't necessary -- I'm mainly after the underlying intuition.
