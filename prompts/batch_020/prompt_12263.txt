Gregory Kavka's toxin puzzle is a standard fixture in graduate seminars on rational choice theory and philosophy of action. Below is a take-home exam question on the puzzle, followed by a student's answer. Please grade the answer.

**Question (20 points).** An eccentric billionaire places a vial of mild toxin in front of you. Drinking it will make you painfully ill for a day but has no lasting effects and poses no danger to your life. The billionaire will transfer one million dollars to your bank account by nine o'clock tomorrow morning if, and only if, at midnight tonight you form a genuine intention to drink the toxin at noon tomorrow. A perfectly reliable device reads off whether you have really formed this intention. You need not actually drink the toxin to receive the money, and whether you drink it or not the next day has no effect whatsoever on your bank balance.

(a) [6 points] Explain why Kavka holds that, although possessing the intention would obviously benefit the agent, a rational agent cannot form it.
(b) [7 points] Explain how the toxin puzzle differs, in its underlying structure, from Newcomb's Problem and from the Prisoner's Dilemma.
(c) [7 points] Present one influential response to the puzzle from the philosophical literature, and assess whether it succeeds in showing that the intention can, after all, be rationally formed.

**Student's answer:**

(a) The puzzle trades on a gap between the value of having an intention and the value of executing it. Once midnight has passed, whether the million dollars is coming is already settled, so drinking the toxin at noon can do the agent no further good and can only make her sick for a day. Kavka's point is that a rational intention is not a mere wish but a settled disposition to act as planned when the moment arrives, absent a relevant change in one's reasons. Since the agent already knows at midnight that she will have no reason whatsoever to drink the toxin at noon, she cannot, in full awareness of this fact, form a genuine intention to do so; at best she can wish she could form it. So although having the intention would clearly benefit her, the state of having it is not one she can bring about at will, because a rational intention must be backed by an expectation of complying when the time comes, and here that expectation is guaranteed to fail.

(b) The toxin puzzle is often mentioned alongside Newcomb's Problem and the Prisoner's Dilemma because all three have been used to probe the limits of orthodox rational choice theory, but the toxin puzzle is really the same kind of problem as Newcomb's Problem in its logical bones: both are cases where straightforward dominance reasoning recommends one option (two-box; don't drink) while the presence of a reliable predictor makes the opposite disposition (one-box; drink) the one correlated with getting the reward, so that in each case orthodox decision theory must choose between rewarding the dominant act and rewarding the disposition a good predictor would detect. The Prisoner's Dilemma is a different sort of case, since it is fundamentally interpersonal: the tension there is that defection dominates cooperation for each player individually even though mutual cooperation is better for both, which is a problem of coordinating with another self-interested agent rather than a problem internal to a single agent's own psychology. Kavka's puzzle, like Newcomb's, needs no second rational agent at all, only an individual and a reliable detector of her state of mind.

(c) One well-known response comes from Gauthier's theory of resolute choice, developed in *Morals by Agreement* to handle problems structurally similar to the Prisoner's Dilemma. On this view, a rational agent chooses in advance the plan that is best from the standpoint of the whole sequence of choices, and then sticks to that plan even at later choice points where a purely local, forward-looking calculation would recommend deviating. Applied to the toxin puzzle, a resolute chooser at midnight adopts the plan "intend to drink, and then actually drink," because that is the plan that yields the million dollars, and she does not treat the momentary unpleasantness of the toxin as a reason to reopen a question she has already settled. This response is at least partly successful: it shows that the conception of rationality Kavka relies on, on which each choice must be independently optimal given only the reasons available at that moment, is not the only coherent conception of practical rationality, and that a plan-based conception can vindicate drinking the toxin as the rational completion of a rational plan. Where the response is weaker is that it must concede that, from the standpoint of ordinary act-by-act maximization, drinking the toxin still looks irrational at noon; resolute choice does not dissolve the tension so much as relocate it, by introducing a different and more holistic standard of rationality that Kavka's original argument did not consider.
