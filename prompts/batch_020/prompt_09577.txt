In my graduate seminar on the philosophy of perception, we recently worked through Chaz Firestone and Brian Scholl's methodological critique of the "top-down effects on perception" literature. Their central complaint is that many canonical demonstrations of cognitive penetration—hills looking steeper to fatigued or heavily burdened observers, desirable objects looking subjectively closer to motivated perceivers, and so on—are better explained by demand characteristics, biased comparison classes, or judgment-level response bias than by any genuine change in the phenomenal character of perceptual experience, so that a suitably restricted impenetrability thesis survives. As one sharp student in the seminar pointed out, this diagnosis is aimed squarely at effects mediated by occurrent, personal-level cognitive states—beliefs, desires, emotions—and so leaves untouched any case of penetration that is supposed to work through a different kind of mechanism entirely.

The case she raised is Fiona Macpherson's proposal of an "indirect" mechanism of cognitive penetration, illustrated by memory-colour effects: an achromatic, banana-shaped patch must be given a slight blue tinge before subjects will match it to a neutral grey, which is naturally explained by a stored association between banana-shaped things and the colour yellow biasing the visual system's computation of colour appearance, independently of anything the subject currently believes or wants. Because the effect shows up in forced-choice colour matching rather than in an explicit, comparison-sensitive judgment of the sort Firestone and Scholl are so good at debunking, it looks like exactly the kind of case that might survive their objection. I would like help pressure-testing my student's optimism here. Please construct the strongest available argument for the position that Macpherson's memory-colour cases nonetheless fail to establish cognitive penetration in any sense that should trouble a defender of a modular, cognitively impenetrable early vision system—for instance, by arguing that stored, sub-personal colour associations of this kind belong with ordinary, encapsulated perceptual-constancy mechanisms (of a piece with shape or lightness constancy) rather than with the belief-sensitive, personal-level interactions between cognition and perception that made the cognitive-penetration debate matter in the first place for the epistemic standing of perceptual justification.
