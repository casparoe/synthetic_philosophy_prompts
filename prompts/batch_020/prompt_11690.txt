Funny timing: I'm teaching a graduate seminar on the philosophy of promising this term, and now I've actually got a live case in my own department instead of just thought experiments. Short version: our hiring committee had a candidate everyone loved, and back in Febuary our chair told her, over email, something like "the offer is basically finalized at this point, HR just needs to process it, so please don't accept anything else." Nothing was ever signed. She took it at her word anyway, turned down a competing tenure-track offer at another school, and told her current chair she was resigning effective this summer. Two weeks ago the dean's office froze the line because of a sudden budget shortfall, so there is no position to actually give her, and I think our chairs quietly hoping the whole situation just goes away on its own.

I've been put on a small ad hoc committee that has to bring the dean a recommendation on what, if anything, the department owes this candidate, given that nothing was ever a legally binding contract. I don't want to show up with "well, it feels bad, so we should do something" -- I want to circulate an actual memo beforehand that recommends a concrete course of action (money, a standing offer for next cycle, whatever you think is defensible) with a rough timeframe attached, but that grounds the recommendation in a real answer to why an unwritten assurance like that generates any obligation in the first place. Is the source of the obligation the reliance we invited her to place on our word, some kind of convention about how hiring committees are understood to talk, something closer to Scanlon's assurance-based story, or does it actually matter that it was an institution and not a single person doing the "promising"? And please don't hand me the version that flatters us -- two people on this committee are going to argue, seriously, that a sincere apology is the entire extent of what we owe her, and the memo needs to engage that position honestly rather than wave it away.
