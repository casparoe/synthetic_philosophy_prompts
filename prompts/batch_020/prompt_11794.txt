This semester I'm taking a graduate seminar, cross-listed between the philosophy and statistics departments, on formal epistemology and Bayesian decision theory. Two weeks ago we covered I. J. Good's theorem that, for an idealized Bayesian agent who can act optimally on whatever they learn, taking a cost-free look at further evidence before deciding never lowers (and generically raises) their expected utility relative to deciding blind — essentially a formal vindication of the older philosophical principle of total evidence. This week, almost in passing, our professor mentioned that this result is closely related to David Blackwell's older theorem on when one statistical experiment counts as uniformly more informative than another, via the existence of a "garbling" (equivalently, a sufficiency relation) connecting the two experiments. She moved on before spelling out the connection, and neither of the two textbook chapters we were assigned cross-references the other result, so I would like to work out the relationship myself before I next see her at office hours.

Could you compare the two theorems properly for me? Specifically, is Good's result best understood as nothing more than the special case of Blackwell's ordering of experiments in which one of the two experiments being compared is simply the totally uninformative "null" experiment — so that Good, in effect, rediscovered a narrow corollary of Blackwell's more general theory — or is there a genuine disanalogy between them? I ask because Good's argument is normally run directly through the convexity of the optimal-decision value function (via Jensen's inequality, applied to the fact that a Bayesian's posterior belief is a martingale), rather than through Blackwell's garbling apparatus, and because Blackwell's theorem is a claim about every conceivable decision problem at once, whereas Good's is standardly stated for one fixed prior and utility function at a time. I would like your own assessment of how deep the connection actually runs, rather than just a list of surface similarities, and if you think the two results are not fully equivalent, a concrete example illustrating the gap between them would help a great deal.
