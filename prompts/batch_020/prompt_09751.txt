I've been reporting on how AI-safety folks keep repurposing old decision-theory puzzles, and last weekend two friends of mine -- one finishing a philosophy MPhil, the other an ML engineer who spends way to much time on rationalist blogs -- got into a proper argument over dinner about the "Death in Damascus" case, and it's been nagging at me since. Quick recap in case it's useful: a man in Damascus runs into Death, who gives him a look of surprise. Spooked, the man rides off that night to Aleppo. Turns out Death was surprised to see him in Damascus at all, because he already had an appointment to meet the man in Aleppo. In the decision-theory version of the story, Death is basically never wrong about which city you'll end up in, and you have to decide where to spend the night knowing that wherever you pick is strong evidence Death will show up there too -- which, obviously, you'd rather avoid.

Roughly how the argument went: (1) The philosopher said the case is basically a knockdown argument against evidential decision theory -- since picking either city counts as "bad news" the moment you've picked it, EDT can never actually settle on an action, it just wants you to switch forever, and a theory of rational choice that can't recommend an action isn't one. (2) The engineer said that's not quite fair -- all the flip-flopping shows is that neither city is "ratifiable," and you could just as well conclude that the ratifiability requirement is the broken part, not the underlying evidential idea that you should act on the news your own choice would deliver. (3) The philosopher answered that dropping ratifiability doesn't save EDT, because even before any of that deliberation-loop stuff kicks in, EDT already rates both cities as equally lousy, so it gives you no actual reason to prefer one over the other -- whereas causal decision theory gets the obviously right answer for the obviously right reason: your choice doesn't cause Death to be anywhere, so you should just flip a coin (or go wherever's cheaper), since the causal expected utilities are identical either way. (4) The engineer wasn't having it: if Death is genuinely that good a predictor, he said, then a coin-flipping habit is exactly the kind of policy Death would predict too, so the causal theorist hasn't actually escaped the correlation, he's just shoved it up a level, from "which city" to "which method you use to pick a city." Neither of them budged, and honestly I couldn't tell you who won.

Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who's got the better of it at each step, rather than just handing down one overall verdict? And if there's something both of them seem to be circling around without quite naming it -- some cleaner way of stating what's actually at stake in the disagreement -- I'd be curious to hear that too.
