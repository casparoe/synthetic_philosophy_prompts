Write a dialogue between a causal decision theorist and a defender of what Parfit calls "rational irrationality," imagining that both have just lived through Parfit's hitchhiker: dying of thirst in the desert, each was picked up only because her driver predicted she would pay the fare once safely in town. Standing together now at the town's only cash machine, the causal decision theorist announces that she is keeping her money, since paying at this point cannot causally affect a rescue that has already happened — a policy she should recognize as exactly McClennen's "sophisticated" choice. Her companion, withdrawing cash for his own driver and defending something closer to Gauthier's constrained maximization and McClennen's "resolute" chooser, tries to talk her into paying anyway. Have each press the other on the hardest version of their view: can the causal decision theorist explain how a sufficiently good predictor ever let her into the car, given that this very refusal is what such a predictor should have foreseen? And can her companion explain what, short of wishful thinking about a past he can no longer touch, makes an earlier resolution binding on a later self for whom paying now serves no further purpose? Neither should be allowed the last, unanswered word.
