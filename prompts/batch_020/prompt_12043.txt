I'm a second-year PhD student in history and philosophy of science working on the early history of probability and expected-utility theory, and I'm currently writing my qualifying paper on Daniel Bernoulli's 1738 memoir for the St. Petersburg Academy, in which he resolves the St. Petersburg paradox by arguing that the utility of an increase in wealth is inversely proportional to the wealth one already possesses, so that a rational agent should evaluate a lottery by its expected logarithm of wealth rather than by its expected monetary payoff. This idea is the centerpiece of my chapter on the origins of expected-utility theory. At my last committee meeting, though, one of my readers — who worked in formal decision theory before moving into philosophy of science — posed what she clearly intended as a friendly stress test: would Bernoulli's diminishing-marginal-utility move also get him out of the two-envelope paradox, or does that puzzle expose the limits of "just posit a concave utility function" as a general-purpose remedy for expectation-based paradoxes? I did not have a good answer on the spot, and it has been bothering me ever since, because it bears directly on how ambitious a claim I can responsibly make about the reach of Bernoulli's idea.

For the sake of a fixed target, the version of the two-envelope paradox I have in mind is the familiar one: you are handed one of two indistinguishable envelopes and told, truthfully, that one contains exactly twice as much money as the other, and you are given the option of switching to the other envelope. Writing X for whatever amount happens to be in the envelope you are holding, the notorious argument holds that the other envelope contains 2X with probability one-half and X/2 with probability one-half, so that its expected value is 1.25X; since this reasoning seems to go through for any value of X whatsoever, and indeed for the newly acquired envelope after a swap, it appears to recommend switching without end.

What I would like from you is not a summary of either paradox — I know both well by now — but an actual reconstruction, worked out rather than merely asserted, of how Bernoulli himself would reason through the two-envelope scenario. First, carry out the expected-utility calculation he would have performed, using his logarithmic value function, and tell me what it yields for the decision to switch. Second, tell me whether whatever you find is a peculiarity of the exact logarithmic form he happened to propose, or whether it would equally be delivered by any utility function exhibiting diminishing marginal utility of wealth more generally — this is really the stronger claim my reader's question was pressing on. Third, and this is the part I most want your honest judgment on: even in the best case for Bernoulli, would neutralizing any temptation to switch really amount to solving the two-envelope paradox as it is normally posed — a puzzle about a piece of reasoning concerning money, prior to any utility function being introduced at all — or would a mathematician whose own uncle, Jacob Bernoulli, had all but founded the mathematical theory of probability have instead recognized that the real fault lies in an illegitimate assumption about the probabilities themselves, one that dressing the argument up in units of utility rather than money does nothing to repair?
