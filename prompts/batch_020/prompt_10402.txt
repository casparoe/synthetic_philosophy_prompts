Three weeks ago I was hit by a delivery van while cycling home from work and broke my hip badly enough that I'm now the plaintiff in a personal injury claim heading to mediation next month. As part of preparing me for the negotiation, my attorney had me work through a short "risk appetite" questionnaire that she says helps her gauge how hard to push for certainty versus upside once we're in the room; the dollar figures in it are purely hypothetical calibration numbers and have nothing to do with what my actual case is worth. Two of the questions have nonetheless stayed with me, because a friend of mine who works as an actuary looked over my answers afterward and told me they don't hold together.

The first question asked me to choose between (A) a guaranteed $1 million, or (B) an 89% chance of $1 million, a 10% chance of $5 million, and a 1% chance of nothing. I chose the guaranteed million without much deliberation. The second question asked me to choose between (C) an 11% chance of $1 million and an 89% chance of nothing, or (D) a 10% chance of $5 million and a 90% chance of nothing. Here I chose D, reaching for the bigger long-shot prize instead of the safer-looking one. My friend's objection is that both questions are built from the same underlying pieces: an 89% slice of probability that pays $1 million in the first question and nothing in the second, plus an 11% slice in which I am, in effect, always choosing between "a guaranteed $1 million" and "a shot at $5 million with some risk of nothing." Since that 11%-slice choice is identical in both questions, he argues, what it pays out shouldn't depend on what the other, shared 89% slice happens to pay -- and yet my preference flipped depending on exactly that.

I have no background in formal decision theory, and normally I'd let this go, but I have to sit in a mediation room in a few weeks and make choices that look a great deal like these, with real money attached, and I don't want to be talked into "correcting" an instinct that might be perfectly sound. Was my friend right that I've contradicted myself, so that only one of my two answers can really be defended, or is there a serious way of thinking about risk and certainty in which both of my instincts hold up at once?
