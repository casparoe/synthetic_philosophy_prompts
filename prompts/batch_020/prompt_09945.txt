I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on the metaphysics of time, and most of my formal training sits squarely in the analytic tradition: McTaggart's distinction between the A-series and the B-series, the resulting debates about tense, change, and the coherence of presentism, and so on. Over the break, though, a friend in comparative literature talked me into finally reading Borges's essay "A New Refutation of Time" ("Nueva refutación del tiempo," collected in *Otras Inquisiciones*), and I was struck by how much real argumentative bite it has — this isn't just the usual labyrinths-and-forking-paths Borges I'd been half-remembering from undergrad.

What he actually does is build a case against a single, unified time by extending Berkeley's denial of matter and Hume's denial of a persisting self: if what we ever actually encounter is not an enduring substance or an enduring subject but only a series of discrete perceptions, then, Borges argues, we have no better warrant for positing "time" as the thread that strings those perceptions together than Berkeley or Hume had for positing matter or the self behind them. And yet the essay's closing paragraph seems to concede the game: Borges all but admits that the very act of mounting this argument is itself an irreducibly successive performance, so that time reasserts itself in the moment he tries to talk himself out of it.

What I'd like help with is thinking through how this stacks up against the argument I already know well, McTaggart's case for the unreality of time via the alleged contradiction in the A-series. Could you compare the two in some depth — not just listing similarities and differences, but working out whether, underneath their very different idioms (an idealist psychology of perception versus a paradox about tensed properties), they are actually doing structurally analogous work? I'm especially interested in whether McTaggart's argument is vulnerable to something like the self-undermining, performative worry Borges raises against his own conclusion: does laying out the argument as a sequence of premises culminating in a conclusion covertly presuppose exactly the kind of temporal ordering, or even A-series-style becoming, that McTaggart wants to unmask as incoherent — or is the ordering of an exposition just innocuous B-series succession that leaves his conclusion untouched? Please don't leave the comparison open-ended: tell me which of the two arguments you think does more damage to our ordinary confidence in the reality of time, and defend that verdict.
