My aunt Carol is retiring this spring after thirty-six years teaching political theory, and somehow I got voluntold to give the toast at her retirement dinner, which is terrifying because about half the guest list is her old colleagues and a couple of grad students who are going to catch every single reference, and the other half is family and old family friends who mostly think "political theory" means she has strong opinions about the news. Her whole career, more or less, orbited something called the boundary problem -- basically the puzzle of who actually counts as "the people" in a democracy in the first place. Citizens only? Residents? Everyone affected by a given decision, even people who aren't born yet? Every answer she ever laid out for me had this maddening circularity built into it, where you apparently need "the people" to already exist as a thing before they can vote on who counts as the people, which is a bit like needing a guest list before you can vote on the guest list. I've been leaning on her a lot for this, actually, because I'm writing a novel that hinges on a nasty school board election, and she's been my free consultant for about a year now.

Here's the bit I can't let go of: the dinner itself needed a headcount for the caterer, there are little name cards on the tables, and somebody's plus-one didn't get cleared in time, and none of that got settled by anything Carol would, on principle, ever call legitimate. I feel like there's a genuinely warm joke sitting right there -- her own retirement party as a tiny instance of the exact problem she spent her life on -- but every draft I try either goes over the family half's heads or comes off glib, like I'm implying thirty-six years of work amounts to "just use name tags, Carol." Could you write the actual toast for me? Something around five minutes, built around that joke or a sharper one if you've got it, that lands for the theory people without loosing the cousins, and that doesn't quietly imply she ever landed on the clean answer -- she'd be the first to tell you, happily, that there isn't one, and the toast still needs to end up feeling like a celebration and not a seminar.
