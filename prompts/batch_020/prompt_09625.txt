So I've been on one of those nostalgic deep-dives through old LessWrong decision theory posts lately (the way you do at 1am instead of sleeping), and I landed back on Wei Dai's old post about the absent-minded driver problem, the Piccione & Rubinstein one. Setup: a driver hits two intersections she genuinely cannot tell apart. Exit at the first one and the payoff's 0, exit at the second and it's 4, sail past both and it's 1. The "planning-optimal" answer, the one she'd commit to back at the pub before she ever gets in the car, is to continue with probability 2/3 at each intersection. Fine so far.

What got me was the comment section. Everybody more or less converges on this being The Answer, full stop, once you frame it as a UDT-style optimization over global policies, and that all the talk in the original paper (and in Aumann, Hart and Perry's reply to it) about "the probability that I'm at X right now instead of Y" was confused from the start. taw and pengvado spend a good chunk of the thread showing why: Piccione and Rubinstein's own way of plugging that probability in is circular, it depends on the very p you're trying to solve for, and even the self-consistent fix, alpha = 1/(1+p), which pengvado works out to about p = 0.53 instead of 2/3, turns out to be answering the wrong question, because it double-counts the one run of the experiment where the driver passes through both intersections. Theres also a much newer comment, from someone going by Ape in the coat, that tries to kill the question outright by arguing "I'm at X and not at Y" was never even a well-formed event for this driver, so there was nothing there to condition on to begin with.

I brought this up in my rationalist book club's group chat and just got "lol UDT already solved this" back, and that's never fully sat right with me. It feels a bit like how halfers wave away thirders on Sleeping Beauty without engaging with why Elga thought waking up should itself shift your credence. So here's what I actually want: give me the strongest case you can build that the driver's in-the-moment credence about which intersection she's at is a fully well-defined, legitimate thing for her to hold and act on, and that it can rationally lead her to depart from her own 2/3 plan once she's actually standing at the intersection, without that being a bug, an inconsistency, or something a sufficiently "updateless" agent would just never allow to happen to it. Don't dodge the two objections above, the double-counting problem and the "that's not a real event" move — those are exactly the two things your argument has to survive, so deal with them head-on instead of just gesturing at Bayesian updating and moving on.
