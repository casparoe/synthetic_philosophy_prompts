One of the students in my section produced the following answer. I haven't annotated it myself yet and would rather form an independent judgment first.

Student's answer: (a) Kant's example of the man whose own sorrows have extinguished his natural sympathy, yet who still helps others, is meant to isolate moral worth from any contribution made by inclination. Kant grants that many people are naturally sympathetic enough that they take real pleasure in making others happy, but he says that when someone acts helpfully out of that kind of pleasure, we cannot tell whether they are acting from duty or merely from the pleasant feeling itself, since both would produce the same helpful behavior. Only when the pleasant feeling is absent or overridden, and the person still acts to help, can we be sure the motive is respect for the moral law, i.e., duty. So the passage seems to commit Kant to the view that moral worth requires that inclination not be present, or at least that it not be doing any of the motivational work.

(b) Herman argues that this "no-inclination" reading over-interprets Kant. Her proposal is that the motive of duty need not be the only thing motivating the agent; an agent can be simultaneously inclined to help and moved by duty. What duty must do, on Herman's reading, is function as a limiting condition: it must be the motive that ensures the agent still performs the right action even if her inclination were to prove weak, absent, or misdirected. Duty operates less like a competing motivational force that has to beat inclination and win out, and more like a standing disposition that only has to take over in the cases where inclination alone would not be enough. This lets Herman say that ordinary "overdetermined" cases of helping — where the agent is both inclined to help and would help anyway from duty — can still have full moral worth, contrary to what the cold-sympathy passage seems to imply on its face.

(c) Arpaly's Huck Finn case is meant to cut even deeper than the cases Herman is addressing. Huck has been raised to believe that helping an enslaved person escape is a sin and a crime; when the moment comes, his explicit, reflectively endorsed principle tells him to turn Jim in, but he can't bring himself to do it and helps Jim escape instead, all the while believing he is doing something wrong. Arpaly's claim is that Huck's action has moral worth precisely because he is responding to the morally relevant fact of the matter — Jim's suffering and his claim to freedom — even though Huck has no correct maxim, explicit or otherwise, that endorses what he is doing. I do not think this is a special problem for Herman, though: her view only requires that duty be ready to take over as a backup if inclination fails, and in Huck's case it is precisely his feeling for Jim, functioning much like Herman's backup motive, that ends up getting him to the right action once his explicit principles fail him. If anything, Huck's case looks like a vindication of Herman's structure, just with sympathy standing in for the role duty would normally play.

(d) Markovits's Coincident Reasons Thesis says that an action has moral worth to the extent that the reasons for which the agent acts coincide with the reasons that actually justify the action. On her view, Huck's action would have full moral worth just in case Huck is moved by the very facts that make helping Jim the right thing to do — Jim's suffering, his claim to freedom, and so on — and the thesis additionally requires that Huck also be moved by the recognition that these facts make helping Jim the right thing to do, since otherwise his sympathy would just be a lucky coincidence rather than a genuine tracking of the moral reasons. In that sense, Markovits's view is best seen as extending Herman's basic insight — that the relevant motive need not be the agent's only motive, just the one doing the essential work — to non-Kantian settings, substituting "whatever the correct theory says the justifying reason is" for "duty."
