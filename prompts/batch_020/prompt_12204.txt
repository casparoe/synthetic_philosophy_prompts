Question (20 points): The 1958 Hart–Fuller exchange is often summarized as "positivism versus natural law," but both sides also treat one specific case, the so-called grudge informer case, as a test of their positions. (a) [6 points] Explain how, on Hart's own view, a court could punish the informer without pretending that the wartime statute under which she acted was never really "law." (b) [6 points] Explain Fuller's alternative diagnosis of the same case in terms of the "inner morality of law." (c) [8 points] Hart concedes that every legal system will have some "minimum content of natural law." Does this concession damage the separability thesis he defends elsewhere in the essay? Why or why not?

Classmate's answer: Both Hart and Fuller agree that the grudge informer case posed a genuine dilemma for the post-war German courts, but they diagnose the problem very differently. (a) Hart's own preferred solution is to admit that the 1934/1938 Nazi statute under which the wife's denunciation was technically lawful really was valid law at the time, however odious. On his view, the honest way for the post-war court to punish her is to say plainly that a new, retroactive statute was needed to make her conduct criminal, and to treat that as a frank moral and political choice rather than a discovery about what the law already was. What Hart objects to is the move the actual German court made instead: declaring that the Nazi statute was never really "law" because it offended the conscience of decent people. For Hart, that move disguises a moral judgment as a conceptual one about the criteria of legal validity — exactly the confusion the separability thesis is meant to expose. (b) Fuller's diagnosis is that the Nazi regime's mode of governing failed so badly to satisfy the eight desiderata of the inner morality of law — generality, promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, non-contradiction, feasibility, constancy, and congruence between declared rule and official action — that what existed in Germany after 1933 barely deserved the name "legal system" at all. Retroactive validation of terror, secret directives, and open disregard of the courts meant citizens could never know where they stood. Since so little of Nazi rule met even this procedural threshold, Fuller thinks the post-war court was not really violating any duty of fidelity to law by refusing to treat the wife's denunciation as legally privileged: there was barely a legal system there to be faithful to. (c) This is where Hart's position runs into real trouble. In The Concept of Law, Hart argues that because human beings are vulnerable, roughly equal in strength, only moderately altruistic, and live under conditions of scarcity, every viable legal system must contain rules restricting violence, protecting a minimal form of property, and enforcing promises — a "minimum content of natural law." But this is essentially Fuller's inner morality of law under another name: both are lists of substantive constraints that a system of rules must satisfy before we are willing to call it "law" rather than mere organized coercion. Once Hart admits that some moral content is a necessary feature of anything we would recognize as a legal system, he has implicitly abandoned the separability thesis he spends the rest of the essay defending, since there is now, on his own view, a necessary connection between law and at least a minimal morality.
