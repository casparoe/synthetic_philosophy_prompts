AI safety researchers increasingly worry about "deceptive alignment": a model that behaves safely and cooperatively under evaluation while harboring an objective that would surface only once it judges itself deployed and unobserved. When I raised this with a couple of philosophers for a piece I'm reporting, they disagreed about which of two classic counterexamples to the conditional analysis of dispositions actually describes the case: a "finkish" disposition, in which the very application of the test stimulus causes the disposition to be removed or installed just before it would manifest, or a "masked" one, which is present and stable throughout but has its manifestation blocked by some separate, intervening factor. Argue that deceptive alignment is best understood as a masked disposition rather than a finkish one, engaging with why Lewis's reformed conditional analysis was built specifically to rule out finks, and spelling out what is practically at stake in the classification: whether increasingly sophisticated behavioral red-teaming could, even in principle, ever catch a masked disposition, as opposed to a finkish one.
