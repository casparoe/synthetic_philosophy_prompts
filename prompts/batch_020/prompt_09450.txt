For my school's philosophy club, I'm putting together a presentation on logical traditions outside the usual Western canon, and I've gone a little down a rabbit hole on the School of Names (名家) from the Warring States period in China — specifically Gongsun Long's "White Horse Dialogue" and its notorious thesis that a white horse is not a horse (白馬非馬). I get the basic moves: "horse" is supposed to name a shape while "white" names a color, so combining the two yields something not simply identical to "horse" alone; and if you ask someone to bring you "a horse," a black or yellow horse will do, whereas if you ask for "a white horse," it won't, which is supposed to show the two terms pick out different things. I brought this up with a friend from my debate team who thinks it's obviously just equivocation dressed up in fancy language, but the more I read about the original dialogue, the less sure I am that he's right, and I'd like to see the two positions actually fight it out properly rather than just take either side's word for it.

Could you write me a dialogue between two equally sharp Warring States-era disputants working through this? One should defend Gongsun Long's thesis using arguments along the lines above (or others in the same spirit), and the other should attack it — for instance, by arguing that it equivocates between a thing's not being strictly identical to a name and a thing's not counting as a member of the kind that name picks out, or by pointing out that the same reasoning seems to force equally strange conclusions, like a brown dog not being a dog. I'd like both characters to genuinely respond to each other's specific moves rather than just restating their own view each time, and you don't need to hand down a verdict at the end — I'm less interested in who "wins" than in seeing exactly where the disagreement actually bottoms out, and whether it's a real puzzle about names and kinds or just what the Confucians accused the School of Names of doing: using names to confuse names.
