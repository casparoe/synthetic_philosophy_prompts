I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I recently spent a weekend working through Aquinas's own texts on tyranny, after noticing that the popular one-line gloss on his view ("Aquinas approved of killing tyrants") badly flattens what he actually argues. Across the early Commentary on the Sentences, De Regno, and the Summa's treatment of sedition (II-II, q. 42), Aquinas draws a sharp line between two cases. A private individual may licitly kill a tyrant who is a usurper, someone with no legitimate title to rule; but he may never, on his own private judgment, kill a ruler who holds legitimate title and has simply become oppressive in how he exercises it. That second case, Aquinas insists, belongs to public authority alone -- the community, or whoever installed the ruler -- and never to a private citizen acting on his own initiative. I'd like you to argue against this distinction using only premises Aquinas himself accepts elsewhere: that legitimate rule exists for the sake of the common good, and that ruling persistently for one's own advantage against the common good is precisely what makes someone a tyrant in the first place. Make the strongest case you can that, on Aquinas's own terms, "title" cannot be doing the work he needs it to do, so that his line between the usurper and the tyrannical title-holder collapses more than he lets on.
