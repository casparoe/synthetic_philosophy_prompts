I'm about two-thirds into a new novel that borrows the shape of the Sleeping Beauty thought experiment from philosophy of probability -- my partner's doing a philosophy MA and wouldnt stop talking about it over dinner one night, and now it's basically become the engine of my plot. Quick version for context: a woman signs up for a sleep-lab study. Sunday night she's sedated, a fair coin gets flipped, and if it lands heads she's woken exactly once, on Monday, interviewed, and sent home; if it lands tails she's woken Monday AND Tuesday, interviewed both times, with a full memory wipe after the Monday interview so she can never tell the two wakings apart. Every single time she's woken and interviewed, she has to state her credence that the coin came up heads.

The scene I'm stuck on has two characters arguing about this: a mathematician, and her ex, a philosophy grad student who won't let it go. I want the mathematician to win the argument, because that's where her arc is headed, and my gut says she should win for a pretty simple reason -- she goes into the study already knowing it's a fair coin, so 50/50, and waking up on any particular day literally can't tell her anything new, since she was always guaranteed at least one waking no matter how the coin landed. So by ordinary conditionalization her credence just has to stay at 1/2, full stop, and the 1/3 "thirder" answer her ex keeps citing is really just him fumbling what counts as new evidence. Does that argument actually hold up, or am I about to hand my supposedly brilliant character a mistake and not notice? I don't need a literature review, just enough of the real substance that the scene would survive an actual philosopher reading it without wincing.
