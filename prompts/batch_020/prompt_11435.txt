I've followed personal-identity debates on the old rationalist blogs for years -- the Parfit-style fission and teleporter puzzles on Less Wrong, and the way they inevitably circle back to Nagel's point that our ordinary concept of a single subject of experience may simply fail to apply once you've actually bisected a brain. I even remember a paper from a few years back, I think by Pinto and colleagues in the journal Brain, arguing that the classic Sperry-era picture of two separate streams of consciousness after callosotomy was too quick, and that some sort of unified agent survives the disconnection after all. Until last month none of this had ever seemed like more than a very good puzzle.

I sit on my hospital's ethics committee, and we now have an actual patient who turns the puzzle into a decision. He is in his late sixties, admitted in respiratory failure from aspiration pneumonia, and underwent a complete corpus callosotomy at nineteen for intractable epilepsy that has otherwise been well controlled ever since. He has no advance directive, and his one involved relative refuses to substitute her own judgment, insisting she will defer entirely to "whatever he wants." The trouble is that he no longer gives us one answer. Asked aloud, he says clearly and repeatedly that he wants the tracheostomy and long-term ventilation. But the covering neurologist, aware of his history, improvised a bedside version of the old lateralized-testing paradigm -- stimuli restricted to his left visual field, answered with his left hand out of his own sight -- and got a consistent, repeated "stop" where his speech says "continue." Two different residents have since reproduced the result, though nobody is confident the test is valid outside laboratory conditions, and the surgical team says the tracheostomy has to be scheduled this week or his window for a safe procedure closes.

Our capacity policy was written for patients who are delirious or ambivalent, not for one patient producing two stable, channel-specific, and opposite answers, so it offers no guidance here. On the committee, one faction wants to treat the spoken answer as simply his answer, since speech is the channel that has constituted him as a legal and social person his entire life; a second thinks that just privileges whichever hemisphere happens to control the tongue, and wants the hand weighted at least as heavily; a third thinks we are chasing a determinate fact about "who really wants what" that Nagel gave us good reason to doubt exists, and wants the committee to reason about the case on different grounds entirely. Could you draft the short memo I will circulate before Friday's meeting? It should recommend one concrete course of action with a real deadline attached, not a survey of the disagreement, and it should take the strongest version of whatever position it rejects seriously rather than caricaturing it.
