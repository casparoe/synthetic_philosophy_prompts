For nearly forty years I ran a neurophysiology laboratory, where our working notion of causation was entirely practical: to show that some neural event caused a piece of behavior, we intervened on it directly — a lesion, a pharmacological block, later some optogenetics — while holding everything else fixed, and asked whether the behavior changed. Since retiring, I have been reading philosophy of mind for the first time, and it turns out that what we were doing in the lab corresponds fairly closely to what philosophers call an interventionist, or manipulability, theory of causation, developed in most detail by James Woodward. This framework has run headlong into a problem that I gather has occupied philosophers of mind for decades: Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument. If a mental state M — a belief, an intention — is realized by, but not identical to, some neural state P, and P is already nomologically sufficient to produce a further physical effect P*, such as a muscle contraction, then M's contribution to producing P* looks either redundant or a case of illegitimate overdetermination. A non-reductive physicalist who insists that M and P are both real, yet distinct, therefore seems forced to conclude that mental properties never do any causal work of their own.

My immediate reaction, shaped by decades of running exactly this kind of intervention, was that the puzzle ought to dissolve: since the same belief or intention can in principle be realized by different neural states in different brains, or in the same brain on different occasions, there should be some possible intervention on the mental variable itself — one that would change the outcome regardless of which particular neural state happens to realize it on that occasion — and this would make M a genuine, autonomous cause, in just the way we happily say a gene, an incentive, or a change in interest rates causes something without needing to trace the underlying physics. When I mentioned this to a philosopher who has joined our retirement reading group, I learned that a number of authors have indeed tried to rescue non-reductive physicalism from exclusion along exactly these interventionist lines, but that other authors think the rescue does not work, essentially because once the supervenience of M on P is taken seriously, there is no legitimate intervention that changes M while leaving that very P untouched, so the interventionist test ends up vindicating only P as the true difference-maker after all.

I would like you to argue, as rigorously as you can, for this more deflationary conclusion: that no interventionist or manipulability account of causation, however refined, can secure genuine, non-redundant mental causation against Kim's exclusion argument, and that my instinct as an experimentalist was too hasty. Please engage with the strongest available version of the interventionist rescue attempt rather than one that is easy to knock down, and feel free to draw on parallel cases from other special sciences, where higher-level variables can seem to compete with their physical bases for causal credit.
