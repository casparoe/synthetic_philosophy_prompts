I'm a novelist currently at work on a literary novel whose narrator restores and translates old manuscripts for a living, and one of the book's recurring preoccupations is how ordinary knowledge-talk seems to buckle the moment something real is riding on being right. Before I let myself dramatize that idea any further, I wanted to get the underlying philosophy straight, so I drafted a short passage — eventually meant to sit inside the narrator's own reflections — that tries to capture the difference between epistemic contextualism and what's sometimes called pragmatic encroachment (or subject-sensitive invariantism). A philosopher friend who read an early draft told me I'd basically collapsed the two views into one another without noticing, which sent me back to the drawing board, and I'd like your comments on whether this version fares any better.

Here is the passage: "For years Ines had told colleagues, without hedging, that the smudged final glyph on the Vareck folio read as '-tor' and not '-tar'; she had examined it under raking light a dozen times, and to her eye the stroke pattern was unambiguous. She said she knew. Then the foundation told her that a private buyer was prepared to purchase the folio on the strength of her reading alone, with no further paleographic review, and would sue if the attribution later proved wrong. Confronted with the very same smudge under the very same light, Ines found she could no longer bring herself to say she knew what it read — only that it was her best guess. Nothing about the ink or the parchment had changed. What had changed was what now hung on being right."

The contextualist, I want to say, locates the change in the word "know" itself: raise the stakes and you raise the standard that word is measured against in context, so Ines's first utterance and her second can both come out true relative to their respective conversational settings, without her underlying epistemic condition having moved at all. The encroachment theorist — I'm thinking of the line running through Jason Stanley and John Hawthorne, and through Fantl and McGrath, as against the contextualism of Keith DeRose and Stewart Cohen — denies that any such semantic rescue is needed, because on this view the fact itself is not stable: practical stakes are among the truth-makers for "S knows that P," so Ines's epistemic position toward the glyph really did get worse once the money and the lawsuit entered the picture, even though her evidence did not.

I'd be grateful if you could tell me, first, whether the passage actually keeps these two positions distinct rather than smuggling one into the other — and whether it inadvertently ends up sounding like a third, more deflationary view, on which the stakes affect only what Ines is entitled to assert or act on, leaving knowledge itself untouched. And second, purely as a piece of prose meant for readers with no background in epistemology: does spelling the distinction out like this illuminate the underlying issue, or does it read as belaboring a difference no one outside a seminar room would think worth making?
