Over the past few months I have been working through Maimonides' Guide for the Perplexed on my own, and I keep returning to Book III, chapter 32, where he argues that the entire sacrificial cult was a divine concession: since it is not in human nature to abandon long-ingrained habits of worship all at once, God allowed the Israelites to go on sacrificing, but redirected the practice toward Himself rather than toward idols, as a step toward the purer worship of prayer he ultimately intended for them. Nahmanides, in his commentary on Leviticus, responds sharply that this reduces the law to a mere concession to human weakness, and points out that Scripture already describes an offering as pleasing to God as far back as Noah — long before any idolatrous habit existed for Israel to be weaned off of.

I would like a dialogue between the two of them that genuinely grapples with this disagreement rather than simply restating each position in turn. Have Nahmanides press the case of Noah and the "pleasing odor" language as substantive objections, and have Maimonides answer in character — for instance, by considering whether his accommodation theory really needs its target to be Israelite idolatry specifically, rather than the older and more general human habit of approaching the divine through offerings. Then have Nahmanides set out his own alternative, on which the details of the ritual — the laying on of hands, the blood, the burning — enact a kind of substitution for the offerer himself, and let Maimonides press him in turn on whether that account can make sense of offerings, such as the thanksgiving sacrifice, that have nothing to do with sin or atonement. Neither should concede simply for the sake of a tidy ending; let the exchange conclude wherever it honestly runs out of road.
