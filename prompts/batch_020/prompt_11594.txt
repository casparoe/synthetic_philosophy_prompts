And here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"A genuine moral dilemma is a situation where an agent ought to do A, ought to do B, can't do both, and neither obligation cancels the other out. The standard argument against such situations relies on two principles of deontic logic: agglomeration (OA & OB → O(A&B) -- if you ought to do each of two things, you ought to do both together) and 'ought implies can' (if you ought to do something, it must be possible for you to do it). Kant's argument runs exactly along these lines: if OA and OB held while doing both was impossible, agglomeration gives us O(A&B), and ought-implies-can then requires that doing both be possible after all, which contradicts the setup. Since a rational moral law can't be self-contradictory, Kant concludes that whichever 'duty' has to give way was never really binding in that situation -- as he puts it, a collision of duties is strictly inconceivable.

Ruth Barcan Marcus, arguing dilemmas are real, blocks the argument at agglomeration rather than at ought-implies-can: the agent really can do A on its own and really can do B on its own, so ought-implies-can is satisfied for each taken separately, but she denies this licenses inferring an obligation to do both together, which is the part that's actually impossible. Bernard Williams brings out the same point using Marcus's example of Sartre's young man, torn between joining the Resistance and staying with his sick mother: whichever he does, Marcus says, he's right to feel what she calls 'agent-regret' about the unmet obligation, and that leftover feeling is evidence the obligation was real all along, not merely apparent -- which is exactly what Kant's picture can't make sense of."
