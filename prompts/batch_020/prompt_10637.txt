Hey, I'm a PhD student in English lit working on a dissertation chapter about Victorian "crisis of faith" novels, and I've been reading through George Eliot's early journalism as background before I get properly into the fiction. I got kind of stuck on a passage in her essay "Evangelical Teaching: Dr. Cumming" (Westminster Review, Oct. 1855 — I'm using the Byatt/Warren Penguin "Selected Essays, Poems and Other Writings" if that matters) and would love some help working through the actual argument, not just the "religion of humanity" gloss I already know.

Here's the bit, from near the end, where she's going after Cumming's idea that an act is only good if it's done for the glory of God rather than out of ordinary human feeling:

"Benevolence and justice are strong only in proportion as they are directly and inevitably called into activity by their proper objects; pity is strong only because we are strongly impressed by suffering; and only in proportion as it is compassion that speaks through the eyes when we soothe, and moves the arm when we succor, is a deed strictly benevolent. If the soothing or the succor be given because another being wishes or approves it, the deed ceases to be one of benevolence, and becomes one of deference, of obedience, of self-interest, or vanity. Accessory motives may aid in producing an action, but they presuppose the weakness of the direct motive; and conversely, when the direct motive is strong, the action of accessory motives will be excluded. If, then, as Dr. Cumming inculcates, the glory of God is to be 'the absorbing and the influential aim' in our thoughts and actions, this must tend to neutralize the human sympathies; the stream of feeling will be diverted from its natural current in order to feed an artificial canal. The idea of God is really moral in its influence—it really cherishes all that is best and loveliest in man—only when God is contemplated as sympathizing with the pure elements of human feeling, as possessing infinitely all those attributes which we recognize to be moral in humanity."

Me and a friend from the philosophy department got into an argument about this over drinks last week. She read the "accessory motives presuppose the weakness of the direct motive" line as basically an inverted Kant: Eliot wants exactly the acting-from-inclination that the Groundwork is suspicious of, and treats acting-from-a-principle (glory of God, standing in for acting from duty) as the thing that corrupts a deed — pretty much the reverse of Kant's picture of what gives an action moral worth. I'm not convinced that mapping survives close reading though, since "accessory" seems to be doing something more specific than just "principled" in her sentence. The other thing I can't untangle: she clearly still wants to keep "the idea of God" around as something valuable ("cherishes all that is best and loveliest in man"), which, given that she'd translated Feuerbach's Essence of Christianity only a year or so before this essay, looks like she's smuggling God back in as a sort of amplifier of human sympathy rather then actually dropping the idea. Does that characterization hold up, and if so, doesn't it just relocate the very problem she's diagnosing in Cumming — using something not directly felt to certify which feelings count as moral — rather than solve it? Any help walking through the inferential steps (as opposed to the general takeaway) would be great.
