Last month my uncle Ray died at seventy-nine, and I've been asked to give the eulogy at his memorial service this coming Saturday. Ray ran the hardware store in our town for over forty years and was not, by his own admission, a reflective or bookish man—except about one thing. At fifty, the year after his own father died, he took a single continuing-education philosophy course and came out of it fixated on P.F. Strawson's essay "Freedom and Resentment," which he treated for the rest of his life as something close to a personal creed. He kept up an informal reading group with two people from that class for the next thirty years, and what he took from it, over and over, was Strawson's distinction between the "reactive attitudes" we extend to someone we hold as a full, responsible participant in a relationship—resentment, gratitude, indignation, real forgiveness—and the "objective attitude" we fall back on when we've quietly stopped doing that and started treating the person as a case to be managed, handled, or explained instead. Ray thought that explaining away someone's bad behavior—"he only said that because he'd had a hard week," "that's just how he is"—was not kindness but a subtle form of contempt, and he said so, often loudly, whenever anyone tried it on his own behalf. Family legend has it that he once went two years without speaking to his brother after a real falling-out, and was prouder of the full, deliberate reconciliation that eventually followed than of almost anything else in his life. Near the end, when his temper and his memory both started to slip, what upset him most was people talking around him instead of to him, or telling him gently that he wasn't himself; more than once he told my mother, half joking, that if anyone stood up at his funeral and excused him instead of reckoning with him, he'd find some way to be annoyed about it from wherever he ended up.

The trouble is that most of the eighty-odd people in the room on Saturday will be neighbors, former customers, and his poker friends rather than philosophers, and one of my aunts is already lobbying me to keep the eulogy gentle and forgiving in exactly the soft, explaining register Ray couldn't stand—"he had a temper, but he had his reasons, and he loved us all in his way." I spent two hours on the phone last night arguing about this with a friend from my graduate program, who thinks I'm overreading the situation: her view is that Strawson's whole apparatus describes something that happens between living people in ongoing relationships, so insisting on "reactive" honesty about a dead man at his own funeral is close to a category error, and that the genuinely humane thing now is precisely the excusing, softening move Ray spent his life resisting, since he's no longer around to be short-changed by it. I didn't manage to fully win that argument, and I'm still not certain she's wrong, which is part of why I'd rather have help thinking it through than just go with my first instinct.

Could you write the eulogy for me? It should run about six minutes aloud, needs to work equally for the two or three people in the room who will recognize exactly what it's doing and for everyone who won't, and has to be genuinely comforting—warm, a little funny in places, appropriate for people who are grieving and in no mood for a seminar on free will. But I'd like it to be built out of Ray's own distinction rather than just gesturing at it: it should find a way to hold his temper, his stubbornness, and his refusal to be handled up to the light alongside his generosity, without either excusing him away in the manner he despised or turning the eulogy into a recitation of grievances. Ideally it would end up showing, in the way it talks about him, exactly why he thought being reacted to honestly was the greater compliment.
