I've been working through Hobbes's *Leviathan* for a political philosophy seminar, and a conversation afterward with a friend who does research in game theory left me with an odd test case for Hobbes's central argument. Hobbes holds that rational agents in the state of nature cannot stabilize cooperation on their own, because even sincere promises leave each party with a standing incentive to strike first or renege once the other has performed: as he puts it, "covenants, without the sword, are but words, and of no strength to secure a man at all." Only a common power able to punish defection — the sovereign — can make keeping one's word rational rather than foolish.

There is a body of work in game theory and decision theory, sometimes filed under "program equilibrium" or "open-source game theory," that looks like it might dodge exactly this argument. Instead of choosing actions directly, the players in these models submit programs — or, equivalently, become fully transparent to one another about their decision procedures — and the programs interact before any payoff is realized. A program can then be built to cooperate if and only if it first verifies that its counterpart's program will cooperate with it too; some versions of this trick use fixed-point constructions related to Löb's theorem in provability logic to show that two such programs can each become certain of the other's cooperation without either having to move first, trust blindly, or answer to any external enforcer. The result is presented as a genuine, non-mysterious way for purely self-interested agents to escape mutual defection through transparency alone, with no sovereign in sight.

Bracket the question of whether Hobbes could have had any conception of programs or formal logic, and just take his actual premises about covenants, trust, and the passions seriously: what would he say about the proposal itself? Would he treat this kind of verified transparency as functionally equivalent to the sword, on the grounds that it removes the very possibility of profiting from defection after the fact — or would he insist that the scheme only relocates the sword, into whatever guarantees that the programs on display are genuine and will run exactly as declared, rather than dispensing with it? And how do you think he would respond to the complaint, familiar from folk-theorem results about these program games, that mutual transparency of this kind stabilizes just as many vicious equilibria — standing threats, mutual spite — as cooperative ones, so that transparency by itself doesn't obviously vindicate reason over the war of every man against every man?
