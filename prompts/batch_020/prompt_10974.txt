I'm working on a magazine feature about how tools from economics have been migrating into moral philosophy, and one idea I keep coming back to is Toby Ord's notion of "moral trade": the proposal that people who hold flatly incompatible moral views can still negotiate deals that leave each of them better off by their own lights, on the model of comparative advantage in international trade. Two parties don't need to agree on what is really valuable, the thought goes, any more than two countries need to agree on which goods are really worth having, in order for both to gain from exchange.

The example I've been using to explain the idea to readers involves a strict vegetarian and an omnivore who objects only to factory farming: the omnivore agrees to switch to pasture-raised meat, and the vegetarian, in exchange, agrees to absorb some other morally costly indulgence that doesn't trouble the omnivore at all, such as buying leather goods. By each party's own moral accounting, the world gets slightly better, even though neither has budged an inch on the underlying disagreement about whether eating animals is wrong.

What nags at me, and what I would like your help thinking through, is that this whole arrangement seems to presuppose that people disclose their moral views honestly and then actually follow through on their side of the bargain, whereas a moral conviction is far cheaper to fake, and a private change in behavior far harder to monitor, than an ordinary consumer preference. That looks like exactly the setup — hidden type before the deal, hidden action after it — that produces adverse selection and moral hazard in markets like used cars and health insurance. Could you construct the strongest argument you can that moral trade is vulnerable to a version of both problems at once, to the point where sincere moral traders would tend to be exploited or crowded out by insincere ones? I'm especially interested in whether you think this is merely a practical wrinkle or something that undercuts the comparative-advantage analogy the whole idea rests on, and I'd like the argument to take the economic parallel seriously rather than just gesturing at it, while being honest about any respects in which moral commitments might resist this kind of market failure in ways ordinary goods don't.
