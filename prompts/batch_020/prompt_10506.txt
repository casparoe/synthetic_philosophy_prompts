Jim Joyce's 1998 paper "A Nonpragmatic Vindication of Probabilism" presents an "accuracy-dominance" argument that purports to vindicate probabilism — the claim that rational credences must satisfy the probability axioms — on purely epistemic grounds, without appeal to betting behavior.
(a) Reconstruct the argument: what does it mean for one credence function to accuracy-dominate another, and how does this yield probabilism as a requirement of rationality?
(b) Why does Joyce present this as an improvement on Dutch Book arguments, and what must a measure of inaccuracy satisfy for the proof to go through?
(c) What gap did Patrick Maher (2002) identify in Joyce's original argument, and how does the subsequent appeal to strictly proper scoring rules (e.g., in Pettigrew's work) address it?

Here is the student's answer in full:

"Joyce's aim is to vindicate probabilism epistemically rather than pragmatically. The Dutch Book argument shows only that an agent with non-probabilistic credences will accept a set of bets that guarantees a net loss no matter the outcome — a purely pragmatic defect, since an agent who simply refuses to bet is untouched by it. Joyce instead treats a credence as an estimate of a truth-value: a credence of 1 in a truth (or 0 in a falsehood) is fully accurate, and any other credence is inaccurate to a degree fixed by its distance from the truth-value. Given such a measure of inaccuracy, Joyce shows that whenever a credence function b violates the probability axioms, some probabilistically coherent b* is more accurate than b at every possible world — b is accuracy-dominated. Since it is irrational to hold credences guaranteed to be less accurate than an available alternative regardless of how the world turns out, probabilism follows as a genuine requirement of rationality — really just the Dutch Book argument run again with accuracy standing in for money as the relevant currency.

For the proof to work, the inaccuracy measure can't be arbitrary: it has to be what the scoring-rule literature calls strictly proper, meaning that, relative to her own credence p, an agent expects reporting p itself to be the unique best option, with no alternative report doing as well or better in expectation. The familiar quadratic (Brier) score has this property; a rule like the simple absolute-value measure — distance |1-p| for a truth and |p| for a falsehood — does not, since it is minimized in expectation by pushing p to 0 or 1 rather than to one's real degree of belief, except for an agent whose credence happens to be exactly 1/2. A measure lacking strict propriety would give agents an incentive to misreport their actual credences, which would undermine the sense in which accuracy-domination tracks anything genuinely epistemic.

Maher (2002) argued that Joyce's own axioms on 'legitimate' inaccuracy measures are already very demanding — in effect close to requiring strict propriety outright — and that hardly any measure of the relevant kind actually satisfies a condition that strong once you look closely at how it behaves across logically related propositions. On Maher's diagnosis, the difficulty is that strict propriety is too exacting a requirement to impose from the start. The response in subsequent work, including Pettigrew's, is accordingly to relax the requirement from strict to merely proper scoring rules, which is enough to recover a version of accuracy-dominance while asking less of the measure than Joyce originally did."
