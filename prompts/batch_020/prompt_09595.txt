I was reporting a piece on epistemic game theory and sat in on a small workshop where, after one of the talks, two attendees fell into a disagreement about the centipede game that neither ever really won. (Quick reminder of the setup, since it matters below: two players alternate moves, and at each turn a player can "take," ending the game and pocketing a slightly larger share of a fixed, growing pot, or "pass," letting the pot grow further; backward induction says both should take at the very first opportunity, even though mutual passing all the way to the end would leave both players better off.) I've numbered the exchange and would like your help sorting out who had the better of it, move by move.

(1) Naomi, a game theorist, said the backward-induction outcome isn't just one prediction among others: Aumann proved that once "rational" means best-responding to one's beliefs at every node of the tree, not merely the ones reached along the equilibrium path, common knowledge that both players are rational logically forces the take-immediately outcome. That's a theorem, not a modeling choice.

(2) Dev, a philosopher in the audience, objected that the theorem trades on something fishy: to say what's rational for player 2 in round five, you need a node where player 1 has already passed four times, which is exactly a node that, on Naomi's own premises, common knowledge of rationality guarantees will never be reached. So the "proof" tells you what's forced by an assumption that, by the time you're at that node, has already been falsified along the path leading there.

(3) Naomi replied that the same could be said of any reasoning about unreached nodes in game theory, and nobody thinks that makes subgame-perfect equilibrium incoherent; Aumann's framework simply treats "player i is rational" as an event that's well-defined at every state of a state space, true or false regardless of whether that state turns out to be the actual one. There's nothing self-defeating about reasoning that way.

(4) Dev answered that the disanalogy is in how much the argument demands: ordinary off-path beliefs are free parameters, chosen only to rationalize the observed path, whereas here common knowledge of rationality itself is supposed to survive at the off-path node - which forces a choice with opposite consequences either way. Either the players still fully trust each other's rationality immediately after witnessing a rationality-violating pass, treating it as a fluke, or they don't, in which case common knowledge has already collapsed and nothing forces backward induction from that point forward. Aumann's proof doesn't tell you which convention is correct; it just picks one.

(5) Naomi said some such convention is unavoidable in any extensive-form game, and that "isolated deviations don't destroy trust" is simply the natural default - indeed, it's the very idea that licenses talk of subgame perfection in the first place.

(6) Dev granted that it might be the natural default, but said that was precisely the point: calling the take-immediately outcome a consequence of common knowledge of rationality dresses up a substantive, contestable assumption about belief revision as if it followed from logic alone.

They went back and forth like this for another ten minutes without either budging, and I couldn't tell who was closer to the truth. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move?
