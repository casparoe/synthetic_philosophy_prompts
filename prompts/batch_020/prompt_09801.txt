I spent my working life running a combustion-chemistry lab, and now that I'm retired I've been slowly working my way through medieval Islamic philosophy for fun (currently the Marmura translation of Ghazali's "Incoherence of the Philsophers"). I'll admit I did a double take in the causality discussion — the seventeenth one, unless I'm misremembering the numbering — where Ghazali's flagship example against necessary causation is literally fire burning cotton. His line is that the fire has no actual power to burn anything; God directly creates the blackening and disintigration in the cotton each time, and the fire's presence is only habitually, not necessarily, connected to it — he calls the connection "custom" ('ada) rather than causation. Kind of funny to meet my own old subfield turned into a 900-year-old thought experiment about God's habits.

Anyway, it got me wondering what Ghazali would make of two bits of physics that came centuries after him. First, quantum mechanics, where at bottom individual events genuinely aren't fixed by prior physical states, only by probabilities — an indeterminism even his rationalist opponents (Avicenna and that crowd) never had to worry about, since they were committed necessitarians. Second, the "simulation hypothesis" / digital-physics idea that the universe might be something recomputed step by step from a fixed rule rather than unfolding under its own steam. Both seem, on the face of it, to hand an occasionalist exactly the discontinuous, God-re-creates-it-at-every-instant picture he wanted. So: which of the two do you think Ghazali would actually seize on as the stronger vindication of his view, what parts of his own account (the atomism, the notion of custom, whatever) would he have to quietly drop or reinterpret to make it fit, and does either one really rescue him from the standard objection that occasionalism just relocates the need to explain regularity onto God's habits instead of removing it?
