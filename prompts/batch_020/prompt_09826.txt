For my History of Economic Thought seminar this week we worked through Book IV of the Wealth of Nations, specifically the long chapter "Of Colonies," and honestly I wasn't ready for how radical Smith gets. He actually sits down and does the arithmetic and argues that Britain was worse off holding onto the American colonies once you add up what it cost to defend them against France and Spain, he says the whole British empire so far has basically been "not an empire, but the project of an empire," and at one point he more or less says the entire colonial system amounts to Britain ruining itself just to manufacture a captive market of customers for its own merchants. Then, almost as a throwaway, he proposes that the colonies should get seats in Parliament in proportion to what they contribute in tax, and jokes that if America keeps growing the way it's going, the "seat of the empire" might eventually just relocate there instead.

Our professor mentioned that a bunch of modern economic historians basically redo Smith's own cost-benefit exercise, but for the whole British Empire rather than just the American colonies - tallying up military and administrative costs against trade profits to argue about whether empire ever actually paid for itself - and that this kind of number-crunching now feeds into the modern reparations debate (she brought up CARICOM's reparations commission as the example). This got me stuck on a question I can't stop turning over: if Smith got dropped into the present and saw this "was empire even profitable" literature plus the reparations movements building on top of it, what would he actually conclude? Would his own logic - that empire was largely a net loss propped up by the vanity of merchants and politicians rather than genuine national benefit - push him toward some sympathy for reparations claims, or does he have other commitments (about sovereignty, about there being no legitimate mechanism for a state to redistribute wealth outside of ordinary taxation, about who even counts as a wronged party generations later) that would make him resist that conclusion even while granting the empire was a bad deal financially? I'd really like you to engage with the actual mechanics of his arguments in that chapter rather than defaulting to the generic "Smith = free markets, full stop" reading, since half my seminar seems to think that's basically all there is to him at this point and its kind of driving me crazy.
