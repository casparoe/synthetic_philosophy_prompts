I'm a retired experimental physicist, and now that I've got time on my hands I've been working my way through decision theory, which I somehow managed to avoid my whole career besides the usual error bars and Bayesian updating. A friend from my old department, who's now just as obsessed with philosophy in retirement as I am, got me into Lara Buchak's book on "risk-weighted expected utility," and I've been stuck on the same question for about two weeks now. From my one stats class in grad school I thought I already understood how you're supposed to handle risk aversion: you just make the utility function over money concave (log, sqrt, whatever fits the data), so a guaranteed $50 beats a 50/50 shot at $0 or $100 even though the expected payout is identical - Bernoulli sorted that out centuries ago with the St. Petersburg paradox.

So when I got to the part where Buchak instead puts a "risk function" on the probabilities and leaves the utility function alone, my gut reaction was that this is the exact same trick just done on a different axis, like solving a problem in polar coordinates instead of cartesian and acting like you've discovered new physics. Squash the risky option down relative to the sure thing on the outcome side, or squash it down on the probability side - either way you end up ranking every gamble the same, no? My reading group (mostly other retired scientists, we're insufferable about this stuff) argued about it for a full hour and nobody could name one actual choice where the two theories would recommend something different. So before I embarrass myself in front of the philosophers in the group next week, I figured I'd ask here first: is "risk-weighted expected utility" really a distinct theory of rational choice, or is it just expected utility with a concave utility function wearing a costume, with decision theorists making a bigger deal out of the relabeling then it deserves?
