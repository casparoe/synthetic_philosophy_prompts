I'm a PhD student in statistics, and a few weeks ago our seminar on estimation theory spent a session on the classic "German tank problem": the World War II puzzle of estimating the total size of an enemy tank production run from the serial numbers stamped on a handful of captured units, by treating those observed serial numbers as a random sample, without replacement, from the integers 1 through some unknown maximum N. It's an elegant illustration of how much can be inferred about a fixed but unknown quantity from very little data, provided the sampling process is essentially uniform over the whole range.

Since then, a friend from college who works as a city planner and started a small newsletter on the side about local transit policy has been trying to apply what he calls "the same trick" to his own project. A little while ago, a new subscriber was welcomed by the sign-up system as "subscriber number 214," and my friend reasoned that if he treats that person as though randomly drawn from the entire population of people who will ever subscribe, he can construct a fairly tight probabilistic bound on how large that eventual population is likely to be - and the bound comes out quite discouraging. He is now half-seriously citing this as a reason to abandon his plans to promote the newsletter more widely, on the grounds that the numbers themselves seem to rule out much future growth.

I told him this felt fishy, but when I tried to explain exactly why, I got stuck. The formal shape of his reasoning does resemble the tank problem's: both assume something like a uniform prior over rank and invert it to bound the total. The disanalogy I keep circling is that all the tanks already existed at the moment the sample was drawn, whereas most of the newsletter's eventual subscribers, if the reasoning is meaningful at all, don't exist yet - so it's unclear in what sense his one subscriber's position counts as a "random draw" from a group that is still being generated forward in time. I can't tell whether that disanalogy actually defeats the inference or whether I'm just being misled by an intuition I haven't managed to cash out formally. Could you help me work out whether my friend's argument holds up and, if it doesn't, pinpoint exactly where the reasoning breaks down?
