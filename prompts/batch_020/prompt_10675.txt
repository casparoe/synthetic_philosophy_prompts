Henry Sidgwick spent much of his career wrestling with what he called the "dualism of practical reason": the unresolved clash between rational self-interest and impartial morality, which he feared left ethics without any fully rational foundation. He hoped that solid evidence of personal survival after death might tip that balance in favor of morality, and that hope is presumably why he co-founded and served as first president of the Society for Psychical Research, spending decades on careful, statistically-minded investigations of mediums, apparitions, and telepathy, only to conclude near the end of his life that nothing conclusive had turned up either way. I keep wondering how he'd react to something like Daryl Bem's 2011 "Feeling the Future" experiments, which leaned on the same basic logic Sidgwick relied on — a statistically significant deviation from chance as evidence of a real, anomalous effect — to argue for precognition in a mainstream psychology journal, and then became one of the sparks for psychology's ongoing replication crisis once the results failed to hold up under scrutiny. Would Sidgwick see the field's later turn toward preregistration and replication as vindication of the rigor he pioneered, or would watching yet another "significant" psi result collapse have only deepened his old melancholy about never closing the gap between duty and self-interest? I'd like your best-supported reconstruction of his reaction, grounded in his actual epistemic standards rather than generic remarks about Victorian spiritualism.
