Something that happened in my friend group this summer turned into a running argument, and I would really like an outside opinion, because I honestly cannot tell who is right. At Thanksgiving, in front of the whole extended family, my friend Marcus promised his younger cousin that he would personally teach him to drive the following summer, before the cousin was even old enough to get his learner's permit. It clearly meant a lot to the cousin, who doesn't get much one-on-one time with Marcus. Then, in the spring, Marcus landed a highly competitive summer internship, the kind that looks great on college applications, which ended up occupying every weekday of the summer -- exactly the time he and his cousin would have used. Marcus never taught him at all, not even one shortened lesson, and just sent a text apologizing once the internship started. His cousin was noticeably hurt.

Two people in my group, Nadia and Ben, have been arguing about this for weeks, and I keep getting asked to referee even though I really don't have a strong view of my own. Nadia's opening position is that Marcus simply broke his promise: he said he would do a specific thing and didn't, and 'something better came along' cannot count as a valid excuse, since there is always some reason available for breaking a promise, and if a sufficiently good reason were all it took to be excused, promises would never really bind anyone in the first place. Ben responded that not all reasons are equal: every promise is implicitly made against a backdrop of ordinary circumstances, and a rare opportunity like this is exactly the sort of thing his cousin, if he'd been asked in advance, would reasonably have waved off, so Marcus isn't violating the actual spirit of what he committed to. Nadia came back by pointing out that this cuts against Marcus, not for him: his cousin obviously would not have waved it off, since he had been counting on it all summer, and, more importantly, Marcus never actually asked -- he privately decided the internship counted as an exception and only informed his cousin afterward. Ben's final reply was that you can't expect someone to get the other person's sign-off every time circumstances change, since that would make promises unworkable; there has to be some objective standard for what counts as a reasonable exception, rather than just whatever the person on the receiving end happens to prefer, since they will almost always prefer that the original promise be kept, no matter the reason.

Could you go through these four moves individually rather than just declaring an overall winner -- for each one, say what it gets right, where it overreaches, and how well it holds up against the move that came before it? I would also like to know whether Nadia and Ben are both missing some further consideration that would actually help settle a case like this one.
