Since retiring from a career in evolutionary biology, I have been reading philosophy for the first time without much formal grounding, and I have drafted some notes toward a short essay on Nietzsche's *On the Genealogy of Morality* that I would value your comments on.

My starting observation is that Nietzsche's genealogical method bears a structural resemblance to a distinction my own field treats as elementary: the separation of a trait's historical origin from an assessment of its current function. Biologists are trained to be suspicious of "just-so" reasoning that reads a trait's present worth or role straight off a story about how it arose — hence Mayr's distinction between proximate and ultimate causation, and the related notion of exaptation, where a structure that originated for one purpose (feathers for thermoregulation, say) is later co-opted for another (flight) that has nothing to do with, and cannot be evaluated by reference to, its origin.

What puzzles me is that Nietzsche's own diagnostic strategy seems to run in exactly the direction my training tells me to distrust. The critical force of the First Essay depends on showing that the "good and evil" of slave morality, unlike the aristocratic "good and bad" it displaced, was born of the ressentiment of the powerless against the powerful — and this historical and psychological derivation is clearly meant to do real work in unseating the authority those values still enjoy. That looks, on its face, like a sophisticated version of the genetic fallacy: inferring something about the present worth of a value from facts about its causal origin.

The best defense I can construct on Nietzsche's behalf is that the genealogy is not really trading on temporal precedence at all, but on a claim about what the values presently express and continue to enforce — a persisting reactive, life-denying orientation, rather than merely a disreputable birth now safely in the past. That would make it something like what I understand, secondhand, to be Bernard Williams's distinction between genealogies that vindicate a practice and those meant to unmask or subvert one; correct me if I have that attribution wrong. But I am not sure this fully rescues the argument, since it only pushes the question back a step: how is this "present function" established, if not by reading it straight off the very same contested historical narrative?

I would welcome your thoughts on three things in particular: whether this is a fair reconstruction of what Nietzsche is actually doing, rather than a strawman version of it; whether the biological analogy sharpens the philosophical issue or merely smuggles in an unearned notion of "function" that does not transfer cleanly to normative claims; and whether there is a cleaner, already-established way of stating what would make an "unmasking" genealogy non-fallacious, so that I am not laboring to reinvent a wheel philosophers have long since built and improved upon.
