Ok so I fell down a rabbit hole this weekend that started with an AI safety podcast, wandered through Newcomb's problem, and ended with me reading old LessWrong posts about "updateless decision theory" (I think Wei Dai came up with this? not totally sure). Havent taken a philosophy class since a gen-ed requirement in college so I'm probably missing context here. The example that hooked me was the "counterfactual mugging" thing: Omega flips a fair coin, and if it comes up heads it walks up and asks you for $100; if it had come up tails instead, it would've given you $10,000, but only if it had predicted beforehand that you'd be willing to pay the $100 in the heads case. Standard causal decision theory says that once you actually see heads, paying is pointless -- the coin already landed, you can't reach back and cause yourself to get the $10k in the other branch -- so a CDT agent keeps its money and ends up worse off on average than an agent that would've paid. Updateless decision theory is apparently the fix, because it wont let you "update" and recompute what's optimal after you already know the coin flip, you just commit up front to whatever policy is best across both branches.

Here's my hangup though: isn't "don't recompute after you get new info" just a fancy way of saying "ignore the evidence"? In my day job that would be a straightforward bug. If I shipped a fraud-detection model that refused to change its behavior after seeing new labeled data because it had already "committed" to a policy back at training time, that's not a feature, thats a stale model, and we'd get paged about it. So why does a huge chunk of the AI safety / rationalist world treat "updatelessness" as the mark of a smarter agent instead of a strictly worse one? I brought it up with a coworker who's way deeper into this stuff than me and he just said "that's kind of the point" like it was obvious, which, uh, did not help.
