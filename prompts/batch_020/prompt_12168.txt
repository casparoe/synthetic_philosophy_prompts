I'm taking a graduate seminar on formal epistemology this term, and this week's reading was I. J. Good's 1967 note "On the Principle of Total Evidence," which proves that for an idealized Bayesian agent, the expected value of cost-free information, calculated in advance, is never negative: since you always retain the option of acting exactly as you would have without the new evidence, learning something can only help you decide, never hurt you, on average. After the seminar, two people in my reading group, Nate and Farah, ended up in a disagreement over drinks that ran long enough that I wrote down the gist of it, and since the group never actually settled who was right, I'd like an outside opinion.

Nate opened by arguing that the theorem has to be too strong, because people routinely and reasonably decline free information: patients who ask not to be told whether they carry a gene for an untreatable disease, for instance, or someone who'd rather not know a rival's exact salary. If Good's proof implies that a rational agent should always want more data, he said, it implies these people are being irrational, which seems exactly backwards. Farah answered that this isn't really a counterexample: in cases like these, nothing about the person's optimal action changes depending on the answer, so the instrumental value of the information is precisely zero either way, and the real work is being done by the fact that merely knowing is unpleasant — a cost sitting entirely outside the decision problem Good's proof is describing. Nate replied that this move makes the theorem unfalsifiable, since Farah can rescue it from any apparent counterexample simply by stipulating a "taste for ignorance," so that it stops predicting anything about what real people should do. Farah conceded that deciding what really belongs in someone's utility function is a judgment call, but held that this is a limit on how the theorem gets applied to messy cases, not a flaw in the theorem itself, which remains a perfectly substantive claim about idealized problems with a fixed, action-based payoff. Nate then offered a case with no dread in it anywhere: a poker player who would rather not glimpse an opponent's tell, because if it shows on her face that she knows something, the opponent adjusts and she ends up worse off for having learned it — a strategic cost with nothing hedonic about it. Farah granted that this example actually succeeds, but explained why: Good's proof concerns a single agent choosing against a fixed, non-reactive state of nature, and once a second agent is drawing inferences from your beliefs and responding to them, you are in a genuinely different, game-theoretic situation that the original theorem was never meant to cover.

Could you adjudicate this move by move? For each of the six moves — Nate's opening objection, Farah's zero-instrumental-value reply, Nate's unfalsifiability charge, Farah's response to it, Nate's poker case, and Farah's closing concession — say who comes out ahead and why, flagging explicitly any move that mixes a genuine insight with a mistake rather than being simply right or simply wrong. Then add a closing verdict on the larger question the exchange circles around: whether the idea that cost-free information is always safe to acquire is a general truth about rationality, or whether it only holds within an idealization that most real cases — including some of the ones just discussed — actually fall outside of.
