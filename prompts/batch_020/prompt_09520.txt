I teach a graduate seminar on trolley problems and the doctrine of double effect, and every year, before we turn to Foot, Thomson, and the more recent literature on trolleys, I have the students work through the doctrine's historical source: Aquinas's discussion of killing in self-defense at Summa Theologiae II-II, Q. 64, Art. 7. Most secondary treatments quote only the opening lines of the "I answer that" section, about an act having "two effects," and move on. This term, though, a student in the seminar pointed out that the article, read in full, seems to run two rather different arguments together without ever flagging the join, and I have not managed to produce an account of the passage that I find fully satisfying in time for our next meeting.

Here is the material, in the standard translation by the Fathers of the English Dominican Province. Objection 1 opens by quoting Augustine: "I do not agree with the opinion that one may kill a man lest one be killed by him; unless one be a soldier, exercise a public office, so that one does it not for oneself but for others, having the power to do so, provided it be in keeping with one's person." Aquinas's own answer runs: "Nothing hinders one act from having two effects, only one of which is intended, while the other is beside the intention. Now moral acts take their species according to what is intended, and not according to what is beside the intention, since this is accidental... Accordingly the act of self-defense may have two effects, one is the saving of one's life, the other is the slaying of the aggressor. Therefore this act, since one's intention is to save one's own life, is not unlawful, seeing that it is natural to everything to keep itself in 'being,' as far as possible. And yet, though proceeding from a good intention, an act may be rendered unlawful, if it be out of proportion to the end. Wherefore if a man, in self-defense, uses more than necessary violence, it will be unlawful: whereas if he repel force with moderation his defense will be lawful... Nor is it necessary for salvation that a man omit the act of moderate self-defense in order to avoid killing the other man, since one is bound to take more care of one's own life than of another's. But as it is unlawful to take a man's life, except for the public authority acting for the common good... it is not lawful for a man to intend killing a man in self-defense, except for such as have public authority, who while intending to kill a man in self-defense, refer this to the public good, as in the case of a soldier fighting against the foe, and in the minister of the judge struggling with robbers, although even these sin if they be moved by private animosity." And in his reply to the first objection he adds, of the Augustine quotation: "The words quoted from Augustine refer to the case when one man intends to kill another to save himself from death."

What I would like help with is the relation between the two justifying strategies this single article seems to contain. On the reading every textbook repeats, the whole permission rests on the intention/foresight distinction: killing the aggressor is licit only because it is a merely foreseen side effect of an act intended solely to preserve one's own life, kept within the bounds of proportionate force. But the closing lines of the respondeo grant something that looks quite different to soldiers and to the officers of a judge: they may, without sin, actually intend the killing itself, so long as that intention is "referred" to the public good rather than arising from private animosity — and this permission does not seem to turn on the death being unintended at all. Is this really a second, independent license to kill sitting alongside the two-effects reasoning, so that the article contains two distinct justifications rather than one general principle? Or should the "referred to the public good" language be read as continuous with the double-effect apparatus, perhaps by treating the public good as a further, licit object of intention under which the killing is itself somehow absorbed? I would also value your view on whether the proportionality requirement introduced partway through the passage is meant to constrain the public case as well as the private one. Most of all, I would like a clear sense of how many distinct moral arguments are actually doing work in this one article, since that bears directly on how much weight we can put on this text as the origin of a supposedly general-purpose doctrine of double effect that gets applied, in the trolley literature, to private bystanders who hold no public office of any kind.
