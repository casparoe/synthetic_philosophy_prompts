I'm drafting a dissertation chapter on choice principles in constructive mathematics, and Diaconescu's theorem (sometimes credited jointly to Goodman and Myhill) is giving me trouble at the level of intuition rather than formal detail. I can verify the standard proof symbol by symbol -- build two subsets of a two-element set out of an arbitrary proposition P, apply choice to pick an element from each, and case-split on whether the two choices coincide -- and confirm that the law of excluded middle drops out at the end. What I can't yet do is see why this collapse had to happen, rather than merely check after the fact that it does. Could you talk me through the intuition behind the theorem, in a way that would let a reader anticipate the result rather than just follow the algebra? I'd also like you to address why the same style of argument doesn't equally force excluded middle out of weaker choice principles -- countable choice, say, or dependent choice -- that Bishop-style constructivists retain without qualm. What, at bottom, makes unrestricted choice so much more classically potent than these restricted versions?
