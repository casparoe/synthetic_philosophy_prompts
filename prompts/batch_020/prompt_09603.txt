Four months ago, my husband accepted a job transfer that will move us next spring from the small industrial city where we currently live to a greener town with markedly better air quality and school ratings. When the offer came through, we talked at length about whether to hold off trying to conceive until after the move, since I had just turned thirty-five and we had already put it off for two years while I finished my degree. We decided not to wait. My sister, who is usually the most level-headed person in the family, argued that this was selfish of us: she pointed out that our current zip code has some of the worst ozone readings in the state and that the elementary school two blocks over has been on the state's underperforming list for years, and said that if we genuinely cared about our future child's welfare, we owed it to them to hold off for the extra few months. We went ahead anyway, and last week I found out I'm pregnant.

I should just be happy, and mostly I am, but my sister's comment has stayed with me in a way I didn't expect now that this is real. The way she put it implied that there is a specific child who will now spend its first year breathing worse air and zoned for a worse school than it "would have" if we had waited, as though we made a choice that is bad for that particular child. The more I turn this over, though, the less it seems to hold together: if we had waited even one more cycle, it would not have been this pregnancy at all, but a different egg and a different sperm, and so a genetically distinct child altogether. There does not seem to be any single child who was made worse off by our timing, since the better-off child my sister has in mind was never actually on offer to us — waiting would not have improved this child's situation, it would simply have resulted in a different child existing in its place. And yet it feels wrong to conclude from this that our timing was therefore ethically weightless, as though it makes no difference at all whether a child is conceived into decent circumstances or difficult ones. I only took one philosophy course in college, years ago, and I don't have great tools for working through this on my own. Could you help me see whether my sister's original argument actually holds up? And if it doesn't, is there nonetheless a more defensible way to think about what, if anything, was ethically at stake in our decision not to wait?
