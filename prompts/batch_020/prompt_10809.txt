I'm working on a longread about the "Boltzmann brain" problem in cosmology — the argument that if the universe keeps accelerating forever because of dark energy, the far future is basically an empty, thermal de Sitter space that lasts for literally infinite time, and given infinite time even wildly improbable stuff happens: a random quantum fluctuation spontaneously assembling an isolated conscious brain, complete with fake memories of a whole life, rather than an actual evolved observer. The unsettling part physicists like Sean Carroll point out is that over the full history of such a universe there'd be vastly more of these fluke brains than "real" observers like us, which some people treat as an actual argument against certain cosmological models — if your theory predicts we're almost certainly a random fluctuating brain rather than a real observer, thats a strike against the theory, not just a curiosity.

My editor wanted a "fun history" hook for the piece, so I went digging and found out Boltzmann basically pre-empted a version of this back in the 1890s, while fending off Zermelo's recurrence objection (the one built on Poincaré's theorem, that any bounded mechanical system has to eventually return arbitrarily close to its starting state). Boltzmann's comeback was roughly: fine, entropy can spontaneously decrease given enough time, and maybe the whole observable universe is just a rare, temporary statistical fluctuation inside a much vaster system that's mostly at equilibrium — and any observers around to notice it would necessarily find themselves inside one of those rare low-entropy patches, since nowhere else could support them. Which is a wild thing to be arguing in 1897, and is basically a proto-anthropic move before anyone had that vocabulary.

So here's what I actually want from you: if you could show Boltzmann the modern, de Sitter-space version of his own idea, what do you think he'd say? He never had general relativity, dark energy, or eternal inflation to work with, and the whole "typical observer" / self-sampling apparatus people lean on now — the kind of thing Nick Bostrom formalized about a century later, and that lets physicists treat "we shouldn't expect to be a freak fluctuation" as an actual constraint on which theories can be true — didn't exist in his day either. Would he take one look and say "yes, exactly, I told you so" and welcome it as his fluctuation hypothesis grown up? Or is there something in how he was actually reasoning about probability and phase space back then that would make him resist the jump from "fluctuations happen" to "therefore isolated brains with fake memories should vastly outnumber real observers, and that counts against the theory"? I'd rather you engage with what he actually argued against Zermelo than the potted textbook version of the story.
