I sit on the provenance and accessions committee for my university's special collections library, and I've got a genuinely weird case for next month's meeting that I'm pretty sure is secretly a metaphysics problem wearing a cataloguing-dispute costume. In the early 90s a donor gave us her personal copy of a fairly obscure 1740s printed volume (the title doesn't matter), and completely seperately, around the same time, the library bought a second copy of the exact same print run at auction for general circulation. Both got accessioned within about a year of each other, both went through the same in-house conservation rebind in the early 2000s using identical materials and technique, and we just discovered, while doing an insurance re-appraisal, that the two accession files got mixed up at some point and nobody caught it for two decades. Conservators have since gone over both copies as hard as anyone can: paper stock, ink, thread, foxing patterns, everything. As far as any test we have or could ever devise is concerned, the two books are completely indistinguishable. But that obviously doesn't mean there's only one book, there are definitely two physical objects sitting in two boxes down the hall, and one of them really is the specific copy the donor personally owned and handed over.

Here's the actual problem: the donor's family is coming for a rededication event in the spring expecting to see "grandma's copy" specifically on display with her nameplate, and the two accession numbers are sitting on our insurance schedule at noticeably different appraised values for old donation-credit reasons, so we can't just quietly treat the books as interchangeable and move on. My committee has split into two camps and it has honestly turned into a weird amateur metaphysics seminar over email. One camp says that if there is no possible way, even in principle, to tell the two copies apart, then there just is no remaining fact about which one is "really" hers, so we should pick one by some fair procedure and stop agonizing over it. The other camp says that's a total non sequitur, basically the same move as assuming that if two things are indiscernible they must be identical, which is exactly what Max Black's old two-spheres thought experiment (the iron spheres alone in an otherwise perfectly symmetrical universe) is supposed to show is a mistake, and that of course one book really is hers as a matter of plain historical fact, we just can't recover which one. I fell down this particular rabbit hole years ago off some old LessWrong thread that linked the SEP page on it, and its apparently stuck with me more than I realized. (I have a vague memory of somebody, Hacking maybe, arguing you could redescribe Black's whole setup as secretly one sphere via a weirdly curved space, but I was never sure that move actually works or how far it generalizes, so no idea if it's even relevant here.)

Anyway, I need to circulate a short written memo before the meeting that actually recommends a concrete course of action -- covering the nameplate, the insurance listing, and the family visit -- with a real timeframe attached, not just "the committee remains divided." I also don't want it to just steelman whichever camp I personally lean towards and call it a day; whichever side loses needs to see their actual best argument taken seriously in there. Can you draft that memo?
