My aunt is retiring after thirty-eight years as principal violist with a regional symphony orchestra, and she has asked me -- somewhat to my alarm -- to give the toast at her retirement dinner. She is also an unusually committed Schopenhauerian, not in the vague "music soothes the soul" sense but in the sense of someone who has actually worked through Book 3 of The World as Will and Representation and holds, as a considered philosophical commitment, that music is metaphysically unlike every other art: it does not present the Platonic Ideas at their various grades of the will's objectification the way architecture, sculpture, or tragedy do, but is instead a direct copy of the will itself, running alongside the phenomenal world rather than depicting any part of it. She takes the pessimism just as seriously: whatever relief this affords a listener is real but strictly temporary, since on her view -- and his -- satisfaction is never more than momentary, giving way to new want or to sheer boredom. She has told me, more than once and not entirely joking, that she will be quietly offended by any toast that flattens this into "music brings people together" or "music heals."

I need a five-to-seven-minute toast, to be delivered to a room split roughly evenly between orchestra colleagues who will recognize exactly what is being invoked and relatives who have never read a page of philosophy, that takes her actual view seriously rather than sanding it down -- including the pessimism, which can't just be quietly dropped for the occasion -- while still working as a toast people are glad to have sat through: warm, occasionally funny, and unmistakably about her thirty-eight years with a viola rather than a philosophy lecture that happens to have her name attached.
