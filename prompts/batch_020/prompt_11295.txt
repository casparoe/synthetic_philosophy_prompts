Over the last year I've gotten quietly into self-binding productivity tools: I use Beeminder for a couple of habits, and a former teammate built a Slack bot for our team that automatically posts a public confession to the whole engineering channel if you miss a deadline you set for yourself. A friend from a philosophy reading group I sit in on now and then (I'm a backend engineer, not a philosopher, so I'm very much the tourist in that room) pointed out that all of this is basically a live version of Gregory Kavka's "toxin puzzle": an eccentric billionaire, who is somehow able to tell a sincere intention from a merely professed one, will pay you a million dollars tomorrow morning if, tonight at midnight, you form a genuine intention to drink a toxin tomorrow afternoon that will make you painfully ill for a day but do no lasting harm. You get paid purely for having the intention at midnight, whether or not you actually go through with the drinking, and you're entirely free to change your mind afterward. The catch is that once midnight has passed and the money is as good as yours, you'll have no reason left to actually drink the toxin the next day, which seems to mean you could never sincerely form the intention in the first place, even though forming it is, by hypothesis, free and enormously to your benefit. My friend also mentioned, which I found sort of wild, that Kavka had written about the logic of nuclear deterrence a few years before this paper, and that some people read the toxin puzzle as a stripped-down version of the same worry -- how it could be rational to intend a retaliation that would be pointless to actually carry out once deterrence has already failed.

From some reading of my own since then, I gather this thought experiment does a lot of heavy lifting in the literature on rational choice and intention -- something about a split between the rationality of a plan and the rationality of the action the plan targets, and a live disagreement over whether a sufficiently "resolute" agent can pull the intention off anyway or whether Kavka was right that no fully rational person can manage it. What I'd like from you is a description of the most important implications of the toxin puzzle for tools like the ones I described above, where the whole mechanism depends on binding a future self who will have zero local incentive to cooperate. Concretely, I keep going back and forth on whether devices like Beeminder and that Slack bot work precisely because I'm not fully rational in the moments that matter, in which case leaning on them so heavily is basically an admission of a flaw I should be trying to correct instead of indulging, or whether the puzzle actually shows that the capacity to bind yourself this way is a rational asset worth deliberately cultivating, in which case I should stop feeling sheepish about needing them. Please don't spend much time re-explaining the puzzle itself -- I've clearly already gone down that hole -- and focus on what actually follows from it for a case like mine.
