I sit on the board of a small manufacturing firm that recently retained an outside risk consultant to assess our exposure to rare but severe disruptions. His method troubles me even though I can't locate a flaw in it: for every scenario he raises, he readily concedes the probability is vanishingly small, but insists the associated loss would be so severe (he favors words like "irreversible" and "company-ending") that the expected loss never actually shrinks. When we push back and try to cap the budget allocated to his recommendations, he simply proposes a new scenario that is proportionally less probable but proportionally more catastrophic, and the arithmetic comes out the same, or worse, every time. Nobody on the board can point to a specific number or inference in his reasoning that is mistaken, yet letting this continue to its logical conclusion would mean handing him an ever-larger share of our budget indefinitely. Is there a principled basis on which we could cap his budget anyway, or would that just be an unprincipled refusal to follow a valid expected-value argument wherever it leads?
