Ok so I'm plotting a subplot where two rival heirs to a shipping fortune get dragged into a legal fight over a will that escalates way past what the estate's even worth, and I keep coming back to Shubik's dollar auction as the shape of it -- except something about how everyone explains that game has been bugging me. The standard line is that its just the sunk cost fallacy, people irrationally throwing good money after bad, but once you're the second-highest bidder and already owe your bid either way, isnt bidding again actually the rational move in the moment, since sitting still means a guaranteed loss while bidding again at least buys you a shot at the dollar? So is the escalation past the prize's value really driven by irrationality somewhere in there, or does the rule that both top two bidders pay (not just the winner) make escalating the locally rational choice at every single step, even for someone thinking totally clearly -- in which case "sunk cost fallacy" is kind of the wrong diagnosis for it? Does that change at all once the "bids" aren't cash but legal fees, reputation, or years of your life, which is basically what real escalation-of-commitment cases like wars or doomed startups actually run on? I want to know exactly where, if anywhere, real irrationality has to sneak in for the blow-up to happen, because that's the difference between writing my heirs as fools versus as people trapped by the shape of the game itself.
