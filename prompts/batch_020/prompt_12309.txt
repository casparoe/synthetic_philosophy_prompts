And here's the student's answer, copied exactly:

"(a) The thesis at issue is Adams' Thesis, sometimes just called 'the Equation': P(A > B) = P(B | A) for every A, B such that P(A) > 0, where '>' is the indicative conditional connective. It was originally meant to capture Ramsey's idea (from his famous footnote) that to evaluate 'if A, then B' you should hypothetically add A to your beliefs and see how confident you'd be in B. On Adams's view, A>B is a genuine proposition, so its probability is just whatever ordinary probability theory says it is, and the whole appeal of the Equation is that it lets that ordinary probability line up exactly with our intuitive conditional credence, which is a really elegant result if it can be made to work.

(b) The heart of the argument is to update the Equation on the two ways A could turn out. Say P(A&B) > 0 and P(A&¬B) > 0. Update on A&B: now A and B are both certain, so B given A is certain, so by the Equation the updated probability of A>B is 1. Update instead on A&¬B: A is certain and B is certain to be false, so B given A is certainly false, so the updated probability of A>B is 0. Since updating on A&B or A&¬B is just ordinary conditionalization, and A>B is an ordinary proposition like any other, these are just P(A>B | A&B) = 1 and P(A>B | A&¬B) = 0. Plugging these into the law of total probability for P(A>B) and comparing that to what the Equation says P(A>B) already has to equal, namely P(B|A), forces P(B|A) to equal P(B) — i.e. A and B come out probabilistically independent.

(c) That's the trivial part: since A and B were arbitrary, the Equation would force every pair of propositions (with all the relevant combinations having positive probability) to be probabilistically independent of each other. But plenty of propositions are obviously correlated — it raining and the streets being wet, say — so a thesis that entails universal independence can't be right. No sensible, non-degenerate probability function can satisfy the Equation across the board.

(d) One escape route, taken by Stalnaker, is to deny that conditionals express genuine propositions with truth values at all. If 'if A, then B' has no truth conditions, there's no proposition A>B for the Equation to be a claim about, so Lewis's argument can't even get started. The cost is that you lose a normal compositional semantics for conditionals embedded under other operators, like negation or disjunction or further conditionals, since it's much harder to systematically state truth conditions for complex sentences built out of parts that don't have truth conditions themselves."
