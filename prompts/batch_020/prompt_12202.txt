After a research career in experimental physics, I have spent the last couple of years teaching a non-credit philosophy-of-mind seminar for fellow retirees, and I am still finding my footing with some of the finer distinctions in the literature on consciousness. One of my students handed in the following answer to a short written exercise on higher-order theories of consciousness, and before I return it with a grade I would like an independent, rigorous assessment of exactly where it succeeds and where it falls short.

The exercise, worth 20 points, read as follows:

David Rosenthal's higher-order thought (HOT) theory holds that a mental state is a conscious state just in case its subject has a suitable, roughly simultaneous, non-inferential, assertoric higher-order thought (HOT) to the effect that she is in that state.

(a) [6 points] Explain how this theory is meant to block the regress objection, according to which making one state conscious by means of a further mental state would require that further state to itself be conscious, and so on without end.

(b) [8 points] Using the case of a "targetless" HOT — a higher-order thought that occurs without any first-order state answering to it — explain what the theory predicts about the subject's experience in such a case, and why this consequence has struck critics as a problem for the view.

(c) [6 points] Briefly characterize the difference between Rosenthal's "actualist" requirement and a "dispositionalist" alternative version of higher-order theory, and say whose view the dispositionalist alternative is.

The student's answer was:

Rosenthal's HOT theory is an attempt to explain creature consciousness: why a given organism counts, at a given time, as a conscious being aware of its surroundings rather than an unconscious one. (a) The theory blocks the regress by denying that the higher-order thought which confers consciousness on a target state must itself be a conscious state. The HOT that makes a perception of red conscious is not itself, typically, an object of further awareness — we are not ordinarily aware of having it, only of the reddish perception it is about. Since no further HOT is needed to make the second-order state itself conscious, the chain stops after one step, except in the special case of introspection, where a further, attentive higher-order state does make the second-order thought itself conscious. (b) A targetless HOT is a higher-order thought that occurs even though there is no first-order state answering to it — for instance, a thought to the effect that I am in pain when no pain state exists. Because Rosenthal ties the qualitative character of an experience to the content of the higher-order thought rather than to any actual property of a first-order state, the theory predicts that such a subject would seem to herself to be in pain, and would sincerely report being in pain, even though no pain state is present. Critics have pressed this as a problem because it lets the theory manufacture a conscious experience out of a mere cognitive error, which seems to reverse the natural order of explanation: we ordinarily think we judge that we are in pain because we are in pain, not the other way around. (c) Rosenthal's original, actualist requirement is that the relevant HOT actually occur, more or less simultaneously with its target, for the target state to count as conscious. Rosenthal later revised this into a dispositionalist view, on which a first-order state is conscious not because an actual HOT occurs but because the state is suitably poised to produce one — a response to the worry that requiring an actual, distinct thought for literally every conscious state at every moment is psychologically unrealistic.
