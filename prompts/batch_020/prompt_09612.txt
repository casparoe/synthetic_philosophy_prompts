Hey, I'm stuck on a philosophical snag in a scene for my novel and did some reading to try to sort my own head out about it, figured I'd get a second opinion before I lock in a dinner-table argument between two characters. Here's the setup and the notes I ended up with — can you tell me if I'm mangling any of the philosophy, and whether the conclusion I land on for my main guy actually follows, or if its just me forcing a tidy answer onto a messy case?

Setup: Ray is a debt collector, not exactly a nice man. Early in the book he watches a man drown because wading in would've cost him with his boss, and it quietly wrecks him for the rest of the book. Later, he pulls a kid out of a canal. Everyone treats it as heroic. But in his head he's not thinking about the kid at all — he's thinking "I let one person die, I am not doing that again." It's about patching a hole in himself, not about the kid. His sister Meera, a philosophy grad student, needles him about this at dinner: does this even count as a good act, morally speaking, or is it just self-therapy wearing a hero costume? That's the scene, so here's my homework:

Kant has that bit in the Groundwork about a shopkeeper who gives everyone honest change, even customers he could easily cheat, but only because word travels and honesty is good for repeat business. Kant's verdict: right action, zero moral worth, because the shopkeeper didn't do it from duty, he did it from self-interest that happened to line up with duty. People have been ribbing him about this since Schiller was alive — something like, "I'm fond of my friends and enjoy helping them, so I guess by Kant's lights I'm not virtuous when I do." There's a nastier version of that worry too, I think it's originally Michael Smith's: if what's actually moving you is the bare thought "this is my duty" rather than the actual person and what they're going through, that's a bit creepy — you'd be fetishizing your own rightness-scorecard instead of caring about them.

Then there's a case I ran into that comes at it from the other direction — Huckleberry Finn helping Jim escape, even though Huck's sincere, consciously-held beliefs tell him this is theft and a sin (he feels guilty the whole time!). He can't make himself turn Jim in anyway, because on some level below his stated principles he's responding to Jim as a person. I think this example is Nomy Arpaly's. Her point, if I've got it right, is that Huck's act has real moral worth precisely because his motives are tracking the actual person in front of him, even though his conscious principles are garbage — almost the mirror image of the shopkeeper: right act, wrong stated principle, but this time the agent still gets credit.

So there seem to be two different tests floating around: do your actual motivating reasons overlap with whatever really makes the act right (regardless of whether you'd ever use the word "duty"), or do you have to be consciously acting on the concept of duty/rightness itself? There's a paper — Markovits, I think, "coincident reasons" was the phrase she used — that runs with the first test: you get moral worth to the degree your motivating reasons coincide with the right-making reasons, full stop, no duty-concept required.

Where does that leave Ray? His motivating reason is roughly "I can't be the guy who lets people die again" — not self-interest like the shopkeeper (no money or reputation in it), but also not obviously tracking the kid's actual danger the way Huck tracks Jim's humanity. My draft answer has him landing in between the two: closer to Huck than to the shopkeeper, because the guilt is at least a (badly aimed) form of caring about the earlier death, but not as clean as Huck because there's no actual person he's responding to in the moment, just his own ledger.

What I want from you: (1) flag anywhere I've flattened or misrepresented Kant, Smith, Arpaly, or Markovits, (2) tell me if "somewhere in between Huck and the shopkeeper" is a real conclusion or just a cop-out dressed up as nuance, and (3) tell me if there's a sharper position out there that would let Meera say something more precise and more cutting than "eh, kind of in between" — I want the scene to land like an actual argument, not a shrug.
