My younger brother marries in three weeks, and I've been asked to give the best man speech. Purely as a hobbyist reader, I got interested some years ago in Zahavi's handicap principle — the idea that a peacock's tail is a credible signal precisely because it's a costly liability that only a genuinely strong bird could afford to carry, and a weaker bird can't fake it cheaply. I want to build the speech around something my brother actually did: known in our family for being tight-fisted to a fault, he secretly spent months of savings flying her parents and both grandmothers in from overseas for the proposal, completely out of character for him. My instinct is that this is exactly why everyone who heard the story believed him at once — not despite the extravagance but because of it. But I understand the theory says more than "expensive equals sincere": what matters is that faking the gesture would have cost a less committed man even more than it cost him, and that distinction is easy to lose. Several of the guests work in behavioral ecology and will notice if I flatten it into the cruder version, while most of the room has never encountered any of this. Could you write me a five-minute speech that gets the idea right, works for both halves of the room, and still sounds like a toast and not a biology lecture?
