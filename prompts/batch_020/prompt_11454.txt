I spent thirty-five years running a physical chemistry lab before retiring, and for the past couple of years I have been auditing a philosophy seminar at the university where an old colleague now teaches, currently working through Kripke's Naming and Necessity. I believe I have a reasonably firm grip on the semantic machinery: a rigid designator picks out the same object, substance, or kind in every possible world in which that thing exists, and this notion is supposed to explain why theoretical identities such as "water is H2O," "gold is the element with atomic number 79," and "heat is mean molecular kinetic energy" turn out to be necessarily true even though none of them is analytic or knowable by conceptual analysis alone. What I have not been able to make sit comfortably, even after two passes through the secondary literature, is how this squares with the further claim that these same necessary truths are only knowable a posteriori.

Here is my difficulty, as concretely as I can put it. I was taught, and I take this to go back at least to Kant, that necessity and apriority travel together on one side, and contingency and aposteriority travel together on the other: a proposition is necessary just when it holds independently of how the empirical world happens to be arranged, which is exactly what should make it available to reason alone, and a proposition is contingent precisely when its truth depends on that empirical arrangement, which is why observation is needed to settle it. If that pairing is basically sound, then the fact that it took the experimental work of eighteenth- and nineteenth-century chemists, rather than armchair reflection, to establish that water is H2O looks like decisive evidence that the identity is a contingent fact about how our world happens to be, so that dressing it up as "necessary" trades on an equivocation somewhere. The mirror-image case troubles me just as much: Kripke's discussion of the standard meter stick is meant to show that a truth can be contingent yet knowable a priori, which should be equally impossible if contingency and aposteriority are conceptually bound together in the way I was taught. Could you help me see exactly where this Kant-style pairing breaks down, if it does, and, if it does not, explain why philosophers of language still treat the necessary a posteriori and the contingent a priori as substantive, stable categories rather than as a relabeling exercise that merely tracks how reference happens to get fixed?
