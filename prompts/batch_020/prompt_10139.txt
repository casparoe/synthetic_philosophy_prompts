I don't have any formal background in philosophy, but over the past year I've been working through some of Marx's writing on my own -- the "Estranged Labour" section of the early 1844 Manuscripts, and separately the chapters on surplus value in the first volume of Capital -- after a pay dispute at a warehouse job got me wondering what "exploitation" actually means in his system, rather than just serving as a general-purpose word for being treated badly. When I raised this with a couple of former coworkers, they used "exploited" and "alienated" more or less interchangeably, which is the point I haven't been able to resolve on my own: as far as I can tell, Marx means something fairly precise and technical by exploitation -- a claim about surplus labor and the value of labor-power -- while alienation is a broader claim about the worker's relation to the product of their labor, to the activity of working itself, to what he calls their "species-being," and to other people. What I can't settle for myself is whether these two are supposed to come as a package under capitalism, or whether they're separable enough that a real situation could score high on one and low on the other.

Could you construct one detailed, concrete example of a job or work arrangement that counts as exploitative in Marx's specific technical sense but not, or only barely, alienating in his sense -- or, if you think it makes the point better, one that is alienating without being exploitative? Please don't just gesture at the two concepts in the abstract: walk the example through the actual criteria involved (surplus value and the value of labor-power, for exploitation; estrangement from the product, the activity, species-being, and other people, for alienation), and then say what the example shows -- whether exploitation and alienation are ultimately two descriptions of the same underlying situation, or two dimensions of a worker's situation that can, at least in principle, come apart.
