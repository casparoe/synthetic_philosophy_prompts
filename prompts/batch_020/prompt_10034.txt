The centipede game is the standard illustration of backward induction, but it also generates a well-known challenge to backward-induction reasoning itself, one that Philip Pettit and Robert Sugden dubbed "the backward-induction paradox." The case for defecting at the first move rests on its being common knowledge that both players are rational and would keep defecting at every later node -- yet that same common knowledge is supposed to guarantee that no later node is ever reached, so it is unclear what grounds any claim about how a rational player would behave there. Robert Aumann later tried to vindicate backward induction rigorously, proving that common knowledge of rationality does entail the backward-induction outcome; Ken Binmore and Philip Reny have replied that this proof only works because it builds in, rather than earns, a contestable answer to what a player is entitled to believe and do at a node that, on the argument's own premises, should never be reached. Write an essay adjudicating the dispute: does common knowledge of rationality really force backward induction in games like the centipede game, or does the paradox show the standard argument to be covertly question-begging? Weigh the strongest considerations on each side and close with a definite verdict, rather than leaving the matter open.
