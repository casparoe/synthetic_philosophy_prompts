And here's the answer one of her students (lets say her name's Mara, for the book) turned in:

"Hume's essay 'Of Miracles' argues that a rational person should almost never believe a miracle happened just because someone testified to it. The argument has two stages.

In the first stage, Hume notes that our belief in a law of nature -- like water needing cold to freeze -- rests on completely uniform past experience, without a single exception. That gives it the highest degree of evidential support experience can provide, what he calls a 'proof.' A miracle is by definition a violation of such a law. So whenever someone reports a miracle, we're weighing the proof from all of uniform experience against the credibility of some testimony. Because a law of nature is defined by exceptionless experience, no testimony could ever, even in principle, be strong enough to tip the balance the other way -- the proof against the miracle is always stronger than any testimony for it, full stop. This is why Hume says a wise man proportions his belief to the evidence: the evidence is already maxed out against the miracle before any witness even opens their mouth.

The second stage is more historical than logical. Hume argues that even setting the first argument aside, no testimony for a religious miracle has, as a matter of fact, ever come from witnesses reliable enough to trust. He gives a few reasons: there aren't enough witnesses of sufficient education and character to rule out either delusion or deceit; people have a natural passion for surprise and wonder that makes them sloppy about this particular kind of story; miracle reports mostly crop up among ignorant, barbarous peoples, or get inherited from them; and miracles from rival religions cancel each other out as evidence, since each is offered in support of a different, incompatible system.

Hume also brings up an Indian prince who refuses to believe travellers' reports of ice, since nothing like that has ever happened in his own experience. Hume's point is that the prince isn't being unreasonable, just going on the best evidence available to him -- Hume actually grants a kind of principle of credulity here, where we default to trusting testimony unless we have some specific reason not to, and then argues that miracle-testimony always runs into one of those specific reasons. So even on that more generous standard, religious miracles still fail to clear the bar.

Overall, Hume concludes that believing in a miracle on the basis of testimony is never rational, and that Christianity specifically can't be believed by any reasonable person without requiring a kind of miracle in that person's own understanding."
