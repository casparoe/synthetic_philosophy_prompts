I'm a PhD student in conservation biology, and through effective-altruism-adjacent circles at my university I somehow ended up on the three-person committee that gives out our little animal welfare fund (something like $8-10k a year to local projects). Our one-paragraph charter says we're supposed to "prioritize interventions that reduce the most severe and intense suffering, treating the prevention of suffering as more important than the promotion of positive welfare" -- which sounded fine and vague enough to never actually matter, until now.

A local TNR (trap-neuter-return) group applied for $4k to expand sterilization and feeding for two feral cat colonies on the edge of a campus nature preserve. Their pitch uses our own language back at us: most kittens born into an unmanaged colony die within months from disease, starvation, or exposure, so sterilizing the adults first prevents a bunch of short, bad lives from ever happening, seperate from (and on top of) the welfare gains to the adults who get vet care regardless. The preserve's staff ecologist, who's on our committee, wants to reject it, or load it with conditions: she says colonies kept healthier and longer-lived under TNR than they'd be unmanaged are still hammering the ground-nesting birds the preserve exists to protect, so best case we're not reducing suffering, we're relocating it onto animals nobody's counting. A postdoc who joined this year actually wants to fund it, but not for the applicant's stated reason -- he thinks "we're preventing suffering by preventing a birth" is a trap as a decision procedure, because if we take that headcount logic seriously we're on the hook for weighing kitten-suffering against bird-suffering every time something like this comes up, and three people with a spreadsheet and $4k have no business doing that; his fix is to fund it purely on the boring ground that it helps cats who already exist, and bracket the kitten part entirely. Her comeback: bracketing the justification doesn't shrink what's actually funded -- healthier, longer-lived colonies next to the preserve either way -- so if predation's the real problem, relabeling why we're paying for it changes nothing, and she thinks "let's not open that can of worms" is really just him avoiding admitting he's already decided the birds matter more here. He didn't fully back down: maybe she's right that they should, but deciding that grant-by-grant is exactly the kind of cross-species tradeoff a three-person committee with no ecological modeling budget has no business making, which is itself a reason to fund the narrow version and leave the wildlife question to people equipped to answer it.

I have to bring an actual recommendation to our vote next week and I'm stuck. Could you draft the memo I'd circulate -- pick approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions, give real reasons, and engage seriously with both her empirical worry and his procedural one instead of just gesturing at whichever's convenient? If you think our charter's silence on whether preventing a birth counts as "preventing suffering" the same way relieving existing suffering does is really the root issue here, I'd love your take on how we should read that going forward too, since this grant almost certainly won't be the last case that turns on it.
