I'm a retired research chemist who's gotten pretty into philosophy since retiring, and joined a little discussion group thats working through decision theory. Last meeting two of the members, Nora and Gil, got into a real back-and-forth over a jar puzzle and I still cant decide who came out ahead.

Setup: Jar A has exactly 50 red and 50 blue marbles. Jar B has 100 marbles, red and blue in some unknown ratio. You draw one and get paid if it's red.

Nora said she'd always take Jar A, since with Jar B "for all I know it's 90 blue," and that's real information she can't just ignore. Gil said that's a mistake: with zero evidence either way, indifference makes Jar B 50/50 too, so treating them differently is superstition dressed up as caution. Nora said two "50/50"s can still be different animals -- with Jar A she knows the split, with Jar B she's only guessing, and no single number captures how far off that guess could be. Gil said fine, call it a range instead of a single number then -- but he'd read that once you get cautious at the edges of a range like that, you can be talked into turning down two separate bets that would together be a guaranteed profit, purely because each one looks bad on its own worst case -- about as clean a case of irrational as decision theory gets. Nora said that only shows caution has a cost, not that it's confused -- and one exact number has a quieter cost of its own: anyone who knows more than she does can build a bet around that exact number to bleed her dry, while she's safe from that, since she only bets when it looks good across her whole range.

Neither backed down. Could you go through it move by move, say who's got the better of it at each stage, and flag anything both of them are missing?
