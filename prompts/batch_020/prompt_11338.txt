In "The Thought" (1918), Frege argues that thoughts — the senses of declarative sentences, the contents we grasp and can judge true or false — belong to a "third realm," distinct from both the physical world and the contents of any mind:

"A third realm must be recognized. Anything belonging to this realm has it in common with ideas that it cannot be perceived by the senses, but has it in common with things that it does not need an owner so as to belong to the contents of his consciousness. Thus the thought, for example, which we express in the Pythagorean theorem is timelessly true, true independently of whether anyone takes it to be true. It needs no bearer. It is not true for the first time when it is discovered, but is like a planet which, already before anyone has seen it, has been in interaction with other planets."

I can follow the individual sentences, but I'm not sure what philosophical work is actually being done by insisting that thoughts are strictly mind-independent and timeless, rather than merely objective or intersubjectively valid in some weaker sense. I'd like help spelling out the real stakes of this doctrine and what, if anything, would be lost without it.

I'm also curious whether it bears on something quite different: the private diary Frege kept in 1924, the year before his death, in which he denounces parliamentary democracy and argues for excluding Jews from full political and civic life. Michael Dummett, among others, wrote that discovering this diary genuinely unsettled his sense of Frege's legacy, while others treat it as biographically startling but philosophically beside the point. Is there a serious argument connecting Frege's insistence on a rigid, impersonal objectivity, graspable only by properly disciplined reason, to the exclusionary assumptions of the diary, or would any such connection be no more than a rhetorical guilt-by-association move?
