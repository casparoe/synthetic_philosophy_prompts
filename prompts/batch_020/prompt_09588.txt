Last night a friend and I had a long, unresolved argument about whether there is a genuine "responsibility gap" — I understand the term comes from Andreas Matthias's work on learning automata — when a fully autonomous weapons system, operating without a human in the loop, kills someone whom no one specifically intended to target. I argued that the system's genuine unpredictability breaks the ordinary chain of culpability; my friend argued that talk of a "gap" is really an excuse, and that whoever deployed the system remains fully its author no matter how the software behaved once it was running. Neither of us budged, so I'd like your help working out what Kant, specifically, would have said. His account of imputation (Zurechnung) in the Metaphysics of Morals turns on distinctions that seem tailor-made for this dispute: between a person, who alone can be the free author (Urheber) of a deed, and a mere thing (Sache), to which nothing can be imputed; and between an action (Handlung), which originates in an agent's own causality, and an occurrence that merely happens. Working strictly from that apparatus, rather than from his more general remarks on autonomy and rational nature, how do you think Kant would answer two questions: first, whether a self-learning system's behavior could even count as a deed capable of imputation, given that it is not the free causality of a person in his sense; and second, whether its effects are simply imputable "upward" to its designers and operators, the way the actions of a tool or a trained animal are imputed to whoever employs them, or whether the system's unpredictability breaks that analogy. Would Kant recognize anything like a genuine gap here, or does his framework leave no room for one?
