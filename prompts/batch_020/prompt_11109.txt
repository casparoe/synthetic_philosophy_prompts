So I'm a retired physicist, and for the last year or so I've properly gotten into philosophy for the first time -- mostly whatever the library has, so go easy on me. At my new philosophy discussion group, two of the regulars got into a real argument over the old Neptune/Vulcan story, which I've known since grad school but never thought of as philosophy before, and I havent been able to stop turning it over since.

Quick refresher in case it's useful: Le Verrier used the exact same method twice. Anomalies in Uranus's orbit led him to predict an unseen planet, and Neptune turned up almost exactly where he said, in 1846. Anomalies in Mercury's orbit led him to predict another unseen planet, "Vulcan" -- never found, and the anomaly wasn't resolved till Einstein's general relativity came along, sixty-odd years later.

(1) Marisol, a philosopher, said this is a clean case of the underdetermination of theory by evidence: keeping Newton and positing a new planet was an equally rational move both times, and nothing in the evidence itself could've told you in advance which time it would pan out.
(2) Devon, an engineer, said that's too strong -- the Neptune numbers fit the anomaly closely, the Vulcan numbers never fit nearly as well and kept needing revising, so there was real evidential daylight between the two cases even at the time, not just in hindsight.
(3) Marisol said "fit well enough" is a judgment call with no principled cutoff, and you can only make that judgment at all by already trusting the Newtonian framework supposedly being tested -- so he's relocated the underdetermination, not removed it.
(4) Devon said that proves too much: by that logic no observation ever really tests anything, including the eventual confirmation of relativity itself.

Neither of them budged. Could you go through this move by move, say who's got the better of it at each step rather than just declaring an overall winner, and flag anything important you think they're both missing?
