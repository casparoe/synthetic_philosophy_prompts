I have been rereading David Lewis's Convention, the book that gave the first rigorous philosophical treatment of common knowledge — a notion that gets invoked casually, and often loosely, in rationalist writing on social norms and coordination. On Lewis's actual analysis, a convention is an equilibrium solution to a recurring coordination problem, sustained not by agreement or enforcement but by common knowledge that everyone conforms, expects others to conform, and prefers to conform given that others do — and it remains arbitrary, since some other regularity could have served just as well. Please describe the most important implications of this account for the unwritten norms that structure the forums and communities I follow (steelmanning, charitable interpretation, 'epistemic status' disclaimers, norms against ad hominem). In particular, what does Lewis's framework imply about how such conventions arise without a founding agreement, and about when we should expect them to erode — say, as a community scales up quickly, absorbs newcomers who lack the relevant common knowledge, or attracts participants who never shared the underlying preference to conform?
