For as long as anyone in my family can remember, we've played a trick-taking card game at holiday gatherings that my grandfather invented sometime in the 1970s, using a standard deck plus both jokers and a handful of extra scoring quirks he never wrote down. Everyone just absorbed the rules over decades of watching and playing. Two of those quirks are that if a player wins the final trick of a hand using both jokers, the hand's points are doubled, and if a player wins the final trick using the two of trump, the hand "flips," so whoever took the fewest tricks scores instead of whoever took the most. My grandfather passed away eight years ago, and in more than forty years of play, those two situations had simply never come up in the same hand until this year.

This Thanksgiving they did: the final trick of a hand contained both jokers and the two of trump at once. My cousin Mara insists the doubling applies first and the flip second, because that's how she always understood the "stacking order" from watching my grandfather settle scoring disputes when she was a kid. My cousin Denny is just as sure the flip cancels the double outright, because he distinctly remembers my grandfather telling him as much years ago, after he once asked a hypothetical version of this exact question. We dug out every scoresheet the family still has, going back to the eighties, and confirmed that every hand ever recorded is completely consistent with both of their readings, simply because the two situations never coincided before now. Several aunts and uncles have opinions too, delivered with total confidence, but they disagree with each other as well, and none of them can point to a single past hand that would actually distinguish the two interpretations.

There's only about forty dollars riding on it, but a great deal more family pride, and I've been turning this over for weeks. What bothers me isn't really the money; it's that I can't decide whether there was ever a hidden "correct" way of combining those two rules, just waiting to be uncovered, or whether nothing in forty years of perfectly consistent, successful play ever actually pinned that down — not even inside my grandfather's own head, since this exact situation never arose while he was alive either. Both possibilities seem strange to me: it feels wrong to say the game never really had settled rules, given that everyone managed to follow them correctly for decades without incident, but it also feels wrong to say Mara or Denny is simply mistaken about some fact that nothing in the entire history of the game could ever have revealed. I don't have any background in this kind of question, so I'd love an outside take: is one of my cousins actually right, is there some principled way for a family with no rulebook and no living founder to settle a case like this, or are we really just going to have to pick an answer and admit that we're legislating the rule rather than discovering it?
