I did fisheries biology for almost 40 years, mostly mark-recapture population surveys, and since retiring I've been going to a local philosophy discussion group just for fun. Last month's topic was the doomsday argument, and it turned into a real argument between two of the regulars, Doug and Aisha, that I couldn't referee even though I wrote most of it down after. Roughly how it went:

(1) Doug: think of yourself as a random pick from everyone who will ever be born. Finding that you're, say, among the first 100 billion or so humans is way more likely if only a few hundred billion humans will ever exist than if its trillions, so — same logic as guessing how many tanks a factory made from one captured tank's serial number — you should shift your credence toward the species ending sooner rather than later.

(2) Aisha: said that's a category error, you don't get randomly drawn, you exist exactly when your particular parents happened to meet, for perfectly ordinary causal reasons, there's no urn here. She pointed out that someone running Doug's argument in the year 1600 would've been just as confident, and dead wrong, which she said shows the method itself is bust.

(3) Doug: said it doesn't need to be a physically random process, only that theres nothing special you know about your own position that should bias the estimate one way or the other, same as the tank case, where nobody thinks serial numbers got physically randomized either. He admitted the 1600 version comes out wrong in hindsight, but said any well-calibrated estimate looks bad in hindsight sometimes, and that isn't itself a strike against the method.

(4) Aisha: came back sharper, said the whole thing only works because Doug's implicitly assuming you're randomly sampled from a "reference class" of observers (she called this the self-sampling move), and that if you instead weight each hypothesis by how many observers it implies exist in the first place — self-indication, she called it — the extra observers under the big-population hypothesis cancel his shift right back out. She added that going her way has its own embarrassing consequence she'd read about, something about a philosopher becoming nearly certain the universe is unimaginably huge purely from an armchair, no telescope needed, so Doug hadn't shown his setup was the principled one either.

Neither budged and that's where the night ended. Could you go through it round by round and say who actually gets the better of each exchange, rather than just picking a winner overall, and flag anything you think they're both missing?
