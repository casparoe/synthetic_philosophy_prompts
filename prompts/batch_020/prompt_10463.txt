I spent the better part of four decades as a theoretical physicist working in statistical mechanics, and only in retirement have I begun reading philosophy in any systematic way, currently working through the literature on the philosophy of mathematics and set theory in particular. A small technical memory from my own field has turned, rather to my surprise, into something of a preoccupation. Deriving the functional form of entropy — whether from Shannon's postulates for information, or simply from the requirement that entropy be additive over independent subsystems while the number of microstates is multiplicative — always comes down, after a logarithmic change of variable, to solving Cauchy's functional equation, f(x+y) = f(x) + f(y). Every treatment I was ever taught tacked on a continuity (or monotonicity) assumption purely "to exclude pathological solutions," as though that were a bit of harmless housekeeping.

It is nothing of the kind. Drop the regularity assumption and build a solution from a Hamel basis of the reals over the rationals, and you get an additive function that is discontinuous at every point, non-measurable, and has a graph dense in the plane — a genuine monster, and one whose existence depends on going beyond the choice principles (countable or dependent choice) that ordinarily suffice for analysis. In models of set theory such as Solovay's, where every set of reals is Lebesgue measurable, no such function exists at all — physics, it seems, never needed the monster in the first place. This has left me wanting to see the underlying dispute conducted properly, by two people who actually know the mathematics, rather than gestured at in a paragraph like this one.

Could you therefore write me a dialogue, evenly matched on both sides, between a set-theoretic platonist who accepts the Axiom of Choice without reservation and welcomes these pathological functions as bona fide mathematical objects, and a choice-skeptic — in the spirit of the French analysts (Baire, Borel, Lebesgue) who first balked at Zermelo's axiom — who denies that anything deserving the name "function" can be conjured into existence by fiat, with no rule, however indirect, for computing its values. I would ask that the two of them fight specifically over the Cauchy-equation case rather than retreating to the more familiar Banach–Tarski paradox, that each directly answer the other's best point rather than simply restate their own position, and that the exchange not resolve too neatly — I have come to suspect that it shouldn't.
