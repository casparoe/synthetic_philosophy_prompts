After a career as a condensed-matter physicist, I retired a few years ago and have since taken to auditing courses in the philosophy department at my old university, partly for pleasure and partly, I suspect, to make amends for having dismissed this sort of question as unrigorous for four decades. This term I have been sitting in on a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of space and time, and the instructor, aware of my background, asked whether I would help mark a set of take-home exam answers on Newton's bucket, chiefly to catch any place where the physics is being handled loosely. I agreed readily enough, but I am discovering that grading philosophical prose calls for a different kind of scrutiny than grading a lab report -- an answer can get most of its individual sentences right and still not add up to a sound argument -- and I would rather have a careful, independent read before I commit to a grade.

The question set for the students was: "Newton's rotating bucket is often treated as posing a direct challenge to relationist theories of space. (a) Reconstruct Newton's argument: why does he hold that the concavity of the water's surface cannot be explained by the water's motion relative to the bucket, and what does he conclude from this? (b) Explain how Leibniz's relationism is supposed to handle the rotating bucket. (c) Outline how Mach's proposal is meant to improve on Leibniz's position here, noting one respect in which it remains programmatic rather than a fully worked-out mechanics. (d) Explain how Earman and Norton's hole argument relates to the bucket argument, and state what, if anything, it establishes about the truth of substantivalism." The question is worth 40 points, 10 per part.

Here is the student's answer, submitted in four labeled parts:

(a) Newton's argument turns on a mismatch between relative motion and dynamical effect. When the bucket is first set spinning, the relative motion between the bucket and the water is at its maximum -- the bucket turns rapidly while the water, not yet dragged along, is still nearly at rest -- yet the water's surface remains flat. Only later, once friction has drawn the water into corotation with the bucket, when the relative motion between the two has fallen to zero, does the water climb the sides of the vessel and take on a concave shape. Since the concavity appears exactly when relative motion to the bucket disappears, and is absent exactly when that relative motion is greatest, the concavity cannot be a function of the water's motion relative to the bucket. Newton concludes that the water's true motion -- the rotation responsible for the concave surface -- must be rotation relative to space itself, which he takes to be a real thing existing independently of any body.

(b) Leibniz denies that space is anything over and above the changing spatial relations among bodies: for a body to move just is for its position relative to other bodies to change, and by the Principle of Sufficient Reason there can be no fact about a body's absolute position or orientation, since shifting or rotating the entire material universe would leave every relation among bodies, and hence every observable fact, exactly as it was. Applied to the bucket, the Leibnizian can say that what the water is doing at every stage of the experiment is rotating relative to the bucket that contains it, and that it is this relative rotation which shows up as the tension we see as concavity; "absolute" rotation adds nothing to this description beyond a name. On this view, the bucket poses no special difficulty for relationism -- it is simply an application of the general thesis that all motion, whether uniform or rotational, consists in changing relations between bodies.

(c) Mach keeps the relationist strategy but changes what the rotation is relative to: instead of the bucket's own walls, he points to the fixed stars, that is, to the bulk of the matter in the universe, and suggests that it is rotation relative to this cosmic mass distribution, rather than relative to an undetectable absolute space, that produces the effects Newton attributes to true rotation. He makes the point vivid by asking what would happen if the bucket were held fixed while the entire shell of distant stars were set spinning around it; the relationist is committed to saying the water should still climb the sides. This is progress on Leibniz because it names a concrete relatum rather than resting on general metaphysical principle alone, but Mach never supplied a mechanics that would derive the size of the effect or explain how such distant matter could act locally on the water in the bucket; in that sense the idea remained a suggestive proposal rather than a worked-out theory, and later attempts to make it precise -- including Einstein's own use of it as a heuristic in building General Relativity -- have had decidedly mixed success.

(d) The hole argument, originally due to Einstein and revived as an argument about substantivalism by Earman and Norton, is the modern, general-relativistic descendant of the bucket, in that it too asks what, if anything, individuates points of spacetime independently of the matter and fields located at them. Take a generally covariant spacetime theory and a "hole," a region containing no matter, and construct a diffeomorphism that acts as the identity everywhere outside the hole but smoothly displaces points within it; this yields a second metric field that agrees with the first everywhere outside the hole and disagrees inside it. If spacetime points are genuine individuals in their own right, as the substantivalist holds, the two fields describe distinct physical possibilities that agree on the entire history up to the hole and diverge afterward, which makes the theory radically indeterministic even though the two scenarios are empirically indistinguishable. Earman and Norton take this to show that substantivalism -- the thesis that space or spacetime exists as a real entity independently of matter and its relations -- cannot be correct, so that the hole argument delivers, within a fully relativistic setting, the same relationist moral that Leibniz had already drawn from the bucket two centuries before.
