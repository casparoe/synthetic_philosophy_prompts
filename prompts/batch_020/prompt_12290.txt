Lately we've been working through Aristotle's sea-battle passage in De Interpretatione 9 and some of the modern logic that grew out of it, and to keep everyone honest I started setting a short written question after each session, with a different person grading it before we compare notes the following week. This week it's my turn to grade, and since I have no formal philosophy training myself, I'd like a more rigorous second opinion before I circulate my comments.

The question I wrote was:

Aristotle's sea-battle passage is often discussed alongside Diodorus Cronus's Master Argument as ancient sources of the problem of future contingents. (a) Reconstruct the fatalistic argument Aristotle is worried about, and state, in your own words, his own way of blocking it. (b) Is the Master Argument the same argument as Aristotle's, a variant of it, or an independent argument for a related conclusion? (c) Two influential modern treatments of the puzzle are Łukasiewicz's three-valued logic and supervaluationism over Prior-style branching time, as developed by Thomason. Do both of these give up the law of excluded middle in order to make room for truth-value gaps? Explain.

And here is the answer one of my colleagues turned in:

Aristotle's puzzle starts from the assumption that a statement about the future, such as "there will be a sea battle tomorrow," is already either true or false today. If it's true today, the battle seems already settled and unavoidable; if it's false today, its absence is equally settled. Either way, tomorrow's outcome turns out to be necessary, which threatens deliberation and the ordinary idea that the future is open. Aristotle's way out, on the standard reading, is to deny that statements like this are true or false yet: they become one or the other only once the event happens or fails to happen, which blocks the slide into fatalism.

Diodorus Cronus's Master Argument is essentially the same argument in a different guise — the Megarian version of the sea-battle puzzle — and Aristotle's discussion in De Interpretatione 9 can be read as his direct answer to it, since both arguments conclude that being careless about tensed truth commits us to saying that only what actually happens was ever really possible. Diodorus's version just makes the modal commitments explicit, arguing from the necessity of the past together with the claim that nothing impossible follows from something possible, whereas Aristotle stays closer to the ordinary true-or-false status of statements.

On the modern side, both twentieth-century solutions weaken classical logic in the same way. Łukasiewicz's three-valued logic and Thomason's supervaluationism both give the future contingent sentence a third status, neither true nor false, and both therefore have to give up the law of excluded middle: "there will be a sea battle, or there will not be" can no longer be guaranteed true, since neither disjunct is determinately true on its own. Łukasiewicz assigns the intermediate value directly to the sentence, while supervaluationism gets the same effect indirectly, by looking at truth across the different branching future histories in a Prior-style tree and counting a sentence true only if it comes out true on every one of them.
