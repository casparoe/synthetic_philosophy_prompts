After thirty-five years as a physicist working mostly on nuclear weapons diagnostics, I only took up philosophy seriously in retirement. A running argument with an old colleague, about whether people like us bore any personal responsibility for how our work was eventually used, sent me first to Michael Walzer's "Just and Unjust Wars" and then to Jeff McMahan's "Killing in War." I had always assumed the doctrine that soldiers on both sides of any war are each other's moral equals -- that the rules governing conduct in war (jus in bello) are simply independent of whether the war itself is just (jus ad bellum) -- was close to self-evident, so the revisionist attack on it has unsettled me rather more than I expected.

I think I follow the shape of the disagreement in the abstract, but I would like to see the two views actually collide on a hard case rather than stated as general theses. Could you write me a dialogue between a traditionalist and a revisionist about the ethics of killing in war, staged as an argument over one concrete scenario: a nineteen-year-old conscript from the aggressor state, raised from childhood on state propaganda and facing execution for desertion, who kills a professional soldier defending his country against the invasion.

I would like the revisionist to press the traditionalist on why a merely legal or conventional symmetry between the two soldiers should be taken to track any underlying moral symmetry, and I would like the traditionalist to get a real chance to run the argument that ordinary soldiers are in no position to adjudicate the justice of the war they are conscripted into, together with the further worry that abandoning combatant equality would make the laws of war unworkable. But I would also like the revisionist to bring McMahan's distinction between being excused and being justified to bear on exactly that case: as I understand him, facts like nonculpable ignorance and duress can reduce a combatant's responsibility for the threat he poses, and so reduce how liable he is to be killed, without ever making him a moral equal of the soldier he is fighting. Please have both sides argued at full strength -- neither should be a straw man -- with each genuinely engaging the other's last move rather than restating their own position, and do not feel obliged to resolve the case tidily; I am just as interested in seeing exactly where the disagreement bottoms out.
