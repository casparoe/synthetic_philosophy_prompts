I teach a graduate seminar on philosophy and literature, and the unit on Borges always ends with a mock interview: one student plays an interviewer, another answers in character as Borges using only claims paraphrased from his own essays and stories, and the rest of the seminar grades the exchange on how well the interviewer manages to press him rather than just collecting the greatest-hits lines about labyrinths, mirrors, and infinite libraries. This year I want to hand the interviewer a stronger set of questions to work from, keyed specifically to his writing on time — above all "A New Refutation of Time," but also "The Garden of Forking Paths," "Funes the Memorious," and the essay "Time and J. W. Dunne" — so that the exercise moves past the vague impression of "Borges thinks time is unreal" that most students arrive with.

Could you draft eight or nine interview questions along these lines? I need at least one question that presses on the actual inference in "A New Refutation of Time" — which borrows Berkeley's case against matter and Hume's case against a persisting self in order to argue against succession — and asks whether this really shows time to be unreal, or only shows that our experience does not require positing an enduring substance behind it; one that puts to him his own postscript, where he concedes that the world remains real and that he remains irreparably Borges, caught in succession and mortality, and asks whether that concession quietly retracts the essay's conclusion or was the point all along; and one that asks him to reconcile that essay's claim that only a self-sufficient present instant is real with "The Garden of Forking Paths," where times multiply and run alongside one another instead of collapsing into a single now. For each of these three, add a short follow-up keyed to the deflection or qualification he seems likeliest to reach for, so that a good answer from him doesn't simply end the exchange. I'd also like a closing question that draws on "Funes the Memorious" or "Time and J. W. Dunne" — on whether forgetting is what makes experiencing time as succession possible in the first place.
