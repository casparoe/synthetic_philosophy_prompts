I somehow ended up chairing my university's research integrity panel this fall (nobody else in the department would take it), and the very first case has genuinely kept me up at night. I don't cast a substantive vote myself, I just run the meetings and write up the panel's findings, but this time I honestly can't tell you what the finding is. The case: a postdoc is accused of reusing a chunk of an old control-group dataset in a new paper without disclosing that it wasn't freshly collected. Our policy says a misconduct finding requires BOTH (a) that the conduct was "a significant departure from accepted practices in the field," and (b) that it was done knowingly, intentionally, or recklessly rather than as an honest mistake -- miss either one and it's sloppiness, not misconduct. Each of the three panelists assigned to this case filled out a form with their own yes/no on (a), their own yes/no on (b), and then, seperately, their own overall yes/no on "misconduct."

Here's the part that's driving me crazy. Renata said departure=yes, intent=yes, so misconduct=yes for her. Kwame said departure=yes, intent=no (he thinks the postdoc was careless, not dishonest), so misconduct=no for him. Sasha said departure=no (she thinks reusing an old control set like this is actually pretty normal in his particular subfield) but intent=yes (she still thinks he knew exactly what he was doing), so misconduct=no for her too. If you tally the "misconduct overall" column, it's 2-to-1 for NO. But out of curiosity I also tallied columns (a) and (b) on their own, and each one comes out 2-to-1 YES -- which, if you just plug those majority answers into our own rule, means the panel collectively found both a significant departure and culpable intent, i.e., misconduct. Same three people, same three forms, opposite verdicts depending on which column you trust. I mentioned this to a friend who's finishing a philosophy PhD and she got weirdly excited and said it sounds like a named paradox about combining group judgments, but she had to run before she could tell me anything actually useful.

Our bylaws never say whether the panel's official finding is whatever's in the "misconduct overall" column or whatever comes out of applying our own rule to the majority verdicts on (a) and (b), and the research integrity officer wants my writeup by Friday -- both what we report for this specific case, and a standing rule for how future panels should tally split cases like this so it isn't a fight every single time. Could you draft that memo for me? I need an actual recommendation on both the specific case and the general procedure -- maybe a one-line bottom line up top, then the reasoning -- with real reasons a skeptical panelist could engage with, not just a description of the problem, and please be genuinely fair to whichever method you end up not recommending, since at least one of my three panelists is going to be unhappy no matter how this comes out.
