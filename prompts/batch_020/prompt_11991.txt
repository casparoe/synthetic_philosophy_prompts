"Bell's theorem shows that no theory of hidden variables that is local can reproduce the correlations quantum mechanics predicts for measurements on entangled particles. Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen had argued that if quantum mechanics is complete, then the perfect correlations between distant measurement outcomes force us to accept a kind of action at a distance that would violate relativity's prohibition on faster-than-light influences; they concluded instead that there must be hidden variables fixing the outcomes in advance, so as to restore locality. Bell took this reasoning further and derived an inequality that any local hidden-variable theory must satisfy. Because determinism is what allows the hidden variable to fix an outcome in advance, Bell's argument depends essentially on the assumption that a particle's behavior is determined by the hidden variable; without determinism, there is no well-defined inequality to derive.

When experiments such as Aspect's found that the statistics predicted by quantum mechanics violate the Bell inequality, this confirmed that entangled particles really do exchange a faster-than-light influence at the moment of measurement, which is why physicists describe the result as showing that nature is 'non-local' in the sense of Einstein's 'spooky action at a distance.'
