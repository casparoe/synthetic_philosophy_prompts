I'm reporting a longer feature on how physicists and philosophers end up talking past one another whenever the phrase "identical particles" comes up, and before I draft the section on individuality I want to think through a puzzle that keeps surfacing in the background reading. The philosophical starting point is Max Black's 1952 thought experiment: a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres, two miles apart, exactly alike in every monadic quality—mass, temperature, chemical composition, and so on. Black uses the scenario to argue that Leibniz's Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles cannot hold as an unrestricted metaphysical truth, since the spheres share every qualitative property and yet are clearly two things rather than one. He also heads off the obvious rejoinder that each sphere could be picked out by its relation to the other, noting that any purely qualitative relational description—"being two miles from an iron sphere of diameter one mile," say—is satisfied equally by both spheres and therefore singles out neither.

What complicates matters for the piece is a later move, associated with Simon Saunders and refined together with Muller, to save a weakened version of Leibniz's principle through what they call "weak discernibility": two objects count as discerned, on this proposal, whenever some irreflexive relation holds between them, even if that relation is symmetric and even if nothing distinguishes which of the two occupies which relational role. On this criterion, Black's spheres turn out to be discernible after all, since "is two miles from" is irreflexive—nothing is two miles from itself—yet true of the pair. The same manoeuvre is then redeployed to defend the individuality of entangled fermions, such as two electrons in a spin-singlet state, which share every reduced, single-particle property yet stand in the irreflexive relation "has opposite spin along every axis to."

I'd like you to compare these two applications of weak discernibility rather than simply explaining the concept. Does the manoeuvre do the same philosophical work in both cases, or is there a disanalogy between a classical pair of spatially separated spheres and a genuinely entangled quantum pair that makes weak discernibility more, or less, convincing in one setting than the other? And in your own assessment, does weak discernibility actually secure numerical diversity in either case, or does it just redescribe the fact that there are two things without explaining what makes them two rather than one? Please defend whichever verdict you reach.
