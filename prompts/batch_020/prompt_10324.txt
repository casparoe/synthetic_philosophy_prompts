I have no academic background in this, just a long time spent on my own reading about money-pump arguments, and one question about them has never quite settled for me. Picture a friend of mine whose preferences among three things loop: A over B, B over C, and C over A. Someone who noticed the loop has walked them through two small "upgrade" trades, each a plain improvement on its own, so they are now one trade away from paying a third small fee to land back exactly where they started.

Write a dialogue between two onlookers who both see the pump coming but draw opposite lessons from it. One defends sophisticated choice: the whole sequence should have been foreseen and refused at the very first trade, even though, taken alone, that trade looked like a good deal. The other defends resolute choice: what my friend needed instead was to fix a plan for the whole sequence in advance and hold to it regardless of how each step looked at the time, treating the plan itself, not each trade, as the proper object of rational assessment. Push them to engage each other directly rather than restate their positions: the sophisticated-choice advocate should press the other on what separates holding to a plan from plain stubbornness once no external device is enforcing it, while the resolute-choice advocate should press back on whether refusing that first trade doesn't already require the very kind of binding commitment sophistication claims not to need. Let the disagreement come down to whether rationality is fundamentally synchronic, a matter of each choice on its own, or diachronic, a matter of the plan those choices belong to.
