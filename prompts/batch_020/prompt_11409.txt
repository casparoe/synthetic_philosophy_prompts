In classical Nyāya epistemology, verbal testimony (śabda) is counted as one of four independent pramāṇas, or means of valid knowledge, standing alongside perception, inference, and comparison. Buddhist epistemologists in the lineage of Dignāga and Dharmakīrti reject this fourfold scheme: on their view there are really only two pramāṇas, perception and inference, and whatever knowledge we gain from being told something is, at bottom, an inference — from the fact that a trustworthy speaker uttered a certain sentence, we infer that the situation it describes actually holds, much as we infer fire from smoke. Naiyāyikas resist this assimilation. They point out that the process by which a hearer comes to understand and accept a sentence, a process governed by conditions like the words' mutual syntactic expectancy (ākāṅkṣā), their semantic fitness (yogyatā), and their proximity in utterance (sannidhi), does not obviously involve grasping any general concomitance (vyāpti) between a sign and what it signifies, which is the backbone of every genuine inference; a small child who simply believes what a trusted parent tells her, they add, is not silently running through an inferential argument.

Write a dialogue of roughly 700-900 words in which a contemporary Naiyāyika philosopher and a contemporary Buddhist epistemologist, having just given competing papers on this question at the same conference panel, stay behind afterward to work through their disagreement directly. Each should be made to confront the strongest version of the other's case rather than a caricature of it: the Buddhist needs a real answer to the child's-testimony case and to the worry that ākāṅkṣā, yogyatā, and sannidhi look nothing like a chain of inferential reasoning, while the Naiyāyika needs a real answer to the challenge that positing a whole extra pramāṇa is unnecessary if the relevant cases can already be handled, even if only implicitly or in compressed form, by inference from the speaker's reliability. The dialogue should show genuine argumentative movement, with each figure's remarks responding to what the other has just said, and it need not end in agreement if their disagreement turns out to run deeper than either initially assumes.
