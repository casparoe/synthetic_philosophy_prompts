I cover science and ideas for a living, and for the past few weeks I've been reporting a feature on what it really means for a mathematical problem to be declared "unsolvable." The continuum hypothesis (CH) — Cantor's claim that there is no infinite set of real numbers strictly bigger than the integers and strictly smaller than the reals themselves — keeps coming up, since Paul Cohen's 1963 forcing proof, completing work Gödel had begun in the 1930s, showed CH is independent of the standard axioms of set theory (ZFC). I wanted to understand what that independence result actually settles, so at a dinner after a small workshop on the philosophy of set theory, I sat next to two people who turned out to disagree sharply about it: a set theorist I'll call Nora and a philosopher of mathematics I'll call Julian. Neither of them backed down, and I wrote up as much of the exchange as I could reconstruct afterward.

(1) Nora opened by saying that CH being undecided by ZFC is a fact about ZFC, not about the universe of sets itself; she noted that Gödel, who had proved CH consistent with ZFC in the first place, came to believe CH is actually false and spent his later years arguing that new axioms — ideally ones "intrinsically" evident from the concept of set, but failing that, ones justified "extrinsically" by their fruitful consequences — would eventually decide it. (2) Julian replied that the analogy to an open empirical question is misleading: with physical theories, observation stands outside the theory to arbitrate between rival extensions of it, but in set theory Cohen's method doesn't just suggest CH might be undecidable, it exhibits models of ZFC on both sides; asking which model is the "real" universe of sets, he said, is as confused as asking whether the parallel postulate is really true independent of which geometry you've decided to do. (3) Nora answered that the geometry analogy proves too much, since nobody treats every consistent extension of ZFC as an equally good description of "set" — large cardinal axioms fall into a single hierarchy of increasing strength that keeps getting corroborated by unexpected equiconsistency results and by unifying previously separate areas like descriptive set theory, and that kind of convergence, she said, is itself evidence we're tracking something rather than just picking a convenient extension the way we pick a convenient geometry. (4) Julian conceded that the large cardinal hierarchy is remarkably well-behaved, but said this is beside the point for CH specifically: a Lévy–Solovay style argument shows you can force the size of the continuum up or down while preserving essentially any large cardinal you started with, so the very axioms Nora treats as tracking truth are provably indifferent to CH — which suggests CH belongs to a forcing-sensitive layer of set theory that large cardinals were never going to reach. (5) Nora responded that this is exactly why serious set theorists stopped resting everything on large cardinals alone and moved on to other principles, such as forcing axioms like PFA, which pins the continuum down at aleph-2, or Hugh Woodin's program aiming at a canonical theory of the reals via Ω-logic; the fact that this research treats CH as a target still to be hit, rather than a pseudo-question, shows that the people closest to the problem don't actually accept the pluralism Julian is defending.
