I am working on a piece about the history of mathematical certainty, and I have been delving into the long-running feud between Thomas Hobbes and the Oxford mathematician John Wallis, which opened with Wallis's 1655 Elenchus Geometriae Hobbianae and Hobbes's 1656 retaliatory Six Lessons to the Professors of the Mathematics, and then dragged on, pamphlet after pamphlet, until Hobbes's death in 1679. For the piece, I am less concerned with whether Hobbes's quadrature of the circle actually works (it does not) than with the deeper disagreement running underneath it: Hobbes insisted that a geometric figure and its definition are legitimate only insofar as they specify how that figure could actually be produced by motion, while Wallis treated definitions as conventions a mathematician is free to stipulate for the purposes of a proof, regardless of their relation to any physical construction. Write a dialogue between the two men, set as their quarrel is heating up, in which each tries to win the other over to his view of what makes a geometric definition or demonstration legitimate, including how it bears on Wallis's use of algebra and indivisibles. Give both figures their strongest arguments and have them genuinely rebut one another, rather than letting either simply concede once the circle-squaring error comes up.
