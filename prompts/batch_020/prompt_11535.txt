So this semester I'm TAing a grad seminar on practical reason, and it's forced me to actually sit with something I'd always kind of hand-waved past before: the gap between Isaiah Berlin's version of value pluralism and Ruth Chang's stuff on "parity." Berlin's line (mostly "The Pursuit of the Ideal," plus bits of "Two Concepts of Liberty") is that ultimate values like liberty, equality, mercy, and justice are genuinely plural and often incommensurable -- there's no master value you can convert them into to tell you which one to give up -- so conflicts between them are just tragic: whichever way you choose, something of real value is lost for good, and no algorithm was ever going to hand you the "correct" answer in advance. He seems pretty content to leave it there, like, that's just the human condition and we should stop looking for a tidier solution.

Chang, on the other hand (the 2002 Ethics paper, plus the TED talk that half my students seem to have watched in lieu of doing the actual reading), looks like she's trying to solve the further puzzle Berlin doesn't really touch: how choice can still be rational once you're stuck in genuinely incommensurable territory. Her move is to deny these cases are incomparable at all -- she argues the usual trichotomy of "better than / worse than / equally good" is incomplete, and that theres a fourth basic relation, being "on a par" (which she distinguishes from rough equality using the small-improvement argument), and that hard choices are exactly cases where the alternatives are on a par. Her further claim is that when reasons "run out" like this we can still choose non-arbitrarily, by exercising a kind of normative power -- committing to an option and thereby creating new reasons for ourselves, rather than just picking blindly.

What I can't decide is whether Chang is actually answering Berlin's question or just quietly swapping it for an easier one. Even granting there's a genuine sui generis "parity" relation, distinct from the incomparability that people like Joseph Raz talk about, I don't see why that gets us all the way to "rational, non-arbitrary choice" instead of just relabeling arbitrariness as "commitment" and calling it a day. Could you lay out both views properly -- including how Chang's picture of parity/incomparability departs from both Berlin's and Raz's -- and then actually argue for a verdict on which of the two gets closer to showing that hard choices between incommensurable values can be genuinely rational? Please don't just tell me "they're both getting at something important" -- I want an actual position, defended.
