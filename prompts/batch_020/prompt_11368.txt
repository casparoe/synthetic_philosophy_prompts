I teach a graduate seminar on African philosophy, and this week's unit was on Ubuntu and other relational conceptions of personhood -- the idea, in John Mbiti's famous formulation, that "I am because we are, and since we are, therefore I am." We built the session around the well-known exchange between Ifeanyi Menkiti and Kwame Gyekye over just how far to take that relational picture, and two of my students, Amara and Noah, ended up in a genuinely good argument about it that ran past the end of class. This is the fourth time I've taught the unit, and it's the sharpest this disagreement has ever gotten, so rather than wave it away I'd like to think it through properly. I'd rather get an outside opinion than referee my own students.

(1) Amara opened by defending Menkiti's claim that personhood, in the relevant normative sense, is not simply conferred at birth but progressively attained through incorporation into community -- through rites of passage, the discharge of social and moral obligations, and moral maturation -- such that an individual can fail to become a full person, or can be said to forfeit personhood through sufficiently grave misconduct; she argued this earns its keep by making sense of the existence of such rites, the special standing accorded to elders over children, and the tendency in many African moral traditions to describe the morally depraved as having lost standing as persons rather than merely having behaved badly. (2) Noah objected that this has a monstrous consequence: infants, people with profound cognitive disabilities, and hermits who never participate in communal life would then fail to be full persons, and the view has no principled stopping point before treating them as owed less moral consideration -- no amount of explanatory payoff, he said, should outweigh that cost. (3) Amara replied that Noah was conflating two different notions of personhood: Menkiti's "person" is a normative, honorific status -- comparable to what we mean by calling someone "a real mensch" -- entirely distinct from bare moral status or entitlement to basic care, and indeed the community's very obligation to nurture infants into full personhood presupposes that infants already matter morally before attaining it. (4) Noah countered that this rescue isn't obviously Menkiti's own view: if losing personhood through wickedness carried no real normative stakes, Menkiti's insistence that such loss is a real possibility that communities structure practices around would be doing no work -- so either the view does put moral status at risk after all, or Amara is saving it with a distinction the text never draws. (5) Amara answered that a position can be committed to a distinction it never states outright, so long as the distinction is needed to make the position coherent; that Menkiti never spells out a separate, non-processual notion of basic moral status doesn't show he needs one, only that Gyekye's famous worry -- that strong communitarianism about personhood cannot underwrite individual rights -- targets a cruder position than anyone need hold. (6) Noah's reply, finally, was that charity has limits: inventing, on Menkiti's behalf, an entire second notion of moral standing that does all the rights-protecting work and that he never articulates is itself a serious cost, even if some repaired descendant of the view survives -- and avoiding exactly that cost is the whole point of Gyekye's "moderate communitarianism," which builds basic individual capacities in from the start rather than treating them as an achievement.
