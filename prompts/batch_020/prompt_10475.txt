For the past couple of years I have followed the "network state" and seasteading discourse that circulates on the blogs I read — Balaji Srinivasan's book attracted considerable attention, and there is periodic chatter about small pilot projects like Próspera and Zuzalu — and last year some friends and acquaintances from that scene pooled resources to start a modest pilot of our own: roughly 140 residents on a fifty-year land lease, with our own bylaws and a five-person resident council, on which I sit. We are now deadlocked over the admissions policy for our next intake. One faction wants something close to open enrollment: anyone who passes a standard background check and pays the fixed buy-in gets a unit, on the grounds that we have no standing to keep out people whose lives would be transformed by joining us merely to preserve a particular in-group feel. The other faction wants to keep our current system, a closed-door interview-and-vote process that lets existing residents screen newcomers for "fit," on the grounds that we are a voluntary association and get to decide who joins us for the same reason a family, a co-op, or a club does. Both sides have started quoting the immigration-ethics literature at each other — Carens and the freedom-of-movement side, Wellman and the freedom-of-association side — as though the state-level debate simply settles our case, but nobody on the council has actually worked through whether those arguments transfer to a community like ours: small, founded for a specific purpose by a self-selected group, and holding real territorial and infrastructural power rather than mere clubhouse membership.

Could you draft the memo I will circulate to the council ahead of our vote at the end of the month? It should recommend one specific admissions policy — a clean version of either side, or a genuine hybrid, is fine, so long as it is concrete enough to actually implement — together with a rollout timeline, and it should take the strongest version of the losing position seriously on its own terms rather than caricaturing it, including the disanalogies between our situation and both a nation-state and a golf club that neither faction's borrowed arguments quite address.
