I'm about two-thirds of the way through a novel narrated by a man named Daniel Voss, who is serving a life sentence for a killing he committed at nineteen, and whose entire self-defense — offered less to the parole board than to himself — rests on something very close to Galen Strawson's Basic Argument. Daniel lays it out for his sister Miriam in a visiting-room scene I've already drafted: you do what you do because of the way you are; to be truly, and not just legally, responsible for what you do, you'd have to be responsible for being the way you are; but you can't be, because that would require some further self, existing prior to and independently of your upbringing and temperament, that chose your formative values — and that self would need its own antecedent self to have chosen its values, and so on, so that only something capable of being causa sui (Strawson's term, borrowed from Nietzsche's derision of the very idea) could bear "true" responsibility, and nothing can be causa sui. I even sat in on part of a free-will seminar at the university near me to get the logic right, but the professor was a committed compatibilist who seemed to lose patience with the argument by the third week, so I'm not confident I came away with a fair picture of how strong it actually is.

What I haven't drafted yet is Miriam's rebuttal, and I don't want to hand her a strawman just so my narrator can sound clever by comparison. Frankly, I go back and forth myself on whether Daniel has hold of something real or is just dressing up a convenient excuse in respectable philosophical clothing, and I'd like her side of the scene to rest on an actual answer rather than my own ambivalence. So I'd like an essay from you that adjudicates the dispute rather than merely surveying it. Lay out the Basic Argument in its strongest form — including why Strawson thinks the regress is vicious and why he insists indeterminism doesn't rescue anyone from it — and then weigh the most serious responses on offer: compatibilist attempts to detach responsibility from self-origination entirely (Fischer and Ravizza's reasons-responsiveness account, Frankfurt-style mesh or identification views), libertarian attempts to ground a genuinely self-forming moment that isn't just further luck (Kane), and skeptical responses that accept the conclusion but try to salvage something practical from it (Pereboom's rejection of basic-desert responsibility alongside his case that a meaningful moral life survives anyway, or Smilansky's franker claim that the whole edifice of blame depends on a useful illusion). Don't let yourself off the hook with "reasonable people disagree" — I want an actual verdict on whether the Basic Argument succeeds, and, if it does, a clear statement of what, if anything, is left of the ordinary practice of praising and blaming people once you accept it, because that's the note Miriam's side of the scene needs to land on.
