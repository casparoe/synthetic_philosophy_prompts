I'm a software engineer, and on the side I help run the governance side of a mid-sized open source project — we've got a foundation, a steering council, actual bylaws, the whole nonprofit setup, even though the project itself is nothing you've heard of. Right now we're in the middle of a genuinely annoying fight: a handful of our more organized contributors want to run as a coordinated "slate" in the next council election — agree on a platform ahead of time, publicly endorse each other, vote together once they're in, basically a mini political party inside the project. The bylaws never anticipated this, so nothing technically stops them. About half the council thinks organized slates are healthier than the current every-person-for-themselves free for all, the other half wants to ban coordinated slates outright before nominations open in three weeks.

Then, right as this was heating up, one of our contributors (a recovering philosophy major — theres apparently a few of those hiding in open source) sent the council mailing list a link to Simone Weil's essay arguing for the total abolition of political parties, full stop, not campaign finance reform or tighter transparency rules, just gone entirely, because of what she thinks the mere existence of a party does to a member's ability to think straight about anything. I've never taken a philosophy class in my life and its honestly been rattling around my head all week. Naturally I got voluntold to write the actual decision memo the council votes on before nominations open, and I don't want to hand them something that either ignores her argument or just name-drops it and moves on. I want a memo that actually takes Weil's reasoning seriously and asks whether it even applies to something as small and informal as us, not just generic "factions: good or bad" pros and cons.

Can you draft that memo for me? I need an actual recommendation — ban coordinated slates, allow them openly with disclosure requirements, or leave the bylaws alone — the reasoning behind it, and a genuinely fair version of the strongest argument for whichever option we don't land on, not a strawman.
