At work I use Judea Pearl's causal-graph framework to reason about our experimentation pipeline, which led me into the philosophical literature on causation. I've now read enough of David Lewis's counterfactual theory of causation (a cause is whatever an effect counterfactually depends on, with dependence fixed by overall similarity between possible worlds) and James Woodward's interventionist theory (a cause is whatever an effect would change in response to, under an idealized "surgical" intervention) to suspect that Woodward's account, for all its advertised advantages over Lewis's notoriously vague similarity metric, is really just a restricted version of the same counterfactual strategy rather than a genuine rival — and that this restriction is bought at the cost of circularity, since specifying what counts as a legitimate intervention already presupposes the very causal facts the theory is meant to explain. Please argue for this claim as strongly as you can, including a fair statement of how Woodward would resist it, rather than giving a neutral summary of the two theories.
