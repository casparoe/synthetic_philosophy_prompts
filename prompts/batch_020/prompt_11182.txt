A friend and I spent most of a long train journey arguing about supertasks — the topic came up because of some reading I'd been doing for a seminar on the philosophy of infinity — and neither of us fully budged. I'd like a more disinterested opinion before the debate inevitably continues.

The disagreement started over Thomson's lamp. I've always found the standard verdict on the puzzle satisfying as a diagnosis of what goes wrong in the thought experiment: namely, that the question of whether the lamp is on or off once the two minutes are up is simply ill-posed, since the infinite sequence of flips never settles down to any limiting value. My friend thinks this "ill-posed" verdict is too quick, and invoked the Ross-Littlewood paradox (the one where balls are repeatedly added to and removed from a vase according to some fixed rule, and you're asked how many remain once the supertask is "complete") to argue that supertasks in general can yield perfectly determinate, if wildly counterintuitive, outcomes. On their view, Ross-Littlewood shows that the "no fact of the matter" verdict on the lamp isn't forced on us just because infinitely many operations occur in a finite stretch of time, so something more specific about the lamp case itself must be doing the real work of generating the indeterminacy.

Could you compare Thomson's lamp with the Ross-Littlewood vase paradox in some depth — in particular, what exactly makes one supertask resistant to a determinate final answer while the other, which relies just as heavily on completing an infinite series of discrete steps, isn't? And, since I'd like an actual verdict rather than a survey of positions: do you think my friend is right that this contrast undermines the usual moral people draw from Thomson's lamp (that supertasks as such are conceptually suspect), or is there something the lamp still shows that the vase paradox doesn't?
