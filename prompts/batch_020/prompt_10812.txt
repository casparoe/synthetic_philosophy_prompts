I have been preparing a seminar session on Gödel's modal ontological argument, and I would like to push the discussion past mere admiration of the formalism. The axioms governing "positive properties" — in the standard formulation deriving from Dana Scott's reconstruction of Gödel's notes — are, once run through S5 modal logic, well known to entail not merely the necessary existence of a God-like being but "modal collapse": the result that whatever is actually true is thereby necessarily true, so that there is, in effect, only one possible world. Could you explain, in intuitive rather than symbolic terms, why the axioms force this collapse — the property "being such that Napoleon lost at Waterloo" should serve well as an illustration — and why logicians treat the result as a genuine embarrassment for the proof, one with nothing essentially to do with theism, rather than a harmless technical curiosity? I would also value your assessment of whether the standard repairs proposed in the literature, which typically weaken the connection Gödel draws between an entity's "essence" and its necessary existence, actually block the collapse or merely relocate the same tension elsewhere in the system.
