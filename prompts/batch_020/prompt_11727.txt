After thirty years directing a research laboratory in cardiovascular physiology, I retired and have since been working through the philosophy of biology on my own, in part by auditing a seminar at the local university. The instructor, a friend, is away at a conference this week and asked me to do a first pass at grading one of the short-essay questions from the class's take-home midterm. I have never graded a philosophy essay before and do not fully trust my instincts about which points are genuinely mistaken as opposed to merely unfamiliar in their jargon, so I would welcome an independent, close reading before I pass my comments back to her.

Here is the question she set:

"Compare Larry Wright's etiological account of biological function with Robert Cummins's causal-role account. Using the heart as your example throughout, explain how each account identifies the function(s) of the heart, and explain how each account handles the possibility of malfunction. Finally, say which account, if either, can accommodate the intuition that a novel, unselected trait can already have a function at the moment it first arises."

And here is the student's answer:

"On Wright's etiological account, the function of a trait is whatever effect of that trait explains why the trait is there in the first place — roughly, what it was selected for. Applied to the heart, this means the heart's function is to pump blood, since pumping blood is the effect that explains, via natural selection, why the heart has persisted and spread through generations of organisms with circulatory systems. The heart also produces a rhythmic sound and takes up space in the chest cavity, but neither effect explains why hearts were selected, so neither counts as a function on this view.

On Cummins's causal-role account, by contrast, a trait's function is whatever it contributes to some capacity of a containing system that we happen to be interested in explaining, regardless of the trait's selective history. Relative to the circulatory system's capacity to deliver oxygen and nutrients throughout the body, the heart's function is again to pump blood, since that is the heart's causal contribution to that capacity. But because the ascription is relativized to whichever system and capacity we choose to analyze, the same heart can count as having quite different functions relative to different systems — relative to a "system" whose capacity of interest is producing an audible pulse, for instance, the heart's function would be to make noise. In this sense Cummins's account is considerably more permissive than Wright's: since it can assign a function to any effect of any trait as long as we pick a suitable system to relativize it to, it is in fact a strictly more powerful theory of function, one that subsumes everything Wright's account can explain and more.

The two accounts diverge sharply on malfunction. Wright's account handles this naturally: a heart with a defect that pumps blood poorly is still correctly said to have the function of pumping blood, because that is what hearts were selected for, so the defective heart is malfunctioning relative to that etiological function. Cummins's account struggles here, since on his view a trait's function just is its actual causal contribution to a capacity; a heart that is not contributing to circulation is not performing that causal role, so it becomes hard to say in what sense it has a function it is failing to perform, rather than simply lacking a function altogether. Cummins was aware of this gap, however, and closed it by building a statistical baseline into the theory: a part malfunctions when its actual contribution falls below the contribution made by statistically normal members of its reference class, which lets him distinguish a heart that merely lacks a capacity from one that is malfunctioning.

Finally, on novel, unselected traits: Cummins's account can assign a function to a brand-new mutant trait the moment it arises, since all that is required is that the trait actually contribute to some capacity of the system, not that it have any selective history. Wright's account, tied as it is to a trait's actual selective history, cannot assign the new trait a function until it has been selected for over some generations — which looks like a real cost of the view, since a beneficial mutation (a novel enzyme that happens to detoxify an environmental toxin the first time it appears, say) seems intuitively to already have a function in the generation it arises, well before any selection could have taken place."

Could you assign a grade out of 20, with a corresponding letter grade, and justify it with point-by-point comments against four criteria: the accuracy of the account of Wright's view, the accuracy of the account of Cummins's view, the treatment of malfunction, and the treatment of novel, unselected traits? I would find it most useful as a JSON object with one entry per criterion, each giving a score out of 5 and a short supporting comment, plus a final "overall" entry with the letter grade, the total score, and a two- or three-sentence summary comment.
