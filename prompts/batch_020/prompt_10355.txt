I'm a phil PhD student working on formal epistemology, and I TA a decision theory seminar that spent a week on Good's theorem — the result that for a Bayesian agent, the expected value of free information is never negative, since worst case you can always just act like you never saw it. Everyone in the seminar left with the slogan "information is always weakly good," which honestly seemed like a pretty settled point to me too.

Then last week I sat in on an econ talk where the speaker went through Hirshleifer's old result (I think people call it the Hirshleifer effect) about a group of farmers who can insure each other against crop failure as long as nobody knows in advance who's harvest is going to fail. Once an accurate public weather forecast becomes available, everybody learns ahead of time who the unlucky one is, the mutual insurance arrangement unravels completely, and the group ends up worse off in expectation than they would of been if the forecast never existed. The speaker was pretty explicit that this is a case of free, accurate, public information making people worse off, full stop, and kind of pitched it as a "gotcha" against the decision-theory crowd.

So which one has to give here? If Good's theorem really guarantees that a rational agent can never be hurt in expectation by cost-free information, doesn't that mean Hirshleifer's farmers (or whoever designed their insurance contracts) must be messing something up — like either they shouldn't want the forecast suppressed, or the contracts are structured wrong somehow? Or are these two results just quietly not answers to the same question, in which case what's actually doing the work in making them compatible rather than competing?
