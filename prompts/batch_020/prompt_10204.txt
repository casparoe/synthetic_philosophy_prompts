I've spent a long time reading rationalist blogs and forums, and Henry Sidgwick keeps coming up in them as a kind of patron saint of epistemic conscientiousness: a Cambridge moral philosopher famous for his rigor in ethics who nonetheless spent decades investigating mediums, apparitions, and telepathy claims as the founding president of the Society for Psychical Research, apparently out of a conviction that a scrupulous mind isn't allowed to dismiss a question just because taking it seriously looks embarrassing. A friend and I got into an argument about whether this reputation for intellectual honesty is deserved or mostly secondhand mythology, which sent me back to Sidgwick's own words rather than the usual summaries. I found the following passage in the address he delivered as the Society's first president, at its inaugural general meeting on 17 July 1882, and I've been stuck on it since:

"…every additional witness who, as De Morgan said, has a fair stock of credit to draw upon, is an important gain. Though his credit alone is not likely to suffice for the demand that is made on it, his draft will help. For we must not expect any decisive effect in the direction at which we primarily aim—on the common sense of mankind—from any single piece of evidence, however complete it has been made. Scientific incredulity has been so long in growing, and has so many and so strong roots, that we shall only kill it, if we are able to kill it at all as regards any of those questions, by burying it alive under a heap of facts. We must keep 'pegging away,' as Lincoln said; we must accumulate fact upon fact, and add experiment upon experiment, and, I should say, not wrangle too much with incredulous outsiders about the conclusiveness of any one, but trust to the mass of evidence for conviction. The highest degree of demonstrative force that we can obtain out of any single record of investigation is, of course, limited by the trustworthiness of the investigator. We have done all that we can when the critic has nothing left to allege except that the investigator is in the trick. But when he has nothing else left to allege he will allege that."

What I can't pin down is what kind of argument this actually is. Read one way, it's a statistical point: no individual report can be made fraud-proof, but if enough independent witnesses and investigators, in different households and under different conditions, all report the same kind of phenomenon, the hypothesis that fraud (or error) explains every single case becomes harder and harder to sustain, so the "heap of facts" does real cumulative work even though no single fact in it is decisive on its own. Read another way, the last two sentences look like a flat admission that the fraud objection is unfalsifiable in principle, since it's described as the one thing a critic can always fall back on regardless of the evidence—which would seem to undercut rather than support the "burying it alive" strategy, since a move that survives every increment of evidence isn't being defeated by more evidence, just deferred indefinitely. Is Sidgwick equivocating between these two pictures without quite noticing it, or is there a more rigorous version of the cumulative argument buried here—something about the shrinking joint probability of independent fabrication—that would make the two halves of the passage consistent after all? I'd appreciate help reconstructing exactly what he is and isn't entitled to conclude.
