I'm a software engineer, and in a few weeks I'm giving one of the toasts at my college roommate Dan's wedding to his fiancée, Priya Shah, who grew up in a fairly observant Jain family. Over the years "anekantavada" has become a genuine private joke between them: whenever they hit a wall arguing about something like the thermostat, whose turn it is to drive, or whether a movie was actually good, one of them eventually sighs, says "okay, anekantavada," and the argument visibly deflates. Priya says she got the word from her grandfather, Dadaji, who will be sitting in the front row and who, by her account, cannot help correcting people at family gatherings who explain his religion via the blind men and the elephant story -- he apparently regards that parable as a later, watered-down gloss that skips past what syadvada and nayavada are actually doing, and he will absolutely clock it if I lean on it as a shortcut.

I'd like the toast to use the real idea rather than the elephant version, but I'm equally worried about overcorrecting into the opposite mistake: making it sound like garden-variety relativism -- "you're both right, it's all relative, you do you" -- which, from the little I've picked up, is close to the exact misreading Jain philosophers went out of their way to head off. My own first instinct, as an engineer, was to reach for an analogy to eventual consistency in distributed systems: different nodes holding different partial, locally correct views that eventually converge on one underlying state. I suspect that quietly turns the doctrine into a claim about limited information rather than about reality itself having many genuine, simultaneously true aspects, so please only keep it if it actually survives contact with the real doctrine.

Could you write me a five-minute toast (something like 650-750 words) that puts anekantavada to real use -- doing something with the actual mechanics of conditional assertion (syadvada) and multiple standpoints (nayavada), not just the "many angles on one thing" headline -- to say something true and specific about how Dan and Priya handle disagreement, while staying warm and funny rather than turning into a lecture? The room will be around eighty people, mostly secular tech friends who've never heard any of these terms, plus a handful of relatives, including Dadaji, who will know immediately if I faked it.
