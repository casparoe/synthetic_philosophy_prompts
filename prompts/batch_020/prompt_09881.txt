I'm preparing a session for my graduate seminar on philosophical method and the relevance of biography, and I want to sharpen a comparison for the students that I haven't fully worked out to my own satisfaction, so I'd welcome your help thinking it through before I commit it to a seminar handout.

The case that interests me most is Frege's. It is now fairly well documented among historians of analytic philosophy that in 1924, the year before his death, Frege kept a private diary expressing vehement anti-Semitism, contempt for parliamentary democracy, and sympathy for authoritarian nationalist politics. The diary went essentially unremarked for decades, surfacing only long after Michael Dummett had built much of his career on rehabilitating Frege as the founding figure of analytic philosophy; by Dummett's own account, learning of its contents left him genuinely disturbed. And yet the working consensus in philosophy of logic and language has been to treat the diary as biographically troubling but doctrinally inert: virtually no one thinks the sense/reference distinction, the analysis of quantification, or the logicist reduction attempted in the Grundgesetze is in any way compromised, or even illuminated, by what Frege privately believed about Jews and democracy.

Now compare Heidegger, where the relation between the man's politics and his philosophy is handled quite differently. His 1933 Nazi party membership, the Rectoral Address, and, above all, the anti-Semitic passages that surfaced in the Black Notebooks are routinely discussed as bearing, in some contested but seriously entertained way, on the content of concepts like historicity, authenticity, das Man, and the "destiny" of a people in Being and Time - not merely on Heidegger's character. I'd like you to compare these two cases in some depth and tell me whether you think there is a principled basis for this asymmetry - some difference in the kind of philosophical work each man was doing, rather than just the differing reputational stakes attached to formal logic versus existential ontology - that would justify insulating Frege's technical philosophy from his politics while refusing to extend Heidegger the same treatment. If such a principle exists, what exactly is it? And if it doesn't, does the comparison push you toward reading Frege's logical work more suspiciously, or toward extending Heidegger more of the benefit of the doubt we currently extend to Frege?
