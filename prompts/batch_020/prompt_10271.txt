I'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology, and this term I've been auditing an evolutionary biology seminar that spent a session on ring species, which are often held up as the cleanest real-world illustration of the species problem. The textbook case is Ensatina eschscholtzii, the lungless salamander whose subspecies form a horseshoe-shaped chain of populations circling California's Central Valley: each neighboring population interbreeds readily with the next, yet by the time you follow the chain all the way around, the two populations that meet back up in the south are largely reproductively isolated from one another -- even though there is no single point anywhere along the ring where a principled line could be drawn between "one species" and "the next." Discussing this with a couple of people in my cohort afterward, what struck all of us is that the case seems to turn the Sorites paradox from a merely verbal puzzle into an empirical fact about a living population -- one you could, in principle, walk around in an afternoon.

I'd like you to write a short story that uses a ring-species-like structure as its organizing device, in the spirit of idea-driven speculative fiction that stages a genuine conceptual puzzle rather than simply illustrating it -- think of how Greg Egan's Permutation City dramatizes puzzles about the continuity of personal identity, or how Ted Chiang tends to build a story around one defamiliarizing premise and follow its consequences to some genuine human cost. You need not use salamanders, or even biological organisms at all: the structure I'd like preserved is a chain of things (populations, dialects, religious sects, generation ships, successive fine-tunings of an artificial mind -- whatever you choose) that are pairwise continuous with their immediate neighbors but discontinuous once the chain closes on itself, such that no consistent, non-arbitrary boundary can be drawn anywhere along it. Please give the story characters for whom this classification is not an academic curiosity but something with real stakes -- a legal status, an inheritance, a marriage, a war, a scientific reputation -- resting on where, if anywhere, a line gets drawn. I'm less interested in a tidy philosophical resolution than in a story that leaves the underlying discomfort of the puzzle intact.
