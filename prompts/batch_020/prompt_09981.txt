So I've been kind of stuck on this ever since my uncle was in the ICU last winter and the doctor casually said he was "brain dead" like that settled everything, even though his heart was still beating and he was warm and his chest was moving up and down (with the ventilator, obviously). It sent me down a huge rabbit hole reading about how doctors actually decide someone is dead, and it turns out there's basically two totally seperate tests that we just call by the same word. One is the brain death thing, where they check a bunch of reflexes to see if the whole brain, including the stem, has stopped working. The other gets used a lot for organ donors whose brain is fine but who are going to die soon anyway - "circulatory death" - where they just wait for the heart to stop for something like 2 to 5 minutes (it varies by hospital) and then call it, so the transplant team can start taking organs.

Here's the part that really got me: the actual law about this (the Uniform Determination of Death Act, i think its called) says the stopping has to be "irreversible," but what the circulatory death protocol is actually checking is more like "permanent" - meaning nobody is going to try to restart the heart, not that it's literally impossible to restart. And it's obviously not impossible, since people have had their hearts stopped for way longer than 5 minutes in totally different situations (like cold water drowning cases) and been revived just fine. So now I can't shake this suspicion that "death" in these two protocols isn't one single biological fact that both tests just detect through different windows - it feels more like doctors picked two convenient cutoff points (whole brain gone / heart down a few minutes with no plan to restart it) and slapped the same word "death" on both of them, because that's the word that legally lets them stop treatment or take organs, not because there's one real underlying event happening both times.
