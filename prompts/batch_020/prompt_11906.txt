I'm a retired experimental physicist (spent most of my career building detector electronics, so more late nights than I'd like debugging FPGA readout logic) and since retiring I've been trying to work through actual primary texts in philosophy rather than just pop-science summaries, which has been a nice change of pace. Right now I'm creeping through Nagarjuna's Mulamadhyamakakarika (Garfield's translation, though I gather there's a few out there) and I got stuck on the verse in the chapter examining the self were he says, roughly, that of any given thing it can be said to be existent, to be non-existent, to be both existent and non-existent, and to be neither of those two - and that this fourfold business is apparently the Buddha's own teaching, not some later add-on by Nagarjuna himself.

Here's what's bugging me. My first reaction on reading that was "oh, that's just four-valued logic" - because in my old job we dealt with signals that could be driven high (1), driven low (0), in conflict because two drivers disagreed (X), or floating with nothing driving them at all (Z), and that maps disturbingly neatly onto real / not-real / both / neither. The more I sit with it, though, the more I suspect I'm just pattern-matching my old day job onto a 2nd century Indian text, which is exactly the kind of glib "ancient wisdom anticipated my field" move I'd roll my eyes at from someone else. I mentioned it to my son (minored in philosophy, naturally) and all he offered was "welcome to Buddhist logic" before going back to his phone, so that was no help.

What I'd actually like help with is this: in the other places I've hit this same four-part move so far - the opening chapter on causation, and again later on in the chapter that puts the Buddha's own existence through the same four options - Nagarjuna goes through the options only to reject all four, not affirm them. So this "everything is real, not real, both, and neither" verse looks like it's doing the exact opposite of his usual trick. Could you walk me through what's actually going on logically in that specific verse: why it isn't just a garden-variety contradiction, whether it genuinely needs something like a paraconsistent logic to make sense of, or whether my signal-states analogy is just a red herring, and how this one verse is supposed to sit alongside all those fourfold rejections everywhere else in the book? I've also seen it suggested that you can rescue the verse by quietly inserting "conventionally" and "ultimately" in front of each of the four clauses so it isn't a flat contradiction, but that feels more like rewriting Nagarjuna than explaining him, so I'd rather that not be the whole answer if there's a better one out there.
