Question (15 pts): State Lewis's Principal Principle precisely, including the role played by "admissible evidence." Give an example. Then briefly bring in one closely related principle or objection from the literature and say whether it raises a genuinely separate problem or just collapses into the Principal Principle.

Student's answer: Lewis's Principal Principle says that your credence in a proposition A, given that you know the objective chance of A at time t is x, should equal x -- formally, C(A | Ch_t(A)=x & E) = x, so long as E is "admissible" evidence. Admissible evidence is background information that bears on A only via the chances themselves; ordinary historical facts and knowledge of the laws count as admissible, while information from something like a crystal ball or an infallible oracle that reveals the outcome directly would not, since that would license setting your credence to 0 or 1 regardless of the stated chance. E.g., if a reliable forecaster says the chance of rain tomorrow is 0.7, and I have no other special access to tomorrow's weather, I should set my own credence in rain at 0.7, since deferring to the chance is my best bet at being right.

Since chance for Lewis just is the long-run frequency with which outcomes of a given type occur, the Principal Principle is basically telling you to line your credence up with the relevant frequency whenever you happen to know it. A closely related idea, van Fraassen's Reflection Principle, says you should set your current credence in A equal to whatever credence you're already certain you'll have in A tomorrow. Since your own future opinion, once you're certain what it'll be, is playing exactly the role a chance plays, Reflection is really just a special case of the Principal Principle, so it doesn't add any genuinely new problem that the Principal Principle hasn't already handled.
