Question: "The paradox of fiction is usually set up as three individually plausible claims that can't all be true at once. (a) State the three claims. (b) Explain how Kendall Walton's make-believe theory is supposed to resolve the inconsistency, referencing his 'Charles and the slime' example. Model answer:
(a) The paradox comes from three claims that all seem true by themselves but can't all be true together: (1) we really do feel emotions like pity or fear for fictional characters and situations — we pity Anna Karenina, we're scared during a horror movie; (2) to genuinely feel an emotion about something you have to believe that thing actually exists or is happening; (3) we always know, the whole time, that fictional characters and events aren't real. If (2) and (3) are both true we shouldn't be able to feel anything for Anna Karenina at all, yet (1) says we obviously do.

(b) Kendall Walton's make-believe theory solves this by saying that when we engage with a fiction we enter a kind of game of make-believe, and inside that game we let ourselves suspend our disbelief and treat the fictional events as if they were actually happening. Walton's famous example is a man named Charles watching a horror movie with a horrible green slime oozing toward the screen: Charles grips his chair and his heart pounds because for those moments he's willingly letting himself believe the slime is real and coming for him. Walton calls the resulting fear a "quasi-emotion" because it only exists inside the pretense — once the movie ends and Charles stops playing along, the belief and the fear both disappear. This solves the paradox by rejecting premise (3): we don't actually know fiction isn't real for the entire time we're absorbed in it, only right before and right after.

(c) One objection is that this doesn't really explain why Charles's heart is actually pounding and his knuckles are actually white — those are completely real physical reactions, not just moves in a game of pretend, so if the fear itself is only pretend it's weird that his body reacts exactly like it would to real fear. I think this objection works, because Walton never really explains why a "pretend" emotion would come with genuine adrenaline — it makes "quasi-emotion" feel more like a label then an actual explanation.
