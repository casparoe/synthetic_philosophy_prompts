I'm a PhD student in philosophy — my own work is in formal epistemology, so debates about practical reason and rationality aren't really my area — but a family situation over the past year has left me stuck on a question I haven't been able to resolve on my own, and I have a nagging feeling it connects to something from a graduate seminar on reasons and rationality I sat in on a couple of years ago, though I can no longer reconstruct what. About eighteen months ago, our uncle cut off contact with the rest of the family and has shown no interest in reconciling since, despite repeated overtures from my parents and siblings; my sister and I have been arguing about it for weeks.

Her position is that our uncle "has every reason" to reconcile: family relationships are irreplaceable, he's getting older, and by his own former standards he will likely come to regret the estrangement. She treats his continued refusal to act on any of this as more or less irrational, maybe even a form of denial. When I've raised these same points with our uncle directly, though, he doesn't contest a single fact my sister cites — he simply says, quite calmly, that none of it moves him in the slightest and that he has no desire to reconcile.

My hesitation is that if literally nothing in his current outlook responds to these considerations — not because he is confused about the facts, but because he genuinely doesn't care — I am no longer sure we can truthfully say he "has" a reason to reconcile, as opposed to saying merely that we, or some improved version of him, wish that he did, or that reconciling would be better by some standard external to him. My sister sees this as a way of excusing him too easily rather than a genuine philosophical distinction. I would be grateful for your take on who is closer to being right here, and, more generally, on what you think is actually at stake in a disagreement structured like this one.
