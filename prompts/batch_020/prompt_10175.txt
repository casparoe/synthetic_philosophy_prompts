So I'm on the curriculum committee at the small liberal arts college where I teach (chemistry, for what it's worth, so I don't have a dog in this fight the way the humanities people do), and we're in the middle of a genuinely annoying fight over gen-ed reform that I could use a memo to help move along. Right now students take five courses at once each term, spread across a long list of distribution requirements (something like a dozen categories -- quant reasoning, lab science, "global cultures," etc.) so everyone graduates with broad exposure. Our new associate dean did her dissertation partly on Whitehead and wants to scrap this for a block-plan model where students take just one or two courses at a time in intensive multi-week chunks. She keeps invoking his stuff about "inert ideas" -- knowledge that just sits there because its never actually used, tested, or connected to anything else -- and his line (I'm paraphrasing, don't hold me to the exact wording) that the two real commandments of teaching are: don't cover too many subjects, and whatever you do cover, cover it properly. Her pitch is that our current five-at-once model is basically an inert-ideas factory.

Some of the senior faculty are pushing back, and not just because fewer simultaneous subjects probably means fewer sections for everyone. Their actual argument is that Whitehead's own developmental story -- the cycle he describes running from "romance" through "precision" to "generalization" -- implies you need a lot of scattered, low-commitment exposure to lots of fields before it even makes sense to narrow anyone down to one thing at a time, especially at eighteen, and that the dean is importing a claim about how to teach one subject well into an argument about how to structure an entire four-year curriculum, which isn't obviously the same thing.

Could you draft the short memo I'd actually bring to Thursday's meeting? I want it to land on a real recommendation, not "there are considerations on both sides" -- including roughly how we'd phase it in or pilot it -- and I want it to take the senior faculty's rhythm-of-education objection seriously as a piece of Whitehead exegesis, rather than just waving it off as people protecting their enrollments.
