The following exam question and student response come from an introductory course on causal inference. Grade the response out of 30 points, using the point values given for each part of the question.

Question:

A randomized controlled trial enrolls 400 patients with a certain infection and assigns them 1:1 to a new antiviral drug or to a placebo. The drug is known to sometimes elevate blood pressure (BP) as a side effect. BP status is recorded during the trial, and recovery outcomes are as follows:

- Among patients whose BP remained normal during the trial: 114 of 120 drug patients recovered (95%); 162 of 180 placebo patients recovered (90%).
- Among patients who developed elevated BP during the trial: 28 of 80 drug patients recovered (35%); 6 of 20 placebo patients recovered (30%).
- Overall: 142 of 200 drug patients recovered (71%); 168 of 200 placebo patients recovered (84%).

(a) [5 points] Verify that this is an instance of Simpson's paradox, and state briefly what makes it paradoxical.
(b) [10 points] Explain, in terms of the composition of the two treatment arms, why the reversal occurs.
(c) [15 points] Given that treatment was randomly assigned and that BP status was recorded during, not before, the trial, should a doctor deciding whether to prescribe the drug rely on the BP-stratified comparison or on the overall comparison to judge whether the drug improves a patient's chances of recovery? Justify your answer in causal terms.

Student response:

(a) This is an instance of Simpson's paradox because the drug has a higher recovery rate than placebo in both the normal-BP group (95% vs. 90%) and the elevated-BP group (35% vs. 30%), yet placebo has a higher recovery rate than the drug overall (84% vs. 71%). What makes this paradoxical is that a relationship holding in every subgroup reverses once the subgroups are combined.

(b) The reversal occurs because the two arms differ sharply in how their patients are distributed across the BP strata: 40% of drug patients (80/200) developed elevated BP, compared with only 10% of placebo patients (20/200). Since recovery is far less likely in the elevated-BP stratum than in the normal-BP stratum regardless of treatment arm (roughly 30-35% versus 90-95%), the drug arm's overall rate is dragged down much more by its worse case-mix, even though the drug performs slightly better than placebo in a like-for-like comparison within each stratum.

(c) Blood pressure status is a confounding variable here: it is correlated with treatment, since the drug causes elevated BP more often than placebo does, and it independently affects recovery. The standard remedy when a confounder produces a Simpson's-type reversal is to condition on it, so the within-stratum comparisons are the ones that control for the confound and therefore give the correct estimate of the drug's effect; the pooled figure should be set aside as confounded. Since the drug shows a higher recovery rate in both the normal-BP and elevated-BP subgroups, the doctor should conclude that the drug modestly improves a patient's chances of recovery, and that its worse showing in the pooled data is simply an artifact of case-mix imbalance that stratifying corrects for.
