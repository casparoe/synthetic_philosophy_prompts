Ok so I'm taking a philosophy of science seminar this semester and we just spent two weeks on the replication crisis, and it's spilled over into this running argument I've been having with my roommate (she's a psych PhD student). She's pretty convinced that once you actually look at the mechanics of it, a big part of the replication crisis basically dissolves if you switch from frequentist to Bayesian analysis. Her argument, which I think traces back to Wagenmakers and that whole crowd of Bayesian psychologists, is that under Neyman-Pearson style significance testing, "peeking" at your data as it comes in and stopping once you hit p < .05 inflates your false-positive rate even if your totally honest about it, whereas Bayesian updating literally cannot have this problem, because of the likelihood principle -- the posterior (or the Bayes factor) only depends on the data you actually collected, never on the stopping rule you used to collect it. So her conclusion is that optional stopping, and by extension a good chunk of the replication crisis, is really just an artifact of the frequentist paradigm, and would basically evaporate if the field fully switched over to Bayesian methods.

I find this pretty convincing, honestly, to the point where I'm tempted to build my term paper around the thesis that the replication crisis is a frequentist problem with a Bayesian solution. But before I commit to that, I wanted a sanity check: is she basically right that this is the fix, or is there some reason switching statistical paradigms wouldn't actually get the field out of the woods?
