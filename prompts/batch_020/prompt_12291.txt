My friend and I spent most of Sunday arguing about Molinism and its doctrine of middle knowledge. She defended it as the only view that lets God providentially order history down to the last detail without turning anyone into a puppet: because his middle knowledge already contains every counterfactual of freedom — what each possible person would freely do in each possible circumstance — he can choose which world to actualize without ever causing a single choice. I kept pushing the grounding objection back at her: before any decision to create, what could make it true that a merely possible person would do one thing rather than another in circumstances that may never obtain? Not that person's nature, or the choice isn't really free; not God's will, or it isn't really theirs; nothing in the actual world, since by hypothesis none of it may ever become actual. Neither of us budged. I'd like a short story, in the spirit of writers like Borges or Ted Chiang who treat metaphysical puzzles as lived predicaments rather than lectures, that dramatizes this specific bind rather than the generic predestination-versus-free-will debate: a character who discovers that their whole history was selected from a menu of "feasible worlds" on the strength of such counterfactuals, and who has to live with the fact that nothing grounded what they would freely do before there was anyone for the counterfactual to be about. Keep the theological machinery — natural, middle, and free knowledge, and the logical rather than temporal priority of middle knowledge to the divine decree — precise enough that a philosopher of religion would recognize it, but write an actual story with stakes for the character, not a dialogue wearing a plot as a costume.
