This term I'm taking an epistemology seminar that has spent the last few weeks working through the regress problem, and there's a comment from class I wanted to ask about during office hours but never got the chance to. We had just finished contrasting coherentism and infinitism as two ways of resisting foundationalism when the professor remarked, almost in passing, that the two theories "pay for their rejection of foundationalism in very different currencies." I can restate the core commitments of each view well enough for an exam, but I don't think I've actually understood what that remark was getting at, and none of the assigned readings spell it out directly.

Could you compare coherentism (something like the version Laurence BonJour defended in The Structure of Empirical Knowledge, before he reportedly abandoned it in his later work) with Peter Klein's infinitism, treating them specifically as two rival responses to Agrippa's trilemma? I'm especially interested in how each theory handles the objection that seems tailor-made for it: coherentism against the charge that a perfectly coherent web of beliefs could still be entirely cut off from the world it's supposed to represent, and infinitism against the charge that no finite human mind could ever traverse, or even possess, an infinite chain of justifying reasons. Given how each theory answers its respective objection, which one do you think gives up less in order to escape the regress, and does either of them end up quietly reintroducing something that functions like a foundational, non-inferentially justified belief after all?
