I'm drafting a chapter on the afterlife of logical positivism and keep coming back to the strangest episode in A. J. Ayer's biography. In June 1988 Ayer choked on a piece of smoked salmon, went into cardiac arrest, and was, by his doctors' estimate, clinically dead for four minutes. The article he wrote about it that August described a red light he took to be governing the universe through two negligent "ministers" in charge of space, and a river he would later connect to the Styx; he had wanted to title the piece "That Undiscovered Country," but his editors ran it as "What I Saw When I Was Dead."

What I keep returning to is the postscript he felt compelled to publish seven weeks later. He insisted that his own sentence describing the experience as having "slightly weakened" his conviction that death would be the end of him was, in his phrase, "literally false as it stands": his estimate of the odds of survival hadn't moved a fraction. What had actually shifted, he wrote, was not the belief but his attitude toward examining it — he had gone from debunking survival hypotheses on principle to being willing to entertain them for their own sake, all while still favoring the mundane explanation that his brain simply hadn't stopped firing during those four minutes.

Write a short story of roughly 1000 words that dramatizes that fine, almost pedantic distinction — a philosopher (Ayer himself, or an invented stand-in) who has just had an overwhelming experience, offers everyone a deflationary causal account of it, and still has to fight, clause by clause, to stop the people around him from hearing a bigger admission than the one he is actually willing to make.
