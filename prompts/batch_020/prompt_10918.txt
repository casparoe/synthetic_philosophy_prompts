I'm a philosophy PhD student currently drafting a dissertation chapter on Henry Sidgwick's "dualism of practical reason" — the argument, in the concluding pages of The Methods of Ethics, that the axioms of rational egoism and of rational benevolence are each individually self-evident and yet can conflict in practice; unless the two always coincide, Sidgwick warns, "the Cosmos of Duty is thus really reduced to a Chaos." Until my advisor pointed me toward Bart Schultz's biography of Sidgwick, Eye of the Universe, I had mostly treated this as a technical problem internal to normative theory. Now I can't stop thinking about the fact that the same man spent nearly two decades, as a founding member and the first president of the Society for Psychical Research, investigating mediums, apparitions, and reports of telepathy — an enterprise that several of his biographers and commentators read as an extension of his search for the kind of theistic postulate that might close the very gap his ethics had opened up. This sits oddly next to the fact that he was also the philosopher who, thirteen years before the Society was even founded, had resigned his Trinity fellowship rather than go on subscribing, against his conscience, to the Thirty-Nine Articles.

I have to present some version of this material at a work-in-progress seminar soon, and I keep going back and forth on how seriously to take the connection, so I'd like to think it through as a dialogue first. Could you write me a conversation between two philosophers, equally sharp and neither one a pushover, who disagree about how to interpret this side of Sidgwick's career? One should argue that turning to psychical research was, given Sidgwick's own premises, a rational and even admirable way of trying to close the gap in his ethics empirically rather than by fiat or leap of faith. The other should argue that it was a mistake — whether because no empirical discovery about survival or an afterlife could really settle what is at bottom a normative question about the authority of practical reason, or because the whole enterprise looks more like a search for a substitute certainty than like disinterested inquiry. I want the two to genuinely engage each other rather than talk past one another: the defender should have to explain why facts about an afterlife would bear on a question of practical reason at all, and the critic should have to reckon with just how methodologically careful, and often skeptical, Sidgwick and his circle actually were, including their well-documented readiness to expose fraudulent mediums and reach negative conclusions.
