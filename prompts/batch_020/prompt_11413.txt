Last year I was injured at work when a new type of automated equipment my employer had just started using malfunctioned, and I am now trying to decide whether to accept a settlement offer from the manufacturer or go ahead with a product liability lawsuit against them. My attorney has been candid that, because this particular technology is so new, nothing quite like this failure has ever been litigated in our state, so there is no real precedent or comparable case history to draw on. When I have asked a few different lawyers for their honest read on my odds of winning at trial, I have gotten answers ranging from roughly one chance in five to better than a coin flip, without anything like a consensus among them. What strikes me most is that even averaging those guesses into some middle figure would not really capture my situation, since a lawyer who genuinely has no idea and shrugs "fifty-fifty" seems to be in a completely different position from one who has carefully weighed strong arguments on both sides and lands on "fifty-fifty" as a considered judgment, even though they would report the exact same number.

My brother-in-law, who works in insurance and is very comfortable thinking in numbers, insists that none of this matters: I should still settle on my own best-guess probability of winning, multiply it by what I would stand to gain at trial, and compare that to the settlement offer, taking whichever option comes out ahead. When I told him that I honestly do not feel I have a specific number in me, only a sense that it could plausibly be almost anywhere across that whole range with no way to narrow it down further, he said that this is not a real position but a refusal to think things through properly. He added that if I truly could never be pinned to one figure, I would end up making inconsistent choices without even realizing it, such as turning down the settlement today while probably accepting a slightly worse one tomorrow, for no good reason. To him, this shows that somewhere underneath my hesitation there has to be one exact number, whether or not I can consciously name it, and that a rational person is obliged to dig it out and use it.

I am not sure he is wrong, exactly, but this has been nagging at me for weeks and something about it still does not sit right. Is my brother-in-law correct that rationality requires collapsing my uncertainty into one precise probability before I can decide properly — or is it legitimate, maybe even more honest, to say that the true state of my knowledge here is a range rather than a single number, and to make the decision on some other basis?
