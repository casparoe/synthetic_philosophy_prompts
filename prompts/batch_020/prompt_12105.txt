I spent almost 40 years as a research chemist before retiring, and I've been using the free time to finally read some philosophy, which is how I stumbled into this whole fight between Nicholas Stern and William Nordhaus over what discount rate you're supposed to use when you do cost-benefit analysis on climate change -- Stern basically wants somthing close to zero, Nordhaus thinks the rate should track what markets actually do. What I didn't realize until I dug a bit further is that the math underneath both of their models goes back to Frank Ramsey's 1928 paper on saving, and that Ramsey himself apparently argued that discounting someone's wellbeing purely because it happens later in time is indefensible -- though he was fine with a discount rate that reflects things like the chance nobody will be around later to enjoy it. Ramsey died in 1930, decades before anyone was doing this kind of climate modelling. So here's my question: if Ramsey had somehow lived to see the Stern-Nordhaus fight play out, which side would his own actual reasoning put him closer to, and why? I'd like the answer grounded in what he really argued about time preference, not just a guess at what a clever mathematician probably would've thought.
