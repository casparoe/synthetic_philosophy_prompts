Over the past year I've been working through effective altruism and longtermist writing entirely on my own -- I have no formal background in philosophy or decision theory, just a habit of following citation trails from one blog post or forum thread to the next. I recently got stuck trying to pull apart two ideas that keep turning up in the same discussions: "acausal trade" and "evidential cooperation in large worlds" (which, as far as I can tell, grew out of something earlier called "multiverse-wide superrationality"). Both are supposed to involve agents who can never causally affect one another cooperating anyway, and some of what I've read treats evidential cooperation as basically a special case of acausal trade, while other pieces present it as a more modest, separate proposal that sidesteps some of acausal trade's baggage. I can't tell whether that's a real distinction or just two overlapping communities using different vocabulary for the same underlying move.

Could you compare the two in some depth, rather than defining them one after the other? I'm especially interested in three things. First, what each idea actually requires in order to work: my impression is that the classic acausal-trade scenarios -- one AI reasoning about deterring, or striking a bargain with, another AI it can never causally interact with -- depend on each side being able to model the other in fairly specific detail, and on something like an updateless or functional decision theory to make sense of that; whereas evidential cooperation is supposed to go through on evidential decision theory alone, purely from the statistical fact that a sufficiently large or infinite world will contain many agents with dispositions similar enough to yours. Second, whether that difference actually buys evidential cooperation anything: does dropping the mutual-modeling requirement let ordinary human moral reasoners extract real, action-guiding conclusions from acausal cooperation, or does the "large world" move just relocate the hard problems -- fixing the right reference class of "similar enough" agents, the fact that the cooperation can never be confirmed even in principle -- rather than solving them? Third, and this is the part I most want your honest opinion on: which of the two strikes you as the more defensible basis for treating agents you will never observe, reach, or identify as having a genuine claim on how you act, and why? I'd rather have an actual verdict, even a tentative one, than an even-handed summary of considerations on both sides.
