Last night, a friend and I argued for hours over the "commitment races problem" from the decision-theory and AI-safety literature: agents able to make credible commitments come under pressure to lock in a bargaining stance as early as possible, before they have properly reasoned the matter through, because in these models earliness rather than soundness of judgment confers the advantage. My friend's position was that this is just Schelling-style brinkmanship in new vocabulary, and that calling one agent's commitment "logically prior" to another's adds nothing that ordinary chronological precedence did not already capture. I would like to go back better armed. Could you explain, for someone comfortable with game theory but new to this specific literature, why earliness rather than quality of reasoning becomes the scarce resource here; what work the notion of "logical time" is doing that ordinary temporal sequencing cannot, especially for agents that can inspect each other's source code rather than just past actions; and why this is usually treated as a distinctive danger of advanced AI rather than brinkmanship that human institutions already manage? I want the part of the argument that is actually novel, not just the observation that commitment is powerful in bargaining.
