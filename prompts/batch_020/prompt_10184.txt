As a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, I've been reading about Hempel's raven paradox in my spare time, and I'm comfortable with the standard resolution: since "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to its contrapositive, a non-black non-raven (a red herring, say) technically confirms the hypothesis too, just by an amount too small to notice, given how vastly non-ravens outnumber ravens. What I can't get past is I. J. Good's 1967 "white shoe is a red herring" counterexample, which compares two possible worlds — one with 100 black ravens and a million other birds, the other with 1,000 black ravens, one white raven, and a million other birds — and shows that drawing a random bird and finding it to be a black raven is actually evidence for the second world, where not all ravens are black. Hempel replied that this smuggles in contingent background assumptions the original puzzle was never meant to depend on, and the debate over whether Good's move really works doesn't seem to have been settled even decades later. Could you walk me through why Good's setup flips the expected direction of confirmation, explain Hempel's background-dependence objection to it, and give me your own sense of what a decisive, background-free version of Good's challenge would have to show — and whether you think such a version is even possible?
