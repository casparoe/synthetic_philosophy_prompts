Last night a friend and I ended up in a two-hour argument that started from a stray footnote in a biography of Kurt Gödel and turned into a real disagreement about method: whether Gödel's private conviction that there is an afterlife amounts to a piece of philosophy worth taking seriously, or whether it is just the wishful thinking of a brilliant man later undone by paranoia. My friend's line was that the whole case collapses once you notice it is really an argument from consequences dressed up in the vocabulary of Leibnizian rationalism: it would be fitting if a rationally ordered world guaranteed that a fully developed mind and character survive death, therefore it does. I pushed back by pointing to the actual structure of what Gödel says in his letters to his mother and in the remarks Hao Wang recorded from their conversations — the idea that a world governed by reason would not produce something as elaborate as a mature human personality only to annihilate it the moment it finishes developing, plus the further, stranger theses Wang reports him holding about other worlds or further lives beyond this one. Neither of us convinced the other, and I would like to keep pressing on the question in a more structured way than we managed last night.

I run a small podcast on the history of logic and its philosophical offshoots, and I have invited a historian of philosophy onto next month's episode: someone who has spent years working through Gödel's unpublished "Max Phil" notebooks and correspondence, and who argues in her forthcoming book that his case for personal survival deserves to be read as a serious, if unfinished, piece of rationalist metaphysics continuous with his Platonism about mathematical objects, rather than dismissed as a biographical curiosity. I do not want the episode to consist of her simply restating the position for listeners, so I need questions that actually press on it. Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for this conversation? Please include at least one question on whether the inference from "the world is rationally constructed" to "there is an afterlife" smuggles in something like a principle of sufficient reason that most contemporary philosophers would reject outright, one on whether the "wasted development" idea is really an argument for the truth of an afterlife rather than for its desirability, one on how (if at all) this private argument relates to Gödel's separate and more formal ontological proof of God's existence, one on whether his anti-mechanist reading of the incompleteness theorems — the claim that the mind is not simply a finite machine — is doing real argumentative work here or is being retrofitted to support a conclusion he wanted on independent grounds, and one that puts to her directly the objection that his later paranoid delusions should make us discount the rest of his rationalist convictions. For each question, please add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so that a well-rehearsed response does not simply end the exchange.
