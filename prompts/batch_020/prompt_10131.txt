So I'm about a third of the way into my new novel, which has a courtroom spine to it — a slow-burn legal thriller where the emotional center is the sentencing hearing for a guy who killed a teenage girl in a hit-and-run. I've written a long back-and-forth between the judge, who's quietly a hardline retributivist, and the defense's expert witness, a prison psychologist who keeps steering everything toward deterrence and rehabilitation instead. My writing group liked the scene's energy, but one guy who did a philosophy minor in college said the judge's position reads like a strawman ("vengeance dressed up in Latin," he called it), and that I'm not giving the consequentialist side its best shot either. Now I'm a little paranoid the whole exchange is philosophically embarassing. (Yes, I know real judges don't usually monologue like this from the bench, but there's a running bit earlier in the book about him being a failed philosophy academic before law school, so I'm hoping it's earned.)

Here's roughly the shape of the judge's speech, since I don't want to paste four pages at you: he opens with a version of Kant's bit about the island — you'd still have to execute the last murderer even as the community disbands, because justice is owed to the dead girl, not to some future the punishment is supposed to buy. Then he goes after the psychologist by raising the classic "frame an innocent man to stop a riot" problem and treats it as basically fatal to consequentialism all on its own. He closes by admitting he can't say exactly how many years her death is "worth," but insists that not being able to measure desert precisely doesn't mean it isn't real. The psychologist's comeback is thinner, mostly just "rehabilitation works, look at Norway," which is probably the weak link my friend was reacting to.

Could you read that over and give me some honest comments? Mainly I want to know whether the judge's argument is a genuinely sophisticated version of retributivism or whether it's leaning on the frame-up case in a way careful consequentialists dealt with ages ago, and if so, what's the strongest reply the psychologist should be making that I'm just not thinking of. I also picked up the term "the gap problem" while poking around online and I'm not sure if it's actually relevant here or if I'm just borrowing jargon I don't fully understand. I'm not after a philosophy lecture, I just want the scene to read like two people who've actually done the reading, not two guys reciting talking points at each other.
