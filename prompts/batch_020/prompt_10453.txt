I teach a graduate seminar on the philosophy of emotion, which is presumably why two colleagues of mine — Renata, who works on moral psychology, and Simon, who works on philosophy of perception — insisted I referee an argument that broke out over drinks after a workshop last month. The case under dispute involved a mutual friend of theirs, a structural engineer, who reports genuine fear — racing heart, white knuckles on the rail — while standing on the glass Skywalk over the Grand Canyon, despite being able to recite from memory the load tolerances showing that the structure could hold many times the weight it will ever bear. Renata and Simon disagreed about what this shows regarding whether emotions are judgments, perceptions, or feelings, and I've reconstructed their exchange below as fairly as I can, though neither struck me as an especially unbiased source afterward.

(1) Renata argued that the case refutes judgment theories of emotion outright: since the engineer's fear coexists with, and is entirely unmoved by, her confident belief that she is safe, fear cannot be, or require, a judgment that she is in danger — at most it is a feeling, or a perceptual state running on a separate track from belief. (2) Simon replied that this only refutes a crude cognitivism; no sophisticated judgment theorist identifies emotion with a fully reflective, all-things-considered belief. On this view, fear is an evaluative construal that the situation is dangerous, held apart from what one believes on reflection — exactly as one can see two Müller-Lyer lines as unequal in length while judging them to be equal, without concluding that seeing has nothing to do with judgment. The engineer's fear, on this view, just is such a construal, coexisting with her better judgment the way the illusion coexists with hers about the lines. (3) Renata countered that this saves the letter of judgmentalism while abandoning its substance: if the construal is, by hypothesis, insulated from her reasoning in a way that a real judgment never is, then Simon has merely relabeled the perceptual theorist's danger-representation and conceded the point at issue. (4) Simon answered that the perceptual analogy cannot be what Renata wants either, for the opposite reason: illusions are famously immune to correction, since no amount of familiarity with the actual lengths makes the Müller-Lyer lines look equal, whereas fears of exactly this kind are routinely extinguished through graded exposure or a change of context — so emotions answer to evidence in a way genuine perceptual illusions do not. Neither budged, and both now claim I owe them a verdict at the next faculty meeting.

I'd like a more considered answer than I managed on the spot. Could you work through moves (1) through (4) in order, judging each on its own merits rather than jumping ahead to who "won," and say in particular whether Simon's positions in (2) and (4) are consistent with one another? Then tell me, on balance, whether the Grand Canyon case still supports Renata's original anti-judgmentalist conclusion, or whether Simon has the resources to save some version of a judgment or construal theory of fear — and flag anything relevant to the underlying judgments-versus-perceptions-versus-feelings debate that you think they are both overlooking.
