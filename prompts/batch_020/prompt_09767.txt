so i went down a rabbit hole on "money pump" arguments -- the setup where cyclic preferences (you like A more than B, B more than C, but then C more than A) let someone trade you around the loop and skim money off you every lap. as a programmer my instinct was: this is just a negative cycle, so why not run a backward-induction pass over the whole decision tree up front and refuse whatever trade starts the loop, like pruning a bad branch before you even search into it? apparently that's basically "sophisticated" choice, and from what i can tell even McClennen (who i think came up with the alternative to it) admits it gets you out of the basic pump.

but people still treat cyclic preferences as a real problem and reach for something called "resolute" choice instead -- commit to a plan up front and actually follow through later even when, taken on its own, the next step looks like a loser. that feels like smuggling irrationality in the back door rather then solving anything. can you explain why plain lookahead doesn't just settle this whole debate, and what's supposed to be genuinely rational about resolute choice instead of it just being "precommit now and then override your own judgement later whenever its inconvenient"?
