The assignment question:

"Christensen's restaurant-bill case—a group of friends each do the mental arithmetic for splitting a check and arrive at different totals—is often used to motivate conciliationism about peer disagreement. (a) Explain what Elga's Equal Weight View recommends in a case like this, and why the case seems to support it. (b) Explain the 'independence condition' on judging whether someone counts as a genuine epistemic peer, and why violating it would make conciliationism trivial or question-begging. (c) State one influential objection to conciliationism and sketch how a conciliationist might respond to it."

My classmate's answer:

"(a) The Equal Weight View holds that once you learn a genuine epistemic peer disagrees with you, their opinion counts as evidence just as good as your own, so you should average your credence with theirs. The restaurant case fits this well: everyone did the same arithmetic on the same information, and nobody has any special reason to think their own math is more trustworthy than anyone else's. So the rational move for each diner is to shift toward the midpoint between their own total and the others', rather than assuming they alone got it right.

(b) The independence condition says that for two people's disagreement to matter, they have to have reached their opinions independently of one another—neither should have known what the other thought before settling on an answer. Without this requirement, conciliationism would be toothless, since you could always insist afterward that you'd secretly agreed with your peer the whole time, leaving no real disagreement to conciliate about.

(c) A well-known objection is that conciliationism seems to undercut itself: it's a substantive philosophical claim that many careful, competent philosophers reject, so by its own lights its defenders should lower their confidence in it once they notice that disagreement. A conciliationist can reply that this only shows conciliationism should be held with somewhat less confidence, not that it must be abandoned outright—lowering your credence in a view because peers reject it isn't the same as being rationally forced to give it up."
