"Herbert Simon pointed out that real people can't be perfectly rational the way classical economics assumes, since we don't have unlimited time, information, or mental processing power to weigh every possible option. Instead of optimizing — finding the objectively best choice — we satisfice: we set an aspiration level for what counts as 'good enough' and go with the first option that clears that bar rather than exhaustively searching for the best one. This is what he called bounded rationality.

Simon later took this idea into ethics with his theory of satisficing consequentialism, which holds that an act is morally permissible as long as its consequences are good enough, even if a better act was available to the agent. This is meant as an alternative to standard (maximizing) consequentialism, which says only the single best available act is right — a view a lot of people think is too demanding, since it would require constant self-sacrifice just to squeeze out the marginal bit of extra good. Because Simon had already shown that even rational agents don't optimize, satisficing consequentialism basically just extends that insight to morality: if its rational to satisfice, it should be morally fine to satisfice too. And since the theory only asks that you clear a threshold, it neatly gets consequentialism out of the demandingness objection without creating any comparable problems of its own."
