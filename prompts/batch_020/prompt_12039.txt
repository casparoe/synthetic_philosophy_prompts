I am reporting on a long-running dispute between Kelly-criterion advocates and their critics, and I would like to get past the popular gloss on both sides. Proponents of the Kelly criterion argue that maximizing expected log wealth is the uniquely rational bet size, since, given enough repetitions, it will almost surely outperform any other strategy — yet betting more aggressively, such as staking one's entire bankroll on every favorable wager, actually yields a higher expected value, even though it drives the probability of eventual ruin toward certainty. Paul Samuelson seized on precisely this gap, arguing that treating "maximize long-run growth" as a free-standing, utility-independent rationality principle merely smuggles in logarithmic utility under another name. More recently, the "ergodicity economics" camp has tried to reframe the same growth-rate criterion not as a matter of risk preference but as a correction to the illegitimate practice of averaging over an ensemble of parallel possible worlds rather than over time. Could you explain, in intuitive terms, how expected-value maximization and growth-rate maximization manage to pull apart so sharply in cases like this, and whether the ergodicity framing genuinely escapes Samuelson's objection or simply restates it in the vocabulary of statistical physics?
