I keep noticing a move in effective altruist and rationalist writing that I don't think gets scrutinized enough: people invoke evolutionary debunking arguments, in the style of Sharon Street's Darwinian Dilemma, to discount parochial moral intuitions—favoring kin, tribe, or the present generation—as better explained by what Street calls an "adaptive link account" (the disposition was fitness-enhancing regardless of whether its content was true) than by a "tracking account" on which our ancestors were actually detecting independent evaluative facts. Yet the same style of explanation seems just as available for the impartial, aggregative intuitions that cause-prioritization work depends on (that a benefit's moral weight doesn't depend on who receives it, that more welfare is better than less), and Street intended her dilemma as a fully general argument against realism, with no built-in exemption for whichever intuitions happen to be impartial rather than parochial. Please describe the most important implications of this for how much epistemic weight I should give my own impartial, scope-sensitive intuitions when they are doing load-bearing work in cause-prioritization reasoning, and assess whether Katarzyna de Lazari-Radek and Peter Singer's Sidgwickian attempt to save axioms such as impartiality and temporal neutrality—by treating them as byproducts of a general rational capacity rather than direct targets of selection—genuinely escapes the dilemma, or merely relocates the same evidential burden one level up.
