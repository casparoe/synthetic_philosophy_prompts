My aunt Miriam died three weeks ago, ten months after a pancreatic cancer diagnosis she met with what her colleagues kept calling "admirable consistency." She spent thirty-five years as a historian of early modern philosophy with a particular devotion to Leibniz, and "this is the best of all possible worlds" had become something of a family catchphrase of hers — delivered, however, with real irritation whenever anyone treated it as shorthand for "everything works out" or compared her to Voltaire's Dr. Pangloss. Over many holiday dinners I picked up fragments of her actual position: that the relevant comparison was never between this world and some fantasy with the suffering edited out, because most of the alternatives we casually imagine were never really on offer as coherent packages — whole bundles of things, good and bad together, either fit together into a single possible world or they don't, a relation she called "compossibility," which, as a software engineer, I have always privately (and probably wrongly) translated into something like a constraint-satisfaction problem. She also insisted that God's choosing the best world didn't make the choice any less free, since the necessity involved was "moral" rather than strictly logical, and she distinguished three kinds of evil in Leibniz's scheme — metaphysical, physical, and moral — with the metaphysical kind, the built-in imperfection of anything that isn't God, doing most of the real work, since it was what made the other two possible in the first place.

I've been asked to deliver her eulogy at the memorial service, which will seat two of her former doctoral students and most of her department across the aisle from my grandmother, my cousins, and the six women from her book club, essentially none of whom has read a word of the Theodicy. I have no philosophical training myself beyond what leaked in at that dinner table, and I'm worried I can only manage one of two failure modes: a generic, comforting eulogy that says exactly the kind of thing she spent her career arguing against, or an accurate one that sounds like a conference paper and loses everyone but the philosophers. Could you write the eulogy for me? It should run about five minutes read aloud, it needs to genuinely comfort people who are grieving and have no interest in metaphysics, it cannot quietly imply that her death was itself "for the best" in the cheap sense she rejected, and it still has to be recognizable, to the people in the second row who wrote their dissertations on her, as a eulogy for someone who really did hold, in a carefully specific sense, that this is the best of all possible worlds.
