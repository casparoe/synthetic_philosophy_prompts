I spent about thirty-five years doing experimental condensed-matter physics before I retired, and these days, mostly to keep my brain from turning to mush, I audit whatever intro philosophy courses the local university will let a retiree sit in on for free. Last term it was epistemology, and the Gettier problem genuinely rattled me -- the idea that you can have a justified true belief that still doesn't count as knowledge, because the justification and the truth turn out to be connected by nothing but luck. My wife thinks Ive completely lost it, but I've been seeing Gettier-shaped things everywhere since, including, awkwardly, in a folder that landed on my desk last week.

I still sit on the prize committee for my old professional society's early-career award, mostly because nobody younger wants to read fourty nomination packets every spring. This year's strongest nominee built a mean-field model of a magnetic phase transition, used it to predict a critical temperature, and that number was later confirmed independently by two different experimental groups to within their error bars -- exactly the track record the award exists to recognize. But one committee member who actually works in the subfield dug into the supplementary material and found that the original derivation had dropped a next-nearest-neighbour coupling term it should have kept. Separately, a coding slip in how the numerical fit was implemented happened to cancel that omission almost exactly, but only over the narrow parameter range relevant to this particular material. So the nominee had good, field-standard reasons to believe the prediction, and the prediction was true, but it looks a lot like an accident that it came out true rather than false -- nudge either mistake slightly and the number's wrong.

Half the committee says the award recognizes results, full stop, and a correct result doesn't get worse for having been reached by a route we'd now call flawed; the other half says this is exactly the sort of case the award should not reward, and that giving it anyway blurs being right with being lucky. I have to circulate a short memo before we vote in three weeks, recommending we award it outright, award it with some acknowledgement of the issue attached, or hold it over a year pending a clean re-derivation. Could you draft that memo for me -- something that actually lands on one of those options and gives reasons the "a result is a result" camp would have to take seriously, rather than just asserting that luck doesn't count?
