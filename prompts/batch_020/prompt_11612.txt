I've spent the past few weeks reporting a feature on the philosophy of free will, and one thread of that reporting keeps leading me back to Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument" against ultimate moral responsibility — an argument that seems to unsettle almost everyone who first works through it. A philosophy lecturer who has been a generous source for the piece let me look at one of her seminar assessment questions along with a real, anonymized student submission, and half-jokingly challenged me to see whether an AI system, working blind and without her answer key, would grade it the way she did. I'd like to take her up on that.

Here is the question, exactly as she set it: "Galen Strawson claims that no one is ever 'truly' or 'ultimately' morally responsible for what they do, whether or not determinism is true. (a) Reconstruct his Basic Argument in premise-and-conclusion form, explaining the role played by the regress concerning one's 'motivational structure' — roughly, one's character, values, and desires. (b) Strawson is explicit that the argument's force does not depend on the truth of determinism. Explain why he says this, and what it is meant to show about the argument relative to more familiar hard-determinist appeals to causal luck. (c) Present, in a few sentences, one serious response to the Basic Argument from the philosophical literature."

And here is the student's answer, transcribed exactly as submitted: "(a) The Basic Argument is meant to show that 'ultimate' moral responsibility — responsibility in the strong sense required for genuine desert — is impossible for anyone. It can be set out like this: (1) Whatever you do, you do because of the way you are — because of your beliefs, desires, and other elements of your 'motivational structure.' (2) To be truly responsible for what you do, you would have to be truly responsible for being the way you are, at least in the mental respects that matter. (3) But you cannot be truly responsible for being the way you are. Being responsible for your present motivational structure would require having chosen it on the basis of some earlier motivational structure — but that earlier structure would itself need to have been chosen, and so on. This generates a regress that cannot be completed, since there is no earlier point at which you already existed as a chooser but had not yet been shaped by anything. (4) Therefore, no one is ever truly or ultimately responsible for what they do. (b) Strawson thinks this result holds regardless of determinism because the regress argument in (3) is really just a sharper version of the standard hard-determinist point about causal luck: if determinism is true, your character is simply the last link in a chain of causes reaching back before your birth, and since you didn't choose those distal causes, you can't be truly responsible for what they produce in you. The Basic Argument is best understood as this causal-luck argument pushed to its natural conclusion, which is also why Strawson takes it to be effectively unanswerable. (c) One serious response comes from Strawson himself, who in 'Freedom and Resentment' argued that moral responsibility does not require this kind of ultimate self-authorship at all. On his view, our practices of blame and praise are grounded in reactive attitudes like resentment and gratitude, attitudes we are not going to give up no matter what the metaphysicians conclude — so ordinary responsibility survives even if 'ultimate' responsibility turns out to be a myth."

These arguments have a way of sounding authoritative even when a detail underneath them has slipped, so I'd especially value your flagging anything of that kind.
