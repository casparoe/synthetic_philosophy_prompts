A friend and I spent much of an evening arguing about how original George Eliot's treatment of religion really is. She published an English translation of Feuerbach's *The Essence of Christianity* in 1854, not long before she turned to fiction, and my friend maintained that everything she subsequently did with religious feeling and dogma — from her polemical essay on the preacher Dr. Cumming to the closing pages of *Middlemarch* — is essentially Feuerbach's method of unmasking the predicates of God as humanity's own alienated powers, worked out in narrative form. I argued that her fiction holds onto something his philosophy has no real room for: a tragic sense of what is lost when dogma dies, together with a respect for inherited custom and habitual feeling that owes more to Comte than to Feuerbach's rationalist anthropology. Neither of us could convince the other. Compare Feuerbach's account of religion as humanity's self-alienated essence with Eliot's own treatment of religious belief and feeling. In which respects, if any, does her position genuinely go beyond his, and in which respects is my friend right that she is, at bottom, Feuerbach dramatized? Please justify your answer with reference to specific concepts from Feuerbach's book and specific moments in Eliot's own essays or fiction.
