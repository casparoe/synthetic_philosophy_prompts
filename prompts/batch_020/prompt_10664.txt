I'm running a graduate seminar on social ontology this term, and last week we worked through David Lewis's *Convention*. I'd like some help sharpening a comparison for the next session, which turns to the game-theoretic literature on the evolution of norms, before I finalize the discussion questions for my students.

Lewis analyzes a convention as a regularity in action that solves a recurring coordination problem: everyone conforms, everyone expects everyone else to conform, everyone prefers to conform given that others do, some alternative regularity could equally well have served the same purpose, and — crucially — all of this is common knowledge among the population, a notion Lewis essentially introduces into philosophy for this purpose. My students always balk at that last clause: it seems to commit Lewis to crediting ordinary convention-followers with an infinite hierarchy of higher-order beliefs — I expect you to conform, I expect you to expect me to conform, and so on — that no finite mind actually entertains.

Brian Skyrms's evolutionary treatment of the same subject matter looks like a rival strategy for reaching the same destination at a fraction of the cognitive cost. On his account, the very same coordination regularities — including Lewis's own signaling conventions — can be driven to fixation by replicator dynamics or simple reinforcement learning, where no party represents anyone else's beliefs at all, let alone achieves common knowledge of them; a shared history of interaction and selection does the work that Lewis assigns to shared cognition. I'd like you to compare the two explanatory strategies in some detail: does Skyrms's dynamical story actually reproduce everything Lewis's account was designed to explain — the arbitrariness of the equilibrium selected, the self-perpetuating expectations that sustain it, the sense in which conforming parties are following a convention rather than each merely doing what happens to be individually best — or does something get lost once common knowledge drops out of the picture? I am particularly interested in whether an evolved regularity can ever be a norm in the sense that carries genuine normative force, something one can be faulted for violating, or whether that normative residue is only available once Lewis's mutual, common-knowledge-backed expectations are in place. Tell me which account is doing more of the real explanatory work in your assessment, and whether the two are ultimately competitors or could be combined so that each covers a different part of the phenomenon.
