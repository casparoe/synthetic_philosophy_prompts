I'm the president of my high school's philosophy club, and this Saturday we're hosting a philosophy professor from the state university who's agreed to come run a workshop for us on free will and moral responsibility. Our faculty advisor asked me to open with about five minutes of remarks before the professor takes over, and the audience is going to be a real mix: most of the thirty or so people coming have never read any philosophy before, a handful of club regulars did the pre-reading I assigned, and the professor herself will be sitting right there listening to how I set up her own talk. I started this club last year after getting into these debates on my own, and this is the first time we've had an actual academic come speak to us, so I really don't want to embarrass the club in front of her.

The pre-reading was the part of Frankfurt's 1969 paper where he goes after the principle of alternate possibilities (PAP) — the idea that you're only morally responsible for something if you could have done otherwise. He makes his case with the Black-and-Jones scenario: Jones decides entirely on his own to do something, never knowing that Black was standing by, ready to make him do it anyway if he'd shown any sign of choosing differently, so Black never actually has to step in. Frankfurt's point is that Jones still looks fully responsible even though, because of Black, he couldn't actually have done otherwise. I want my opening to walk everyone through that scenario so the newcomers are hooked before the professor gets into the responses to it, but our advisor specifically warned me against the rookie mistake of implying this settles whether determinism is true, since apparently Frankfurt cases are supposed to stay neutral on that and only target PAP itself. A couple of the regulars have also read ahead into the "flicker of freedom" objection and will almost certainly bring it up afterward, so I don't want to accidentally give away whatever the professor is planning to say about that — just leave an opening for it.

Could you write these opening remarks for me? I need something that gets the newcomers genuinely curious rather than lost, welcomes the professor properly, and still holds up for her and for the regulars who already know the material, all without me getting the philosophy wrong in front of someone who actually does this for a living.
