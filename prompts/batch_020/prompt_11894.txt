A co-author and I are finishing an introductory philosophy textbook and need to agree with our publisher on how to divide the royalties. The contract is not a fixed sum to be divided: depending on choices such as foreign rights, audio rights, and print run size, we could end up in many different overall income arrangements, and if we cannot agree on one, the deal falls through and we would each earn essentially nothing from it. My co-author has proposed a rule for choosing among these arrangements: each of us first works out the most we could conceivably earn under any version of the contract, and then we pick whichever real arrangement gives each of us the same fraction of that personal maximum. Because my co-author's specialty sells far better abroad, their theoretical ceiling is much higher than mine, so this rule hands them noticeably more than half of everything. It sounds even-handed in the abstract, but pegging my share to a lavish hypothetical that neither of us would ever actually sign strikes me as an odd basis for fairness. A colleague of ours who works on formal decision theory instead suggested picking whichever real arrangement maximizes the combined product of what each of us stands to gain over getting nothing, which yields a substantially more even split. I cannot tell which of these two proposals is the principled way to divide a joint gain like this, or whether my unease with the first one is tracking something real. Which of them has the better claim to being fair, and why?
