So I got into it with my uncle over the weekend about whether lying is ever okay — we were going back and forth about that classic scenario where soldiers show up asking if you're hiding refugees in your house — and he's pretty devout and ended up sending me this old translation of Augustine's "Against Lying" to prove his point that lying is wrong no matter what, full stop. From what I can tell Augustine wrote the whole thing to argue against some heretical group back in the day who thought it was fine to lie about your beliefs to protect them, and at one point he gets into the Bible story about Rahab (the woman who lied to protect the Israelite spies) and the Hebrew midwives who lied to Pharoah to save the baby boys. This is the bit that's really messing with my head:

"But, as for that which is written, that God did good to the Hebrew midwives, and to Rahab the harlot of Jericho, this was not because they lied, but because they were merciful to God's people. That therefore which was rewarded in them was, not their deceit, but their benevolence; benignity of mind, not iniquity of lying... But some man will say, Would then those midwives and Rahab have done better if they had shown no mercy, by refusing to lie? Nay verily, those Hebrew women... would both eschew to say anything false, and would most frankly refuse that foul service of killing the babes. But, you will say, themselves would die. Yea, but see what follows. They would die with an heavenly habitation for their incomparably more ample reward than those houses which they made them on earth could be: they would die, to be in eternal felicity, after enduring of death for most innocent truth."

I've read this like five times now and I think I sort of follow the first part, he's saying God was rewarding the mercy and separately just forgiving the lie, like the two got bundled into the same moment without actually being connected. But the second part sounds like he's genuinely saying the midwives SHOULD have let the babies get killed rather than lie, because dying honest gets you a better deal in the afterlife than living with a lie on your conscience?? Am I reading that right, or is there a more charitable way to take what he's arguing that doesn't end with "letting the babies die was actually the better option"? And either way, could someone help me figure out what's actually doing the work in his reasoning for why a lie can never be justified even to stop something way worse — is it basically just "because God/the Bible says so," or is there a real chain of logic under there that would still make sense to someone who isn't religious at all?
