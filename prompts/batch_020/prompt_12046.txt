Ok so a few weeks back I went down one of those late-night internet rabbit holes and ended up reading all about Roko's basilisk -- the idea that some insanely powerful future AI might go back and punish everyone who ever heard about the possibility of it and didn't help bring it into being sooner, because if it decides now (well, "now" for it, way later for us) that it's going to do that, that's apparently what's supposed to make it rational for us to help build it today. Everybody treats it as a joke and honestly a lot of it is silly, but the actual reasoning under it took me way longer to shake off than i expected, especially once i found out the guy who ran the forum where it first got posted, LessWrong, deleted the whole thread and wouldnt let people discuss it for years because he thought just knowing about it was genuinely dangerous. I tried explaining the whole thing to my roommate and she just stared at me.

What's been stuck in my head since is how much the shape of this is basically just Pascal's wager with a robot instead of God -- something you can't prove is real, absolutely massive stakes if it turns out to be, "better safe than sorry" logic nudging you toward one option. And it's not just me making that connection up either -- people in that same online scene already had a name, "Pascal's mugging," for this exact kind of tiny-probability/huge-stakes reasoning going wrong, way before the basilisk thing happened.

So here's what I actually want to know: if you sat the real, historical Pascal down and explained Roko's basilisk to him properly, jargon and all -- not some modernized version of him, but the actual guy, with his Jansenist beliefs about grace, his own math on expected value, and that bit in the Pensees where he says belief isn't something you choose outright, you basically train yourself into it through practice (holy water, mass, going through the motions) until it sticks -- what would he actually conclude? Does the logic of his own wager sort of trap him into agreeing that helping build the thing is the "safe" bet, or does something specific about how he actually reasoned get him out of it in a way a present-day person using his same wager structure couldn't manage? I'd also love to know what he'd say about the classic objection to his own wager -- that there could be a bunch of different possible gods (or here, AIs) all demanding different, incompatible things -- once you translate it into basilisk terms. Please actually reason it through step by step the way he would've, don't just tell me "he'd probably say it's like his own wager" and stop there.
