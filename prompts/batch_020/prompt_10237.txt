Three weeks ago, the small manufacturing company I co-own with my brother had its order-management server encrypted by a ransomware gang. The attackers are demanding roughly $85,000 in cryptocurrency and threatening to publish our customers' contact and payment information on a public leak site if we don't pay within five days. As the partner who handles our IT contracts, I'm the one who has to make the call, and right now our insurance broker and outside counsel are both quietly advising me to pay: the sum is fully covered, this particular gang has a track record of sending working decryption keys once paid, and the reputational damage from a public leak would probably cost us far more than the ransom itself. Yet nearly every piece of official guidance I can find, from law-enforcement agencies to industry associations, insists that companies in our position should refuse as a matter of firm policy, even in cases where paying looks like the better option when judged on its own terms. From what I've read, gangs like this one choose their targets partly based on how likely companies like ours are, as a class, to end up paying, so our general disposition on this question isn't entirely beside the point when it comes to whether we got attacked in the first place, even though that disposition feels beside the point now, given that the decryption key either already exists on their server or it doesn't.

I described all this to a friend from college who studied philosophy, and she said the right way to think about it might depend less on the merits of our particular case than on what kind of policy we would have wanted to lock ourselves into before any of this happened, though she admitted she couldn't quite explain why that should still carry any weight now, given that the attackers have already decided what they'll do with our data depending on how we respond. Setting aside appeals to solidarity with future victims of this same gang, is there a genuinely self-interested, rational case for holding to a strict no-payment rule even in a situation like ours, where breaking it seems to leave us clearly better off? Or is my friend gesturing at something that doesn't actually survive contact with our specific situation?
