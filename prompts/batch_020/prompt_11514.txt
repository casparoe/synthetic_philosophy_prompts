I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of physics, and we have just spent several weeks working through the measurement problem and its three best-known solutions: the Copenhagen interpretation, Bohmian mechanics, and the Everett, or many-worlds, interpretation. My students can reproduce the formal commitments of each view well enough on an exam, but what still eludes most of them, I find, is a felt sense of just how much these interpretations disagree with one another — that the choice between them is not a matter of taste, but changes what a rational person should expect to happen to herself in the world. So alongside the technical readings this term, I want to assign a short story, and I would like you to write it.

Center it on a physicist who becomes convinced that the "quantum suicide" thought experiment — a device, triggered by a genuinely quantum-random event, that has a fifty percent chance of killing her instantly on every run — can be turned into an actual, repeatable experiment vindicating Everett's interpretation. Her reasoning: if many-worlds is correct, then on every trial some continuing branch will always contain a version of her who survives and notices nothing unusual, so from her own first-person point of view she should expect to keep finding herself alive no matter how many times she pulls the trigger. Give her a Bohmian colleague and a Copenhagen-minded colleague who each try to talk her out of it, but for reasons that are not interchangeable: I want each objection to grow out of that interpretation's own distinctive account of what "probability" and "branching" actually mean, rather than have both colleagues simply warn her, in different words, that she is taking a terrible risk.

You may choose the setting, period, and ending — a university laboratory, a garage, decades hence, whatever best serves the story — and the piece need not resolve the underlying physics; I would rather it close on an unsettling or ambiguous note than a tidy one. What matters most to me is that a reader who finishes the story and then tries to state the Bohmian's objection and the Copenhagenist's objection side by side should find that the two do not collapse into the same argument.
