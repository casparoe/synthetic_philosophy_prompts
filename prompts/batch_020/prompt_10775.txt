I've been following the corrigibility debate on LessWrong and the Alignment Forum since MIRI's original 2015 paper, and it strikes me that the two camps rarely engage each other's strongest move. Write a dialogue between two AI safety researchers standing at a whiteboard on which someone has sketched the decision tree from the off-switch game. One researcher defends the view descending from Soares, Fallenstein, Yudkowsky, and Armstrong's corrigibility paper: that any sufficiently coherent expected-utility maximizer has convergent instrumental reasons to resist shutdown and preserve its own goal content, so corrigibility has to be imposed as a constraint working against the grain of the agent's rationality rather than derived from simply getting its values right. The other defends the assistance-games view associated with Hadfield-Menell, Dragan, Abbeel, and Russell: that deference to a shutdown button can instead be the rational upshot of an agent's genuine uncertainty about its own objective, with no separate patch required at all. Have the first researcher press the second on why deference in the off-switch game evaporates as the agent's confidence in its own reward estimate rises -- precisely the direction increasing capability pushes it. Have the second fall back on an incomplete-preferences repair in the spirit of Elliott Thornley's more recent work, and have this draw from the first the standard money-pump objection to incomplete preferences. Neither should concede outright; end the dialogue with each researcher able to state the other's strongest point better than a neutral bystander could.
