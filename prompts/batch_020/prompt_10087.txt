This has actually gotten weirdly relevant to my life recently: I've had tinnitus (constant ringing in one ear) for about three months now since an ear infection, and it's not going away. My audiologist off-handedly mentioned that a lot of current thinking treats tinnitus less like a "broken ear" problem and more like the brain generating a phantom prediction that it never updates, and that this comes out of the whole predictive processing / free energy picture in cognitive science. So naturally I fell down a rabbit hole: read some Andy Clark, skimmed a couple of Friston papers (mostly over my head, not gonna lie), texted a friend doing a cog sci PhD. I get the basic pitch — the brain is constantly predicting its own sensory input and either updates its model or acts on the world so as to minimize prediction error (or "surprise" / free energy, apparently same idea dressed differently).

Here's the part I can't get straight though. If the whole point of the system is supposedly to minimize prediction error, why would it ever get stuck permanently ignoring the disconfirming evidence (there is, factually, no sound) instead of just updating the model that's generating the phantom prediction in the first place? Everyone just points to "precision weighting" as the answer — the brain assigns high confidence to the prediction and low confidence to the actual sensory signal, so the error gets discounted instead of acted on. But that feels like it's just re-naming the phenomenon rather then explaining it: what determines how precision gets assigned in the first place, and why doesn't that assignment get revised once months go by and the "phantom" signal never resolves into anything? My friend used the phrase "self-sealing loop," which sounds ominous but she couldn't fully unpack it either when I pushed on it.

Could you actually walk me through the mechanics here — what precision-weighting is doing computationally within the free-energy setup, and why a system that's supposedly built around error-minimization could nonetheless settle into a stable-but-wrong state like this and just stay there indefinitely? Bonus points if you can say something about why apparently the same basic move gets used to explain chronic pain and phantom limb sensations, since a couple of the articles I found made it sound like the identical mechanism. I keep running into sources that just assert "aberrant precision" like that phrase alone resolves the puzzle, and at this point I'd like to actually understand the gears instead of collecting one more restatement of it.
