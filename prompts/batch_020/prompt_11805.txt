My college roommate Danny is getting married in a few weeks, and I've been asked to give the best man speech. Danny did a PhD in economics and now works in what he calls "epistemic game theory," with a particular focus on something he refers to as "common knowledge of rationality." Over roughly a decade of friendship he has explained this to me, entirely unprompted, at more dinners than I can count, so as a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, I've absorbed the following: there's a real difference between everyone individually knowing something and everyone knowing that everyone knows it (and knowing that they know that, and so on), and a surprising amount of his field's arguments about when rational people can actually be expected to coordinate on anything hinge on that difference. I already had a rough version of this from a distributed-systems course, in the form of the Two Generals Problem: two armies on opposite hills need to attack in unison, but can only communicate via a messenger who might be captured, so that no finite exchange of messages can ever make it certain to either general that the other will actually attack at the same time. Danny was delighted when I first described this to him, since it turns out to be a famous illustration of exactly the kind of common-knowledge problem he studies, and he's since told me about results in his own field where assuming that rationality is common knowledge among the players seems to make cooperation collapse rather than hold it together.

I'd like the toast built around this. Danny's PhD advisor and several people from his old cohort will be at the wedding and will catch a reference to common knowledge or the Two Generals Problem instantly; his parents, my parents, and most of the other guests will not, and just want a warm, funny few minutes about two people they love getting married. Could you write that toast for me (something that would read aloud in about four or five minutes)? I'd like it to genuinely use the distinction between everyone privately assuming for years that Danny and his fiancée Priya would end up together, and whatever it is that the public ceremony itself accomplishes that all that private assuming never did. I'd also like it to make something real, rather than a throwaway line, out of the fact that Danny's own field is full of cases where getting a room to common knowledge of anything, let alone of everyone's rationality, turns out to be surprisingly hard or even provably impossible. I don't want this to turn into a game theory lecture, though — it should sound like something a best man would actually stand up and say, not a paper he's citing.
