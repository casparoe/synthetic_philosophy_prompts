I'm several chapters into a campus novel whose central point-of-view character is a graduate student who works as a teaching assistant for a large undergraduate course on nineteenth-century political thought. I served as a TA for something similar myself, long before I left the discipline for fiction, but not recently enough that I trust my instincts about where a genuinely careful grader would draw the line between a strong answer and a merely fluent one. For a scene I'm writing now, a long night of grading near the end of term, I've drafted a plausible exam question on Marx and a plausible student response to it, written the way an engaged but somewhat rushed student might actually write under exam conditions. Before I let my character mark it up, I would like to see how the answer actually holds up under a careful reading, so that her comments in the finished scene feel earned rather than invented for convenience.

Here is the question, worth 20 of the exam's points:

"In the 'Estranged Labour' section of the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844, Marx distinguishes several dimensions along which the worker is alienated under capitalist production. Identify and briefly explain these dimensions. Then, drawing on the account of surplus value in Volume I of Capital, explain the economic mechanism that Marx thinks generates exploitation. Finally, take a position: is 'exploitation,' as Capital defines it, simply the 1844 account of alienation restated in economic terms, or is it doing different conceptual work? Defend your view in a page or two."

And here is the student's answer:

"Marx argues that under capitalism the worker is alienated in four interlocking ways. First, she is alienated from the product of her labour: what she makes does not belong to her but becomes the property of the capitalist and confronts her as an alien, even hostile, object, since the more value she produces the more powerful this world of objects becomes relative to her. Second, she is alienated from the very act of production: labour is not a spontaneous expression of her powers but a forced activity performed only to survive, so that she feels herself only outside work and like a stranger to herself while working. Third, she is alienated from what Marx, borrowing the term from Feuerbach, calls her 'species-being' (Gattungswesen): for Marx, what distinguishes human beings from animals is our capacity to treat our own life-activity as an object of free, conscious, universal creation, but capitalism reduces this distinctively human productive capacity to a bare means of physical survival, so that what should express our free species-life becomes merely a means of keeping the individual alive. Fourth, and as a consequence of the first three, she is alienated from other people: unable to relate to her own activity as a free human being, she cannot relate to others as one either, so fellow workers, and even the capitalist, come to appear to her chiefly as rivals or as an alien power.

Capital gives this picture an economic backbone. Labour-power is itself bought and sold as a commodity, and like any commodity it has a price, the wage, that tends toward the value required to produce and reproduce it, essentially the cost of the worker's subsistence. Because the wage covers only part of the working day, the remaining hours are worked for nothing: the capitalist pays the worker less than the value of what she actually produces and pockets the difference as surplus value, and it is this systematic underpayment, this departure from a fair exchange between equals, that makes the wage relation exploitative rather than merely unequal. In this sense, Capital does not really introduce a new idea so much as translate the 1844 picture of estrangement into the vocabulary of value, price, and the working day: 'exploitation' is 'alienation' wearing an economist's clothes, and a student who has understood the earlier text has already understood the mature theory in outline."

Could you grade this the way a strict but fair teaching assistant would, someone more interested in precision than in encouragement? Please do not soften anything for my sake: I need the assessment to be accurate, not kind.
