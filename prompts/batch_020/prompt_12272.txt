I'm a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, currently drafting a dissertation chapter on Kepler's engagement with astrology, and a puzzle about how to extend his position is proving more interesting than the chapter itself. Kepler's mature view, laid out mainly in the *Tertius Interveniens* and in the fourth book of the *Harmonices Mundi*, is a curiously selective one: he wanted to strip away the twelve zodiacal signs, the house system, and most of the predictive machinery of judicial astrology as arbitrary human conventions with no purchase on nature, while retaining a restricted doctrine of aspects. On his account, the angles formed by converging planetary rays could excite a real, if modest, response in the "anima telluris" and in the human soul's innate geometrical sense, roughly the way a plucked string sets a nearby string vibrating in sympathy — hence effects on weather, mood, and vitality, but nothing resembling a fated horoscope. It amounts to a thoroughly reformist, quasi-physical astrology that satisfied neither the defenders nor the detractors of the practice in his own day.

What complicates any tidy story here is a much later development. Wolfgang Pauli, in the essay he published alongside Jung's piece on synchronicity, argued that Kepler's own cosmological "archetypes" — the geometric forms he took to be impressed on the world-soul and mirrored in the human soul — directly anticipated Jung's psychological theory of archetypes. That endorsement helped legitimate a whole subsequent tradition of archetypal or psychological astrology, associated first with Jung himself and later with figures such as Liz Greene and Richard Tarnas, which abandons any causal or physical mechanism in favor of Jung's acausal "synchronicity," while happily restoring the very signs and houses Kepler had tried to abolish, now reinterpreted symbolically rather than physically.

I would like your help reconstructing Kepler's likely verdict on this descendant of his own reform project. Given the specific, fairly idiosyncratic shape of his actual commitments — physical or geometric causation as the only legitimate basis for any astrological effect, contempt for what he regarded as baseless symbolism, and a targeted rejection of signs and houses in particular — would he read the archetype lineage that Pauli traces as vindicating a real continuity with his own project, or would the abandonment of physical causation, together with the return of signs and houses, strike him as a relapse into precisely the sort of superstition he spent his career trying to purge from the discipline? I am less interested in a simple verdict than in how you would weigh these lines of continuity and discontinuity against one another in building the reconstruction.
