I recently moved from a philosophy PhD into underwriting strategy at a life insurer, and last month a routine product meeting turned into an argument I never expected to have outside a seminar room. We were deciding whether to let applicants voluntarily submit results from consumer genetic-risk tests in exchange for better rates. Our chief actuary firmly opposed the idea, invoking Hirshleifer's point that once information about a pre-existing risk becomes available before contracts are written, it tends to unravel the very risk-pooling that gives insurance its value -- so its arrival can leave nearly everyone worse off even though no one involved is reasoning badly. Our head of pricing, who holds a master's in decision theory, responded with Good's theorem: a rational Bayesian who will act optimally on what she learns can never expect free evidence to lower her expected utility, only to raise it or leave it unchanged, so refusing information as a matter of principle is simply incoherent. Neither of them was being sloppy, and I would rather understand the disagreement properly than referee it badly if it resurfaces. Rather than a transcript of that meeting, could you write a fresh dialogue between a Bayesian decision theorist and an insurance economist who build both positions up carefully from first principles? I want them to work out whether Good's theorem and the Hirshleifer effect are genuinely in tension or are compatible results quietly answering two different questions, with each pressing on the other's weakest point rather than settling for an easy truce.
