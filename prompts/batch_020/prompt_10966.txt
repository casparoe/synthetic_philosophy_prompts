I've been reading about split-brain patients — people who've had the corpus callosum severed, usually to treat severe epilepsy — and I can't shake a question the classic experiments raise: how many subjects of experience are actually in there? In the standard paradigm, a word flashed only to the left visual field is registered by the right hemisphere and correctly identified by the left hand, while the patient's speech, produced by the left hemisphere, insists nothing was shown at all. Nagel argued that cases like this show our ordinary concept of "a single mind" just has no determinate application here: not one, not two, but something our concepts weren't built to handle. Puccetti pushed the opposite conclusion much further, arguing that the evidence actually supports two fully, simultaneously conscious minds sharing one skull — and that this duality is present, merely masked by cooperation, in ordinary intact brains too.

Write a dialogue between a philosopher defending Puccetti's two-minds view and one defending Nagel's indeterminacy view. Ground the exchange in concrete cases — the naming/pointing dissociation above, the chimeric-face experiments, and Gazzaniga's "interpreter" confabulation cases are all fair game — rather than letting either side coast on slogans. Press the two-minds defender on why ordinary, integrated behavior outside the lab — cross-cueing and all, essentially two processes with no shared memory coordinating by watching each other's side effects — doesn't just prove there's one subject after all. And press the indeterminacy defender on whether "indeterminate" is a real conclusion or just a polite way of declining to answer a hard question. Neither should give ground easily.
