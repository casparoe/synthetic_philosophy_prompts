Since retiring from a career in experimental condensed-matter physics, I have finally had time to read the metaphysics I always meant to get to, and this past month a small reading group I joined at the local university worked through David Lewis's "On the Plurality of Worlds." I will admit that my first instinct, as a physicist, was to assimilate Lewis's plurality of worlds to the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, but further reading disabused me of that: Lewis's worlds are not branches of a single wavefunction but wholly disconnected concrete universes, and "actual," on his view, is merely an indexical label with no more metaphysical weight than "here" or "now" — every world is actual relative to its own inhabitants. That is a strikingly different picture from the one that first made "possible worlds" a philosophical commonplace, namely Leibniz's, on which an infinity of non-actual worlds exist only as objects of the divine intellect, and exactly one of them is rendered actual, in a full-blooded and non-relative sense, because God freely chooses to create it as the best.

I would like your help working out how Leibniz, presented somehow with Lewis's book, would likely have reacted to it, on two specific points rather than in generalities. First, consider Lewis's indexical analysis of actuality: if every world's inhabitants are equally entitled to call their own world "actual," is there still room for the claim, central to Leibniz's theodicy, that God selected our world as uniquely and non-relatively the best, and thereby actual, or does Lewis's semantics quietly dissolve the very distinction that theodicy needs? Second, consider trans-world identity. I recall that in his correspondence with Arnauld, who was alarmed by the claim, made in the Discourse on Metaphysics, that an individual's complete concept fixes once and for all everything that will ever be true of it, Leibniz was pressed on whether Adam could have failed to sin, and answered that a non-sinning Adam would not be our Adam living a different life, but a distinct possible individual, since sinning is itself among the predicates the complete concept fixes. That looks close to what some commentators call Leibniz's "superessentialism," and I gather it has occasionally been likened to Lewis's own counterpart theory, which similarly denies that one and the same individual is ever shared between worlds. Does Leibniz's doctrine of complete concepts, quite apart from his views on God and creation, commit him to something functionally like counterparts rather than to genuine identity of individuals across possible worlds? I would appreciate a careful reconstruction of his likely position on both fronts, rather than a restatement of the standard contemporary objections to Lewis.
