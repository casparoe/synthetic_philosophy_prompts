(1) Nate opened with: there's an actual theorem here, not just a vibe. Good's theorem says a Bayesian facing a decision should always weakly prefer to see a costless signal before acting, since worst case you just ignore it and do whatever you'd have done anyway — so refusing free evidence when nothing is stopping you from looking can never raise your expected utility, and her declining the test is a plain mistake. (2) Farah pushed back: the theorem is stated relative to a fixed decision problem, it proves information helps you act better given some choice you're already facing. There's no treatment for Huntington's, so there's no act the result would change, so the theorem doesn't even get a foothold here. The moment "finding out" carries a cost that isn't mediated by any downstream choice, she said, you're outside what the theorem was ever about, so Nate doesn't get to just cite it and call the matter closed. (5) Nate's comeback: doesn't that move generalize to justify anything? A gambler could just as well say the cost of finding out he lost is a real cost, so it's rational to never check the score.
