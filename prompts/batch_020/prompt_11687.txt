The question, worth 20 points, read as follows: "In the literature on consequentialism, it is sometimes argued that our ignorance of the long-run effects of our actions undermines our ability to know what consequentialism actually recommends -- a problem often called 'cluelessness.' (a) [6 pts] Explain the distinction between what has been called 'simple' and 'complex' cluelessness, and identify which author introduced this specific distinction into the literature. (b) [6 pts] Illustrate simple cluelessness with an example of your own choosing, and explain why a consequentialist facing it can typically proceed as though she weren't. (c) [8 pts] Explain whether the reasoning that resolves simple cluelessness in part (b) is also available to a consequentialist facing complex cluelessness. Here is the student's answer, transcribed exactly as submitted:

"(a) Consequentialists face a problem that goes beyond ordinary uncertainty about outcomes: even with unlimited computational power, we could not trace out all the effects of an action, because those effects ramify indefinitely into the future (saving a life today changes who is born a hundred years from now, and so on). James Lenman calls this problem 'cluelessness,' and in his work on the topic he distinguishes two versions of it. 'Simple' cluelessness is the situation where the unforeseeable effects of an act, so far as we can tell, are just as likely to be good as bad, so that in expectation they wash out and can be safely ignored. 'Complex' cluelessness is the situation where we do have some evidence bearing on the unforeseeable effects, but that evidence doesn't add up to a determinate expectation, so the effects cannot simply be bracketed the way they can be in the simple case.

(b) Consider the choice between giving a stranger correct directions when asked and deliberately giving wrong directions as a prank. Both choices have enormous unforeseeable ripple effects on the future -- who this person bumps into, who they marry, who is eventually born as a result, and so on indefinitely. But we have no particular reason to think the unknowable long-run effects of correct directions are systematically better or worse than those of incorrect ones; whatever considerations might make us worry that one choice's ripples are bad are matched by parallel considerations on the other side. Since the foreseeable effects clearly favor giving correct directions, and the unforeseeable effects are a wash, a consequentialist is licensed to act on the foreseeable balance of reasons alone.

(c) The same reasoning extends naturally to complex cluelessness. Just as the unforeseeable ripple effects in the directions case cancel out in expectation, the unforeseeable effects in a case like funding a global health charity -- for instance, the worry that saving lives today increases population growth and therefore long-run resource consumption and emissions -- will likewise average out, since for every consideration suggesting the long-run effect is bad there is a comparable consideration suggesting it is good. So a consequentialist facing complex cluelessness can reason exactly as she would in the simple case: bracket the unforeseeable effects and act on the foreseeable balance of considerations. Cluelessness, on this view, is ultimately just a special case of ordinary risk, which expected-utility reasoning already tells us how to handle."
