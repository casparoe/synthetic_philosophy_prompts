I've been going down a bit of a rabbit hole lately because the novel I'm drafting has a main character who's a jaded opera critic, and to get her voice right I've been reading through Bernard Williams's opera writings (the "On Opera" collection, plus whatever I can find of the Mozart essays). What strikes me is how insistent he is that opera isn't in the business of handing you a tidy moral verdict - his whole angle on Don Giovanni, for instance, is that the music makes the guy's amorality genuinely seductive rather then something we're just supposed to tut-tut at, and more broadly that opera thrives on the kind of "thick," particular human situations that his own ethics (all that stuff about "the morality system" in Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy) says our normal moral vocabulary is bad at handling.

Anyway, I mentioned all this to a friend of mine who coaches singers at an opera house, and she said something that's been rattling around my head ever since: apparently there's a whole live debate right now among directors and dramaturgs about how to stage the "problematic" bits of the classic rep for a #MeToo-ish audience - content warnings before Rigoletto, endings quietly rewritten so Carmen isn't just a corpse by curtain, that kind of thing. Since Williams died in 2003 he obviously never weighed in on any of this directly, but given what he actually thought about opera, ethics, and the specific kind of seriousness he thought music-drama was capable of, what do you reckon he'd have said about it? Would he have been sympathetic to directors trying to defuse or reframe this stuff for modern audiences, or would he have seen that as exactly the kind of moralizing flattening he spent his career arguing against? I'd like something more specific than "philosophers would disagree" - I'm trying to put an actual defensible position in my character's mouth, not a strawman, so the more grounded in his real arguments the better.
