I've been TAing our phil of mind seminar's week on the Turing test, and I keep getting stuck on section 9 of the 1950 paper, "the argument from extrasensory perception," which everyone in class just laughs off as a weird throwaway. But Turing's actually pretty serious there: he flat out says the statistical case for telepathy is overwhelming, and floats running the imitation game in a "telepathy-proof room" so a psychic human contestant cant get an edge over the machine by guessing hidden cards better than chance. That's a real load-bearing part of his argument, not a joke. And it's the same guy who more or less pioneered a Bayesian weight-of-evidence approach to evaluating hypotheses at Bletchley (the deciban stuff), so he clearly wasn't naive about how to weigh evidence when he wanted to be.

He died in '54, decades before Daryl Bem's "Feeling the Future" precognition experiments blew up in 2011, and before Wagenmakers and colleagues redid Bem's numbers as Bayes factors and showed the "overwhelming" evidence mostly evaporates once you use a properly skeptical prior on psi, and before the failed replications and preregistration reforms that followed. Based on what Turing actually argues in that section, not just "he was brilliant so obviously he'd side with the skeptics now," who do you think he'd side with in the Bem affair, and would his own way of reasoning about ESP evidence have made him more or less prone to the exact mistake Wagenmakers accuses Bem of?
