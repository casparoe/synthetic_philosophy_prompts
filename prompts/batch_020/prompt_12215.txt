(a) Define each, and explain the specific mechanism that generates each under capitalist production. (b) Explain why, for Marx, a worker could be paid the full value of their labor-power and still be exploited. (c) Discuss whether alienation, on Marx's account, is simply the subjective experience of being exploited, or whether the two could in principle come apart -- could someone be alienated without being exploited, or exploited without being alienated? Here's the answer, copied exactly as the student wrote it (typos and all): "Marx distinguishes between labor and labor-power: what the worker sells to the capitalist is not a fixed quantity of labor but their capacity to labor for a given period. The value of labor-power is set, like any other commodity, by the socially necessary labor time required to reproduce it -- basically the cost of the workers food, shelter, and the next generation of workers. Because a worker can typically produce, in a few hours, enough value to cover that cost (necessary labor time), but is made to work a full day, the remaining hours (surplus labor time) generate surplus value that the capitalist keeps for free. This is exploitation: it happens even if the capitalist pays the worker the complete market value of their labor-power, because the source of profit is precisely the gap between what labor-power costs and what labor actually produces. Marx measures this with the rate of exploitation, s/(c+v) -- surplus value over the total capital laid out -- and argues that as capitalists mechanize production and this ratio shifts, we get the famous tendency of the rate of profit to fall, one of his major predictions about capitalism's long-run crises. Alienation, which Marx lays out mainly in the 1844 Manuscripts, is really the subjective flip side of this same process -- basically how exploitation feels from the inside. He describes four faces of it: workers are alienated from the product (which belongs to someone else the moment its made), from the act of production itself (labor becomes a forced means to survive rather than free self-expression), from their own 'species-being' (Gattungswesen) -- humanity's nature as creative, world-transforming beings -- and from other people (competition and private property turn other workers and even the capitalist into strangers or rivals rather than fellow humans). Since alienation just names how it feels to be exploited, the two rise and fall together for Marx -- you wouldn't expect to find one without the other under capitalism, and a non-exploitative economy would, on this view, also be a non-alienating one."
