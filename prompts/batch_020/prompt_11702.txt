I'm a philosophy PhD student working mainly on moral psychology, but this term I'm auditing a cross-listed seminar on rational choice theory, and a recent session has left me with a question I'd like to resolve before I present on it next week. Early in the course we covered the classic money-pump argument for the transitivity (or at least acyclicity) of preferences: an agent who prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A can be charged a small fee at each step to trade her way around the cycle, ending up back where she started but strictly poorer, which is supposed to show that cyclic preferences of this kind are irrational. More recently we covered hyperbolic discounting: the finding that someone who, thirty days out, prefers $110 in thirty-one days to $100 in thirty days will nonetheless prefer $100 today to $110 tomorrow once that thirtieth day actually arrives. The professor mentioned in passing that this, too, makes an agent exploitable—for instance, by a party who sells her a commitment device now and then, once her preferences flip, sells her a way out of it.

That remark has left me thinking that "dynamically inconsistent" is really just another name for "has a preference cycle," with the cycle spread across time instead of across simultaneously available options, so that the money-pump argument applies to hyperbolic discounters directly and in precisely the sense it applies to the classic A-B-C case: in both, the agent's overall pattern of choices lets an outsider extract a guaranteed profit via a sequence of trades she individually accepts at the time. Is that right, or is there a genuine structural difference between an agent whose preferences are cyclic at a single moment and an agent whose preferences, while perfectly acyclic at every moment, simply change from one moment to the next—and if there is such a difference, does it mean that hyperbolic discounters are not irrational in quite the same sense, or on quite the same grounds, as agents with a bona fide preference cycle?
