My younger sister is a behavioral ecologist -- stotting in gazelles, some sexual selection work in birds -- and she's marrying her partner, also a biologist, in a few weeks. She's asked me, her older brother and (unfortunately, according to her) the philosophy professor in the family, to give the wedding toast. I keep circling back to Zahavi's handicap principle -- that whole costly-signaling idea that hard-to-fake signals are what make communication honest, precisely because a cheap "trust me" can be produced by anyone regardless of whether its true -- because I think there's something genuinely moving hiding in there about weddings: the whole ridiculous, expensive, public, impossible-to-take-back production of the thing being the point rather than a regrettable side effect, the way a peacock's tail proves something its call never could.

I'm nervous about actually pulling this off though. Half the guests are people from her department who will notice immediately if I get Zahavi wrong or if the analogy doesn't actually hold up -- and honestly I'm not even sure the principle is supposed to apply to signals within an already-formed pair, as opposed to signals aimed at rivals or prospective mates, so there's a technical worry sitting right next to the emotional one. The other half is our grandmother and a dozen cousins who did not come to a wedding for a lecture on sexual selection, they came to hear that these two people love each other. Could you draft me something, four or five minutes read aloud, that actually uses the handicap principle as the engine of the toast rather than a cute reference dropped in once and then abandoned? It has to survive both crowds: it can't come across as "your marriage is just a fitness display," which would genuinely hurt my sister's feelings, but it also can't chicken out of the idea once it's raised. Bonus points if it ends on a line people can actually raise a glass to.
