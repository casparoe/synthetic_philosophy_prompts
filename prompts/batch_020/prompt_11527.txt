I'm several months into a novel set inside an underfunded university materials-science lab, and part of my research has involved interviewing former lab technicians for texture and detail I couldn't otherwise invent convincingly. One interview gave me more than local color. A technician I'll call Nadia told me she spent nearly a year flagging that a shared instrument was producing subtly skewed readings, only to be told repeatedly by the postdocs and the lab's principal investigator that the problem was almost certainly her technique — until an outside audit eventually traced it to an undetected calibration drift that had already worked its way into two published papers. I recounted the story at dinner to two old friends, Marcus and Elena, and what followed was a genuine, unresolved argument that I now suspect is more useful to me, as material, than anything I could have scripted myself.

Marcus, a former bench scientist turned science journalist, and Elena, who left a philosophy PhD in social epistemology for science-policy work, spent most of the meal on it. (1) Marcus opened by calling this a textbook case of testimonial injustice: Nadia's word was discounted below its true evidential weight because of identity-linked stereotypes about young women in a male-dominated lab being anxious or not technically minded, and that discounting, not any honest reading of her track record, is what cost a year and two papers. (2) Elena objected that labs are hierarchies of earned credibility for good epistemic reasons — a principal investigator's extra fifteen years of training justify a stronger prior about the instrument's behavior than a first-year technician's hunch — and that if every case of a junior person turning out to be right counts as an injustice, the concept does no more work than "hindsight bias" dressed up in theory. (3) Marcus answered that the relevant comparison settles it: by Nadia's own account, when two junior men raised similar doubts about the same instrument months later, their concerns were checked within days, while hers had been waved off for most of a year, once with the words "don't worry your head about it" — a pattern, and a form of words, that track gender rather than seniority. (4) Elena granted this was suggestive but not dispositive: Nadia might simply have been newer to that particular instrument, or raised the problem less forcefully for reasons that needn't be caused by her gender just because they correlate with it, and the theory she was defending requires the deficit to track identity prejudice specifically, not any old correlate of it. (5) Marcus then shifted ground: even setting testimonial injustice aside, he said, there was a hermeneutical injustice underneath it, since Nadia told me she spent weeks unable to put into words what was wrong because no one had taught her the statistical-process-control vocabulary that would have let her name a systematic drift as such — and being denied that vocabulary because of her non-PhD status is exactly the kind of interpretive disadvantage the concept was coined for. (6) Elena rejected this last move flatly: hermeneutical injustice, as she understood it, names a gap in the interpretive resources available to an entire community, such that nobody yet has the concept in question, whereas here the vocabulary already existed and was fluently used by everyone else in that very lab — what Nadia lacked was training, not a concept the community itself was missing, and calling any unequal distribution of existing know-how a hermeneutical injustice empties the term of its point. Marcus admitted that one rattled him, but by the time coffee arrived neither had conceded the larger argument.

Could you go through the exchange move by move, from (1) to (6), and say who has the better of it at each step rather than just declaring an overall winner? A sentence or two of justification per move would help. I'd also value a closing judgment of your own on the question that started it all: did Nadia suffer testimonial injustice, hermeneutical injustice, both, or neither, and is there some consideration bearing on that question that neither Marcus nor Elena thought to raise? Partly I'm asking because a version of this conversation may end up as dialogue between two characters in the book, and I'd rather understand the philosophical terrain properly than let it settle by whoever I happen to be rooting for as I write.
