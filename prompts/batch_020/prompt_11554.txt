I'm several drafts into a novel whose central section turns on a Dominican friar—a moral theologian formed entirely in the Thomist manner, meant to be formidable rather than quaint—who is co-opted onto a university panel on the ethics of technology. In the scene I'm drafting, a psychologist on the panel walks the room through Joshua Knobe's well-known 2003 result, usually just called the Knobe effect: told that a company chairman adopts a new policy purely to raise profits, knowing but not caring that it will also damage the environment, most people say he damaged the environment intentionally; told the identical story except that the foreseen side effect is environmental benefit, most people say he did not help the environment intentionally.

What makes the scene harder to write than I expected is that the asymmetry doesn't stop at the word "intentionally": later work on what's called the epistemic side-effect effect finds the same harm/help split even in whether people will say the chairman knew what he was doing—which cuts closer to the bare foreseeing that double effect actually presupposes than "intentionally" does. A friend who read an early draft assumed my friar would simply dismiss the whole line of research as reductive social science, but that struck me as the easy and probably wrong choice, given that double effect is built out of exactly this distinction between what an agent aims at, foresees, and merely permits.

Before I write his lines, I'd like to get the philosophy under them right, independent of the novel. Reconstructing Aquinas's actual account of intention and foresight—the apparatus he works out through the self-defense article of the Summa (II-II, q. 64, a. 7)—would he expect this pair of results, or would they embarrass the framework? Double effect already contains its own asymmetry at the level of permissibility, since a foreseen harm needs a proportionate justifying reason and a foreseen benefit needs none, so there may be a genuine Thomist resource for absorbing Knobe's result rather than being surprised by it; I'm less sure what he'd do with the finding about "knew," since that seems to threaten the neutrality of the foreseeing double effect is supposed to start from, before intention or permissibility even come into it. And separately, Aquinas tends to treat the common way of speaking as evidence about what a concept is rather than noise to be explained away, so would he take the ordinary-language asymmetry as telling him something true about intention and knowledge that should worry anyone who wants valence-free versions of those concepts to do moral work—or does he have the resources, something like the distinction between what is voluntary in itself and voluntary only in its cause, to keep his own technical vocabulary insulated from however loosely people talk on the street? I'd rather be corrected by you now than by a reader who knows the literature better than I do.
