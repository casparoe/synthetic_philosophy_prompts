I recently decided to finish the philosophy degree I put on hold a decade ago, and the first graduate seminar I've been allowed to sit in on as a returning student happens to be on ancient skepticism. For my term paper I picked what I assumed was safe, well-trodden ground: the observation, repeated in more or less every epistemology textbook, that the regress problem forces a choice between foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism. My professor's response was that this "trilemma" is really just a modern repackaging of the Five Modes of Agrippa as reported by Sextus Empiricus, and that I ought to build the paper around his actual text rather than the textbook gloss, since the ancient version doesn't line up with the modern one as neatly as everyone assumes. That sent me to R. G. Bury's translation of the Outlines of Pyrrhonism, and now, with the paper due in a week, I'm stuck on exactly the passage the whole argument has to turn on (Book I, roughly §166 through §169).

"The Mode based upon regress ad infinitum is that whereby we say that the thing adduced as a proof of the matter proposed needs a further proof, and this again another, and so on ad infinitum, so that the consequence is suspension of judgement, as we possess no starting-point for our argument. [...] The Mode based upon hypothesis occurs when the Dogmatists, being forced back ad infinitum, take as their starting-point something which they do not establish by argument but claim to assume as granted simply and without proof. The Mode of circular reasoning is the case in which the proof itself which ought to establish the matter of inquiry stands in need of confirmation derived from that very matter, so that, being unable to assume either the one or the other in order to establish it, we are compelled to suspend judgement about both." I've deliberately left out the mode of relativity, which sits between the regress mode and the hypothesis mode in the actual text, because I'm honestly not sure it belongs with the other three at all — but my professor's offhand comment implied it's doing real argumentative work that the modern trilemma quietly drops, rather than being an unrelated fourth item that just happens to be on the list.

Could you help me work through this properly? Specifically: (1) reconstruct the logical structure of the argument as it runs across the regress, hypothesis, and reciprocal modes — what exactly is functioning as "proof" of what, and why does this particular sequence of moves force suspension of judgement rather than merely showing that one specific chain of reasoning is defective; (2) say something about Sextus's claim, made just after this passage, that literally any object of inquiry can be referred to these modes, and whether the text as given actually earns that exhaustiveness claim or simply asserts it; and (3) give me your honest, critical take on how well "foundationalism," "coherentism," and "infinitism" actually track Sextus's own three distinctions, as opposed to being an anachronistic gloss that flattens some real differences — including, if you can reconstruct it, what work the missing relativity mode ought to be doing that the modern trilemma leaves out entirely.
