Been in a population ethics reading group this semester and we just worked through the core of Benatar's *Better Never to Have Been* — I'm actually presenting on it next week, so I want to make sure I've got this right before I embarass myself in front of the committee members sitting in. I think I've got the asymmetry argument down at the mechanical level: presence of pain is bad, presence of pleasure is good, but absence of pain is good even with nobody around to enjoy that absence, while absence of pleasure isn't bad unless someone actually exists to be deprived of it. He uses that to conclude that coming into existence is always a harm.

Where I got stuck is an argument I had with a friend doing her PhD in psych last week. She pointed out that the subjective wellbeing literature is pretty consistently upbeat — life-satisfaction surveys across countries and demographics mostly have people rating their lives as decently good on average, even accounting for the obvious adaption and hedonic-treadmill effects. Her point was that this basically sinks Benatar's whole project: if the actual empirical balance of pleasure over pain in most people's lives is positive, then his "harm of coming into existence" claim just can't be right, since existence turns out to be net-good for most of the people going through it. I tried to push back by bringing up the bit where Benatar argues our self-reports of wellbeing are systematically unreliable (I think he even borrows a term from psychology for it, something like the Pollyanna principle?), but that felt like I was just explaining away inconvenient data rather than actually answering her. Is she right that the wellbeing survey stuff is a real problem for the asymmetry argument, or is there something about how the argument is built that makes it indifferent to whatever the empirical hedonic balance turns out to be?
