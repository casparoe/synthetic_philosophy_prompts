"Explain the core argument for evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL). In your answer, be sure to address (a) why the argument depends on the world being 'large,' (b) why a causal decision theorist and an evidential decision theorist would react differently to it, and (c) what kind of agreement in values, if any, is required between the cooperating parties. (10 points)"

And here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"ECL, an idea due to Hofstadter's notion of superrationality applied to the whole multiverse, holds that if the world (or multiverse) is sufficiently large -- for instance if the Everett many-worlds interpretation is true -- then there exist many other agents out there who reason exactly the way you do, even though you can never causally interact with them because they're in branches or regions you're cut off from. Since your decision procedure is correlated with theirs, choosing to act cooperatively is evidence that they decide the same way, so in effect you should act as though your decision determines theirs too, even absent any causal link. This is exactly why a CDT agent doesn't buy the argument: CDT only cares about the causal upshot of your own act, and by hypothesis there's no causal channel to these other agents, so cooperating changes nothing from a causal point of view. An EDT agent, by contrast, treats the correlation as actionable, since what matters for EDT is the conditional probability of outcomes given your act, not the causal pathway to them. As for the values question: because the whole mechanism rests on the other agents being relevantly similar to you, cooperation on this picture only pays off between agents who already share the same terminal values -- otherwise there's nothing to correlate on, and the 'cooperation' would just be one-sided altruism rather then a mutually beneficial trade."
