I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist, and now that I've got nothing but time on my hands I've been finally reading all the literature and philosophy I skipped past during my career. Lately I've fallen into a real Borges phase -- "The Garden of Forking Paths," "The Library of Babel," "Funes the Memorious," and especially the essay "A New Refutation of Time," where he basically recycles Berkeley's and Hume's arguments to try and argue that succession itself might be a trick of grammar, that each perceived instant could be self-sufficient rather than strung into a genuinely flowing "time" -- and then, famously, undercuts it's own title at the very end by admitting that he still feels himself caught in time no matter what the argument says.

That collided in my head with a book an old colleague pushed on me years ago for completely unrelated reasons: Julian Barbour's "The End of Time." Barbour takes the timelessness of the Wheeler-DeWitt equation from quantum gravity seriously and argues there's no dynamical flow of time at all -- just a static, fully-populated landscape of possible instants he calls "Nows," sitting inside a bigger configuration space he names "Platonia." On his view, the feeling that time is passing comes only from the fact that some Nows happen to be internally structured as "time capsules" -- they contain records, fossils, memories, etc. that are all mutually consistent with some coherent history, even though no such history ever actually elapsed moment-to-moment. Borges died in '86, well before any of this got popularized, so obviously he never got the chance to react to it.

So here's my question, and I'd like a real answer, not a cop-out: digging into what Borges actually wrote about time, eternity, memory and infinite regress (not just the lazy "oh he liked labyrinths" reading), how do you think he'd have responded specifically to Platonia and time capsules? Would he have taken it as a vindication of his lifelong hunch, or would something about Barbour's picture -- it's fully deterministic, fully-populated, non-branching character -- actually clash with the more forking, subjunctive kind of timelessness he preferred in stories like "The Garden of Forking Paths"? Three of us in my philosophy reading group (funnily enough, all retired scientists) argued about this for a whole session last week and never landed anywhere, so don't leave me hanging with a wishy-washy "he'd probably have found it thought-provoking" -- give me an actual verdict and defend it with specifics.
