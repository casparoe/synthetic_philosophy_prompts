So a few weeks ago my group for our history project kind of fell apart bc one kid just never did his part, and it got me thinking about what actually makes something a "joint" action in the first place instead of just a bunch of people doing their own thing near each other. I went down a bit of a rabbit hole reading about "collective intentionality" and kept running into two names that seem to be arguing with each other: Michael Bratman and Margaret Gilbert. From what I can tell, Bratman thinks a shared intention is basically built out of each person's own individual intentions (like "I intend that we do X," and you intend the same thing), plus stuff like "meshing subplans" and common knowledge of all this, so theres nothing over and above the individuals and their own attitudes, no group mind needed. Gilbert on the other hand says a shared intention comes from a "joint commitment" that creates an actual obligation between the people involved, like a "plural subject" that owns the commitment, which is apparently why you're allowed to rebuke someone for not doing their part even if they never personally promised you anything.

Can you compare these two theories for me, and specifically say which one does a better job explaining that feeling of being wronged when a groupmate flakes on something you're supposed to be doing together? Like, can Bratman's individualist picture (just intentions + beliefs + mesh, no extra "we" ingredient) actually earn that sense of obligation, or does he secretly have to borrow something Gilbert-ish to make it work? And on the flip side, is Gilbert's plural subject/joint commitment thing too strong, like is it kinda "spooky" that a mutual, promise-less expression of willingness could generate a real obligation basically out of nothing?
