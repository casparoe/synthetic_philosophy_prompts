I spent thirty-five years as a condensed-matter theorist specializing in critical phenomena, and only since retiring three years ago have I begun to read the philosophy of science with any seriousness. Below is a short essay in which I try to make a case that has been forming in my mind for some time. I do not yet trust my own philosophical instincts, and before I inflict this on anyone with proper training in the subject, I would value your comments.

On the classical account of reduction associated with Ernest Nagel, one theory reduces to another when the laws of the first can be derived from the laws of the second together with bridge laws connecting the vocabulary of the two. Whatever the merits of this picture for tidier cases, such as the derivation of the ideal gas law from the kinetic theory, I want to argue that it cannot accommodate one of the genuine achievements of twentieth-century physics: the explanation of universality at continuous phase transitions.

Consider two systems that could hardly be more different at the microscopic level: a simple fluid such as carbon dioxide, held at its liquid–vapour critical point, and a uniaxial ferromagnet, held at its Curie point. The first is a disordered collection of molecules interacting through short-range van der Waals forces; the second is a lattice of localized magnetic moments coupled by exchange interactions. Yet the two systems share, to considerable numerical precision, the same critical exponents governing how the order parameter and correlation length behave as the critical point is approached — and so, for that matter, does every other system with the same dimensionality and order-parameter symmetry, however unlike it at the microscopic level. The renormalization group explains why: under repeated coarse-graining, a system's effective Hamiltonian flows through the space of possible Hamiltonians toward a fixed point, and only a handful of "relevant" directions near that fixed point survive to determine the universal behaviour; everything distinctive about the original microscopic Hamiltonian is an "irrelevant" direction washed out by the flow. Consequently, there is no single, determinate microphysical description standing in the bridge-law relation to a given critical exponent: infinitely many microscopically inequivalent Hamiltonians flow to the same fixed point and so produce the identical macroscopic number. This, I think, is not a practical failure of derivability, of the sort a Laplacean demon could still overcome by brute calculation — the irrelevance of the microscopic details is a structural feature of the fixed point itself. That looks to me like emergence in a full-blooded, and not merely epistemic, sense: close in spirit to Philip Anderson's old insistence that being able to reduce everything to fundamental constituents is not remotely the same thing as being able to reconstruct the phenomena of chemistry, biology or condensed-matter physics by working back up from those constituents alone.

I would be glad of your comments on three things in particular: first, whether the multiple-realizability of the fixed point really does defeat bridge-law reduction, or whether Nagel's requirement can be restated — at the level of the space of Hamiltonians rather than any single Hamiltonian — so as to survive the example; second, whether I have moved too quickly from a claim about the structure of renormalization-group explanations to a metaphysical claim about emergence, and if so, exactly where the argument overreaches; and third, whether there is a standard existing reply to this line of thought that I am failing to engage with — if so, I would rather you set it out on its own terms than simply point me toward it.
