So I'm a PhD student in econ working on social choice theory, and this week's rabbit hole has been Harsanyi's 1955 aggregation theorem. Everyone gets taught the one-line version in first-year micro: if individual and social preferences over lotteries both satisfy the vNM axioms, and the social ranking respects the Pareto condition, then the social welfare function has to be an affine (weighted) sum of the individual utility functions, with nonnegative weights. Harsanyi treated that as basically a proof of utilitarianism, weights and all. My advisor thinks its one of the great results in formal ethics, but she had me read Diamond's 1967 comment and some Sen from the 70s/80s for my quals, and now I'm stuck between them. The Diamond ex-ante/ex-post coin-flip case especially bugs me -- the idea that Pareto-at-the-level-of-lotteries can force you to be indifferent between a fair randomization and an arbitrary deterministic pick, as long as the expected utilities line up, even though pretty much everyone's fairness intuitions say the coin flip is better. And separately theres Sen's worry that the u_i's in the theorem are just vNM indices over gambles, with no guarantee they track "welfare" in any classical utilitarian sense -- so maybe the theorem is formally airtight but doesn't actually get you utilitarianism, just some affine aggregation of whatever preferences happen to satisfy vNM, which is a much weaker and less interesting claim.

Could you write me a dialogue between two people who are both genuinely good at this stuff -- one who thinks the aggregation theorem really is a substantial formal vindication of utilitarianism (or close enough to count), and one who's essentially channeling Diamond and Sen in thinking it doesn't deliver what Harsanyi advertised. I don't want a surface-level "here's what the theorem says" back-and-forth -- I want the skeptic to actually press the coin-flip case and the welfarism/interpretation worry, and I want the defender to genuinely engage with those specific moves (biting the bullet, redescribing the outcome space so fairness gets priced into the u_i's, denying that Sen's distinction is doing real work, arguing the objection proves too much against any aggregation procedure, or whatever route seems strongest) instead of just repeating that the math is valid. Have them respond to each other's actual last point rather than restate their own opening position, and let it run a good number of exchanges. It doesn't need to end in agreement, but I'd like them to at least pin down precisely where the disagreement really lives -- an axiom to reject, how to interpret "utility," what ex ante Pareto is even supposed to track, or something else.
