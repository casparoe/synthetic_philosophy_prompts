That interest eventually pulled me toward the Liar paradox, and from there into what logicians call the "revenge" problem: the observation that almost every serious solution on offer — Tarski's hierarchy of languages, Kripke's fixed-point construction with truth-value gaps, dialetheism, revision theories, contextualist views where the sentence's content is said to shift as you talk about it — seems to generate a new liar-like sentence that the theory's own resources can't handle, so the paradox just resurfaces one level up. I brought this up with a friend of mine who has a background in formal logic, expecting a quick answer, and we ended up going back and forth for an hour without agreeing on whether any of these solutions actually escapes revenge.

I'd like you to write out that kind of conversation properly, as a dialogue between two characters who are equally sharp and know the terrain well: a dialetheist, who holds that the Liar sentence is a "glut" — both true and false — and defends this using a paraconsistent logic in which the contradiction doesn't explode into triviality, and a paracomplete theorist working in Kripke's tradition, who holds instead that the Liar is a "gap," lacking a truth value altogether because it never gets grounded in the fixed-point construction. Please don't let them just restate their headline positions past each other. I want the paracomplete theorist to confront the dialetheist with a strengthened liar built specifically to rule out the "both" verdict (something like "this sentence is false and not true") and ask how a glut theorist absorbs that without triggering an infinite regress of ever-stronger contradictions, and I want the dialetheist to push back by asking whether the fixed-point theorist can actually say, in their own object language, that the Liar is a gap — without either regenerating the paradox or quietly borrowing the resources of a richer metalanguage, which would suggest the Tarskian hierarchy Kripke was trying to escape is still lurking underneath his construction. Each side should have a genuine rejoinder to the other's strongest move, so the dialogue ends with the disagreement sharpened rather than tidily resolved.
