I help run a small reading group that has spent the last few sessions on the units-of-selection debate: Dawkins' The Selfish Gene, several chapters of Sober and Wilson's Unto Others, and Hamilton's original 1964 papers on inclusive fitness. After our last meeting I set everyone a short written question, and before I circulate feedback I'd like an independent, careful grade on one participant's answer — mostly so I can check that I'm not being either too harsh or too generous myself.

The question: "Dawkins has argued that disputes over 'the unit of selection' are largely a matter of bookkeeping — any apparent case of group selection can be redescribed without loss as selection on genes or individuals. Sober and Wilson deny this: in their view, selection among groups can sometimes be a distinct causal process, not merely an alternative way of describing the same underlying facts. Using Hamilton's account of worker sterility in the Hymenoptera as your test case, explain this disagreement and say which side the example actually supports."

The participant's answer, verbatim:

"Because of haplodiploidy, a worker's relatedness to her full sisters (r = 3/4) exceeds her relatedness to her own hypothetical offspring (r = 1/2): she shares an identical paternal chromosome set with every sister, on top of the ordinary 50% chance of sharing any given maternally-derived allele. Since Hamilton's rule, rB > C, is easier to satisfy when r is larger, workers that help their mother raise more sisters instead of reproducing themselves can be favored by selection. This is exactly the kind of case Sober and Wilson have in mind: sterility is costly to the individual worker but beneficial to the hive, and it spreads because hives with more cooperative workers out-produce hives with fewer of them. Dawkins can redescribe this in the language of allele frequencies if he likes, but that doesn't change which process is actually doing the causal work — the trait is being maintained by competition between colonies, not simply within them. So although the 'bookkeeping' framing may fit other examples well enough, Hamilton's own result actually counts in Sober and Wilson's favor: it shows that group selection, in their strong sense, is really out there producing real instances of evolved altruism."
