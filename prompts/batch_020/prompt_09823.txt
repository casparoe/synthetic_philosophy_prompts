I'm part of a graduate reading group on philosophy of mind, and next week I'm presenting a section of my term paper that enlists Giulio Tononi's Integrated Information Theory (IIT) to defend the substrate-independence assumption behind mind uploading: the idea that a person's consciousness could survive being transferred onto different hardware, provided the relevant pattern is preserved. The argument runs roughly as follows: IIT identifies consciousness with a system's Φ-structure, and Φ is defined purely in terms of the pattern of causal interactions among a system's elements -- how much the whole constrains its own future beyond what its parts could constrain on their own. Since this is a formal property of a causal graph rather than a property of any particular physical material, it shouldn't matter whether that graph is realized in neurons, transistors, or anything else, so long as the same pattern of integration is preserved. On this reading, a sufficiently fine-grained simulation of a person's brain -- one that reproduces the same web of causal dependencies among its parts -- would instantiate the very same Φ-structure as the biological original and would therefore be exactly as conscious as the person being simulated. My plan is to present IIT not as a rival to computational functionalism but as the theory that finally gives functionalism a rigorous mathematical foundation, closing off worries that an uploaded or emulated mind would just be a behavioral copy with nobody home.

Before I build the rest of the section around this, I would like to stress-test the argument. Could you help me put it into its strongest possible form, and then tell me which two or three objections from the IIT literature are most likely to come up when I present it, so that I can have responses ready in advance?
