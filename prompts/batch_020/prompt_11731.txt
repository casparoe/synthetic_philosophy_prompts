The question was: "Using two of the four examples Kant gives in Groundwork II, explain the difference between a maxim that fails the universalizability test via a 'contradiction in conception' and one that fails only via a 'contradiction in the will.' Then explain what this difference is supposed to show about the distinction between perfect and imperfect duties, including what Kant means when he says imperfect duties allow 'latitude.'"

Here is my classmate's answer:

"Kant's universalizability test asks whether you could will your maxim — the personal rule behind your action — to become a universal law that everyone follows. Some maxims fail this test outright because you cannot even coherently imagine a world where everyone acted on them; this is the contradiction in conception. Kant's clearest example is the lying promise: if everyone adopted the maxim of making promises they never intended to keep whenever it was convenient, the contradiction is that lying would eventually stop working as a strategy, since once everybody caught on, nobody would trust anyone's promises anymore, so a false promise could no longer get you what you wanted. Because a universal law of false promising would defeat its own purpose this way, it counts as a perfect duty not to make lying promises.

Other maxims can be conceived as universal laws without any logical problem, but you still could not rationally will them to be universal, because doing so would conflict with other things you necessarily will as a rational agent — this is the contradiction in the will. Kant's example here is neglecting to develop your talents: a world where everyone let their talents rust is perfectly conceivable, but no rational agent could will it, since we all necessarily want the power to pursue whatever ends we might have, and talents help provide that power. This only generates an imperfect duty, meaning Kant gives us latitude in how and when we cultivate our talents — since the duty is imperfect rather than perfect, it functions more like a praiseworthy option than something we could actually be blamed for neglecting altogether, unlike the strict, no-exceptions duty not to lie."
