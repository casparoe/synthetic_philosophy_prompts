I've spent years reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum, and one post that has stayed with me is Richard Ngo's "Realism about Rationality," which traces much of the disagreement in AI safety to whether one expects intelligence to be governed by some simple, unified, discoverable theory, or instead expects it to be an agglomeration of heuristics with no elegant core. I recently found myself arguing with a friend who works adjacent to an interpretability team about whether an analogous "realism about interpretability" holds: when techniques like sparse dictionary learning turn up a set of "features" or "circuits" inside a trained network, is there a fact of the matter about which decomposition is the network's real ontology, or is every such decomposition just one convenient compression among many, chosen for legibility to us rather than discovered in the network?

I would like you to help me think this through in some depth, rather than just gesturing at "it depends what you mean by representation." Concretely, I'd like you to engage with three specific complications that any realist view has to reckon with: first, that superposition means a network can represent more features than it has dimensions, so that any recovered "feature" is already an inference rather than something read directly off the weights; second, that sparse autoencoders are known to exhibit feature splitting, where a single feature found at one dictionary size resolves into several more specific ones at a larger dictionary size, apparently without a principled stopping point; and third, the privileged-basis worry, namely that most of a network's computation is invariant under changes of basis except at the pointwise nonlinearities, which makes it unclear why directions that happen to be convenient for a human-chosen sparsity penalty should track anything the network itself "cares about." Does the non-uniqueness of these decompositions settle the question against realism, or could a more careful realist accommodate it, in the way that, say, Dennett's "real patterns" lets a pattern be genuinely there in the data while still being somewhat description-relative? I'm less interested in a survey of positions people have staked out than in your own reconstruction of the strongest argument on each side and, if you're willing to commit, where you actually come down.
