Two former students of mine, now financial analysts, told me about an argument they'd had over a colleague of theirs, Ben. In the same week, Ben recommended two different speculative biotech stocks to two different clients, in each case after reading nothing but the executive summary of the due-diligence report. One trial succeeded and that client tripled her money; the other failed and that client lost most of his retirement savings. Roughly, the argument went like this.

(1) Elena said Ben is equally culpable either way: his conduct was identical, the trial outcomes were things he neither caused nor foresaw, and judging the second case more harshly is not moral seriousness but superstition -- like blaming a driver more for hitting a pedestrian who happens to be someone's grandmother.

(2) Sam objected: we hold people liable for the harms they actually cause all the time, in tort law and in ordinary life. That isn't confusion -- it's most of what distinguishes negligence from carelessness that comes to nothing.

(3) Elena pressed back: if outcomes did real moral work, the lucky client should be sending Ben a thank-you note and his firm should be considering him for a bonus, purely because a coin landed his way. Nobody thinks that -- so whatever explains the harsher reaction to the second case isn't defensible on reflection.

(4) Sam replied that the asymmetry isn't ad hoc: failures can deserve more blame than flukes deserve praise. We already prosecute negligence that kills someone far more severely than identical negligence that harms no one, and that's not a bug in the practice, it's the point of it.

(5) Elena pushed further: if only what's within Ben's control matters, Sam owes an account of why that standard applies downstream of the recommendation but not upstream of it. Ben's sloppiness -- his overconfidence, his impatience that week -- is as much a product of temperament he never chose as the trial results were. If downstream luck doesn't excuse on Sam's view, upstream luck, which made Ben careless to begin with, shouldn't either -- yet Sam wants to count one and not the other.

(6) Sam answered that this proves too much: accept it, and nobody is responsible for anything, since every trait traces back to some earlier cause the agent never chose. Since we obviously do hold people responsible, he said, something in Elena's argument must be wrong -- probably the assumption that only what's fully within one's control can be assessed at all.

Neither of them budged, and I kept my own view to myself.
