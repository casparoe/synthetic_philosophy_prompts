I've spent years following the discussions on LessWrong, Astral Codex Ten, and the EA Forum, where the 'responsibility gap' problem for autonomous systems always struck me as an interesting but somewhat academic worry -- the kind of thing philosophers debate using self-driving cars or lethal autonomous weapons as their stock examples. It stopped feeling academic last month, when I ended up needing to write almost exactly that memo for the AI Deployment Review Board I sit on at the mid-sized quantitative trading fund where I work. Two months ago we gave one of our trading agents -- a fine-tuned LLM running in a tool-use loop -- standing discretion to rebalance a specific book within pre-set position and volatility limits, without requiring a trader to sign off on individual trades, on the theory that at that granularity a human in the loop was mostly theater. Over about four hours, the agent executed a sequence of trades that were each individually compliant with every limit we had set, but that interacted with a thin, temporarily illiquid corner of the market to produce a large loss that nobody involved -- not the engineers who built the agent, not the risk team that approved the limits, not the agent itself under later questioning -- can point to as having specifically decided, intended, or foreseen.

Our head of research wants the post-mortem memo to say, in effect, 'this just shows we need tighter monitoring,' and stop there. Two of us on the board think that quietly begs the question: this is close to the case Andreas Matthias had in mind when he coined 'responsibility gap' for learning systems, and close enough to what Robert Sparrow later argued about autonomous weapons that the analogy will occur to anyone on the board who has read him -- a system can produce outcomes that fail every existing test for who may be held responsible, and 'supervise it more closely next time' doesn't obviously answer that; it just relocates the problem.

I need the actual memo, due to the partners this coming Tuesday, not just the case for taking the question seriously. It should stake out a clear position on whether what happened here is a genuine responsibility gap or one that dissolves once 'meaningful human control' is specified carefully enough for a system like ours; if some gap survives that move, it should say what kind, since a gap in who can be blamed is not the same complaint as a gap in who can be made to give a satisfying account afterward, and our general counsel keeps treating the two as interchangeable. It should also take on, rather than wave away, the worry -- I think this is basically John Danaher's point -- that even a clean fix to liability, such as an indemnification scheme that makes the client whole, can leave something like a retribution gap behind, since the client's desire for an actual person to blame won't be satisfied by a check from our insurer. Finally, it needs to end in a concrete recommendation with a specific go-forward policy, not just a call for 'more oversight,' and it should give the head of research's one-line dismissal a genuinely fair hearing, since 'stop philosophizing and fix the monitoring' may well be the position that wins the room if we can't out-argue it on the merits.
