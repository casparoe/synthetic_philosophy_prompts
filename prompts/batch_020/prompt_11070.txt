Over dinner last week, a friend and I spent nearly two hours arguing about Robin Hanson's futarchy proposal — the idea that institutions should "vote on values but bet on beliefs" by using conditional prediction markets, rather than ordinary politics, to choose between policies. She thinks it's one of the few genuinely promising mechanisms for getting real information into decisions that are currently dominated by rhetoric and dueling consultants; I kept coming back to the worry that the scheme doesn't remove politics from a decision so much as relocate it, and that it invites a specific kind of gaming that ordinary voting doesn't. Neither of us budged, and I've been turning the argument over ever since.

It isn't purely academic for me, because I sit on my city's Civic Decision-Making Task Force, a small body the mayor convened two years ago to evaluate unconventional governance mechanisms before they reach the council, and we have just been handed a real test case. The city has been deadlocked for three years over a $400 million transportation bond, split between a bus-rapid-transit expansion ("Plan A") and a road-capacity-and-repaving package ("Plan B"), with each side's hired consultants producing studies that conveniently favor their sponsors' preferred plan. A local economist and a civic-tech nonprofit have jointly proposed that, instead of another referendum, the city run a one-time futarchy pilot: the council would first vote to fix a single composite "Quality of Movement Index" (weighting commute-time changes, small-business tax receipts, pedestrian and cyclist injury rates, and a resident satisfaction survey), then open two subsidized, two-month conditional markets — one pricing the Index five years out if Plan A is built, one if Plan B is built — to any resident and to vetted professional forecasters, with the city bound in advance by charter amendment to build whichever plan the markets imply will produce the higher Index value. The Task Force is splitting roughly along the same lines my friend and I did: some members think this is exactly the sort of mechanism that could break the gridlock without pretending either side's consultants are neutral; others worry about how thin the market will be in a city our size, about the coalition that loses the index-weighting fight going in already convinced the dice are loaded, and especially about the incentive of whoever loses the bet to quietly under-deliver on the winning plan so that their prediction looks vindicated after the fact.

I've been asked to draft the Task Force's recommendation memo to the council ahead of our vote next month, and I want to think it through properly before I commit anything to paper. Could you write that memo: a recommendation — endorse the pilot, reject it, or endorse some modified version — together with reasoning that the losing side on the Task Force could actually engage with, rather than a diplomatic non-answer? I'd especially like it to take seriously the sabotage/moral-hazard problem and the fact that, since only one branch of the bet is ever actually implemented, we will never be able to confirm which market was right, and to weigh those honestly against whatever is genuinely strongest in the pilot's favor.
