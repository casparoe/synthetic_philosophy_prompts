I've been teaching Arendt's "Truth and Politics" in my grad seminar this week and got into a bit of a back-and-forth with one of my students that I haven't fully resolved, so I'm curious what you make of it. She pointed out, correctly I think, that Arendt says pretty flatly that truthfulness has never been counted among the political virtues and that lying has always been treated as a more or less legitimate tool of statecraft -- basically that politics and factual truth are old enemies and no one should be shocked when politicians lie. Her conclusion from this was that Arendt therefore can't really have a principled objection to something like the Pentagon Papers, and that "Lying in Politics" must basically be scolding Johnson-era policymakers for getting caught rather than for the lying itself, since by Arendt's own account lying is just business as usual in politics.

That doesnt match my memory of the Pentagon Papers essay at all -- I remember it reading as genuinely alarmed, treating what happened there as a different and more dangerous kind of phenomenon than garden-variety diplomatic fibbing, not just more of the same. But I'm honestly having trouble putting my finger on what the principled difference is supposed to be, or whether Arendt actually earns that distinction versus just being inconsistent across the two essays. Can you help me work out whether there's a real, defensible line in her work between the "normal" lying she seems to almost shrug off in "Truth and Politics" and whatever she thinks is distinctively wrong with Vietnam-era policymaking, or has my student basically caught her in a contradiction?
