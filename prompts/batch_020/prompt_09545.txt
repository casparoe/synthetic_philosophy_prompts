I've been reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum for years, and one write-up that has stuck with me since it came out is Paul Christiano, Ajeya Cotra, and Mark Xu's ARC report on Eliciting Latent Knowledge (ELK). The setup, if I recall it correctly, asks us to imagine a very capable predictor trained to answer questions about a vault containing a diamond, based on camera and sensor input, and the worry is that once someone tampers with the sensors (say, by hiding a theft behind a screen that shows the diamond still sitting there), a "reporter" trained to answer "is the diamond still in the vault?" has no training-time incentive to distinguish between two strategies that perform identically on the training distribution: one that translates the predictor's actual internal representation of the world (what the report calls, in effect, the direct translator), and one that instead simulates what a human overseer would conclude from the possibly-tampered sensor data (the human simulator). Since both strategies agree wherever the human isn't fooled, nothing in the training signal itself seems to favor the former over the latter.

Talking this over with a friend who is more of a philosophy person, she immediately said this reminded her of Quine's indeterminacy of translation from Word and Object: a linguist trying to translate a native informant's utterance of "gavagai" upon seeing a rabbit can never determine, from the totality of possible behavioral evidence, whether it means "rabbit," "undetached rabbit part," or "rabbit stage," because every translation manual compatible with the evidence is, on Quine's view, equally correct -- there is simply no further fact about "the" meaning left for the evidence to settle. That does sound uncomfortably close to the underdetermination between the direct translator and the human simulator.

I'd like your help thinking through whether this analogy actually holds up, rather than just taking it on faith. Concretely: is the ELK problem best understood as a special case of Quinean indeterminacy applied to neural networks -- in which case Quine's own moral, that there is no fact of the matter to be discovered rather than merely an epistemic difficulty in discovering it, would suggest ELK is unsolvable in full generality rather than just difficult -- or are there principled disanalogies (for instance, that researchers have causal access to the model's internals, can run interventions, and can shape the training distribution and architecture in ways a radical translator confined to observed behavior cannot) that make the two problems only superficially similar? I would also be curious whether Davidson's later radical interpretation project, with its principle of charity, offers anything here -- either a resource ELK researchers could borrow, or a further disanalogy -- that Quine's version doesn't. Please defend a specific verdict rather than simply cataloguing similarities and differences.
