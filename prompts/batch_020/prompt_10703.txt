okay so I'm procrastinating on my diss chapter on social choice theory and this thing has been bugging me for weeks, figured I'd make you deal with it instead of my advisor. Harsanyi's aggregation theorem gets thrown around constantly as basically a mathematical proof of utilitarianism: if both individuals and society satisfy the vNM axioms and you add a Pareto-indifference condition, the social utility function has to be an affine combination of the individual ones. Sounds like a knockdown argument for (weighted) utilitarianism. But everyone whos actually read Sen brings up the fact that vNM utility is only unique up to affine transformation per person, so the weights a_i in that combination are basically hostage to an arbitrary choice of scale -- you don't get equal-weight classical utilitarianism out of the theorem, you get some "generalized" weighted version that could in principle favor one person over another for no good reason. Then Weymark tried to referee the Sen/Harsanyi fight and only sort of succeeded, and seperately there's Diamond's old coin-flip counterexample suggesting that even the ex-ante Pareto condition Harsanyi needs has kind of unfair implications once you care about the fairness of a procedure and not just expected outcomes.

Write me an essay that actually takes a side instead of doing the diplomatic "there are considerations on both sides" thing: does Harsanyi's theorem give real philosophical support to utilitarianism, or is it a neat piece of math that's been way oversold as ethics? Engage properly with the interpersonal-comparability problem and the ex-ante/ex-post worry, don't just gesture at them, and land on a verdict you're willing to defend.
