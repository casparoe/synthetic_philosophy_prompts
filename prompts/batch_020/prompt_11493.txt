I work as a backend engineer on my company's experimentation platform -- the internal system that runs our A/B tests -- and a few weeks ago we had a small blow-up over a growth-team experiment. The team had been watching the dashboard for a checkout-flow test and stopped it the moment the p-value dropped below 0.05, only for our resident data scientist to point out that this kind of "peeking" invalidates the p-value, since the whole logic of the test assumes the sample size was fixed in advance rather than chosen based on the data itself. That set off a long Slack thread in which someone linked to an old Bayesian argument claiming that the stopping rule a researcher uses shouldn't matter at all to what the data actually tell you, because the resulting likelihood function ends up being essentially the same either way. I don't have the statistics background to referee that argument myself, and even after reading around a bit -- people keep bringing this up in connection with the replication crisis in psychology and medicine -- I still can't tell whether both sides have a point or one of them is just wrong.

Could you write me a dialogue between a committed frequentist statistician and a committed Bayesian statistician who are each independently handed the exact same log from an experiment like ours -- one where the team stopped as soon as it crossed significance -- and asked to say what, if anything, it shows? I'd like them to get into the actual technical substance rather than trade slogans: the frequentist should explain concretely why the sampling distribution, and therefore the p-value, depends on the stopping rule (the classic comparison between a fixed-sample-size design and a design where you sample until you observe some fixed number of successes would be a good illustration), while the Bayesian should lay out the likelihood principle and argue for why identical data ought to license identical conclusions regardless of when and why the experimenter decided to stop. I'd also like each of them to press on the practical cost of the other's position -- the frequentist on what happens to real long-run error rates once teams are free to peek, the Bayesian on whether fixes like pre-registration and alpha-spending are really solving the underlying problem or just papering over a framework that cares about the experimenter's private intentions rather than the data that was actually collected. Neither of them should have an easy time of it against the other; I'd like to come away still genuinely unsure who's right.
