I started a short-term research fellowship with a small AI-safety group about three weeks ago, and among my first assigned readings was the old MIRI material on "tiling agents" and the so-called Löbian obstacle to self-trust: roughly, the fact that a formal reasoner generally cannot certify, within its own proof system, that whatever it proves is true, since Löb's theorem forces any theory that proves "if I can prove P, then P" to already have proved P outright. My background is in mathematics, but I never took formal logic beyond a first course, so I can follow the statement of Löb's theorem and even reconstruct the short standard argument that recovers Gödel's second incompleteness theorem from it by letting P be an explicit falsehood like "0 = 1." What I don't yet have is a real feel for why this is a load-bearing obstacle rather than an artifact of an unnecessarily rigid formalization, and I'm presenting a summary of all this to the group this coming Thursday.

Concretely, I'd like help with three things. First, an intuitive, non-symbol-pushing explanation of why a consistent theory can never adopt the schema "if I can prove P, then P" for arbitrary P about itself — the kind of gut-level story one gives for why Cantor's diagonal argument or Arrow's theorem has to come out the way it does, rather than a restatement of the diagonal lemma. Second, I keep wanting to propose two "obvious" fixes, and I'd like to understand exactly why each one fails: (a) why doesn't it help for the successor agent to run on a syntactically distinct but logically isomorphic copy of the predecessor's proof system, since that seems to sidestep any literal self-reference; and (b) why doesn't simply adding an explicit soundness or reflection axiom solve the problem, rather than merely relocating an identical difficulty one level up the hierarchy?

Finally, if it isn't too much of a tangent, I'd appreciate a brief and honest assessment of whether newer proposals such as logical induction or reflective oracles genuinely dissolve the Löbian obstacle, or whether they instead substitute a different, non-proof-theoretic notion of trust with its own, differently shaped limitations. I've seen people gesture at this contrast without really spelling out the argument, and I'd rather not repeat a claim on Thursday that I can't actually defend if someone pushes back on it.
