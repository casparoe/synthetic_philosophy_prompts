In AP Biology this year, we spent a few weeks on molecular genetics, and my teacher kept calling DNA "the code of life," pulling up the codon table the same way you'd pull up a cipher key. It got me curious enough that I brought it up with my older sister, who is finishing a philosophy of science degree, and she told me this is actually a live dispute among philosophers of biology, not the settled fact it's presented as in class.

On one side, she said, there's a position — she specifically mentioned John Maynard Smith — holding that the genetic code is a real, literal code, not just a convenient figure of speech. Part of the case rests on the fact that codon assignments look arbitrary rather than chemically forced, which is apparently what Crick meant by calling the code a "frozen accident": an arbitrary-but-reliably-followed correspondence is exactly what you'd expect from a genuine code backed by a convention, unlike a merely lawlike physical signal. Combine that with the fact that natural selection shaped both the sequences and the machinery that reads them (ribosomes, tRNAs) to work together, and you get something that behaves like a message passed between a sender and a receiver who share a code, not just a correlation between two variables. On the other side, she pointed me to developmental systems theorists like Susan Oyama and, later, Paul Griffiths and Russell Gray, who defend what she called a "parity thesis": since development depends on plenty of other factors that get passed down just as reliably as DNA does — cytoplasmic structure in the egg, maternal effects, epigenetic marks — singling out DNA as "the" carrier of developmental instructions is arbitrary, more a holdover from old preformationist thinking than a real discovery about how genes work.

Could you compare these two positions in some depth and say which one strikes you as more defensible? What I'm most stuck on is whether the parity thesis actually shows that DNA isn't a code at all, or only that it isn't the sole carrier of developmental information — those seem like they could come apart, but as my sister explained it, the argument treats the second point as though it establishes the first, and I can't tell whether that move actually works or whether it's the weak link. Please don't just declare a winner; I'd like to know what each side is getting right as well as wrong.
