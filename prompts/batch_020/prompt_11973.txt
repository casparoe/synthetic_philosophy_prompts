I'm a retired field ecologist — spent about thirty years doing population surveys and then arguing with committees about the best way to present the numbers to non-specialists (city councils, funding bodies, the general public). So a few months ago, at the philosophy discussion group I joined after retiring, someone brought up Otto Neurath's Isotype project — the Vienna pictorial-statistics system with the little repeated icons, where one symbol always stands for a fixed number of unemployed workers, or tractors, or whatever's being counted — and I got a bit obsessed, because its basically the exact argument I had a hundred times over the years about bar charts vs. pictograms vs. plain tables.

Reading more about Neurath, though, something started to bug me. This is the same guy who, against Schlick and to some extent Carnap, spent years arguing that there's no neutral, incorrigible layer of "protocol sentences" that knowledge can rest on — his famous image of scientists as sailors who have to rebuild their ship plank by plank while still out at sea, since there's no dry dock to retreat to. That's about as thoroughly anti-foundationalist and holistic a picture of knowledge as you can get. And yet his whole pitch for Isotype leans hard on pictures being a uniquely transparent, unifying medium — his actual slogan was "words divide, pictures unite" — readable across languages and education levels in a way ordinary verbal reports supposedly aren't. He hedges a little, admittedly: he calls Isotype a "helping language" that never fully replaces words, and he's upfront that it takes a trained "transformer" to turn raw data into a picture. But the overall sales pitch still sounds to me like its smuggling back in exactly the kind of privileged, almost unmediated access to "the facts" that the boat metaphor was supposed to rule out for verbal reports.

So here's what I'd like: can you argue, as strongly as you can, that Neurath's optimism about picture-statistics as a specially clear and universal medium for empirical fact is in real tension with his own anti-foundationalist, holistic epistemology from the protocol-sentence debate? Don't give me a wishy-washy "well, it depends, both sides have a point" — pick a side and build the strongest case you can that these two halves of his thought don't actually cohere, and deal with the obvious rejoinder (the "helping language"/pragmatic-tool defense) head-on rather than dodging it. I've read enough dense journal articles in my life by now that you don't need to hold back on the jargon.
