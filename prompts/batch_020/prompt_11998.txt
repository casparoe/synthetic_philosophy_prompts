I've noticed that St. Petersburg is the paradox that always gets invoked in LessWrong-style discussions of unbounded utility functions and Pascal's mugging, while Nover and Hájek's Pasadena game — introduced in their 2004 paper "Vexing Expectations" — receives almost no attention there, even though it strikes me as posing the deeper problem. Recall the setup: a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads, and if that happens on toss n, you are paid 2^n/n dollars when n is odd and must pay 2^n/n dollars when n is even. Summed in the natural order n = 1, 2, 3, ..., the expectation is the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2 — but because that series is only conditionally convergent, Riemann's rearrangement theorem guarantees that reordering the very same countable set of outcomes can make the sum equal any real number, diverge to either infinity, or oscillate without ever settling on a value. Compare this to the classical St. Petersburg game, whose analogous sum has only non-negative terms and so diverges to infinity however you reorder it.

Is the Pasadena game's failure a genuinely distinct and deeper problem for expected utility theory than St. Petersburg's mere divergence to infinity, or is it ultimately just a dressed-up instance of the same underlying difficulty with unbounded gambles? And does a move like the one proposed by Christian Tarsney and others — falling back on stochastic dominance once expected value breaks down — actually address what's distinctively wrong with the Pasadena case, or does it merely paper over the St. Petersburg-style symptom while leaving the deeper issue untouched?
