Question 3:

Consider the following case. Naomi is an experienced accountant reviewing a client's tax return for an error. She is informed, truthfully, that she has been given a pill, as part of an unrelated drug trial, that is known to impair arithmetical reasoning in roughly a third of the people who take it — causing them to make mistakes while feeling every bit as confident and lucid as usual. (The other two-thirds are unaffected, and there is no way to tell, from the inside, which group one is in.) Naomi works through the return twice, locates a specific transposition error, and becomes highly confident she has found the mistake. As it happens, she is one of the two-thirds unaffected by the drug, and she is right.

(a) (5 points) Explain what epistemologists mean by "higher-order evidence," and explain why the information about the pill counts as higher-order evidence for Naomi with respect to her belief about the transposition error.

(b) (5 points) "Level-splitting" views hold that Naomi can be rational in retaining full confidence in her answer while also being rational in believing that her total evidence does not support that level of confidence. Explain the main consideration in favor of a level-splitting view of this case.

(c) (10 points) Sophie Horowitz has argued that level-splitting views commit us to the existence of rational epistemic akrasia, and that this is a strong reason to reject them in favor of views on which higher-order evidence like the pill can rationally require lowering confidence even when, as here, it turns out to be misleading. Reconstruct her argument as best you can, and then briefly assess it.

Student's answer:

(a) Higher-order evidence is evidence about the reliability of one's own reasoning or evidence-gathering, as opposed to first-order evidence, which bears directly on the truth of the proposition in question. Learning that you took a pill that causes arithmetic errors doesn't tell you anything about the actual transposition error on the tax return — it doesn't point to any specific step in Naomi's reasoning being wrong — it just tells her something about how trustworthy her process of reasoning is likely to be on this occasion. That is exactly why it counts as higher-order rather than first-order evidence in her case: it bears on her reliability as a reasoner, not on the return itself.

(b) The main motivation for level-splitting is that the pill evidence is purely statistical: it tells Naomi something about people who take the pill in general, but it doesn't engage with any actual step of her calculation. Since she in fact checked her work twice and reasoned correctly, it seems wrong to say she should doubt her conclusion on the strength of a fact that has nothing to do with whether she actually made an error — especially given that, by hypothesis, there is no way for her to check from the inside whether she is in the affected third. If merely statistical evidence about a reference class could always override impeccable first-order reasoning, good reasoners would become extremely easy to talk out of correct conclusions.

(c) Horowitz's argument trades on the analogy with Aristotle's discussion of akrasia in the Nicomachean Ethics, where the weak-willed person judges that she should not do something and does it anyway; Horowitz borrows this diagnosis to argue that a Naomi who is rational in believing the return contains a transposition error while also rational in believing her evidence doesn't support that belief is in exactly the same kind of divided, self-undermining state, and that no plausible theory of rationality should permit such a state. Since level-splitting views generate exactly this state in cases like Naomi's, she concludes that we should instead accept the equal-weight view: that Naomi's confidence should track her total evidence, including the merely statistical evidence about the pill, so she should lower her confidence even though, as it happens, she was not affected by the drug. I think this is basically right: it seems clearly irrational to hold a belief that you yourself think your evidence doesn't support, and asking Naomi to hedge a little here seems like a small price to pay for ruling that out in general.
