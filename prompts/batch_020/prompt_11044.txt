I'm a PhD student in economics, and a few of us from the stats and CS departments run an informal reading group on decision-making under uncertainty. We recently spent a couple of sessions on the Kelly criterion and on the broader idea, sometimes labeled "geometric rationality," that maximizing the long-run geometric growth rate of wealth deserves some privileged normative status, rather than just being what a log-utility maximizer happens to want. Two regulars, Felix and Naomi, got into a running disagreement about this that the rest of us never managed to settle, and I'd like a more disinterested read on who was actually right.

Felix's opening claim was that full-Kelly betting isn't merely a reasonable strategy relative to a taste for log utility -- it's uniquely rational, for two reasons: first, a Kelly bettor almost surely ends up wealthier than anyone running a materially different fixed-fraction strategy, given a long enough horizon; second, the same "geometric," multiply-then-take-a-root logic keeps turning up as the right answer in other choice problems too (Nash bargaining solutions, Thompson sampling), which suggested to him that ordinary expected-value reasoning, not geometric growth maximization, is the thing that actually owes us a justification. Naomi's response targeted both prongs: mathematically, maximizing long-run growth rate just is maximizing expected log wealth per period, so whatever is "unique" about Kelly betting is exactly as unique as log utility is among concave utility functions -- an application of ordinary expected-utility theory, not a rival to it -- and the fact that formally similar geometric-mean formulas surface in bargaining or bandit problems doesn't yet show they're all expressions of one deep principle rather than an algebraic coincidence. Felix's comeback appealed to ergodicity: the wealth process generated by repeated multiplicative bets is non-ergodic, so the average outcome across a large ensemble of hypothetical bettors -- exactly what ordinary expected-value reasoning tracks -- diverges from the average outcome that any single persisting bettor actually lives through over time; on this view, log wealth isn't an arbitrary utility choice here, it's forced by which statistic corresponds to anyone's real trajectory. Naomi's rejoinder was that this only shows the time-average is the typical or representative outcome of a trajectory, not that anyone is rationally required to maximize it: an agent who genuinely cares about the tails -- the small chance of doing much better or much worse than the almost-sure long-run number -- hasn't made any mistake that the ergodicity point identifies, so we're still owed an independent reason why log wealth, rather than some other concave or convex transform, is the one rationality demands.

Could you go through Felix and Naomi's exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'm also curious whether either of them is quietly conceding, or missing, something the other one needs. Please close with your own explicit verdict, in a sentence or two, on the harder question underneath their whole argument: does the ergodicity point actually make full-Kelly betting rationally mandatory -- and not merely permissible -- for someone who expects to face many repeated bets, or does Naomi's last move basically defeat that stronger claim?
