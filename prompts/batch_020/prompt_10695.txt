I'm about two-thirds of the way through a novel about three generations of a German academic family, and there's a chapter I keep avoiding because I don't trust myself to write it convincingly yet. The character stuck in it is the grandfather, a retired philosophy professor who wrote his dissertation decades ago on Hegel's Philosophy of Right and never really recovered from it. At Christmas dinner his grandson -- who works for some arms-control outfit in Geneva and is a little drunk -- half-mockingly asks the old man what his beloved Hegel would actually make of nuclear deterrence. I need the professor's answer to be the emotional and intellectual center of the chapter, and it has to be something that would make an actual Hegel scholar nod along, not just something that sounds impressive to everyone else at the table.

Here's where I get stuck, and why I need your help rather than just winging it. I know enough to know Hegel didn't treat war as simply a evil to be minimized -- he thought it had a real positive role: it reminds a society that private property, comfort, even individual life aren't absolute; it keeps civil society from going soft and corrupt during long stretches of undisturbed peace; and there's that whole business, going back to the master-slave section, about people only really proving their freedom when they're willing to risk death rather than cling to bare survival. He also thought Kant's dream of "perpetual peace" was naive, since there's no judge standing over sovereign states the way there's law standing over individuals, which is why war remains the last resort that settles what treaties can't. What I can't work out on my own, and what I need the professor to actually wrestle with instead of dodge, is whether any of that survives once the weapons in question don't produce a winner, or a contest, or even really a "war" in his sense at all -- just simultaneous, silent annihilation. Does his whole framework quietly assume there's always a history left over afterward to redeem the suffering, and does that assumption just snap once the weapons can end the story instead of advancing it? I'd honestly rather you tell me it collapses and show me exactly where, than help me fudge a graceful save -- the scene only works if he's actually grappling with something real, not winning a rigged argument.
