I've been working through Frege's philosophy of language for a seminar paper, alongside some contemporary work on two-dimensional semantics, and I keep getting stuck on the same question, so I'd like your help thinking it through properly. In "The Thought" (1918), Frege argues that thoughts (Gedanken) belong to a "third realm," distinct from both the physical world and the private, perishing mental images (Vorstellungen) that occupy individual minds: a thought is objective, timelessly true or false, and graspable by many thinkers at once rather than owned by any one of them. In that same essay he also allows that fully expressing a thought sometimes requires the sentence to be supplemented by facts about the context of utterance that the words themselves do not contain: someone who wants to assert today the very thought he truly expressed yesterday using the word "today" must replace it with "yesterday," on pain of expressing a different thought with the same words. And in a well-known footnote to "On Sense and Reference," he concedes that different competent speakers may attach somewhat different senses to one and the same proper name -- his example is "Aristotle," where one person's associated description ("pupil of Plato and teacher of Alexander") differs from another's, which instead builds in facts about his birth in Stagira -- treating this as a tolerable imperfection of ordinary language rather than a threat to his theory of sense.

None of this stopped Kripke, decades later in Naming and Necessity, from arguing that names cannot in general have their reference fixed by descriptions competent speakers associate with them, since names are rigid designators while descriptions typically are not, and from insisting that truths like "water is H2O" are necessary yet knowable only a posteriori. In response, philosophers working in a broadly two-dimensionalist tradition -- starting from Kaplan's distinction between the "character" and "content" of an indexical and from Stalnaker's context-sensitive "diagonal" propositions, and developed into an explicitly epistemic apparatus by Frank Jackson and David Chalmers -- proposed assigning every expression two intensions at once: a primary (epistemic) intension, mapping epistemically possible scenarios to extensions and tracking what a reasoner can know a priori, and a secondary (subjunctive) intension, mapping metaphysically possible worlds to extensions and tracking Kripke's necessary a posteriori facts. The explicit hope, especially in Chalmers's work, is to preserve something close to Frege's original cognitive-significance intuitions -- why "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is informative even though Hesperus is Phosphorus -- while fully honoring Kripke's modal arguments.

What I would like from you is a specific, well-defended verdict, not a survey of considerations on both sides, on how Frege himself, read on the basis of his actual and sometimes surprising doctrines rather than the flattened "definite-description theorist" of introductory courses, would most plausibly have judged this rescue attempt. Would he have welcomed the primary intension as finally making rigorous something he had only gestured toward with "sense" and with his remarks on indexical thoughts, or would he have rejected the whole apparatus as one more piece of the psychologism he spent his career opposing, on the grounds that "epistemic possibility" individuates its scenarios by what an idealized reasoner can or cannot rule out a priori rather than by the mind-independent standing he insisted senses have in the third realm? Does his own low-tech handling of "today" and "yesterday" -- adjusting the words while treating the thought itself as doing all the work -- suggest he would see Kaplan's two-tiered character/content machinery as an unnecessary complication rather than as a formalization of his own idea? Defend one answer over the other, engaging closely with the specifics of Frege's texts rather than the secondary-literature caricature.
