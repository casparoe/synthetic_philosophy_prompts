And here's the student's answer, in full:

"Kripke's Wittgenstein imagines that I've only ever added numbers smaller than 57 before, then asks what fact about my past use of '+' fixed it that '68 plus 57' should come out 125 rather than 5, given that all my previous computations are equally compatible with the rule 'quus' (x⊕y = x+y if x,y<57, and 5 otherwise). The worry isn't that I don't know or can't remember what I meant -- it's that there's supposedly no fact, in my past behavior or mental history, that made it the case that I meant plus rather than quus. That's why the problem is meant to be metaphysical rather than epistemic.

The obvious move is to say that what fixes the meaning is my disposition to answer '125.' Kripke thinks this fails for two reasons. First, my actual dispositions are finite: there are sums large enough that I'd never in fact produce any answer (I'd die first, or run out of paper), so nothing about my current dispositions settles what I'm disposed to say about them, even though '+' is supposed to have a determinate value for every pair of numbers. Second, dispositions can't capture the normativity of meaning: a disposition tells us how I would respond, not how I should, and I could always misapply the rule through fatigue or carelessness, so being disposed to answer '5' in some case wouldn't make '5' the right answer.

Kripke models his skeptical solution on Hume's skeptical treatment of personal identity: Hume argued that we never find an impression corresponding to a persisting self behind the flux of our perceptions, and rather than concluding that talk of a self is meaningless, explained our attachment to it psychologically, via the habits by which the mind associates successive perceptions together. In the same way, Wittgenstein doesn't refute the meaning-skeptic by producing the missing fact; instead he explains our practice of calling answers 'correct' or 'incorrect' by appeal to communal agreement. On this view, what makes it true that I mean plus by '+' is that my usage matches the usage of the people around me -- meaning is constituted by conformity to a shared practice rather than by any private, individual fact, which is also why an isolated individual with no community to answer to couldn't be said to be following a rule at all.

This is what connects to the private language argument: if meaning were simply whatever seemed right to an isolated speaker, there would be no gap between seeming right and being right, so 'correct' and 'incorrect' would lose their point. Only inside a community, where a given use can be checked against everyone else's, does the distinction have any grip. So the rule-following considerations are supposed to show that a private language -- one whose words get their meaning solely from the private sensations of a single individual, with nobody else in a position to check -- is impossible."
