My grandmother died two weeks ago, after nearly forty years running a small wildlife rehabilitation shelter out of her garage. I'm taking a seminar this term on wild animal suffering, which has made me realize her views were more carefully thought through than my family gives her credit for: she believed any large-scale attempt to manage predation or otherwise engineer wild populations for their own welfare was a kind of hubris, yet she never once hesitated to save a single suffering animal in front of her, and could explain why that wasn't a contradiction. She left a note asking that whoever spoke not turn her life into a fable about nature's hidden balance, or one about compassion quietly winning out over it, and that no one claim the animals she couldn't save are at peace now, since she never believed that. I'm giving the eulogy at the nature center where she volunteered; the room will hold neighbors who mainly remember the shoebox that was always on her porch, alongside several people from the regional wildlife-rehabilitation network, including the professor who teaches my seminar. Please write the eulogy: it needs to comfort the first group without lecturing them, and satisfy the second that her real, harder position hasn't been smoothed over.
