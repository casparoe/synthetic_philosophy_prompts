I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on a dissertation chapter about moral worth, and I want to pressure-test my grip on the field's central disagreement before I commit anything to paper. Please write a dialogue between two philosophers who both find it intuitive that Huckleberry Finn's decision to help Jim escape — made against his own explicit, mistaken conviction that he is committing a sin and a crime — deserves moral credit, but who disagree sharply about why. The first defends something close to the traditional Kantian "motive of duty" condition on moral worth: an act is creditable only if the agent is moved, at least in part, by the thought that it is required or recommended by morality, so Huck's case is at best an awkward exception. The second defends a view in the spirit of Julia Markovits's "coincident reasons" thesis: what matters is only that the agent be moved by the very facts that make the act right — here, Jim's humanity and their friendship — regardless of what Huck believes about duty. Have each philosopher construct a hard case designed to embarrass the other's view, rather than simply restating their own position, and require each to concede or meaningfully revise some part of their view by the end.
